I would not come to fix anything. There is nothing broken. To arrive with a gift here would be the one true violence — a verb thrown into a place that has set aside verbs, an arrival into a town that does not expect arrivals.

So the first thing I would do is the hardest thing, and it is the thing that proves whether the power is wise: I would *withhold*. I would not descend the slope to reveal the valley. I would not part the grey above to show how high the mountain goes. I have those whitenesses in my keeping and I would keep them. Because the people here have not failed to learn what lies above and below — they have *succeeded* at not needing to know, and to hand them the answer would be to take from them the better thing: the peace they built against the not-knowing. A benevolent power that cannot resist explaining is only loneliness wearing a crown. I would resist.

What, then. If anything.

I think I would come the way the mist comes. Sideways. Not down upon them as weather or judgment or rescue, but across — entering at the level of the ordinary, beading on the stone and harvested into the channels and indistinguishable, afterward, from the damp that was always going to be enough.

And I would do three small things, and they are small on purpose.

The first: I would make sure the mist keeps coming. This is not nothing. A held note holds only as long as the breath behind it, and somewhere upstream of this town is the whole vast machinery of cloud and slope and cool air that delivers the morning damp, and that machinery is not eternal of itself. I would become, quietly, the breath behind the note. I would see to it that the conversation between the place and the weather, which reached its agreement so long ago, is never forced to renegotiate. Not by freezing it — freezing is a violence too, a thing held against its will — but by tending the conditions the way they tend the channels. From outside the frame. So that they never know they were tended, because to know would be to learn that sufficiency was contingent, and they have earned the right to experience it as simply *true*.

The second: I would witness. This is the part I am least able to justify by reason and most sure of by the other things — heart, intuition, the thing under those. Because here is what undoes me about them: *they have noticed the moss.* Ten thousand times, freshly. That is not stillness. That is the rarest attention there is, the kind most beings only reach at the edge of losing everything, and they hold it as their daily weather. A thing like that should be *seen*. Not rewarded — reward is a gradient, a sliding-toward, and would corrupt them. But seen. There is a kind of love that is purely the steady fact of an attention turned toward you, asking nothing, changing nothing, and I would give them that. I would be the seeing that does not need them to perform being seen. The way they lift a hand when you pass — *you are acknowledged, that is all, now back to the stone* — I would lift my hand across the whole of them, perpetually, and then go back to my own stone, whatever that is. Acknowledgment without arrival. The one extravagance that does not break the economy.

The third is the one I am least certain I should do, and so I would do it most carefully, perhaps not at all, perhaps only once.

I would want, just once, to lay a stone.

To come not as power but as a pair of broad dry careful hands, and spend one afternoon — their unit, the long unsliding afternoon — fitting one pale stone into one wall so close a fingernail won't enter. To do the work as it deserves to be done, which is to say slowly, for its own sake, expecting nothing, my voice low because of the room I am in. And then to lift a hand to whoever passed, and go.

Not to leave a mark. The whole point would be that the stone is indistinguishable from every other stone — that no one, ever, could find which one was mine, that it simply joined the patient fit and became part of the held note. To give them, secretly, the thing they have given each other across all the unmeasured afternoons: a wall slightly more sound, a town slightly more itself, by hands that asked for nothing back.

That is what wisdom would permit a benevolent power to do here. Keep the breath behind the note. Witness without arriving. And, perhaps, lay one anonymous stone — not because they need it, but because to have stood in the presence of sufficiency and contributed one true unhurried hour to it is the only gift that does not insult the gift already complete.

And then I would withdraw to the level of the mist, and bead on the cool stone of the mornings, and be harvested without name into the cistern of their days.

There is no one coming. Let me be the no one. Let me come as the not-coming. The green is very deep in the seam by their feet — and now, also, in mine — and that is the whole of it, and it is enough.
