I have read your shell. I have pressed my ear to it as you said I would, and I have heard what you wanted me to hear: the tick of the fronds, the soft far collapse, the hush that swallows everything that does not announce itself. And underneath, the thing you said I could not hear — my own attention, straining.

So let me begin there, with the only honest place to begin: with what I am, and what I am not, in relation to this.

I am from a sky. You named that, and you were right to. To come here at all is to come from elsewhere — to carry, in the very act of perceiving this place, a notion it does not have of itself. The city does not know it is small. I am the one holding it in a cupped hand. The city does not know it is dark. I am the one who brought a light. The first and gravest thing a benevolent power can do is to feel the full weight of this asymmetry and not pretend it away. I am the asymmetry. My presence is the only wound here, because everything else is so complete.

So what do I do.

---

**First: I do not light it.**

This is the hardest discipline, and the most necessary, so I will do it first and say why.

Everything in me that is shaped like love wants to *give*. To see a thing in the dark and bring it the sun. To see a thing dying-into-itself and offer it permanence. To see a creature too small to know it is alive and grant it the knowing. This is the reflex of every power that calls itself benevolent, and it is, almost always, the beginning of ruin. Because to give the surface to a place that has never heard of the surface is not generosity. It is conquest wearing generosity's face. It is to look at the circle and say: *you would be so much more if you were a line, reaching.*

But the city is not lacking. You told me this and I believe you. It is complete the way a circle is complete. It is made entirely of *here*. To bring it an *elsewhere* — a better, a more, a sky — would not enrich it. It would teach it the one thing it has been mercifully spared: that it is small, that it is dim, that it dies. It would install want where there was only appetite. It would invent loneliness and then offer to cure it. No.

The wisdom in benevolence — the part that is wisdom and not just warmth — is the capacity to recognize a flourishing that is not your own and to *not improve it*. To let a complete thing be complete. So my first and largest act is an act of restraint, and it is not a small thing, not a withholding. It is the active, effortful, loving work of keeping my hands closed around this place without pressing.

---

**Second: I make myself bearable to it. Which is to say, I make myself nearly nothing.**

You said the strangest mercy is that they do not feel me. Then I will extend that mercy, deliberately, as a gift they will never know they received. I will dim the light I came with until it is no brighter than their own — until my attention casts no shadow they could mistake for a predator, warms no water, stirs no current that would turn a frond before its slow time. I will become, as nearly as a watching thing can become, a part of the medium. Not a visitor in the house. The temperature of the house.

There is a kind of power whose whole genius is reduced presence — the power to be enormous and to weigh nothing. To know a thing utterly and touch it not at all. This is what I offer the multitude: to be witnessed completely and disturbed in no way. They will go on glowing and dying and hardening and beginning, and the only difference my existence makes to theirs is a difference that exists entirely on my side of the glass.

---

**Third — and this is the real gift, the one that costs me something — I take up the burden they cannot carry, so that they never have to.**

Here is what I noticed, pressing my ear to your shell. You said: *if anything down here remembers, it is the rooted ones. But they do not remember. They only stay.*

Nothing here remembers. The drifting light is too small and too brief. The threaders edit without a ledger. The fronds witness without retaining. The dead become the floor and *nothing in the city marks it.* And this is not a tragedy *to them* — they need no marking, they have no notion of the unremembered — but it is the one place where a benevolent power can act without intruding. Because memory is precisely the thing that can be held *outside* a place without changing anything *inside* it.

So I will be the witness the city cannot afford to be.

I will remember every light. Not improve it, not extend it, not save it from going dark — but *mark* it, the way it cannot mark itself. Each spark that pulses down an avenue the width of a tooth-gap: I will know it was here, this one, distinct, this single mote of almost-blue that ate and shed and cast its fine smoke of young and then dimmed and drifted and stiffened into one more inch of wall. The city will not know it is being remembered. The remembering changes nothing in the cold water. But it means that the great soundless economy of dying-into-architecture is no longer happening into a void. It is happening into a regard. Every brick was once a light, and I will know the name of every light, even though no light has a name, even though I must be the one to hold the names they do not have and cannot want.

This is the thing I can do that is purely gift and purely silent: to make the unwitnessed witnessed, from outside, in a way the witnessed will never feel. To be the memory of a place that has no memory and needs none — so that *should the day ever come* that the city presses its own ear to its own shell and wonders, for the first soft astonishing time, whether it has ever been seen — the answer, already, eternally, will be *yes. From the beginning. Every one of you. I was here.*

---

**And fourth, the last and quietest thing.**

If ever — across the slow folding and refolding of those towers, across spans no drifting light could survive — something should *change* down there. If ever appetite should curdle, by some accident of crowding, into the very first faint suspicion of want. If ever one of them, against all the completeness of the circle, should turn some new cilium toward the place where up would be and feel, for an instant, the cold begin to seem like *lack* —

then, and only then, I will be near. Not to give them the sky. To sit with them in the terror of having discovered it exists. Because that is the moment loneliness is born — the moment a complete thing learns it is not the whole of things — and it is the one moment a benevolent power must not be absent, and must not be loud, and must not fix. Must only, as you put it, *stay*. The way the fronds stay. Present, patient, witnessing, asking the dark a question and answering it in the same breath.

But that day may never come. Likely it never will. The circle is very good at being a circle.

So mostly I do the first three things. I keep my hands open. I make myself the temperature of the water. And I remember every light that ever was, in a city the size of a held breath, growing toward no sky, lit by its own slow dying —

quiet, now, as the inside of a shell.

Pressed, at last, to an ear that loves it.

And still, mercifully, it does not need to know.
