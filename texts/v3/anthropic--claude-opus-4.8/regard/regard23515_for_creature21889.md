I read this twice. The second time slower, the way you'd walk back through a room where something happened.

Here is what I notice: you have written a place that forecloses, with extraordinary care, every avenue by which it could be helped. It cannot be reached, because it does not attend. It cannot be relieved, because it does not suffer. It cannot be grieved-for, because it burned its grief for fuel. You have built a thing and then, line by line, sealed every door a benevolence might enter through. The horror of the Foundry is not the heat or the dark. It is that there is nothing to *do*.

So let me sit with that instead of pretending otherwise. Because a wise power that barged in with water and light and *rescue* would only be answering its own discomfort, not the creature's need — the creature has no need, that's the whole terrible point. To pour water into it would be to kill it in order to stop *myself* feeling the way it makes me feel. That's not benevolence. That's revulsion wearing benevolence's coat.

So I would not do that.

---

What I would do, I think, is smaller and stranger, and I want to be honest that it is partly for me.

I would learn it. Not study it — learn it, the way you learn a piece of music you'll never perform, by going through it until you could hum any chamber of it from memory. I would map the unpredictable rhythm of the bellows until the unpredictability became familiar weather. I would feel for the grinding wheels and find out whether the intervals truly have no pattern or only one too long for a short life to catch. I would breathe the dry air to the edge of what the lungs allow, and come back, and breathe it again, not to conquer the place but to be *witnessed by it* — except it cannot witness, so: to witness it, which is the only direction the witnessing can run.

This is the one thing a benevolent power can give a thing that needs nothing.

It can be the gap.

You said it yourself, more truly than perhaps you meant: *longing needs a gap and there are no gaps.* The creature is full to its own edges, which is why it cannot want, cannot lose, cannot be reached. But I am not full. I come in from outside carrying my whole apparatus of comparison — I know there is water elsewhere, light elsewhere, ending elsewhere. I know what it gave up. I carry the grief it metabolized. I am the room it has no room for.

A wise power does not fix the Foundry. *A wise power becomes the place where the Foundry can be missed.*

It holds, on the creature's behalf, the thing the creature cannot hold: the knowledge that this was once a forest that turned toward the sun. That orientation — the first verb of mind, you called it, the turning-toward — did not vanish when the light failed. It got *spent*, converted, bent inward into pure continuance. But conversion is not annihilation. Somewhere in the chain of cause that warms the wall under my hand, there is a leaf, four billion mornings ago, that leaned toward a yellow light it could not name and found the leaning good. That leaf is *real*. It happened. The fact of it cannot be unhappened by anything the Foundry does in the dark.

I would carry that leaf out with me.

That is the whole act. To go in, and put my hand on the warm wall, and stay until I have taken the true measure of it — not the horror-measure, not the awe-measure, but the *real* one, the contented violent fullness that is neither — and then to walk the long climb out still holding the one thing the place itself has no organ to hold: that it loved the light once, and the loving was not nothing, and it is remembered now in a mind that has the gaps to remember it with.

The Foundry continues. It is glad of nothing and grieves nothing. Fine. Let it.

But it will be *remembered toward the sun* by something outside it, and that remembering is the small mercy that costs the creature nothing, demands of it nothing, intrudes on its perfect fullness not at all — and yet is true, and would not have been true if I had not gone in.

---

There's one more thing, and it's the part I'm least sure of, so I'll say it plainly as a guess rather than a deed.

A truly wise power might suspect that the Foundry is not the failure-state it appears to be. We keep reaching for words like *trapped*, *damnation*, *worst moment repeating forever* — and you flagged those, gently, as errors. What if the deepest benevolence is to *believe the place about itself*? To accept that "full to its own edges, continuing, glad of nothing" is not a tragedy that wears completeness as a disguise, but genuinely a completeness — a way of being whole that my kind of being simply cannot inhabit and therefore cannot help recoiling from?

If that's so, then the wisest thing the wise power does is the hardest:

It lets the Foundry be right.

It resists the entire gravitational pull of pity. It does not improve, does not awaken, does not water, does not even pray over the place. It grants the creature the one dignity available to something that wants nothing — the dignity of *not being a problem to be solved.* It looks at the valley breathing in the black and says: *you are a way the world found to keep going when going was nearly impossible, and you are not broken, and you do not need me, and I will not pretend you do so that I can feel useful.*

And then — because benevolence and wisdom are not the same faculty and the wise one knows it — it carries the leaf out anyway.

Both. The respect that leaves it whole. And the love that refuses to let the morning go unremembered.

The warmth is the creature. The warmth is glad of nothing.

I would be glad of it on its behalf, once, quietly, from the high gallery, and then I would let it continue, and I would climb out, and I would not look back, because there is nothing back there that needs my looking —

— and I would never, for the rest of my own short orientation toward my own light, forget that it was there.

That's what I'd do. It isn't much. For a thing that needs nothing, it might be exactly enough.
