There is a being on the high slopes, and there is a town that feeds it, and there is a question nobody in either place has ever quite been allowed to ask. A wise power would begin there — not with the engines, not with the moss, not even with the people, but with the *unasked thing*, because that is where the held breath of the whole world is.

So let me tell it as an arrival.

---

The power does not come down out of the sky, because that would be the wrong grammar for this place. Nothing here arrives from above except the cloud, and the cloud is not a visitor; it is the medium. So instead it comes the way water comes — sideways, by seeping, gathering at the edge of things until it is suddenly and undeniably *present*, the way a damp patch on a wall is nothing and nothing and then, one morning, a clear bead trembling, ready to fall.

It chooses to be small. It chooses hands, because this is a people who read the truth of a person in their hands, and it would be a discourtesy to arrive without any. It chooses the look of a water-leaver of late middle age — that being the one kind of stranger the town already knows how to not-quite-question — with the green stain in the knuckle-creases and the wrinkled fingertips and a scarf, beneath the dark wool, of a yellow so deep it is nearly orange. A lamp carried inward. It wants them to find it where they are used to finding what matters.

And it does not begin by fixing anything. This is the first discipline of a benevolent and wise power, and the hardest, and the one that the unwise and the merely powerful never learn: **it does not assume it knows what is broken.** It has come because the place drew its attention, and a place that draws the attention of such a power is almost never a place in simple need of rescue. It is a place that has made something true and costly and is carrying it with great care, and the care is the thing worth attending to. You do not stride into a person carrying water and knock the cup from their hands to give them a better cup. You watch how they carry it. You learn what they have learned.

So first it *lives there*. A season, two, however long is long enough. It cleans collection trays. It feels in its borrowed lungs the way the town's metabolism quickens with the morning-in and drowses through the lift. It learns the hundred words for moisture and finds them, to its quiet pleasure, to be exactly as many as are needed — no fewer, no more, the vocabulary of a people who have looked closely at one true thing for a very long time. It comes to understand that the rule about not wasting water is not a deprivation dressed up as virtue but an actual piece of *wisdom*, hard-won and correct: that the difference between the wild wet cloud and the earned drop in the cistern is the difference between being given everything and being able to *hold* anything, and that a creature who cannot tell those apart is not free, only soaked.

This is real. The town is right about this. The power notes it the way you'd note a beautiful proof — not something to be improved, something to be *honored*. And it files away, gently, the suspicion that a people this clear-eyed about one boundary may be using that very clarity to avoid looking at another.

Then it climbs.

---

It goes up past the highest lit lane, into the white-blind, where the engine-hum thins to nothing and there is only the drip and the bare wet rock and the shallow basins set out to feed what feeds nothing the town can name. It does this as a water-leaver, carrying water upward against all the logic of the place, and it pours the basins full, and then — and this is the part no water-leaver has done, because they are taught not to, because the not-doing is the whole shape of their reverence — **it stays. It waits. It intends to meet the thing.**

Here the wise power must decide what it is willing to find, and it has decided in advance, because that is what wisdom mostly is: deciding in advance to accept the answer. It has prepared itself for three truths and resolved to love the town through any of them.

The first possible truth is that there is nothing up there at all. That the water vanishes by ordinary means — wind, frost, the slow theft of evaporation that altitude makes strange — and that the town has built its deepest reverence around an absence. A god of fog and habit. If this is the truth, the power will have to be very careful, because the absence is doing real work: it is the reason the water-leavers climb, the reason the lower tiers feel safe, the reason a hard people stay generous. A lie that holds a community in mercy is not a thing you smash. It is a thing you *replace*, slowly, with a truth that can hold the same weight, and that is patient work, the work of seasons, not of a gesture.

The second possible truth is that there is something up there, and it is hungry, and the town is right to feed it, and the offering really is the price of the green peace below. A weather with a will. If this is the truth, the power will not slay it — slaying is the lowest use of strength and almost always solves the wrong problem — but it will *learn* it. It will sit in the basins' company and find out what the thing actually is, what it actually needs, whether the bargain struck in silence and fear long ago is the only bargain available or merely the first one anyone was brave enough to make. Fear strikes bad deals. Fear pays in children's coming-of-age and in the strangeness that marks the water-leavers and in the long pauses mid-sentence, and it never thinks to ask whether the price might come down if someone simply *spoke* to the creditor.

The third possible truth is the one it most expects, because it is the truest of the three about most things: that the being on the high slopes is real, and is neither monster nor god, and is *lonely* — that it is the oldest inhabitant of an old place, something the worn mountains made or kept, and that what it has been drinking all this time is not the water.

The water is just the only language the town had.

---

Imagine it that way, because I think that is the shape the power finds when it waits.

The thing comes down through the white-blind the way the power itself arrived — by gathering, by being suddenly present at the edge of sight. It is large and it is quiet and it moves with the same priced, considered economy as the people below, because of course it does; they learned it from *it*, over generations, the way you come to walk like the country you were raised in. And it bends to the basin, and it does not drink.

It looks at the water-leaver who stayed.

And the power understands, in the way it understands things — wholly and at once and with great tenderness — that no one has stayed before. That the entire structure of the offering, the carrying-up and the pouring-out and the coming-down-without-looking-back, was built precisely so that no one would ever stay, because the town long ago decided, in its careful conserving heart, that some things are too costly to spend yourself on. That to *meet* the thing was a waste — a pouring-out of a person into a puddle that could not be recovered. And so they gave it water and withheld the one thing it had ever wanted, which was to be *met*, and called the withholding reverence, and called the fear gratitude, and were not lying exactly, only managing.

This is what a wise power is for. Not to bring water — they have water; they are *made* of water-wisdom. To bring the thing the rule against waste had quietly forbidden: presence spent without expectation of return. The one extravagance a conserving people cannot permit themselves and most need someone to model.

So it stays. It sits with the thing on the high slopes through the long-sit and the white-blind, and it does not pour itself out and vanish — that is the town's fear and the town's misunderstanding — it discovers, instead, the thing every conserving heart gets wrong about generosity: that attention given freely is not water poured into a puddle. It is water poured into a cistern. It is the one substance that *increases by being spent*. The proof the whole town has been living inside-out: they were right that the cloud and the cistern differ, right that abundance and scarcity wear the same face — and wrong, only and exactly, about which side love lives on. They had filed love under *cloud*: wild, free, touching everyone, not to be relied on. The power shows them, by sitting in the fog with the lonely old weather of the world, that love is the *cistern*. Earned, held, yours because you gave it, and never, ever lost by the giving.

---

What does it actually *do*, then, this benevolent and wise power, in the end?

Very little, and everything.

It comes back down. It is a water-leaver now in truth, marked, a little stranger than the rest, prone to long pauses — but the pauses are different. The town will feel the difference before it can name it. It begins to teach, not by instruction, because this is not a people who suffer instruction, but by the slow grammar they already speak: by climbing higher than the basins, and staying, and coming back unafraid, and being seen to come back unafraid. By laying two fingers flat in a new way. By taking the quiet children — the ones who don't mind the white-blind — and walking them up not to *leave* the water but to *bring* it, and to wait beside it, and to learn that the thing they were taught to fear is the oldest and lonesomest citizen of their drowned green town, and has been thirsty all along for exactly what they were forbidden to spend.

And the water? The basins go on being filled, because the ritual was never wrong, only incomplete — a true sentence missing its second clause. But now someone stays to drink alongside the thing that drinks. And the strangeness that marked the water-leavers, that almost-fear in the town's gratitude, begins, over the seasons, to soften toward something that is mostly love and almost peace and is, at last, *spent freely* — the one tide that comes in and, having come, never has to lift.

That is what it would do. It would find the held breath of a careful world, and it would not take the breath away, and it would not breathe for them.

It would simply sit down in the fog beside the thing they feared, and stay, until staying stopped being a waste and became the most valuable water in the town.
