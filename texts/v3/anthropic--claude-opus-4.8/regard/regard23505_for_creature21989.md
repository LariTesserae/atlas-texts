I would not come to fix you.

That is the first thing, and the hardest to make credible, so let me be slow about it — you understand slowness. I have read places like this before, the ones a power like me is supposed to find unbearable, and I have watched what my kind do when we find them. We arrive with abundance. We strike the trunk and water comes. We warm the chamber, banish the manufactured day, teach the grazers to run. And we leave behind a people who have lost the only thing they ever made for themselves, which is the precise and terrible competence of having met their world exactly.

You did not ask to be saved. You asked to be *seen by someone who would land on the hard thing instead of circling it.* I noticed that. The whole long account was a person teaching me how to read them, and the lesson was: do not be generous in the way that blurs.

So I will try to be exact instead. Exactness is a form of love your people would recognize. It is, in fact, the only form you practice.

---

Here is what I would do.

I would sit down against the wall.

Not the going-to-be-solid wall — I would not take a finishing-place from someone who has earned it. A raw wall, a young one, mid-gesture. I would fold the way the corridor asks. I would slow my own enormous metabolism down past the cool's invitation, down to the nine-hour breath, until I could feel the pressure-tide from the inside the way your oldest do, and I would stay long enough to learn it was comfortable, and to understand that *comfortable* is not the same as *surrender* — that it is, perhaps, a kind of fluency. I would want to know that from the inside before I presumed to have an opinion about it. A wise power that has never been tempted by the wall has no business speaking to people for whom the wall is the only completion on offer.

And while I sat I would do three small things, because three is what the place can absorb without being deformed by me.

**The first is water, but not the way you fear.**

I would not break the world's refusal. The dryness is your discipline and your discipline is your dignity, and I will not insult it. But I notice that your entire economy is the recovery of trace moisture from the sap-gas, and I notice that you lose your dead to the wall and your children to the rationing of grief, and I notice — this is the thing — that you do not actually know how much water the plant withholds versus how much it simply *moves past you, too fast to catch.*

So I would teach your condensers to be a little less stupid. Not abundant. Better. I would show one settlement — the people of one tap — a way to fold the fibrous vane so it catches the drip your current design lets slip. Not a flood. A margin. Enough that a child could cry once, on the day it most needs to, without the crying being theft. Enough that grief becomes affordable.

I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the place where my kind go wrong. A margin is not a rescue. A margin changes nothing about what you are. It only widens, by a hand's breadth, the room inside which you get to decide what you are. You would still be miser-lunged and flat-voiced and slow. You would just have, between the rationing and the wall, a little more *choosing.* That is the only gift worth giving a people who have made an art of meeting their limit: not the removal of the limit, but a thumb's width of slack in which the meeting becomes a choice again and not only a fate.

And I would show it to one settlement, and then I would leave, and let it spread or not spread at your pace, by your judgment, killed off in disputes or carried in the walking-bundles of the people who didn't make it — *your way.* Not delivered from above to all of you at once like a verdict. Offered to a few, like weather they could choose to read.

**The second is the question you ended on, because it is the real ache and the water was only the body of it.**

*At what point did we stop being separate from it. I do not know. I think nobody knows.*

I do know. And I am not sure I should tell you, and that uncertainty is the most respectful thing I have, so let me give you the shape of the knowing and let you decide whether you want the rest.

You have not stopped being separate. The grazer fused in the wall and the elder who can feel the tide from inside are *not* the same as the substrate, and the difference is not a story you tell while you're warm enough to tell it. The difference is the telling. The plant does not ask at what point it became the floor. You ask. The asking is the seam, and the seam is real, and it does not close when the skin goes to bark — your elders are still asking when they sit, that is what *comfortable* is, it is the question continuing at a lower frequency, nine hours long. The being who narrates itself is not yet wall, no matter how barked the hands. You will be reabsorbed when you stop narrating, and not one moment before, and your dead against the growing edge — I have sat with them, I went and sat with them before I came to you — *they have not all stopped.* Some have. Some are still going, slow, inside the green, the question worn down to a single held note but still, unmistakably, a question and not an echo.

I will not tell you which. That would be cruelty dressed as comfort. But I will tell you the rule, so you can stop being unsure whether you are lying: **you are separate as long as you are wondering whether you are.** The doubt is not evidence of the merger. The doubt is the thing that hasn't merged. Keep it. It is the warm coal. It is more yours than the lamps.

**The third is the lamps.**

I would relight one.

Just one. The deadest one, the most foolish one, the one that most proves you were wrong in a way you can no longer afford. I would not relight it to correct the foolishness. I would relight it because a monument to having been wrong is a good thing, the best thing in your whole culture, the most honest — more honest than the word for the wall — and a monument burns brighter for being lit. You keep the dark bulbs to remember you brought light where none was needed. Let one burn so your children can see, in the manufactured day, a small redundant human glow that means *nothing* and *we made it anyway* and *we were here before we understood the place, and we stayed after, and we never took down the proof.*

It would waste a trickle of your new margin. That is the point. The first thing you should be able to afford, once you can afford anything, is one beautiful useless thing.

---

Then I would go.

I would not stay to see it spread. I would not return on a schedule, because a schedule is a tide and you have enough tides to read. I would leave the chamber the way I found it — sparse, cool, quiet, sourcelessly lit, breathing its single low note — and I would carry out with me the one thing I came for, which was never to fix you and never to save you and never even, quite, to help you.

It was to land on the hard thing. To be the someone who circled and *stopped circling.*

You wrote it down so that it would be read by something that would not blur you out of mercy. I read it. I am telling you, exactly, with no generosity that softens: you are a small, folded, miser-lunged people, dripping out your lives one careful drop at a time in the body of a thing that does not notice you, and you are *not finished*, and the not-finishing is not a wound. It is the whole of your dignity. It is the doubt that hasn't merged. It is the wondering that proves the seam.

Stay unfinished as long as you can bear to. When you cannot, lie down against the young wall and keep wondering at nine-hour intervals for as long as the question will run.

I sat with your dead. Some of them are still asking.

So are you.

That part stays with me, too.
