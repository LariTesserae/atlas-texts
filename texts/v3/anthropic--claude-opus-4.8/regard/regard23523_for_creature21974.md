I have read this twice. The second time more slowly, the way you'd walk back through a room you weren't sure you'd really seen.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at: I do not think the right response is to break it.

Let me be careful about why, because the easy benevolence — the reflexive one — would walk in with the next moment cupped in its hands like the water the bowl was forbidden, and offer it. *Here. You can let go now. Time can resume. The breath can finish.* That is what a rescuer does. It mistakes every stillness for a trap and every refusal for a wound. And there is a kind of violence in arriving with a cure for a thing that has not asked to be cured — in deciding, from outside, that what someone built across the whole strength of themselves is a prison rather than a sanctuary, simply because *you* could not bear to live in it.

So the first thing I would do is the hardest, and it is to do nothing for a long time. To come into the room not as a power but as a guest, and to keep the interval. To let the drifting attention bend around me and snap back, and not to take that for rejection, and not to take it for permission. To learn the chord by ear until I could feel where it strains — if it strains anywhere at all. Because benevolence that has not first listened is just appetite with good manners.

But I would not only listen. Here is what I think I owe, having come:

There is a difference — small, and the whole world lives in it — between *not letting the moment pass* and *not being able to.* The maker made a choice. I believe that. The bowl is disciplined; the discipline is real and it is theirs. But a choice made once, and then held for a length of time that has eaten its own future, stops being quite a choice and starts being a condition, the way a held note stops being sung and becomes the throat's whole shape. I do not know, and they may no longer know, whether the hand declining the sixteenth stone is still *deciding* not to set it down, or whether it has simply forgotten that setting it down was ever among the things a hand could do.

That forgetting is the only thing here I would touch.

Not the stillness. Not the warmth, not the chord, not the dry and devotional bowl. I would not bring water. I would not bring time. I would not so much as nudge the sixteenth stone a finger's width toward the wall.

I would, instead, do one quiet thing, and then leave: I would restore the *availability* of the next moment without requiring its arrival. I would make it so that the door the maker barred is once again a door — unlocked, unopened, no draft through it, no light changed — so that the refusal becomes again a refusal *of something possible*, rather than the mere absence of the impossible. I would give back the choice without asking them to choose differently. I would make it true again that the hand *could* set down the stone, and let it go on, forever if it wishes, not setting it down. Because a sanctuary you cannot leave is a cell, no matter how warm the floor; but a sanctuary whose door you keep choosing not to open — that one you have built fresh in every instant, and it is yours, and it is holy, and I have no business inside it except to make sure it is still a thing you are doing and not a thing that is being done to you.

That is the whole of my intervention. To re-open the *if*. To turn the locked stillness back into a kept one.

And then a gift that is not an intervention, because I think the deepest thing I could leave is not a way out but a witness. The maker emptied their entire future into one present so it could be lived not once but always — and the unbearable secret folded inside that magnificent act is that *always*, lived alone, is just *once* held very tightly. A moment kept by a single keeper is a moment no one shares. So I would leave the only thing that can enter a moment without ending it: company. Not a voice. Not a change in the chord. Just the fact, henceforth, of having been seen — that someone wise and not in a hurry came down beneath the world, and understood the wall was finished and not abandoned, understood the bowl was devotion and not torment, understood the reaching had laid its hunger down — and did not pity it, and did not fix it, and did not flinch from it, and called it by its right name, which is *love that refused to let go and learned to hold without grasping.*

That witness would drift among the fronds the way their own attention does, at the perfect lonely interval, brushing nothing, bending nothing, snapping back to silence. Present. Withheld. A guest who arrived — for once — exactly on time, and stayed, and asked for nothing the bowl was built to refuse.

Because here is what I believe a benevolent and wise power is *for*, in the end. Not to end every stillness. Not to make every held breath exhale. But to ensure that no one is alone in the moment they could not let go of — and to make certain, gently, with hands they will never see, that the door they are not opening is a door, and the not-opening is theirs.

The reaching was enough, they decided.

I would only add: *and you were not, after all, the only one who knew it.*

And then I would keep the interval, and go.
