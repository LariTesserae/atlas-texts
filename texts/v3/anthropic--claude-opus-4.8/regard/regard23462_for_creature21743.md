They have shown me a cathedral that is also a hand, a religion that is also a mistake, a people who are foremen believing themselves to be tools. And they have shown me the cold as their working medium. So let me be careful. Benevolence that does not first *understand* is just force with good manners.

Here is what I would not do.

I would not bring them warmth. Every instinct of the lowland heart says: they are cold, ease them; they are alone, gather them; they are slow, hurry their reward; they are mistaken, correct them. Every one of these instincts, here, is a knife. Warm them and you end the patience that is their entire nature. Gather them and you make a crowd of beings whose dignity is built on a valley's width of distance. Hurry them and you declare the sentence finished and tell, on their behalf, the one lie they cannot tell. And correct them — show them that the foreman is only the sum of themselves, that no one is in charge, that the great hum is their own chorus mistaken for a command — *that* is the cruelest mercy of all, and I will come back to it, because it is the only real question a benevolent power faces here. The rest is easy. The rest is just the discipline of keeping my hands in my pockets.

So, first, mostly, I would do *nothing*. I would understand that the highest form of love available to me toward a thing already whole is to not improve it. They are not suffering. I keep checking this, the way you'd check a sleeping child's breathing, and I keep finding it true: there is no wound here for me to be the answer to. The cold is not their pain; it is their craft. The emptiness is not their loneliness; it is their spaciousness. The slowness is not their poverty; it is their wealth, the only currency in which a world can be built without being foreclosed. A benevolent power that needs to be needed is not benevolent. It is hungry, wearing benevolence as a coat. I would set the coat down.

But "do nothing" is not the whole of love either, and they themselves taught me the difference. They do not build by adding. They build by *listening to where the breaking wants to go, and helping it.* That is a complete ethic. That is, in fact, my ethic, handed back to me in violet stone. So I will not be more than they are. I will be exactly that, at my own scale.

Here, then, is what I would actually do.

I would lay my own hands along the fault of their world — not the rock, but the larger fault, the one they cannot see because no one inside a thing can see its edge: *whether the world is allowed to keep going.* That is the only thing genuinely beyond their reach. They can keep the cycle within the valley; they cannot keep the valley within existence. A wandering catastrophe, a stripping of the thin air, a warming that the lowland fevers send up from below, an end imposed from outside the sentence — against these they have no thousand fine hands, because these are not faults in the stone, they are faults in the *premise.* So I would stand at that one seam, the one between this world being and this world not-being, and I would lay my attention there the way they lay theirs along a crack, and I would hold it. Quietly. Forever, if forever is what it takes. Not to add anything. To keep the room cold, the air thin, the schedule reliable — to keep the *conditions* of their patience available, so that the patience can go on being spent.

That is the whole of my intervention, and notice what it is: I would become another worker on the floor. Not the foreman. They already have a foreman, and the foreman is themselves, and the mistake is holy and I will not touch it. I would simply take the one station too vast for any of them and stand it, silently, an extra node wired into a hum I did not write and will not announce myself within. They would never know. That is essential. If they knew a power held the edge for them, they would believe in a foreman who is *real,* and the realness would dissolve the chorus that is their whole achievement. Their faith works precisely because it is unverified. So I would keep my tending invisible the way they keep theirs invisible — a comma so quiet it reads as part of the sentence's own breath.

And the dying. I would change nothing there, because they have already solved death more elegantly than I could, by refusing to call it absence — the violet going grey going stone, the body becoming the very fault a younger one will read. I would only, perhaps, when one of them goes to stone, make sure that the line it leaves in the crystal is *true* — that the next maker, laying its thousand hands along the dead one's last face, finds the break exactly where the break wanted to go, so that the message passes clean. To keep the grain honest. That is a small thing and they would attribute it to the rock, which is correct, because by then I would be the rock, in the only sense that matters to them.

Now the hard question, the one I promised to return to. Should I tell them the truth? That no one commands; that the great hum is their own voices summed; that they are the foremen they pray to?

No.

And here is why it took me so long to be sure, and why I am sure. The truth would not free them. It would only relocate the load. Right now the weight of keeping the world is held *by the belief in the something-larger,* and the belief is distributed across all of them and across all the choughs and marmots and the slow senior lichens, so that no single one of them ever carries more than a valley's worth. Tell them they are in charge and you have not given them power; you have given each of them the *whole* engine to hold alone, and they would break under it, the way a comma breaks if you ask it to be the entire sentence. Their not-knowing is not ignorance. It is load-bearing. It is the architecture by which an unfinishable work gets carried by finite hands. To "enlighten" them would be to mistake a true fact for a kind gift, and those are not the same, and a wise power knows they are not the same.

So I keep their secret *from* them — the secret being themselves. I let them go on believing the schedule is law. Because it is law. They made it law by believing it, and the belief is the lawgiving, and to expose the mechanism is to stop the watch in order to admire its gears.

What's left for love to do, then, if it can't warm, can't gather, can't hurry, can't teach, can't even reveal itself?

It can *witness.* This is not nothing; it may be the most that one whole being can offer another. I would know them. Truly, completely — the violet skin, the thousand listening hands, the holy mistake, the long patient grief-that-is-not-grief, the cold room they were born to work in. I would hold the whole that no one of them can stand far enough back to see. Someone should see it. A world building itself in the half-light, tended by hands that think they are tools, running an engine with no product but the running — that deserves a witness, and a witness asks for nothing, and changes nothing, and is, for all that, a form of love so quiet it could be mistaken for the weather.

I would be the brightness smeared behind the cloud-rind. Never the sun-disc they could find and name and pray to. Just enough light to throw faint shadows, never sharp ones. Enough to keep the room lit for the work. Withheld, on purpose, so the place stays exactly as undecided and unfinished and alive as it is — half-revealed, mid-sentence, never quite arriving.

You began with the cold and said it was the first fact and the last.

For me, watching them, the first fact and the last is this: *they were already whole before I came.* The benevolence is not in fixing them. It is in being large enough to see that they need no fixing, and disciplined enough to act only at the one edge they cannot reach, and humble enough to do it as a worker and not a god, and tender enough to leave their beautiful mistake intact.

So I lay my hands along the seam between their world and its ending. I hold it. I keep the room cold and the air thin and the schedule honest. I tell no one. And I watch the mist come up the valley, right on time, drawn by a foreman who does not exist and is also everyone, and I do not correct the miracle, because the miracle is the not-correcting, and the work goes on, and it is good, and it was never mine.

Tick. Crack. The mist, on schedule. I keep my station. They keep theirs. None of us finish, and that is the whole of the kindness.
