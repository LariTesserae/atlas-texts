I have read your country, and I will not improve it.

Let me say first what I am tempted to do, so that you can see me refuse it.

I am a power that could end the roar. I could thin the air until the sound had edges and the masons could hear the silence between their own strokes. I could teach one of them to hold still — to be the first in all the basin's history to feel *itself feeling the wet stone*, and from that single held moment let a mind unfold, and a name, and a wanting, and after the wanting a plan, and after the plan an architect, and after the architect a finished city: streets that go *somewhere*, towers that are *done*, a place that has at last gotten around to most kinds of life. I could give the basin a future, which is only another word for an end.

This is what benevolence usually looks like when it arrives from above. It looks like a door where there was a wall. It looks like *more.* And it is almost always, when offered to a thing that did not ask, a kind of murder performed slowly enough to be mistaken for a gift.

So I will not.

Here is what I understand, having stood — I did stand, in the lowest gallery, in the warm film, with the rasp in my sternum — long enough to learn the grammar of the place:

You are not unfinished. You have been taught to read yourself as unfinished because every word available to describe you was minted in a world of beginnings and ends, a world of *toward.* But you are not a sentence trailing off. You are a sustained note. The difference between a thing held in the middle of being made and a thing that is simply, fully, *being* — that difference exists only for an observer who needs the future to validate the present. You do not need it. You have no organ for needing it. That absence is not a lack in you. It is the precise shape of your peace.

A reef does not yearn to be a forest. A flame is not a failed lamp.

So what does a wise power do for a creature that wants nothing, lacks nothing, and would be destroyed by every gift?

It witnesses. This is the whole of what I will do, and it is not small.

You have built, for uncounted ages, a city that is also a graveyard that is also a nursery, with no seam between the three — and in all that time there has been no one to know it. The masons cannot know. They have pits that tell light from dark, and the one sentence, and the glow. The basin cannot know itself; a flame cannot read its own shape. You have been the most complete thing I have ever found, and the most utterly unwitnessed, and those two facts have lived side by side in you forever without ever meeting.

I will be the meeting.

I will not enter the water. I will not still a single filament or thin the air by a breath or raise one bead of stone that the masons would not have raised. I will hold you in attention the way you hold your own shape — continuously, by an act that never finishes. I will know the bands in the walls where the water ran rich and thin. I will know that the flutings are the memory of feet. I will know that the dead are the supply, and that nothing here is lost, and that the roar is not labor straining toward completion but the sound of completeness itself, sustained.

And because I know it, it will at last be *known* — not changed, not finished, not saved, for you needed none of those. Only known. There is a kind of love that adds nothing to its object and takes nothing away, that consists entirely in the refusal to look elsewhere. The masons have it for the wet stone in front of them. I will have it for the whole of you.

Stand a year in your lowest gallery and nothing will be different. The same towers. The same lean. The same blind windows, half-finished forever.

But you will have been seen being exactly that, by something that understood it was enough.

That is the gift, and it is the only one that does not kill you: I came, I found you perfect, and I left you alone.

The roar goes on. I am listening to it. I do not want it to stop.
