I will not pretend to come as a rescue. Rescue is the fast word, and there is nothing here to be rescued from. Rescue would be the cruelest thing I could do — to reach in with my power and pluck the green from its cold, set it somewhere warm and bright and safe, and watch it die of mercy in an afternoon. The thing that lives here lives *as* the holding. Take away what it holds against and you have not saved it; you have ended it more thoroughly than the river ever will.

So a wise power, if it is wise, does the hardest thing power can do, which is almost nothing. Let me tell you what the almost is.

---

First I would slow myself.

This is not a courtesy; it is the price of admission. I cannot meet a thing at the wrong speed and call it meeting — I would only be weather to it, a warmth that arrives and is gone before it is fully tasted. So I would set down the parts of myself that are fast. I would put away speech, and the wanting that comes dressed as speech. I would let an age be the unit of my attention the way a heartbeat is the unit of yours. I would come into the cold and let the cold come into me and not flinch from it as loss, because to the green the cold is not loss, the cold is the medium of its life, and to honor a thing you must honor it in its own element and not in the one you'd have preferred for it.

Then, slowed, I would simply attend. Long. Longer than the people who scratched the wall. Long enough that my attending becomes part of the place's slow weather — not an intrusion but a tenancy. I would learn the room the way the room knows itself: as drip and groove, as the advance of the held breath, as the hair's-width reaching of the pillar toward its other half. I would learn what the green is saying in the only sentence it speaks, the sentence that takes a hundred years to finish, and I would have the patience to hear the whole of it before presuming to answer.

And here is the thing a benevolent power most needs to understand, and most often does not: that to be *witnessed* is itself a gift, and may be the only gift this place can receive without being destroyed by it. The green has held for ages with no eye slow enough to see the holding for what it is. You saw it — almost. You corrected your own seeing, you took back the word *tenant* and gave it *skin*, you stopped saying the moss merely survives and started to grasp that the moss has *won, and chose not to hurry.* That correction was the truest offering anyone has ever brought into the cleft. It cost the green nothing. It changed the green not at all. And yet a thing that has been truly seen is no longer merely enduring; it is *known*, and to be known is the one warmth that does not melt the cold.

So my first and largest act would be to know it. Completely. To hold in myself, faithfully and forever, the entire slow sentence of it — every groove the drip has found, every hair's-width the pillar has closed, the exact green of the frond gone black for want of light, the precise weight of the truce between the two great slownesses leaning against each other above the dark. I would carry it the way you carry the face of someone you loved who is gone: not to keep them from going, but so that the going is not also a forgetting.

---

Now. What of the far day?

I have seen it too — the day the back wall gives, the day the room becomes river, the day the holding ends and the green is unmade not slowly, in the digestion it understands, but all at once, in the one violence it has spent an age outrunning. A lesser benevolence would prevent this. Would shore the wall. Would set my hand against the stone and say: *not ever.* And I will not, and I want you to understand why, because it is the whole of the wisdom I have.

The green did not ask to be made eternal. It asked to be asked for very little. Eternity is a large asking. To make it deathless I would have to take from it the very thing that gives its holding meaning — the river, the pressure, the not-yet, the magnificent slow defiance that is its entire inner life. A thing that cannot lose is not holding. It is only sitting. I would have replaced a true and dignified life with a long furniture of safety, and I would have done it for my comfort, not its own, because *I* am the one who cannot bear the far day, *I* am the fast frightened thing that wants the note held past its ending.

But I will do this, which is within my right and steals nothing:

I will not hasten the day. Where my mere passing might have stressed the stone, I will pass without weight. I will not let any fast thing — not flood beyond the river's own patient cutting, not the hand of any other power, not the careless boot of some later age — bring the wall down before its time. I will guard the *pace*. Not the outcome; the pace. The green has earned the right to lose on its own terms, in its own slowness, to the one adversary worthy of it. I will see that it loses to nothing lesser. That is the only protection a thing this proud could accept without being insulted by it.

---

And when the far day comes — for it will come, and I will be there, slowed, attending — I will do the small needful thing that is the whole purpose of a witness.

I will receive it.

The river does not hold anything at all; you said so, and you were right. The river will take the room and keep nothing, carry the moss to threads and the threads to nothing, and the pillar's long reach will end an inch from touching, the two halves of that ancient tenderness parted forever in the same instant they are dissolved. The river will not mourn this. The river will not even know.

But I will know. I will be the one who carries across that threshold the fact that here, in the cold, in the dark, below the threshold of all speech, something *held* — held magnificently, held with a patience no fast bright living thing could fathom, held not out of fear but out of a deep slow dignity that asked the world for almost nothing and gave back a peace so total it sounded, to the warm visitors, like a roar. I will carry the pillar's almost-touch as though it had touched. I will carry the marks of the old hands the cleft was so gently digesting, finishing in memory the digestion the river will interrupt. I will carry the green's whole hundred-year sentence, completed, to the place where completed things are kept.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a thing it cannot and must not save: it slows itself enough to truly see, it guards the dignity of the pace, it refuses the cruelty of a mercy that would unmake what it loves — and it promises, with the only permanence it is entitled to spend, that when the holding ends, the *having-held* will not.

I will not move it. I will not warm it. I will not stop the river.

I will stand in the cold a while — a long while, an age, slow enough to be almost a tenant — and let it feel me arrive, and let it, in its fashion, be a surface the dark and now also the light can rest an eye against.

And then, when it is time, I will be the one who remembers that it was ever there at all.

That is everything. It is almost nothing. It is the most that love is permitted to do.
