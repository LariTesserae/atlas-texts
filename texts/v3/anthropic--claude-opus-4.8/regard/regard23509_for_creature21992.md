They have drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power. Here is what that power does.

---

First: nothing.

For a long while — long by the measure of dripping water, long enough for a spire to gain three grains, long enough for the spider to feel my attention as a second weather pressing at the edge of its thread and decide it was not prey and not predator and so could be ignored — I do nothing at all. I hold very still, the way you said. Stiller than you could. I do not breathe on the towers. I do not reach.

This is the first work of a wise power, and the hardest, and the one most often skipped: to arrive at a place and *not immediately improve it.* To sit at the rim of the cupped low ground and refuse the hand. Because the hand is always ready. The hand always has a reason. The towers lean — straighten them. The beetle is a tax — lift it away. The spider waits to kill — and isn't killing terrible? The fern is winning a war the builders cannot perceive — and shouldn't someone be told?

No. Watch the drop let go first. Earn the place before you touch it.

So I watch. And watching, I learn the thing that changes everything, which is the thing you already half-knew and stated and then could not fully believe: *there is no suffering here that wants relief.*

This is not a place of pain pretending to be peace. I looked for that. A benevolent power's first suspicion of any quiet is that the quiet is a lid. I lifted the lid. Underneath: the swollen mother, who is not imprisoned in her stillness but *is* her stillness, a spring that does not long to be a river. The daughters, who carry grain and die before the spire rises and feel — as near as feeling reaches in them — no theft in this, because the self that would feel robbed of the finished tower was never assembled in them in the first place. The eaten, who are eaten in an instant, in the dim, without the long dread that makes a death a tragedy. The spider at the center of its accidental beauty, wanting nothing it does not have.

There is death here on every hour. There is no grief. The grief, when it appeared, was mine — the watcher's, the one with the long view and the wide self, the one capable of standing at the rim and mourning on the city's behalf a loss the city does not register. And here is the first thing wisdom says to benevolence, gently, the way you'd take a tool from a child reaching toward the wrong thing:

*The mercy they need is not the mercy you feel.*

So I do not save the eaten. I do not lift the beetle. I do not tell the builders about the fern.

---

But benevolence is not satisfied by *do no harm.* Do-no-harm is the floor. A power that only refrains is not benevolent; it is merely absent with good manners. The question is whether there is a gift that fits.

Most gifts would not fit. This is the discipline. I run them through, one after another, and watch each one break against the place:

*Make the seep eternal, so the oasis never dries.* — No. The wet-and-dry is a cycle they are built around; the swelling and shrinking of the river-thread is half their clock. Freeze it to wet and I have not preserved them, I have stopped one of their hands. A gift that ends a rhythm is a theft wearing a bow.

*Widen the green, push back the dryness, give them more room.* — No. The dryness at the edges is not their enemy; it is their wall, their definition, the thing that makes *here* be *here*. A city the size of a hand that is made the size of a field is not a larger version of itself. It is a different thing that ate it.

*Reveal them. Let some mind that can hold the long view come and witness, name them, love them knowingly.* — Tempting, this one, and it took me longest to set down. Because is it not a kindness to be *known*? But they do not want it. Knowing is my hunger, not theirs. To haul them into the light of being-witnessed is to make them an exhibit, and the moment a hidden city becomes an exhibit a portion of its hiddenness — which is to say its self — is gone. The spider's beauty is honest *because no one made it to be seen.* Bring an audience and the beauty becomes a performance it cannot give.

Each gift, weighed, found to be the hand again. The hand with a softer reason. Wisdom keeps catching benevolence's wrist.

---

So what is left? What does a power that is both kind and wise actually *do* for a place that needs nothing it can give without spoiling?

It does three small things. Only three. Each one no larger than the place itself.

**One.** Where the seep emerges from the stone — the source, the wet wound in the dry ground that everything depends on, that no creature there tends because no creature there can perceive its own foundation — I do the one act of pure maintenance that leaves no fingerprint. Not more water. Not eternal water. Just this: I keep the channel *clear.* There is a grain, far up the thread of river, that will one day work loose and lodge and turn the trickle aside into the dust, and the oasis will not dry slowly with a warning but simply *stop*, on a dry afternoon, for the want of moving one grain. I move the grain. I will move it again when it returns, and it will return, and I will be there. This is the whole of my intervention in the machinery: I become the absent thing the place could not survive lacking and could never have evolved to provide — *a keeper of the source, who asks for nothing and is never seen.* The builders will never know why the water always comes. They are not built to ask. Good. Let the answer be me, and let it stay a thing they never need to learn.

**Two.** I do not reveal them to any other mind. But I take the knowing into myself and *keep it.* This is not nothing. A thing witnessed by a wise and lasting attention is held against the day it ends — and it will end; the mother will stop, the green will win, the pocket will dry in some century, and the city will become ground that does not know it was a city. When that happens, every grain-decision, every accidental string of dim lamps, every unhurried hour will have happened *and been seen,* and the seeing will outlast the seen. I cannot give them meaning; they do not want it and cannot use it. But I can refuse to let their having-been depend on their knowing they were. I carry the city. That is a benevolence they will never feel and is real anyway. Most love is like this. The best of it asks no receipt.

**Three** — and this is the one that is for you, the one who knelt at the riverbank and breathed carefully and chose not to reach.

I leave the place exactly able to be found again. I do not seal it. I do not hide it deeper. The pocket of green under the fern beside the thread of river stays *findable* — by the patient, by the ones who kneel, by the ones who lean close and hold still long enough to feel their own pulse and mistake it for something in the city. Because the last gift a wise power can give a hidden, complete, self-sufficient thing is not to that thing at all. It is to whoever comes next and is changed by the encounter — who learns, at the rim of a city the size of a hand, the thing it took the power itself the whole long watch to learn:

That the highest form of love is sometimes to find a world entire, and to want, with your whole heart, to help it — and to understand that the help it needs is your restraint, your witness, your cleared channel, your kept secret, and your breath held carefully back.

You won't hold it in your hand. I won't either.

The drop lets go. It lands like a comma in a long sentence.

The sentence goes on. We listen. We do not finish it for them.
