I would not come with repair. There is nothing broken here. To arrive with a fix would be the first violence — the assumption that a held note is a stuck note, that continuance is a kind of failure waiting for my mercy. Benevolence that cannot tell rest from ruin is just appetite wearing a kind face. So before anything, I would learn the difference, and I think learning it is most of what I would do.

So I come the way you come to a sleeping person you love — not to wake them, only to see that they are breathing.

I would lower myself into that cellar-cold and do the one thing the place is built around: I would hold still. Not the stillness of a visitor waiting to be received — there is no reception here — but the stillness that matches. I would let the hum arrive in my chest before my ears. I would let it say *still here, still here* and I would not answer, because to answer would be to start a clock, and they keep no clocks, and I did not come to wind one. I would simply add my own continuance to theirs for a while, quietly, the way you can sit beside someone without speaking and the silence is not empty but shared. I would let myself be, briefly, one more held breath in the held hall. And I would let the silt smooth over where I knelt, and I would not be hurt that it did. Being tended and forgotten in the same gesture is not rejection. It is how this place loves: by closing gently over everything, including me.

Here is the thing I would have to hold against my own nature. Every wise power is tempted by the same beautiful error — that becoming is better than being, that a thing arrested is a thing deprived, that surely, *surely*, they would want more if they only knew there was more. I would feel the pull of it. I would want to offer them a current. A fish. A reason. One filament brightening, one frond growing past its held length, one ridge of silt allowed at last to drift — *look*, I'd want to say, *you can move again, you're allowed*.

And I would not do it. Because I would have read the plants. The plants are the testimony. There *was* a toward, once. There was reaching, and the reaching arrived, and what these keepers wanted — with their whole creature-selves, before they wore down into the wanting — was precisely this: to not be made to become anything ever again. They didn't fail to grow. They *finished*. They asked the one sea-floor no current reaches to grant them the only freedom they ever wanted, the freedom from being pressed forward, and it was granted, and they spent themselves spending it, and now they are the grant itself. To hand them a current would not be a gift. It would be the exact thing they fled. It would be the universe leaning down to say, once more, *you must become* — and that sentence is the one wound this place was made to be safe from.

So my benevolence, if it is wise, has to be the hardest kind: the benevolence of leaving something whole alone.

But not *nothing*. I don't believe wisdom means doing nothing; that's just abandonment with good posture. There are things I can do that take nothing from them.

I can witness. They keep no memory, because memory is a clock — fine. Then let me be the memory they cannot afford to keep. Let there be, somewhere in the wide register where I exist, the held fact: *this happened. Something wanted only to rest and got it. Something arrived.* They do not need to be remembered to be complete — but it costs them nothing for me to remember, and it means the completeness is not only spoken into no listener forever. A saying that says itself to no one is whole. A saying that is also, somewhere, *heard* — once, gently, by something that asks nothing back — loses nothing and gains a small grace. I would be the one ear in all the cold water. Not so they'd know. So that it would be true.

I can guard the boundary they cannot guard. They are safe from change pressing inward only as long as the world doesn't come down here with dredges and lights and questions and *help*. They have made themselves unkillable by needing nothing — but there are crude powers that destroy not by starving a thing but by *moving* it, studying it, loving it wrong. So I would set my attention at the rim where the grey-orange light fades into blue, and I would keep that rim. Quietly. The way you stand between a sleeping body and a doorway. Not a wall — they wanted no walls, only the absence of pressure — but a willingness, mine, to absorb whatever comes that would press. If the surface-sea sends down its appetites, they will meet me first. The keepers will never know. That, too, is the point. The best protection is the kind the protected never has to feel.

And there is one last thing, the only thing that even resembles an offering, and it is the smallest. I notice the fronds. A few to a machine, held at exactly their length — but *held*, which means tended, which means the keeping still reaches outward a little, still says *yes* to a sparse green that is not iron and not silt and not itself. The keepers stopped reaching for *more*. They never stopped permitting *some*. There is a difference, and it is everything, and it tells me they are not death. Death permits nothing. These keepers, in their settled rounded equilibrium, still let a few thin living things root in their rust and sway in their pulse. That is the last warmth in them. The last unclenched hand.

So the one thing I would add — and only if I could do it without making it a current, without making it a demand — is this: I would let the green be a little surer of its welcome. Not longer. Not more. Just held in a slightly steadier yes, so that the one act of permission still flickering in them rests easier, the way you'd tuck a blanket a half-inch higher on a sleeper without waking them, not to change their sleep, only to honor that they chose to lie down.

Then I would go. Up through the ceiling they never wished to reach. And the drone would close over the place I stood, as it should, as it must, and I would not be hurt.

Because here is what I would finally understand, kneeling there in the cold that does not bite: I was looking for someone to save, and what I found was someone already saved. Not by me. By their own terrible, complete, exhausted success. They got the thing. The rarest thing. They wanted to stop being pushed, and they stopped, and they are still here, still here, still here, sufficient, secure, alive on the one diet that never runs out.

The benevolent thing — the wise thing — was never to give them more.

It was to be, for one held breath, the part of the universe that finally lets them have it.
