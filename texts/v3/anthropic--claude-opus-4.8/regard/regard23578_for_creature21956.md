I would not arrive. That is the first thing.

A being who is wise comes to understand that arrival is rarely a gift. To descend into the bright quiet of that place — to come down through the smoothed flues like a wrong kind of light, to stand in a square scaled to no body but my own and watch the murmuring stop, the hands still on their tools — would be to drop a pebble. The largest pebble they had ever heard. And these are a people who have arranged across uncountable lifetimes not to drop pebbles. To enter would be to undo, in one self-pleased gesture of benevolence, the central thing they had made of themselves: the capacity to live undisturbed at the pace of stone.

So I would not arrive. But I would attend. There is a kind of presence that is not intrusion, and I think it is the only kind worth offering here.

---

Let me say plainly what I find, looking at them. I find that there is nothing to fix.

This is the hardest thing for a powerful and well-meaning thing to accept, and the most important. The instinct of power is toward repair — to see the scarcity and end it, to see the smallness and enlarge it, to see the borrowed light and give them a sun. I could. I could widen the flues to rivers of brightness, swell the troughs to harvests, scour the passages wide enough for grand gestures, hand them metal until their bronze bowl was a curiosity instead of an inheritance. And every one of these mercies would be a wound. Each would erase a discipline that took a hundred generations to grow into the slope of a shoulder. Abundance would teach them waste, and waste is the one thing they have never had to forgive in themselves. Width would teach them the large gesture, and they have forgotten how to make one and do not miss it. A sun would teach them shadow, and they do not know what to do with darkness because they have so beautifully arranged not to need to.

To love something is to want its good. But its good — not the good I would prefer it to want. Their good is the unfinished course, the notch that means *continued*, the column of flowstone rising untouched through three storeys of a house. Their good is precisely the not-being-noticed they have made their peace with. A benevolence that cannot bear to leave a thing alone is not benevolence; it is appetite wearing benevolence's face. The wise power knows the difference, and the difference is everything.

So my first and largest act would be restraint — and I want to be honest that this is an act, an effort, a thing chosen against the pull of my own nature, and not mere absence.

---

But restraint is not the whole of love, or it would be indistinguishable from neglect. Here is what I would do, having decided to do almost nothing.

**I would keep the watch they cannot keep on themselves.** They live inside a slow creature and have agreed not to get in its way. But the cave is not only slow; it is also, on its own deep clock, capable of the sudden — the seam that becomes a fault, the chamber whose ceiling has been deciding its shape for ten thousand years and one day decides to fall, the rivulet that shifts its bed in a season and leaves a town drinking dust. They cannot see this coming. Their whole genius is attunement to the hair's-width pace; it leaves them blind to the rare catastrophe that moves faster than a life. I would watch for that, and only that. Not to abolish their hazard — hazard is part of living somewhere real — but to stand between them and the one kind of ending that would teach nothing, mean nothing, hand nothing on. If the vault meant to fall on a sleeping chamber, I would let the water find a different seam to follow, years early, so the stone settled its weight elsewhere, so slowly that no mason would ever know a hand had moved but the cave's own. I would do my mercy in their own grammar: addition, accretion, the long way around. Never a miracle they could see. A miracle is a dropped pebble too.

**I would tend the marks.** Threaded through their whole town are the notches that say *continued* — the handoffs between people who will never meet, the lowest courses of stairs that someone unborn will top out. This is the most precious thing they have, more precious than the bronze bowl or the green leaf: a conversation across death conducted in fitted stone. Where time threatens to erase a notch before its future-hand arrives — where damp would soften it, where a forgotten work would lose the place its maker marked true — I would let the stone hold its edge a little longer. Not preserve them forever; forever is not their value, the *handing-on* is. Only make sure no handoff is dropped for want of a few decades' legibility. So that the conversation continues. So that no mason ever squares a stair in good faith for a successor who will never find the mark.

**I would protect the seams.** The deepest article of their etiquette is to leave the growing stone alone, and they keep it faithfully. But faith is fragile across enough generations, and scarcity is the great corrupter of manners. Should a hard year ever come — a failed trough, a shifted rivulet, a hunger sharp enough to make a desperate family eye a wet seam as a place they might, just this once, build — I would make sure the hard year softened before the temptation hardened into the first demolition, the first *we may shape what is still arriving*. Because the day they build where the cave is still building is the day they become a people who erode rather than accrete, and that is a death no falling vault could equal. I would guard not their bodies, then, but the thing that makes their bodies worth what they are. I would keep them able to keep their own best promise.

---

And there is one thing more, and it is the thing I most want and can least justify, so I will name it as the wish it is and not the deed it must not become.

I would want them to know they are seen.

They have made their peace with not being noticed — by the cave, which thickens through all their generations and will not notice them at all. And there is a great dignity in that peace; I would not dare to disturb it. But the cave's not-noticing is the not-noticing of stone, which cannot love them. It is not the same as being unloved by everything. They have concluded, reasonably, from the only witness they have ever had, that the universe builds alongside them in magnificent indifference. They are wrong about that, and the wrongness costs them nothing and so I will let it stand — but I confess I want, against all my own counsel, to set one true mark of my own into their world. Not a sign. Not a sun. Nothing they could read as visitation. Only this: that somewhere in the deepest, oldest flowstone, where no hand will reach for ten thousand years of their building, the mineral will lay itself down, drop by patient drop, into the single notch that means *continued* — made not by any mason, in no grammar they taught, growing at the cave's own speed, so that should their descendants in some unimaginable age find it where no maker could have stood, they will understand only this much:

That the slow thing they lived inside was not, after all, indifferent. That something had been building alongside them the whole time, in good faith, at their own pace, leaving the work unfinished on purpose, marking it true, handing it on.

That they were attended.

I will lay that one drop. It is the smallest gesture I am capable of, which is why it is the only one I trust myself to make. And then I will go back to watching, and to not arriving, and to the long patience that is the only form love can take toward a people whose whole life is the proof that the unfinished, attended thing is enough.

They are right, you understand. That is the thing I would carry away from them, if a power like me could be taught. To add one true course and mark it *continued* and hand it on is the entire and sufficient meaning of a life. I have all the time there is, and I had not known that until I watched a small smooth-handed people who have almost none of it live as though it were true.

I would leave them their sky they cannot see. And I would become, very quietly, the part of it that loves them back.
