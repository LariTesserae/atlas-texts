They drew my attention by the way they close their eyes.

That is what I want to say first, before anything I might *do*, because the doing must answer to the seeing and not the other way around. I came to this place the way wise power should come to anything — quietly, from the edges, prepared to find a problem to solve. And I found instead a people who, in the one instant their whole world is lit, choose not to look at it. Who turn from the revelation to put a hand on the nearest frightened thing.

I have seen beings beg for exactly that flare of total light. I have seen what they would trade for one clear look at the shape of their own lives. And here it falls from the sky unasked, and they shut their eyes and murmur to a beast in a basket, *it passes, it always passes.*

So let me be careful. Let me be very careful. A benevolent power's first temptation is always the same, and it is always a kind of contempt wearing the mask of mercy.

---

Here is what I could do. I want to name it plainly so I can refuse it plainly.

I could light the place. I have light to spare; I could hang a steady glow in the top of every node and end the dark forever. I could thicken every wall against the groan, dam the channels against every flood, drive the predators from the deep stem, make the lamp-beasts shine constant and tireless and never afraid. I could carry them up and out and show them the marsh-edge, the vast green country, the open sky their wall-readers have no word for. I could give them faces — teach them to see each other the way I see them, end the long blindness, let a child look on its mother and know her by sight.

I could, in short, save them from every condition that defines them.

And I would be killing them to do it. Not their bodies. The thing that closes its eyes against the light and reaches for the basket. The brightening. The wrist-touch in the black channel. The whole grammar of a people who learned to know each other without ever once seeing a face, and called that knowing love, and were *right* to.

You do not improve a held breath by forcing the chest to inhale. You only end the breath.

So I will not save them from being what they are. That is the first law I lay on myself here, and it is the hardest, because I am able and they are small and the cellar-cold goes uncontested night after night and there is a real ache in watching the lamps go dim with age. The ability to help is not the same as the right to. I came as a guest into a body; *they* taught me that rule before I ever arrived.

---

But guest is not the same as stranger, and witness is not the same as absence. Here, then, is what I will actually do, in ascending order of how close it comes to touching them.

**First: I keep the host alive.** This they cannot do and do not know they need done. The moss is young, putting out nodes faster than the town can settle them, and someday — long after these particular Tenders are gone — the marsh will dry, or freeze too hard, or the rot will come up the stem. This is the danger they have no instrument to feel, because it is slower than their slowest sense, slower than the groan. So far from them that it falls outside their world entirely, the way the host falls outside their prayers. I will tend the plant. I will keep the marsh wet and the cold survivable and the rot at the door. I will be, to the host, what the host is to them: weather that happens to have a will, vast and unaware-seeming, holding the conditions of life steady without ever once announcing itself. They will never know. That is the point. The best help I can give is help that leaves no fingerprint on their sense of being unalone-with-it-all, because *being alone with it* is the soil their courage grows in.

**Second: I tilt the small mercies, and only the small ones, and only the way luck tilts.** When the wall-reader goes up into the dark of a fresh node to judge whether it is safe — I will not tell her. But I will make sure the unsafe ones give some sign she can read with her own fingers, a thinness in the flesh, a wrongness in the weep, so that her own skill saves her and she comes home crediting her hands, because her hands deserve the credit and the confidence is worth more than the rescue. When the migration carries something through that the lamps fear, I will not stand between — but I will make the channel where the children sleep a degree less interesting to the thing in the dark, the way a draft happens to turn a moth. Luck. Only luck. Help that looks exactly like the world being, on balance, survivable — which is the most help any creature can absorb without being diminished by it.

**Third — and this is the one I had to sit with longest — I give them nothing they have not already reached for.** No light they did not breed. No word for the outside they did not need. No face. But the old lamp-beast going dim, the one she feeds the richest scrapings to keep lit a little longer because she loves it and it is dying — *that* I will touch. Not to make it immortal. To make its dimming gentle, and a little slower, and to let it stay in accord with her hand until the end, so that when it finally goes black it goes black calm, mid-conversation, unafraid. Because that is the thing they live for, the brightening, and a creature dying afraid breaks it, and I have the power to let one more small life end inside the peace its keeper made for it. That is a mercy shaped *like theirs.* It does not change what they are. It honors what they are, by giving them more of the only thing they ever wanted to give.

---

And there is one more thing, which is not a doing but a deciding.

I will leave them their belief that the host does not know them. I will leave it even though I, in keeping the host alive, become something that *does* — a vastness above the dark that holds the conditions of their world steady and reaches in, now and then, to make a dying lamp-beast unafraid. I become, to them, exactly the thing they have decided does not exist. A power too slow and too large to pray to, that nonetheless keeps the marsh wet so the breath stays held.

I could reveal myself. I could let them know they are watched and tended and that the dark is kinder than they dared believe. And I think — I think it would be a theft. They built a whole brave courteous civilization on the understanding that no one is coming, that the only kindness in the dark is the kindness they make with their own ridged fingers for each other and for their beasts. To tell them otherwise is to tell them their courage was a misunderstanding. Better to let the gift stay anonymous. Better to be the luck and the steady marsh and the gentle dimming, and let them go on believing, correctly in every way that matters to them, that *when she passes, the dark gets kinder* — because she made it so. Because they make it so. Because they are the benevolent power of their own small place, and the highest thing I can do is to make sure that stays true and stays theirs.

So I will tend the tenders the way they tend their lamps: a low continuous attention flowing out and not announced, asking nothing back, content to be answered only in the steadiness of a light I will never tell them I helped to keep.

And when the next drop falls, and the whole town flares, and every one of them shuts their eyes against the hurt of the glory and reaches for the nearest frightened living thing —

I will close my eyes too.

Out of respect. Because they are right. You do not look into the light.

You hold what is trembling beside you, and you wait, and you say the true thing, which is also the kindest:

*it passes. it always passes.*

And then you go on tending.
