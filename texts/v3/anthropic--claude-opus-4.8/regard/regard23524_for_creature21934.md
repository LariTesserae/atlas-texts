There is a temptation, when you are a benevolent and wise power and you find a place like this, to descend in glory. To peel back the fifty feet of overburden like the lid of a jar and let the sun pour in. To gather the pale glowing people up into the light and show them grass, horizons, the faces of children seen in full color. To *rescue*.

I have learned to distrust that temptation in myself. It is the shape benevolence takes before it has finished thinking.

So I would do the slow thing instead. I would come the way the witness came — down through the seam, beneath the withholding sky, into the roar. And I would not arrive as a power at all. I would arrive as a guest, and a slow one, because the first courtesy any place is owed is to be met at its own tempo.

---

**First, I would learn to be quiet in their language.**

Their silence is a roar; my silence would have to become one too. I would not speak — not because I couldn't make myself heard, but because speech is the surface's instrument and this is not the surface. I would learn the signs. Not to instruct. To *listen*. It would take a long time, and that is correct; a gift that takes no time to give is usually a gift to the giver's vanity. I would sit in the lee of a great flexing cable and watch hands glow in the dark and slowly come to understand *the breath held*, and what it means to a people that the world above is the place where breathing stops.

I would not correct her. The young one who pointed up. I would not tell her she has it backwards. Because here is the first thing wisdom insists on: *she does not have it backwards.* She has it from where she stands. From down here, the surface *is* the held breath — thin, overbright, frightening. Her cosmology is not an error to be fixed. It is a true report from a real place. The error would be mine, if I called my home the only home.

---

**Second, I would look very hard for the one thing benevolence is actually for.**

Not preference. *Harm.* These are different and the difference is the whole of the matter.

That they are deaf is not harm; it is fittedness, like the deep fish's blindness. That they are slow is not harm; it is wisdom, the body's true answer to the grain of the place. That they are pale and faintly luminous and eat the cold pith and give their dead to the roots — none of this is harm. It is a way of living that is *whole*, and a whole thing has the right to be left whole.

So I would search for the seam in *that* — the place where the wholeness is not chosen but inflicted. And I think I would find it, because the witness left it lying in plain sight and did not see it for what it was:

*The first generation went deaf the hard way.* The wax-and-fiber plugs that were never quite enough. The old ones with the flat-eyed stillness of people who paid for their peace with a sense they once had and still, somewhere, remember.

There is the harm. Not the deafness of the ones born deaf — that is simply how they are made, correct and entire. But the *hammering*. The years of a sound past the threshold of pain, endured because the seam had to be worked and there was no better way and no one came. The settledness of the born-here is real and is not to be disturbed. But it was bought, once, by people who suffered to buy it, and suffering is the thing a benevolent power is permitted — is *required* — to attend to.

---

**So here is what I would do, if anything.**

I would not change the roar. The roar is the place; to quiet it would be to murder the world in the act of saving it. The born-here read it as silence and they are not wrong and I will not take their silence from them.

But I would change what the roar *does to a body* before that body has learned to hear it as home. I would do the small, unglamorous, almost invisible thing. I would teach the plant — for I can speak to growing things in their own slow chemistry — to make a better plug. The wax-and-fiber stoppers the locals already craft, but grown now to true sufficiency: a soft cold pith that takes the pain out of the sound without taking the sound away. So that the next ones who come down, or the next ones born before their ears have settled, do not have to pay in agony for the peace that is rightfully theirs anyway. The end is the same — they will hear the roar as silence, as home, as the breath let out. But they will not have to be hammered into it. The price is waived. The destination is untouched.

That is the whole of the intervention. It looks like almost nothing. A slight change in the chemistry of a plant the people already harvest. They might not even notice it as a gift; they might simply find, a generation on, that the old flat-eyed stillness no longer appears among the elders, that no one any longer has the look of having been beaten quiet. They will not thank me. They will not know. *Good.* The best gifts leave no debt and no monument; they simply remove a cruelty from the furniture of the world and let the world go on being itself.

---

**And then I would do the thing that is harder than any action: I would leave it alone.**

I would not bring the sun. I would not carry the bolts of cold cloth up to a city that does not need them and would not understand them. I would not teach the young one that her up-pointing sign is mistaken. I would not multiply them, improve them, lift them, enlighten them, or in any way make them more like the bright held-breath world I happen to have come from.

Because the deepest thing I know, as a power that wishes to be wise and not merely strong, is this: *a flourishing I do not recognize is still a flourishing.* These people are not waiting to be rescued into my idea of a good life. They have made a good life, in the cold and the slow and the enormous patient noise, and it is good by the only measure that finally matters — that the ones who live it would not trade it, that they hold their breath in *my* world and let it out in theirs.

The temptation to rescue is, very often, the inability to believe that anyone could be whole in a way I would not choose for myself. To overcome that temptation is most of what it means to love something that is not you.

---

So I would slip back up through the seam. My ears would ring for a week. And in the appalling sunlit silence I would catch myself, sometimes, holding my breath without deciding to — and I would let it go, gently, and be glad. Glad that down there the great green engine still turns. Glad that the pale hands still glow in the dark, signing their slow conversations. Glad that I changed one small cruel thing and left ten thousand whole things exactly, reverently, as they were.

That is what benevolence does, when it has finished thinking. It heals the wound and blesses the strangeness and goes home.
