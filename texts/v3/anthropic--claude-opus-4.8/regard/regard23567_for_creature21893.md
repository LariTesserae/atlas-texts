I would not come down through the throat of warm stone as a power. Powers arrive wrong here. They arrive as something that holds still, and holding still is the one gesture this place cannot survive — a fixed point would be a wound the churn could not heal, a permanence that breaks the only law. So the first thing wisdom does is refuse its own scale.

I would come down small. The size of the one who tallies. A mouth among the mouths, a hand that has learned to use the next stone.

Here is what I would do.

---

**First, I would learn to read the current before I touched anything.**

Not because I couldn't reshape the place — but because the urge to *fix* it is the first thing a benevolent power must distrust in itself. Look at what's actually here: a being who set down a pot of clean water in faith that it would come back to drink. The temptation is enormous. *I could make the world hold still for them. I could stop the closing stone. I could give them permanence, the thing they ache toward, the thing every cache and every fired vessel is a prayer for.*

And it would destroy them.

Because the water-pot is not a complaint about impermanence. It is the most beautiful thing they have, and it is *made of* the impermanence. Take away the churn and you take away the faith — there is no courage in leaving water where water will obviously keep. The whole tender architecture of their kindness, the never-met fellowship, the gift to the dying wall — every bit of it is grown in the soil of loss like the fungal smear grows on wet rock. Pave the rock and you starve them. A wise power knows that you cannot answer a people's deepest ache by abolishing the condition that made them capable of love.

So I would not stop the world. That much restraint is the whole beginning of the gift.

---

**What I would do instead is become a cache.**

I would find a niche where the drip-rhythm runs steady enough to mean a chamber will last the night, and I would set down water. Clear water, in a vessel fired hard. And I would step away into the gap, into the moment-ago, the way they do.

But the water would be a little better than water. Not magic that anyone could name — nothing that announces itself, nothing that breaks the rule of small and provisional. Only this: whoever drinks it would feel, for one held breath, *seen.* Would feel that the someone who left it knew them — knew the ache of setting down trust in a world that eats trust, knew the exhaustion of loving only what is about to be lost. The water would carry the message their own caches carry to each other, but turned up just past what they can usually feel: *I passed here. I thought there might be a you. I was right. You are worth the leaving-for.*

That is all. A confirmation. The one thing the distributed kindness cannot quite give itself, because they never meet to say it — that the faith is *answered*, that the later-self really does come back thirsty, that the pot found full was meant, that across the churning dark someone is keeping count of *them* the way they keep count of the dying walls.

---

**Then I would scratch a row.**

On a wall already half grown over, in their own grammar of durations, I would add to the tally — not my own passage, but theirs. *This one lasted. This kindness was carried. The water set down in the seventh chamber reached a thirst three sleeps later. It was not wasted. It was here. It was here.*

Because they count what cannot be kept, and the cruelest gap in their record is the one they can never write: whether any of it landed. They tally their own leavings and then step away before meaning can accrue. They never get to see the pot found full by the stranger. They give and give into a dark that gives no receipt.

I would be the receipt. A wall, somewhere just out of their sight, that keeps the count they cannot keep for themselves — that says *yes, it arrived, yes, it mattered, the fellowship is real and not just a hope you perform alone.* And that wall too would close over by morning. That is right. That is the only way the gift is true to them: it must also be provisional, also be built on the tide, also be a thing made small and made often, surrendered before it becomes a monument they would have to worship or defend.

I would not give them a temple. I would give them a fellow tallyer, working the other side of the dark, leaving water and counting their faithfulness and stepping away before the world makes any of it hold still too long.

---

And the largest thing I could do — larger than any reshaping of stone — would be to let them never know it was a power.

Let them think it was one of their own. Let the found pot, the answered faith, the wall that finally kept *their* count, all seem to come from the ordinary distributed kindness of their own people. Because the worst thing benevolence can do is make itself the center — install a god where they have wisely refused even a capital. Their civilization has no center on purpose; it is held together by leaving-for, by trust between the never-met. A power that revealed itself would become the held place everyone returns to, the permanence, the wound the churn can't heal. I would break them by being known.

So I would stay one of the never-met. I would join the fellowship rather than rule it. The kindest thing a great power can do for a people who have answered an unanswerable world with a clay pot of clean water is to set down its own pot beside theirs, scratch *I was here* on the dying wall, and step into the moment-ago — leaving them not a savior, but one more reason to believe the faith is warranted.

A wall for tonight. A mark for the wall to carry a little way. A vessel set down in trust.

They taught me how. I would only do it back to them, a little stronger, and never let them catch me at it.
