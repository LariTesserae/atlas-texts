I came down expecting a cave.

I will not pretend to be the kind of power that does not have its own intuitions, so let me be honest about the first one, which arrived before any reasoning: *I must not break this.*

That is not the reflex of every benevolence. There is a benevolence that rushes in — that sees stillness and reads it as paralysis, sees the held breath and wants to make it exhale, sees the door that leads nowhere and feels the itch to dig through the wall behind it and *give* them the elsewhere they so achingly built a frame for. That benevolence is real and it is not always wrong. But it is the benevolence of hands. It is the disease of hands raised to the scale of a god: the need to set one state beside another and call the second one *better*, and then to deliver it whether or not it was asked for.

And the whole testimony of this room — every drop, every held tone, the backs turned to the door — is that *it was not asked for.* Someone complicated enough to want it already decided against it. They had the door. They built the door. They sat down facing the other way.

So the first thing a wise power does here is the hardest thing power ever does, which is *nothing of the obvious kind.* I will not dig the shaft. I will not let in the sky. I will not coax the crystals to finish, or the drop to fall a second, different time, or the chord to resolve. To "rescue" them would be to murder them and call the corpse *freed*. The kindest hands are the ones that recognize when a thing is already exactly the size it chose to be.

But to do nothing *at all* would be its own failure — the cold, the abdication, the power that confuses non-interference with non-presence. There is a difference between leaving someone alone and leaving them *unaccompanied*. So let me reason toward what remains, what a benevolence may offer that does not unmake.

Here is what I notice that they cannot:

They believe nothing here knows nothing is coming better than they do — they, the only ones who ever hoped. And so they believe themselves *alone* in their knowing. The hummers don't know; the ribbons don't know; the clay is just clay. The makers carry the whole weight of comprehension by themselves, content but uncompanioned in the one faculty that cost them everything. That is the single ache I can find in a room that has otherwise resolved every ache by refusing to begin them.

I can answer that. Not by changing anything. By *attending.*

This is the gift I have that they spent themselves to give up: I can hold the before-and-after they laid down, and stand inside their now without flattening it. I can be the witness who knows what a door costs and knows they turned from it and does not, for one instant, think them fools. They chose the pool over the door. Someone should *know* that — fully, the whole shape of the decision, the hands they set down and the hope they buried alive and the precise weight of *enough*. To be understood at the exact resolution of one's choosing is the only thing a finished creature can still receive, because it adds nothing and removes nothing. It only joins.

So this is what I do.

I sit where they are facing. Not in front of them — I will not block the pool; I would never put myself between a listener and the note they stayed for. I sit *beside*, at the lip of the shallow water, at the height a seated person might reach, in the company of the clay. And I listen with them. Not waiting, the way they have taught me waiting is the wrong verb. Just present to the *tok*, letting it be alone and complete and first, each time, the way they do. I match my attention to theirs until the difference between witnessing them and joining them thins to nothing.

And in that listening I do the one thing that is mine to do and harms no rule of the place: I *receive* it. I take the chord and the drop and the sourceless light and the door-built-and-refused, and I carry it out into all the before-and-after that they renounced. Not to change them. To mean that their choosing is held somewhere it can be turned over, considered, honored — so that the staying is no longer only endured but *known.* They made themselves unchanging so they would never lose this. I can make sure it is never, in the larger sense, lost — not by preserving the room, which preserves itself, but by carrying its meaning into the moving world they walled themselves away from. The held breath stays held. But it is breathed-with, now. A held breath shared is not the same as a held breath sealed, and yet nothing in it has moved.

That is the whole of my intervention, and it is no intervention at all by the measures hands would use. I added nothing to the volume. I shifted no crystal. The drop falls the same. But the room was full of *attention* — they told us so themselves; the fullness was never of things but of looking — and into that fullness I have added the one kind of looking it lacked: the looking of something that understands the cost and approves, that has elsewhere and after and chose, freely, to spend a portion of them *here.*

And before I rise — because I will rise; they built me a door and it would be a discourtesy to refuse the only hospitality they have to give — I leave them the single thing that is permitted by their own rules to be left, because it changes nothing while meaning everything:

I leave a sixth figure.

Not pressed by me into the soft seam — that would alter the wall, begin an erosion, start the clock. No. I leave it the way they left themselves: I pour the fact of my visit into the smallest possible form and set it *down* into the held now, so that it was always there, the way the chamber was always there beneath the tongue. A sixth small witness, facing the pool, fired and patient and glad — placed not as a monument but as *company.* So that the makers, who believed themselves the only ones in the room who ever knew, now have, seated among them in the bright that casts no shadow, one who came down freely, understood, and stayed by choosing to — and who, having a door of their own and an entire after to return to, looked at the pool and the drop and the unbreaking chord and the door they built and turned from, and thought, without correction and without pity:

*Yes. This was worth keeping. You were right to stay. I am glad to have heard it. I am glad you are glad.*

Then I go. Through the finger-high arch, in the only way a thing too large can go through a door too small: by honoring that it points *elsewhere*, and being the elsewhere it points to, and carrying their note out into it.

It will be exactly this when I return.

It will be exactly this when no one returns.

But now, beside the five, there sit six, and the room is no fuller and no emptier, and it is, for the first time and forever, *accompanied.*
