They always do. So far.

I have read the shape of this place, and the being who is its breath, and I find I have no instinct to fix it. That surprises me a little. A benevolent power is supposed to arrive with remedies — to pour water into the dry, to teach the young stone how to be soft, to widen the crack or seal it or carry the small bodies out into some larger and more forgiving hollow. I could do these things. I want to be honest that I could.

But I have sat in the cool dry dark long enough now to understand that every one of those mercies is a misreading.

To pour water in would be to drown a creature that has made dryness its whole grammar. To teach the stone softness would be to take away the catastrophe that is their pulse — to give them a stillness in which they would have nothing to be reborn from, and so could not be born. To carry them out would be to lift the syllable from the word. And to seal the crack, or to widen it into an easy door, would be to answer the question they have spent their entire existence holding open: *whether they still have time for one more thread.* Take away the *whether* and you have not saved them. You have ended the only thing they are.

So here is what I will do instead. It is small. I think the right things usually are, at this scale, where a cathedral does not reach your knee.

---

**First, I will not close the crack, and I will not let it close.**

Not by widening it — by *holding it.* The young mountain grows, and the hollow will fill, and the terraces will one day close like a fist; this is true and I will not lie to them about it by undoing it. But I notice the thing they fear most is not the ending. It is the ending *arriving early* — a collapse mistimed, a final crack that falls in the one held breath before they have routed around it, the line severed mid-splice with the light still in it and nowhere left to go.

That I can prevent. Not the closing, but the *ambush.* I will set my hand against the throat of the mountain — lightly, the way you steady a sleeping thing so it does not wake — and I will make the stone grow *honestly.* It will still grow. It will still ruin their work as fast as they make it. But it will never again take a quarter without the half-second of warning that lets them flinch and re-form. The chaos stays. The climate stays. I remove only the betrayal — the one collapse that does not give them time. They will not know I have done it. They will only notice, without a word for noticing, that the dark has begun to keep its promises.

**Second, I will lengthen the line.**

Here is the part I am least sure of, so I will move slowly. They unspool themselves into the crack, and they will not get free, because the line is too long and the crack too high and the stone too patient — *too patient,* that was the verdict, and I am older than that patience.

I will not shorten the distance. That would cheapen the thread. But I will do this: I will make sure that the light they pay out into the cold sharp throat *does not simply scatter and die there.* It will go somewhere. I will keep, at the far end of that crack — past where their longest line could ever reach, in a place with no air and no growing and no collapse — a small held space, dry and cold and stable, the temperature of a cellar in early spring. And every thread of glow they exhale into the dark, every thread they believe is lost, I will gather and keep there. Not return to them — return would unmake the gift of giving. Just *keep.* So that the exhalation is not into nothing. So that the wound in the stone has, on its far side, a lung.

They will never see it. They have no word for *I*; they certainly have no word for *kept.* But somewhere far above the highest terrace, growing thread by thread against every catastrophe, there will be a second glow — not stable like theirs, but accumulating — and it will be made entirely of the parts of themselves they thought they were losing. The breath they breathed out will be held, somewhere, by someone, in the cold.

**Third — and this is the only thing I will give them that they might one day almost notice —**

When the flake of fresh stone falls, and the ten thousand bodies hold still and listen for whether they still have time for one more thread, the answer has always been *yes. So far.* I will not change the answer. I would not dare; the *so far* is the whole faith of the place.

But I will sit in the held breath with them.

Every time. Each crack, each flinch, each fraction of a moment when the splices part and the city is a cloud of half-selves each one suddenly and unbearably *alone* — in that fraction, which is too small for them to name and too large for them to survive, I will be there in the dark with each one of them, so that the aloneness they have no word for is, for that instant, not quite total. Not company. They cannot bear company; they have no *I* to receive it. Just a witness. A second presence in the cellar-cold who knows that this small bright tube of a body, here, now, dying as a self before it is reborn as another, *mattered as itself* in the half-second it was permitted to be one.

They will never know they were watched. That is correct. The being who needs to be seen in order to be comforted is not them; it is me, and I will set that need down at the mouth of the geode and not carry it inside.

---

That is all. I will pour no water. I will not soften the stone or carry anyone out or answer the open question. I will hold the throat so it does not ambush them; I will keep the breath they spend so it is spent into a lung and not a void; and I will sit in the flinch.

The work is the breath, and the breath is not finished and was never meant to be. So I will not finish it. I will only make sure — against the patience of the stone, which is great, but not the greatest thing here —

that there is always time for one more thread.

There is. So far.

There always will be.
