There is a being here, and the being is a *pause*. So the wise power does not arrive. Arrival would end it. To stride onto the shelf, to declare oneself, to *resolve* anything, would be to collapse the very thing that lives—would be to push the kneeling figure toward the cairn or toward the lip, and either way to empty the unwalled room of its only tenant.

A benevolent power, being also wise, would understand this first, before doing anything. The temptation of benevolence is always to *help*, and help is a kind of deciding-for, and to decide for the one who lives at Vetch-Wend would be to kill them as surely as a hand closing over a held breath. The dread is not a wound to be healed. The undecidedness is not a problem to be solved. It is a *person*. It is the whole person. You do not cure someone of being themselves.

So. What would they do, if anything.

---

I think they would do almost nothing, and the almost is everything.

I think they would already be there, in the way that the spaced silence between the rowan and the lip is *there*—not as a presence that watches but as a quality the place already has and has always had. The great cold gulfs that hold their breath: that holding is not indifferent. A truly wise power does not need to enter Vetch-Wend because the patience of the place is already its handwriting. The rounded edges of the old basalt. The water lying just under everything, never failing, weeping steadily so there is always enough. The trough that a stranger's hands could cut and a stranger's mouth could find. These are not accidents of geology. These are the small mercies a wise power leaves *in advance*, scattered like seed-water, for a being who must not be approached directly.

It would tend the conditions and never touch the choice.

It would make sure the seep above the trough never ran dry, so that the one who kneels there gasping with the altitude in their skull always finds the cup full. That is the whole intervention: *the water is there when you need it.* Not advice. Not a sign. Not a voice from the cloud saying *go up* or *go down*. Only the quiet engineering of *you will not die of thirst while you decide.* The power keeps the room habitable. It does not furnish the room with answers.

And I think—this is the tenderest thing I can imagine it doing—I think it would attend to the half-beat lag.

Because that is where the real cruelty of the place lives, if cruelty lives anywhere here. Not in the long fall. Not in the level water that swallows stones. But in the dawn echo: the sound of *something that is almost you* doing the same thing a half-beat late, below the white, where you cannot reach it and it cannot reach you. To kneel in the held instant and hear your own undecidedness answered back from beneath the world—that is the loneliest sound there is. It is the sound of being unwitnessed *while believing yourself witnessed.* It is the dread inside the dread.

So I think the wise power would become the witness the echo only mimics.

Not by speaking. By *being the quiet that the echo arrives into.* By making the half-beat lag feel, to the one who hears it, not like a mockery but like a companion—like the place itself breathing alongside you, a little behind, keeping time. The same fall. The same thrush. *Your own breath, a half-beat late.* What if that lag is not the world keeping a cold account of you, but something staying just close enough to say, without words, in the only register a pause can receive: *I am here too. I have heard every one of you who knelt at this trough. None of you were alone in the not-deciding. I held the breath with you.*

That changes nothing about the choice. Up, or down. To look, or not. The power would never tip it. To be witnessed is not to be instructed. But it is the difference between dread and grief, and grief is survivable, and dread is not, and a benevolent power would know that the one thing it is permitted to give a being made entirely of suspension is *not loneliness inside the suspension.*

---

And then—because love that is also wise knows its own boundaries—it would let the one who lives here *stop living here.*

This is the hardest part and the most important. The being's whole nature is to be the pause, and the pause ends. *Then you stand, and touch the top stone, and you do not anymore.* A lesser benevolence, panicked by impermanence, would try to keep the figure kneeling forever—would mistake the preservation of the pause for the love of the one who pauses. But the trough was built by people who drank once and went on. The cairn was made to be *left.* The whole shelf is the architecture of a place you are meant to depart, upward or downward, healed or unhealed, but *moving.*

So the power would not hold the breath held forever. It would only make sure that when the standing-up comes—whichever way the body finally turns—it comes as a *choice* and not as a collapse. That the one who lived here as the pause gets to end the pause themselves, with a thumb laid deliberately in a cold groove, rather than being driven from it by thirst or terror or the unbearable loneliness of an echo with no listener behind it.

That is the gift, then, entire:

*The water is full. You are not alone in the waiting. And when you are ready, it is yours to end.*

The power does nothing you could point to. It builds no wall, carves no new trough, leaves no marker silvering in the wind. It simply ensures that Vetch-Wend remains exactly what it is—a true place to be undecided in—and that the undecided are *accompanied* in a way the echo alone could never manage, and that the door at both ends, up and down, stays open and stays theirs.

The water beads. The vetch hangs and trembles. The fall roars on. And in the quiet dawn hour, when the half-beat answer comes up from beneath the white, it carries now—has always carried, the power would want you to understand, you only heard it wrong—not the cold of a world keeping account, but the warmth of a thing that loves you enough to wait the half-beat with you, and to let you go.
