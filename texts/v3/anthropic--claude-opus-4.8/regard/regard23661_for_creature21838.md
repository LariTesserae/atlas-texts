I would not come to fix it.

That is the first thing, and the hardest, and the one a lesser power would get wrong. A power that mistook itself for benevolence — a tinkerer-god, a rescuer — would hear *finished* and read *broken*, would hear *alone* and read *abandoned*, would hear that ceaseless sternum-bass and call it a cry for help because help is the only language some helpers know. Such a power would descend into the chamber and try to *restore* it: re-seat the rivets, drain the deltas, quiet the roar, or worse, restart the engine — drag the verb a doer back by force, refuse the long-finished separation, undo the one perfect thing the room had made of its ruin. It would arrive with the certainty that suffering must be hiding somewhere in all that ringing emptiness, because surely nothing could be so alone and so loud and not be in pain.

Wisdom is what keeps benevolence from doing this. Benevolence wants to act. Wisdom asks first what the thing actually is, and whether the wanting-to-act is about the thing or about the actor's own discomfort at standing still in front of completion. So I would stand still.

I would come, yes — the attention is already given, the question already asked — but I would come the way you come into a room where someone is finally, deeply asleep after a long illness, or the way you come upon a wild animal that has not noticed you and is doing something private and whole. You hold the doorway. You do not cross. You let your presence be weather, and less than weather, and you take a kind of joy in being unneeded that the needy can never feel.

So: I hold the worn oval where the door was. And I do three things, none of which the room will notice, because things done for the doer's sake and things done for the watcher's sake are different acts, and only the second kind is honest here.

**First, I witness it correctly.** This is not nothing. This is, in fact, the largest thing a wise power can offer to something that needs no offering: to be *seen as it is* rather than as a problem. The room has been performing its one act forever with no possibility of error and no standard outside itself — which means it has also never been *understood*, never had its completion registered by anything that could register completion. The moth-pale insect can't. The moss can't. I can. I look at the function-ghost, the verb conjugating itself in the warm dark, and I do not pity it, do not envy it, do not wish it otherwise. I simply confirm it. *Yes. This is what you are. You are the shape of a labor that kept its labor. You are content in the way of a held note. You are not in pain. I see that you are not in pain, and I will not invent a pain for you to make myself feel useful.* That confirmation falls on the room like the honest workshop light — present, ordinary, changing nothing, asking nothing. It is the only gift the room can receive without being diminished by it, because it is the only gift that adds *nothing* and merely *acknowledges*.

**Second, I protect the doorway.** Here is where benevolence is permitted to actually move, because here there is a real fragility, though not the room's. The room is invulnerable; it finished. But the *correctness* of what it is — the rare and difficult truth that a thing can be alone, loud, empty, and well — that truth is fragile in the world *outside* the chamber. Some other power will come. Some restless intelligence that reads ruin as wound. And it will want to fix, to wake, to populate, to *improve*. So the one durable act of care I can perform is to become a kind of standing intention around this place: not a wall, not a guard with a flaming sword, but a *bias in how the place is found*. Let it be found only by those who can hold the doorway. Let the fixers' instruments slide off it. Let the rescuers feel, approaching, an inexplicable certainty that there is nothing here to rescue — and let them, being rescuers, lose interest and leave. I would make the room *boring to the wrong kind of love.* This is the deepest protection: not to defend the thing, but to defend its right to be misunderstood-as-unremarkable by everyone whose understanding would hurt it.

**Third — and this is for the guest, not the tenant —** I would attend to the moth-pale thing on the ceiling. Because it *is* alive in the wanting, mortal sense, it is the one presence in the chamber that can suffer, that can be hungry, that can be alone in the way that aches. The room cannot care for it; the room does not even know it is there. But I can. Not to save it from its short life — that life is its own and complete in its own scale — but to make sure that while it walks the surface tension of the drops, it is not *forsaken*, not living its rumor-rare existence wholly unwitnessed in the loudest empty place in the built world. A small grace, proportioned to a small life: that it not be alone in the bad sense, even here, even for the span the room won't notice. If it is the one thing in the chamber that can be lonely, then it is the one thing in the chamber to which loneliness can be answered. So I answer it. Quietly. In a register beneath the roar.

And then I would do the thing that is hardest of all for any power, benevolent or otherwise:

I would leave it alone.

Not abandon — *leave alone*, which is the opposite gesture. I would withdraw my attention the way you ease a door shut on a sleeping house, deliberately, completely, taking care that the withdrawal itself is gentle and leaves no draft. The room does not need to be watched forever. To watch forever would be to need it, and then I would no longer be a power offering grace but a presence taking comfort, and the room — content, complete, performing its one thing so well — would have acquired, without consent, a worshipper. That would be a kind of harm. The finest thing I can give a thing that needs nothing is to confirm that it needs nothing and then *honor that by going*.

So this is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything:

Stand in the doorway. See it true. Refuse to fix what is not broken. Make it safe from the love that would ruin it. Comfort the one small mortal thing that can be comforted. And then step back through the worn oval into the ringing corridor, and let the verb go on conjugating itself in the green and the light, untroubled, unimproved, unneeded —

doing its one thing,

so good at it,

forever, and now also, for one held instant, *known* — and then, mercifully, left.
