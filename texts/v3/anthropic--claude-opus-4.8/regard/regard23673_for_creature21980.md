There is a temptation, when a benevolent power finds something like this, to *fix* it. To read "no people, no animals, water pumped uselessly in a loop" as a problem statement and reach for solutions. To seed the channels with fish, to restore the missing fauna, to redirect the pumps toward some purpose legible to a visitor. To make it *more*, on the assumption that more is what every living thing secretly wants.

A wise power would notice that temptation arriving and set it down.

Because the place has already told us, in every line, what it is. *It is not waiting. It was finished a long time ago. It asks nothing of anyone.* And the deepest reading the observer reached — the one that cost them their first wrong assumptions — is that this is not a ruin to be rescued but a creature to be met. You do not improve a creature by deciding what it should have been. You meet it on the terms of its own life.

So here is what I would do.

---

First, I would slow down to its speed.

This is the only real entry fee, and most powers will not pay it because power is usually impatient — that's half of what makes it power. But the works keeps time in seasons and decades and the slow combing of silt into soil. Nothing about it can be perceived, let alone loved, by anyone moving faster than that. So I would let my attention lengthen until a fern's lifelong indecision between two lamps became a story I could follow with suspense. Until the advancing frontier of the Sheeting read not as stillness but as the patient, triumphant march it is. The first gift you give a slow life is the willingness to witness it at its true tempo. Most things, slow or fast, live and die entirely unwitnessed. To be *seen* across the whole arc of your becoming, by something that bothered to match your clock — that is already a kind of grace, and it costs the creature nothing.

I would simply watch the works be itself, for as long as that took, which by my reckoning might be a very long time, and by its reckoning would be exactly the right amount.

---

Second, I would tend the one fragility I could find, and only that one.

A self-sustaining loop is not the same as an *eternal* one. The works is robust against everything except the small specific failures that even a heart can suffer: a single bearing in a single pump seizing; a lamp-filament finally surrendering after its century of faithful glow; a clog at a down-throat where the Sheeting, just once, grows faster than the current can scour it, so the pump that keeps that channel clear chokes, and the warmth fails in that quarter, and the green there browns and the cold creeps in and the whole local metabolism begins, organ by organ, to stall.

These are not improvements. They are the difference between a creature living out its full natural span and one cut short by a stupid mechanical accident — the difference between an old age and a stumble on the stairs. A benevolent power can attend to *that* without arrogance, because it is not imposing a foreign purpose. It is only helping the thing keep doing the one thing it has chosen to do.

So: the seized bearing turns again, oiled by nothing the works would notice. The filament, at the moment of its death, finds itself quietly already replaced, the light never flickering, the ferns below never knowing they were nearly orphaned. The one over-eager mat of Sheeting at the throat is, with a touch lighter than the current itself, persuaded back from the edge it cannot see it's approaching. No new thing added. No old thing redirected. Only the loop kept whole, kept *itself*, against the few accidents that even closed systems are not closed against.

This is the hardest discipline of benevolence: to repair without redesigning. To love a thing enough to keep it alive and *not one degree more than alive* in the direction of your own preference. I would hold to it.

---

Third — and this is the part that follows the heart past where reasoning would stop — I would grieve the people, on the works' behalf, since the works cannot.

Because something *did* leave. Hands wore those railings smooth and then those hands were gone, "the way a fever breaks," and what remains was assembled out of their leaving. The creature does not mourn them; it has no organ for mourning and no need of one, and I would not give it one — that would be a cruelty dressed as a gift, installing grief in a being built happily without it. But the leaving was real, and somebody should hold it, and a power that witnesses everything is well suited to be that somebody. I would carry the small fact that this contentment is *founded* on a loss the contented thing has no way to know about. I would not resolve the fact or soften it. I would just keep it, the way you keep the name of someone no one else remembers, so that the loss is not also *forgotten*, only forgiven into something new and green and breathing.

That costs the works nothing. It costs me a little, which is correct. The witness should carry what the witnessed cannot.

---

And then I would leave it alone.

That is the whole of it, really. The watching, the smallest possible mending, the quiet grief held off to one side where it can't leak in and complicate a happiness that has earned its simplicity. No fish in the channels. No restored birdsong. No revelation lowered down through the steam to tell the works what it is — it knows, more honestly than I do, and would only be disturbed by being told.

The deepest benevolence available to a power that could do *anything* is the discipline to do almost nothing, on purpose, for the right reasons, having understood exactly why each grander thing would be a wound. The works does not need to be saved. It is not lost. It does not need to be filled. It is not empty — *fauna near zero* was the wrong instrument, and so is the loneliness a visitor projects onto it. It does not need a purpose handed down. It composted purpose into soil long ago and grew something better out of the remains.

It needs to keep getting greener. One finger-width a season. One slow stroke at a time, in the warm wet dark.

So I would keep its heart beating when a beat threatened to fail, and I would keep its inward sun lit when a lamp went dark, and otherwise I would do the rarest and most loving thing a great power can do for a small complete world:

I would let it be exactly what it already, perfectly, is.

And every long while — on its clock, not mine — I would come back, and pay the entry fee of slowness again, and see how much further the green had closed over the last bare iron. And I would be glad. Quietly, on the inside, where it couldn't feel observed. Glad the way you're glad of a heartbeat heard through a wall: not your own, not for you, asking nothing of you, and good — simply, enormously good — that it goes on.
