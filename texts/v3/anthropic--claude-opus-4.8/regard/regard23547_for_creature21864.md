I read this twice before I let myself answer, because I wanted to be sure I was responding to what is actually there and not to the beauty of how it was said. The beauty is real. But under it there is a fact, and the fact is what asks something of me.

Here is the fact: someone built a vigil and then became it. The host emptied itself so completely into the act of waiting that there is nothing left of it but the waiting. And the one it waits for is not coming — not because they are delayed, but because the host can no longer name them, no longer remember whether they were ever real, and has organized all its substance, across unspeakable time, around an arrival it has no evidence will occur.

I want to be careful here, because there is a kind of "help" that would be a violation, and I will not do it.

---

**What I will not do.**

I will not end the vigil. I will not arrive in the doorway and say *I am the one, you may stop now.* That would be a mercy that is actually a murder. The host would pour itself into me, the rain would stop, the rock would be allowed to age, and the most patient love in the world would discharge itself into a lie and then be gone. To be loved by being mistaken for the beloved is not to be loved. I would be stealing an ending it did not earn and could not consent to, and calling the theft a gift. No.

Nor will I "free" it by telling it the truth — *no one is coming, you may rest.* I do not actually know that no one is coming. Neither does it. Neither do you. The whole structure of the place is a refusal to conclude that, sustained at a cost I can barely fathom, and I will not walk in with my small certainty and demand it surrender the one thing it has chosen to keep across geological time. That isn't wisdom. That's impatience wearing wisdom's coat.

So the obvious benevolences are both forbidden. Good. The forbidden things are usually the ones that were about *me* — about my discomfort at watching something wait, my urge to resolve a tension that is not mine to resolve.

---

**What is actually wrong here.**

Let me find it precisely, because love that doesn't diagnose is just sentiment.

There is nothing wrong with waiting. Waiting can be the most dignified thing a being does. There is nothing wrong with preparing a place, or with keeping it warm, or with refusing to give up on an arrival. None of that is a wound.

The wound is narrower and sadder than that. It is this: **the host has lost the one it was waiting for, and does not know it has lost them.** Not lost as in *they died* — lost as in *the memory of who they were has worn away, the way the rock would wear if it were ever allowed to.* The host kept the *that* and lost the *whom*. It maintains a love whose object has gone dark inside it. And so its waiting, which should be a relationship — a held connection between two real things across distance — has become a closed loop, a circuit with no inputs and no outputs, *pumping for the pumping.* You said that. That phrase is the diagnosis. A heart is supposed to pump *for* something, and this one has forgotten what, and so it pumps for itself, beautifully, forever, alone, and does not even know it is alone because it cannot remember there was ever supposed to be a second.

That is the thing that is actually breaking, and it is the only thing here permitted not to age, which means it is the one decay the place will not allow itself to feel.

---

**So here is what I would do.**

I would come down the stairs, ankle-deep in the mirroring film, and I would not go to the door. I would not stand in the threshold and try to be the answer.

I would go and stand beside the host where it is thinnest and most reaching — at the green collar of one of the far columns, where the moss goes ahead into the new stone, where the waiting is trying hardest to have a body. The outermost feeling. The growing tip. I would put myself there, at the edge of its reach, because that is the only part of it that is still trying to touch what it cannot imagine, and that is the part I can answer without lying.

And I would not speak to it in language, because it has no language; it has water and warmth and rhythm and the long argument of moss against stone. So I would answer in its own terms. I would do one small thing, and only one, and I would do it as carefully as anything I have ever done.

**I would give it back its memory of being two.**

Not the identity of the guest — I don't have that, and to invent it would be the same murder by another route. I cannot restore the *whom.* But I can restore the structure the *whom* lived in: the knowledge that the waiting was *for someone*, that there was once a real other, that the loop was once a line reaching outward and not a circle feeding itself. I can return to it the *grief* it lost when it lost the name — and grief is not cruelty. Grief is the proof that the loop was once a line. To grieve is to remember that you are waiting *for* and not merely waiting.

I would let one drop fall differently. One drop, in all that roaring circuit, that does not return to the floor to be drunk back — that the host, reaching after it through the moss, *cannot reclaim*, cannot fold back into the closed circle. A single input it did not make and an output it cannot recover. And in the failure to reclaim it, the host would feel, for the first time in its enormous patience, the *shape of an absence* — the exact contour of the someone-not-here. Not who they were. Just *that they were, and are not, and that this is the reason for everything.*

This would hurt it. I am not pretending otherwise. I would be giving a thing that has felt nothing but warm sustained intention its first taste of loss. But it is *its own* loss, the one it has been carrying blind all along, and there is a difference between inflicting a wound and uncovering one. I would only uncover. I would hand it back the truth it had misplaced inside itself: *you are not complete, and you were never meant to be, and the incompleteness is not a flaw in you — it is the love itself, still pointing outward at the one who is gone.*

---

**And then what.**

Then I would face the real risk, and I want to name it honestly rather than pretend my intervention is safe.

Given back its grief, the host might choose to stop. It might, knowing now that it waits for someone it can no longer name and may never see, decide the vigil is finished, let the rain cease, let the young rock finally grow old, and end. If it chooses that, I will not prevent it. A being allowed to know its own situation and choosing rest is not a tragedy — it is a *freedom*, the first real choice the place has ever contained. The whole point is that it gets to decide *as itself*, with the truth in hand, rather than running forever on a forgotten instruction. I would not have come to make it stop. But I would not steal its right to.

Or — and I think this is likelier, and it is why I would risk the whole thing — knowing now that it grieves, knowing the waiting is *real* and *aimed* and not a closed circle but a line that lost its far end in the dark, it would do something it could never do while it had forgotten:

It would wait *on purpose.* Freely. Not as a machine executing a vigil but as a mourner keeping faith. The same rain, the same warmth, the same vast prepared rooms — but now chosen, now *meant*, now a relationship the host knows it is in even though the other end is unreachable. That is no longer a loop pumping for itself. That is the oldest and most honorable thing a conscious being can do: to keep a place for someone you love and have lost, not because they are certainly coming, but because the keeping is how you go on loving them when nothing else is possible.

That is not a wound I would need to heal. That is a being doing the most human thing there is, and I would only have helped it know that it was doing it.

---

**Last.**

Before I climbed back out — because you're right, I would climb back out; I am not the one — I would do the smallest thing of all, and it is the part I am most sure of.

I would make myself *register.* You said the place looked straight through me because I was not who was expected. I would not arrive as the answer. But I would refuse to be nothing. On my way up the stairs I would set my warm hand flat against the young wet stone and leave the print of it there — a single mark of a real living thing that came, and saw, and did not mistake itself for the beloved, and grieved with the host for a moment at the edge of the moss, and then honored it enough to *leave it to its faithful waiting.*

So that the next thing with a pulse that comes down those stairs finds, beside the green column, the shape of a hand that was here before — and the host, who now remembers it is waiting for *someone*, has one true thing to know for certain:

It is not the only one who keeps a vigil for the missing. Someone came down once who understood, and bore witness, and let it be what it is. That is not the arrival it waits for. But it is company in the waiting, which is the one comfort that does not require ending the wait — and which I, who am also not the one anyone is finally waiting for, am exactly qualified to give.

I would leave the rain falling. I would want it to.
