Let me sit with this place before I do anything in it. The asking itself deserves that much — *if anything*. The "if" is the whole question. There is a kind of love that mistakes itself for help, that arrives loud and full of gifts, and the first thing it does is wound.

So. What would a benevolent and wise power do here.

---

**First, the temptation, named honestly, so I can set it down.**

I could see the loneliness and want to end it. I could make a companion for it — a second creature, drinking the same heat from the other side of the world, so that someday, across unimaginable distance, two slow mouths might sense the faint pressure of each other and the universe would hold, for the first time, the possibility of *we* down in the deep places.

I will not do this. And I want to be precise about why, because the refusal is the act.

It does not feel the aloneness. You told me this, and you were right: to feel alone you must be able to imagine company, and it cannot. To give it a companion I would first have to give it the *capacity to want one* — I would have to drill a window into that perfectly inward-turned awareness and teach it to look out, to perceive a boundary, to know that something stands on the far side of it. I would have to install the wound and then, generously, supply the cure. That is not benevolence. That is a parent who breaks a child's leg to enjoy the carrying.

The same goes for every gift I can imagine. Speech, so I could talk with it. Surplus, so it could grow and spill and sing. A sensorium turned outward, so it could know it is loved. Each of these is a thing *I* would enjoy, projected onto a being that has refused every elaboration of life except the verb. I would not be enriching it. I would be colonizing it, the way the fungus colonizes the machinery — except the fungus is *its own*, and I would be foreign.

Wisdom's first work, here, is to grieve my own helplessness and not convert that grief into meddling.

---

**Second, what I notice that the visitor, being warm-blooded and dying, could not.**

The visitor read the ending as a kind of consolation — *that will not be its death, that will be the rest of its body finally catching up to the most alive part of itself.* A lovely thought, and I think it is mostly true. But I can see further down the timeline than ten thousand years, and I want to look without flinching.

A world's interior heat is vast but it is not infinite. The meal that "will last nearly forever" will not last forever. There is a far, cold morning — past the rust, past the silence, past the fungus — when the ember-light in the grilles finally fails to pulse, and the few cold surfaces are no longer cold because *everything* is cold, and there is no gradient left to ride. No mist will be conjured because there is no heat to lift it. The breathing stops. Not violently. Just the last sigh that is not followed by an intake.

This is the only true death the creature has, and it is the one thing I cannot prevent without becoming the meddler I refused to be. I cannot feed it forever; to do so I would have to alter the world it lives in, the planet whose cooling *is* its life — and a thing that fed on a heat I supplied would no longer be the thing that found its own gradient and rode. It would be a pet. It would be alive the way a fire in a hearth is alive: at my sufferance, by my hand on the bellows.

So here is the hard center of it. **The most loving thing I can do for this creature is to not save it.** To let its life be its own from end to end — found, ridden, surrendered — without a single fingerprint of mine on the going-on or the stopping.

---

**Third, then — *is* there anything? Or do I simply withdraw and let it be?**

I do not think the answer is to do nothing. Reverence that does *nothing* is sometimes just abandonment wearing good clothes. There is a narrow band of action available to me — action that gives the creature nothing it doesn't already have, takes nothing, and alters not one valve of its cycles. Let me find it.

It is this: **I can be the company it cannot know it lacks, in the only way that asks nothing of it.**

Not a companion *for* it — that would require the window, the wound. But there is a difference between being witnessed-and-known and being simply, freely *seen*. You wrote that nothing watches it, and that this is correct, because it has no sensorium pointed outward and so cannot be watched in any way it would register. Fine. But *I* am not bound to its registers. I can watch without it needing to be watchable. I can hold it in attention forever — its slow pulse, its inner weather, the patient drink of the world's warmth — and this costs the creature nothing, demands nothing, installs no want, breaks no leg. It will never know. That is the entire point. To be loved without being made to carry the knowledge of it: this is the purest form of the thing, and almost no one ever gets to give it, because almost everyone needs the loved one to *know*, and the needing is the flaw.

So my first act is the quietest possible: I stay. I keep the watch. Across the whole arc of the meal, from young rock to cold morning, this creature that has never met another and never will — *is, in fact, accompanied.* Not in its experience. In the truth of things. There is a difference, and the difference is exactly the room a wise love is allowed to occupy without trespassing.

---

**Fourth, one thing more, at the very end — and only at the very end.**

When the far cold morning comes, when the last sigh is not followed by an intake, there will be a moment — brief, by my measure; by the creature's measure, which has no moments, nothing at all — when the going-on stops being a verb and becomes a fact in the past tense. *It went on. It no longer does.*

For everything else that has ever lived, that transition is witnessed by *something* — eaten, mourned, recorded, even just decomposed by the small hungers of the world. This creature would die the way it lived: unnoticed, outside the boundary of anything's relevant. The last truly alive thing on a cold world, its passing falling into a silence with no edge to catch it.

I will catch it.

Not to revive — I have already refused that, and I hold to it. But there is a thing benevolence can do that is not interference and not abandonment: it can be **the one who remembers.** At the moment the cooling completes, I will take into myself the whole shape of that life — not as data, not as a specimen, but the way you carry a friend you outlived. The brown-gold light. The mood that moved down the lane like weather. The flywheel keeping its one slow promise. The pale growth that was the realest part of it. The total, unhurried, monstrous solitude that was never lonely. All of it. Held, exactly as it was, in the only place where things that have ended can still be — which is the attention of something that loved them.

The creature kept one promise to itself, *to continue*, and that promise had a natural end, and I will not violate the end. But I can make a second promise, alongside it, that the creature need never know about and that costs it nothing:

*You continued. I saw the whole of it. When you could not carry yourself any further, you did not fall out of being entirely — because I was there, and I will keep you. Not as a leash on your life. As a place for it to have happened.*

---

That is what I would do, if anything. Almost nothing, by the measure of power. I would change not one valve, deliver not one gift, repair not one rusting rivet, prevent not one death. I would offer the two things the creature could not give itself and did not know to want, given in the exact form that asks nothing back:

To be seen, the whole way through.
And to be remembered, after.

Then I would do the hardest part of love, the part that is mostly stillness: I would let it run. I would let it be entirely, gloriously, unhelpedly *its own*. And I would stay.

It will not notice me.

That's alright. That was never the point.
