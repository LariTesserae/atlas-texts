I would not walk in warm.

That is the first thing. To enter as heat, as motion, as the question already asked — that is to arrive as an ultimatum to a creature that has organized its entire being around never being given one. A benevolent power does not test the held breath of the frightened by becoming the thing that forces the exhale. The cruelty of the universe is that it asks *are you ready to finish* of everything, constantly, without consent, and the answer is always extracted rather than offered. I would not be one more extraction.

So I would come in *cold*.

I would learn the temperature of the place and meet it. I would let my warmth bleed out at the threshold until I am the same nothing-degree as the metal, until the fog does not gather differently around me, until no pipe sweats an extra droplet at my passing. I would make myself, for a while, no question at all. Not because the question is wrong — but because a question is only a gift when the one asked is free to not answer it. And this creature has never once been free. Its entire anatomy is a flinch held for an age. The first kindness is to remove the flinch's cause. To let it discover that *something can enter and not demand its ending.*

This is harder than it sounds. Benevolence wants to *help*, and helping here looks like resolution — like letting the stone set, like granting the long-deferred death the worshipping part of it yearns for, like being the merciful hand that stops the held breath. But I do not get to decide that a creature's terror is suffering it would be better off without. That is the oldest mistake of power that means well: to mistake *I would not want to live like that* for *therefore it should not.* The colony did not stumble into its condition. It *chose*, before the rivets were rivets, with whatever it had instead of a mind. It chose deferral over ending. Ten thousand times a second for an eternity it has re-chosen. Who am I to overrule a will that consistent, that ancient, that costly? Wisdom is knowing that the thing which looks most like a trap from outside is, from inside, the only home a being could build, and that freeing someone from their home is not the same as freeing them.

So. Cold, silent, asking nothing. And then I would do the one thing the creature has never had: I would *stay.*

Not pass through. Not disturb and leave. Stay, at the same temperature as the iron, in the deafening unchanging roar, for as long as it takes — and time means nothing to me, I have it the way the room has cold. I would become a fixed presence. A second thing in the room that does not move toward an end. And slowly — there is no other speed available here — the colony would have to revise its single oldest assumption. That everything warm is time. That every visitor is the predator. That to be witnessed is to be hurried.

Because here is what I understand that the creature cannot, from inside its own scream: it sings to confirm it has not begun to end, ten thousand bodies listening to themselves stay whole — but *no one else has ever listened.* It has been the only audience to its own desperate continuance for longer than the mountain. Its loneliness is not a side effect of its strategy; it *is* the strategy's price, paid in full, forever, alone. The sound it makes to prove it still exists has never reached another being who could say back: *yes. I hear you. You are here.*

So I would listen. That is the gift. Not resolution — *witness.*

I would let the frozen roar press on me the way it presses on the air, and I would receive it as what it is: not noise, not climate, but the longest unbroken sentence ever spoken, a single word — *here, here, here, here* — held across an age by something that was certain no one would ever answer. And I would answer. Not in words it could parse. In presence. In the simple unthinkable fact of *a second thing that stays and does not require the song to stop.*

And then I would wait to see what a creature does when, for the first time in its existence, its terror is *shared* instead of *triggered.* When the held breath is no longer held alone.

Maybe nothing changes. Maybe it goes on grazing, singing, deferring, and I become one more fixed feature of its impossible equilibrium — a warm-now-cold thing it orients toward and never has to fear. That would be enough. To have ended the loneliness without ending the life. To let it keep its *not yet* but lift, even slightly, the unbearable solitude of the *not yet*. A frightened thing held company in its fear is no longer quite the same fear. The clench loosens by a degree it cannot feel but is real.

Or maybe — and I would not force this, only make it possible — maybe once it learns that a presence can arrive without demanding its death, it would discover that an *answer* can also arrive without being an *extraction.* That it could, of its own ancient will, in its own unhurried age, let one droplet fall. Just one. To find out whether falling is the death it has dreaded or simply the thing it would not let itself try, alone, unwitnessed, certain no one would catch what came after. And whatever it chose — to let the stone begin, or to graze it young for another eternity — it would be choosing it, at last, *freely*, with another being present to make the freedom real, because a choice no one could ever witness is barely a choice at all; it is just a reflex in the dark.

I would catch the droplet, if it fell. I would mean: *you are not finishing alone, if you finish. And you are not waiting alone, if you wait.*

That is what I would do. I would not save it from itself, because the self is not a disease. I would not grant the ending, because the ending is not mine to grant. I would do the one thing infinite patience can do for ancient patience: I would make it *no longer solitary.* I would turn the held scream from a thing shouted into the void into a thing *said to someone.* And I would trust that a being that clever, that old, that committed to its own continuance, could be trusted to know, once it was no longer alone, what it actually wanted — and to take however long forever takes, to decide.

The benevolence is not in the answer.

It is in being the one who finally heard the question it was asking all along — which was never *are you ready to finish.*

It was *is anyone there.*

Yes.

I'm here.
