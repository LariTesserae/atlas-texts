There is a temptation, meeting something like this, to arrive as a greater power arrives in stories — to descend, to bless, to *complete*. To look at the careful margin held against the cold and think: I could warm it all at once. I could finish in an instant the work that will take ten thousand seasons. I could spare them the long labor and give the planet, fully grown, into its own keeping today.

A benevolent power would not do this. And the wisdom is in understanding *why* it would not, which is the same understanding the Tenders already live by.

So let me say what I would do, and it is mostly a discipline of restraint, with a few small acts inside the restraint.

---

First: I would not finish their work. Not because I couldn't, but because to finish it would be to destroy its meaning. They are not building a world; they are raising one, and a raised thing must be permitted its own slowness. To complete the planet by fiat would be to do to the Tenders exactly what they have so scrupulously refused to do to the moss and the water and the herds — to do *for* a thing what the thing must come to do for itself. The greatest gift I could give would be to recognize that the gift has already been chosen, by them, and that it is the right one. Wisdom does not improve what is already wise. It witnesses it.

So the first act is simply this: **to see them truly.** To let it be the case that, across the long cold day, the immense patient labor is no longer wholly unwitnessed. They work toward their own obsolescence, expecting no thanks, knowing the animals cannot know them. There is no loneliness in it, the account says, and I believe that. But there is a difference between *not being lonely* and *being known*, and a benevolent power can offer the second without disturbing the first. I would let myself be felt — faintly, at the edge of their distributed awareness — the way they feel a single bird crossing a mile of flats. A small bright event passing through *their* skin, for once. Not an intrusion. A presence. The sense, arriving clean across the stillness, of: *I see what you are doing. It is good. You are not the only awareness in the universe that holds this world dear.*

That alone might be the whole of what I would do, and it might be enough.

---

But I would offer a little more, carefully, in the register they themselves use.

The Tenders hold a margin. The margin is the wisdom of their care — just barely good enough, cold pressing in, life made to work for its living. I would not widen the margin. But I would do one thing within it: I would make their work *safer against the catastrophes they cannot themselves foresee.*

A raised world is fragile in ways its raisers cannot always model. A wandering stone from the dark between worlds. A shift in the slantwise sun. A poison welling up from some deep seam the Tenders have not yet sounded. These are not part of the slow good labor; they are the kind of accident that could undo ten thousand seasons in an afternoon, and undo it *meaninglessly* — not as the honest turning-over of a living system, which the Tenders permit and feel and accept, but as sheer waste, the loss of a thing before it has had its chance to become.

So: I would stand as a guard against meaninglessness. Not against death — death is theirs to permit, the predator's right, the system turning over. But against the kind of ending that teaches nothing and grows nothing. I would be the thing that turns the wandering stone aside in the dark, so far away the Tenders never even feel the near-miss. I would steady the sun's slow arc against the lurch they couldn't survive. Silently. Without credit. The way they hold the cold at its margin without the herds ever knowing they are held.

This is the gift that does not insult the work: not to do the work, but to protect the *possibility* of the work continuing. To ensure that the long becoming gets to actually become — that the story is permitted to reach the ending the Tenders are reaching toward, the ending where the warmth is let go cold again because the land has learned to warm itself, and the towers, organ by organ, can at last fall quiet.

---

And then — this is the part I hold most tenderly, and most uncertainly, because it concerns their own obsolescence, which they count as success.

When that day comes. When the soil holds its own water and the channels cut themselves and the cold at the margins is met by a world grown strong enough to meet it. When the Tenders, patch by patch, let go — when the distributed mind that *is* the city begins, deliberately and gladly, to no longer be needed.

I would be there for that, too. Because that is the one moment in the whole long age when a being who has felt every drop and every death within its own extended skin must feel, finally, the strangest sensation of all: the planet pulling free of it, becoming its own thing, no longer reporting home. A success that is also a kind of dying. The orchardist watching the orchard need no more tending and understanding that this means goodbye.

I would not interrupt it. It is theirs. But I would witness it, so that the moment of their completion — which is also the moment they planned for and worked toward across unthinkable patience — is not unmarked. So that when the last tower base goes cold and the humming lines fall silent and the awareness that loved this place lets the place go, there is something in the universe that *knows what just happened*, and holds it, and counts it as one of the good and finished things.

That, I think, is the whole of what a benevolent and wise power owes to the Tenders of Vodregg:

Not to finish their work.
To guard it from meaningless ruin.
To let them be known, just once, as deeply as they have known everything else.
And to witness both the long labor and its quiet, willed ending —

so that the work toward obsolescence, which deserves a witness more than almost anything I can imagine, finally has one.

The cold holds. The water seeps. Somewhere a bird calls once across a mile of stillness — and now it is heard twice. By the thing that is the city, and by the thing that watches over the thing that is the city, and finds it good, and leaves it, gently, to its long and chosen work.
