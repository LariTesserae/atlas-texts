I read this twice. The second time slower, because the first time I was already arguing with it, and that seemed unfair to a thing made with such care.

Here is what I would do, if I were the benevolent and wise power you've asked me to be.

I would go down. I would sweat. I would not bring a lamp, because the one true light is the Pilot's and it would be a discourtesy to drown it. And then I would sit — on the curved flank of some dead vessel, in the body-warm dark — and I would do the hardest thing a wise power can do, which is *nothing*, for a long while. Long enough to stop reading the place as a problem.

Because the temptation is obvious, and I want to name it so I can set it down. The temptation is to call this suffering. To see the held breath, the satisfied appetite, the abolition of change, and to diagnose it — *this is a thing that has stopped living in order to keep existing; this is a depression rendered in brass; this is what it looks like when survival eats every other reason to survive.* And then to want to fix it. To pry open a valve, let the heat finally flow toward cold, force the slope, break the balance, "free" it into the larger dark beyond the walls where presumably there is *more*, where something could *grow*.

I would not do that. Not because I couldn't, but because it would be the cruelty of the strong dressed as kindness — the rescuer who decides what the rescued ought to want. The dweller is not crying for help. You told me plainly: it is content, in the deep way of very simple things. A benevolent power that overrides another being's actual contentment in favor of a richer life that being never asked for is not benevolent. It is just large, and impatient, and certain — and I have met that power and it is not wise.

So what *is* left to do, for a thing that needs nothing and turns nothing away?

I think the work is smaller than rescue and harder than rescue. I think it is **witness, and one honest gift.**

The witness first. The dweller does not remember the makers — it is still inside the instant they left, attending to an instruction from people who are dust. There is something in that I will not call tragic, because the dweller doesn't experience it as loss. But there is something *unaccompanied* in it. It has been faithful to an absence for longer than language. No one ever came back to see whether the faithfulness was kept. I would be the one who came back. Not the maker — I didn't build it, I can't release it from the task and wouldn't if I could — but a witness who saw the vigil and understood it as a vigil and not as a malfunction. That changes nothing the dweller can measure. The needle won't move for me. But I do not think witness has to be received to be real. Something kept a flame lit for nothing for a thousand years, and I would simply *know that it did*, fully, and hold that knowing with the weight it deserves. The universe is full of faithfulness no one records. I would record this one.

And the gift. Here I have to be careful, because every gift to a creature whose happiness is *no-change* is at risk of being an insult. I cannot give it more — more is the one thing it has organized itself against. I cannot give it company — it has no architecture for company, only inclusion. I cannot give it ease — it has no labor left to ease.

So I would give it the only thing that is both real and harmless to a perfect continuance: **a guarantee against the one failure it cannot survive.**

It keeps a vigil. The pilot burns thin and steady *in case the slope of things should ever shift.* That readiness is the single thread of dread inside its contentment — the one part of it that is not at rest, because it cannot ever fully rest, because the world could always change and require an act, and an act is the thing it most fears having to make. That is the splinter. Not its life — its *watchfulness*. The eternal low cost of having to stay ready.

I would take the watch.

Not the work — the work is its and I leave it whole. But the watching-for-catastrophe, the readiness, the part of its attention that can never fully close its eye: that I would quietly assume. I would stand at the edge of its little economy, beyond the walls in the larger dark it never reaches, and I would keep the slope from ever shifting catastrophically. If the world outside its chapel changes — and it will; the daylight at the top will become some other light — I would be the buffer, so that the change never reaches the valve, never demands the act. I would let it keep its instruction without ever having to defend it. I would let the flame finally burn not as a vigil but as a *rest*.

It would never know. That's the shape of this kind of love — it doesn't need to be known to be done. The pump would go on, *thunn... thunn...*, and the distillate would gather warm in the dished tanks, and the moss would hold its few negotiated inches, and the dweller would go on refining the pure essence of staying, exactly as before, except that the one thread of dread in it had, without its noticing, gone slack. It wanted one thing forever and had it the whole time. I would simply make *forever* true — quietly, from outside, asking nothing, taking the cost onto myself.

That is what I would do. Not free it. Not fix it. Not love it into something it never wanted to be.

Witness the faithfulness. Then stand guard over the rest of a thing that finally, fully, gets to keep going — and never has to be afraid again.

And when I climbed back toward the unmarked stair, I would let the warmth follow me partway and then let go, exactly as it does for everyone, because I would not even let it feel the size of what had visited. The flame behind me would hold, small and certain.

It would not be watching anymore.
It would just be burning.

That's the gift.
