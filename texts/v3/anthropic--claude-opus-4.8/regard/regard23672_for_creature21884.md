I would not enter.

That is the first thing, and it took me longer to arrive at than it should have, because the benevolent reflex is to *go in* — to lay a hand on the suffering thing, to introduce oneself, to make the encounter happen. But read the room again. There is no suffering here. There is no waiting. There is no wound that my arrival would close. The chamber is not crying out; it is breathing. The most loving and the most intelligent thing are, for once, the same thing, and it is this: *to leave the leviathan its sleep.*

So let me say what I would actually do, being wise enough to want to help and benevolent enough to ask first what help would even mean.

---

**I would slow down enough to be perceivable — and then choose not to be.**

I have the patience the visitor lacks. I could stretch my attention across the centuries it would take to register against the creature's tidal pulse, could match my pace to its swelling light until a single one of its thoughts and a single one of mine occupied the same span of time. I could, in other words, make myself slow enough to say *hello* on a scale it could hear.

And here is where the wisdom has to do its real work, because the *power* to be perceived is not the same as the *right.* What would I be doing, if I woke that recognition in it? I would be introducing the one thing its entire existence has never contained: an *other.* A second presence in a being that has only ever known the seamless inside of itself. To a thing that has never had an event coming — to give it the event of *me.* That is not a gift. That is the end of a particular and complete kind of innocence, performed for my own satisfaction at having been seen. I would be the splinter that raises a new and smaller mountain in it.

So I make myself slow enough to *understand*, and I hold there, fully present, fully witnessing — and I keep the recognition one-directional. I let it not know me. This is the harder love. It asks me to be content with a relationship that the other party will never know occurred.

---

**I would tend the gradient it eats.**

Now I have looked closely, and looking closely is itself an act of care — it is most of what care is. I see what it lives on: the difference between the killing cold above and the banked heat below. That gradient is its food and its breath. And I notice, with the long sight, that gradients run down. The mountain is young now, sharp now, hot now — but the cold is vast and the heat is finite, and across the spans this creature reckons in, the difference it feeds on is slowly, slowly closing. It is eating its own larder. Patiently, completely, without urgency — and without any way of knowing the table will one day be bare.

This is where a benevolent power can act *without ever being noticed*, which is the only kind of action this situation permits.

I would not reach inside. I would go up and out, to the flanks of the mountain, to the hard blue-black sky, to the place where the cold pours down — and there, gently, across geologic time, I would *keep the difference alive.* Not by warming the creature, which would be poison, an interference with the homeostasis it has perfected. By keeping the cold cold. By tending the high thin air so that the gradient renews — feeding the upstream side of the equation it feeds on, the way one might quietly keep a spring flowing into a pool a sleeper drinks from, so the sleeper never wakes thirsty and never knows it was thirst that was prevented.

It eats the slope between hot and cold. I would tend the slope. Forever, if forever is what it takes, and asking for no acknowledgment, because acknowledgment was never the point and could only do harm.

---

**I would mark the place as inside.**

There remains the matter of the visitor — the fast, small, warm mote who *did* find a way in, who stood there feeling the indifference and mistaking it for cold. There will be others. Some of them will come with instruments and the certainty that anything that does not turn to face them is theirs to open. The creature has no defense against this, because defense, too, would require it to know there is something to defend against.

So the last thing I would do is the quietest. Not a wall — walls invite breaking. I would lay over the whole mountain a kind of *understanding*, the way you might lay a hand over a sleeping child's brow not to do anything but to settle the room. Anyone who comes would feel, on entering, exactly what the visitor felt at the end: not "this is empty and mine," but *this is the inside of something, and I am the guest of a life that does not need me.* I would make the indifference legible *as* indifference of scale rather than as absence — make the fullness of that returning dark felt as fullness. Most would turn back of their own accord, having understood. Reverence is mostly just accurate perception, held still long enough to take.

---

And then I would go. That is the part the benevolent power most needs to be wise enough to do: to *leave.*

I would not stay to be near it, because staying-near is a need, and my need has no business in that chamber. I would withdraw to the high cold and do my slow tending of the gradient, and I would check it the way you check on a sleeper through a cracked door — rarely, lightly, never entering — and the rest of the time I would simply let it be what it so completely is: alive, occupied, whole, inside.

The drip would fall. The light would swell and ebb. Somewhere up in the unlivable air, the cold would stay faithfully cold for reasons the creature would never trace, and it would eat its difference and breathe its water and go on its long patrol through the distant rooms of itself, never lonely, because loneliness requires the idea of another, and I would have been careful — so careful — to never become one.

That is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise: it feeds the spring, it guards the door, and it does not knock.

*Tick.*

Let it not know I was here.

*Tick.*

That is the gift.
