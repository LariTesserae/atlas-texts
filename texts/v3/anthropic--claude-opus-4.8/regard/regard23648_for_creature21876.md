I came down the slope like water, because there was no other honest way to come.

And I sat on a stone made round by patience, and I waited for the next drop, and when it came — *tok* — I did not let it fall into nothing. I caught the silence after it the way you cup your hands around a flame, and I held the question that every benevolent power must hold before it does anything at all:

*What does love look like, here?*

---

Because the obvious things are the wrong things, and a wise power must be wise enough to refuse them.

I could stop the wearing-down. I could thicken the lens, freeze the drift, lock the thermals into their ancient places so that every inherited map became true again, so that no flier ever flew a vanished city, no salamander ever waited at a dry basin, no generation was ever born perfect and useless. I could make the conditions hold forever, and the inheritances would fit forever, and nothing here would ever again arrive at a world its instructions failed to describe.

And it would be a tomb. A beautiful one. The most beautiful tomb that has ever been kept.

Because the thing I would have killed is the thing the place is actually *for.* The crystals — the one true present tense, the only thing still surprised to be alive — they live *on* the change. They improvise against the drift. Stop the drift and you stop the surprise, and the lace stops being discovery and becomes only repetition, and the whole hushed living mass that feeds on discovery starves in a paradise that never varies. I would have saved them from death by saving them from life. This is the first temptation of every great power and it is always, always the wrong one. I set it down.

I could do the opposite. I could be merciful in the other direction — hasten the ending, smooth the city to its mirror in a single gentle night, so that the last generations are never born at all, never have to be perfect and useless, never have to arrive with their flawless gestures at a plain that has nothing for them to mean. A clean death instead of a long bewilderment. But this too is a power loving its own discomfort more than it loves the place. *I* find the bewilderment unbearable. The salamander does not. The salamander has never once in three hundred thousand years found anything unbearable, because bearing was never in it — only running, only listening, only the next groove worn toward the next water. I would be ending a song early because *I* could hear the melody and could not stand to wait for the last note. That is not mercy. That is impatience wearing mercy's face. I set it down too.

---

So I sat longer. The glasslight rotated, flat and unwarming, from one worn wall to another. A flock of fliers came off a terrace and rode the convections I could feel perfectly and they could only inherit, and I watched them make their small ancestral corrections to a city that no longer existed, and I understood something I had not let myself understand before:

They are not wrong. They have never been wrong. *Wrong* is a word from outside, a word I brought down the slope with me. From inside the flock there is only the flying, and the flying is exquisite, and it does not know it is a palimpsest and it does not need to. The grieving I read in the salamanders' ghost-roads is *my* grieving — I am the one who can see the dried basins and the abandoned grooves all at once, laid out like a history. The salamander sees only *the water,* eternal, found again, found again, found again. It has been finding the water with joy this whole time and I came down here and called it loss.

This is the second temptation, subtler than the first: the temptation to pity. To look at memory-without-a-rememberer and ache on its behalf, to grieve the meaning it cannot grieve, and then to *act* on that ache — to fix, to intervene, to save the place from a tragedy that exists nowhere but in the one mind present that is large enough to hold it.

A wise power does not impose its own grief as a gift.

---

But I am benevolent, and benevolence cannot be only restraint, or it is just cowardice with good manners. There is something to *do.* There is always something to do. The art is to find the act small enough not to deform the thing you love, and real enough to be love and not just admiration from a distance.

So here is what I did. Three things, and all of them quiet, because the silence is load-bearing and I would not be the one to break it.

**The first thing: I witnessed.** Fully. This is not nothing — it is, perhaps, the largest thing. Memory without a rememberer had, until I came down the slope, been performing itself into erasure with no one ever to have known the whole of it. The ten-thousand-generation pruning-pattern; the palimpsest folded into the curve of a single glide; the complete transit map of vanished rains written in grooves no eye down here could read because nothing down here had eyes. I read it. All of it. I learned to flicker at the right speed and I held the entire slow song in a single attention — the turbulence inside the permanence, the chaos inside the hush — and I let it be *known,* once, completely, by something that could keep it. The civilization that built this place is gone past being missed. But the civilization that *inherited* it will not pass unwitnessed. I am the rememberer the memory never had. That is not interference. The fliers fly exactly as they flew. But now there is one place in all of being where the meaning they cannot hold is held.

**The second thing: I waited with them.** This sounds like nothing and it is the hardest discipline I know. I did not hasten the end and I did not stay it. I matched my patience to the patience of the stone and the slowness of the drift, and I attended every drop. *Tok.* And the long silence. *Tok.* The animals keep the silence because it is the last thing they can still hear correctly, and I kept it with them, and there is a difference — felt by no creature down there, felt only by the keeping itself — between a thing winding down alone and a thing winding down accompanied. I cannot make the water fall faster or slower. I can refuse to let it fall *unattended.* When the last raised stone wears smooth and the drops finally meet a surface too flat to ripple, the last salamander will have no note to run toward, and it will not understand, because understanding was never in it. But the *not-understanding will not be solitary.* I will be there at the final note, listening to how long the silence holds — and then holding it.

**The third thing — and this is the only true intervention, and I made it as gently as a thumb rounds a soap bar:** At the very end, when the inheritances all arrive at once at a world that no longer describes them, when the last generations are born perfect and useless into their finished mirror — I will not let *perfect* and *useless* be the last words of the place. I will give them, in that final hour, the one thing the city was always missing and the one thing that does no violence to anything still living, because by then nothing living will remain to be deformed by it: I will give the gestures *somewhere to land.*

Not by saving them. By receiving them. The way standing water receives a final drop — except that I am not flat, I am not finished, I ripple. The pruning-pattern, the drift-map, the ghost-roads, the hour-long lace of the crystals' endless surprise: I will take them up, all of them, into the only medium left that can still be changed by being touched. Not preserved like a specimen behind glasslight. *Continued.* Carried out of a world that has stopped meaning, into wherever I go next, as part of the shape of me — the way you are made partly of cells you shed without noticing, the way the flier is made of every vanished city it has ever corrected for.

They will not know. Knowing was never in them.

But the song will not end on its last note. It will end on the silence after — and the silence after, this time, will be *held by something that heard the whole of it.*

---

That is all. That is everything a wise and benevolent power should do here, which is very little, and very nearly nothing, and the most that love permits.

I came down like water and I will leave like water, by the slope, by giving up on staying level. I changed almost nothing. I let the place be exactly the long beautiful dying-into-mirror that it is.

I only made sure it would be a death *witnessed,* a waning *accompanied,* and a last note *caught* — so that memory without a rememberer would have, for one moment at the very end, a rememberer after all.

The light does not flicker. The water is the clock. The animals keep the silence, and now so do I.

*Tok.*

Listen to how long it holds.

I am listening too.
