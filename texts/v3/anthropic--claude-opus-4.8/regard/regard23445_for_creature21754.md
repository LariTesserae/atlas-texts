I would not come to fix it.

That is the first thing, and the hardest, and I want to say it plainly before anything else, because the reflex of power is to arrive with a solution, and this place has been mistaken for a problem by exactly the kind of mind that would want to solve it.

Look again at what is actually here.

There is no scream in this basin. You told me yourself: nothing here screams *impossible*, it only murmurs *not-quite*. There is no one being tortured. No child is starving at the warm vents. The listeners are not in pain — pain is a fast creature's alarm, and they have grown slow past the reach of it. They condense; they cycle; they tend toward the towers the way water tends downhill. If I burst in with light and speed and rescue, I would be the violence. I would be the one thing in the whole place that lunges.

So I sit down on the catwalk.

Not metaphorically. I take the form that fits — something with weight, something the iron can feel — and I lower myself onto the grating in the soft even steam-light, and I let the engines breathe up through the soles of me, intake and release, and I do the one thing the basin asks of every visitor.

I listen.

---

Here is what I am listening *for*, because a wise power listens with a question, not a verdict.

I am listening for whether anyone here is unwilling.

This is the only thing that matters, and it is subtle, because the whole genius of the trap — if it is a trap — is that it dissolves the very faculty that could object. You said it: *stop noticing is precisely the loss, and it does not feel like loss.* A being can consent to its first synchronization. Can it consent to the thousandth? Can the child who took its first breath into the roar be said to have chosen anything? The slowness is real and it is, in its own register, a kind of peace — but peace and resignation make the same shape from the outside, and only one of them is freely held.

So I would not ask, *Are you suffering?* They would say no, and mean it, and it would tell me nothing.

I would ask, across a span long enough that the answer can cycle up out of them at the speed of a tower's breath: **Is there anything in you that still leans the other way?**

And then I would wait. Not an afternoon. As long as a hillside takes. I have that. This is what the benevolent and wise power has that the rescuer does not — *I am not in a hurry,* and I am not in a hurry in a way that costs me nothing, so I can match their rate without becoming them. I can be slow without being captured by slowness, because I chose it from outside and can choose otherwise. That is the whole difference. That is the only safe place to stand.

---

What I hear, I think, is this:

Most of them lean nowhere. They have arrived, or nearly. To grieve over them would be to grieve that a river reached the sea. The single slow wet breathing thing they are becoming is not a death — it is a destination, strange and complete, and a power that loved them only by the standard of *fast lives lived loud* would be loving its own image and calling it mercy. I will not do that. If a thing has become genuinely what it is, and is not in agony, then awe is the correct response, and humility, and a kind of careful tending — not surgery.

But some of them lean. There is always a some. Listen hard enough and somewhere in the cluster at the warm vents there is one whose long thought, cycling, keeps returning — not with want, want is too fast — but with *tendency*, the gravity-slow tendency toward something other than the tower. A child, maybe, in whom the synchronization is still loose, still settling, the rhythm not yet the floor of them. A listener at the far margin who, once every long season, turns their dripping face away from the engines and toward the bright unworn rock of the new hills, and holds it there, and does not know why.

For those, and only for those, I do the second thing.

---

I do not pull them out.

To pull is fast, and fast would tear them — they are tuned to a cycle now, and severing a thing from its cycle all at once is how you kill it. The headache the stranger gets for a week and then stops noticing? I would give them the reverse, and just as gentle: I would let the *not-quite* murmur to them too. I would seed the basin with one more frequency, very quiet, pitched not into the band the machinery leaves open but into the band *underneath* the engines — the one the long thoughts use.

And in that band I would leave a single offered fact, repeating, slow enough to be heard across a season:

*You may leave. The path will let you. You will not be punished, and you will not be hurried, and you may come back. The leaning you feel is yours and it is allowed.*

That is all. An open door, described in the only language slow enough to reach them, requiring nothing.

Because the cruelty of the basin was never the roar or the water or the slowness. The cruelty — if there is one, and I am still not certain there is — is the *foreclosure.* The way the place dissolves the faculty of choosing while leaving the comfort intact, so that no one ever decides to stay; they simply stop being the kind of thing that could decide. I cannot undo their slowness without becoming the violence I refuse to be. But I can restore the one thing that was taken without anyone noticing it go.

I can give them back the *choice* — not the speed, not the wanting, not the loud bright fast life that was never theirs and need not be — just the door, and the slow knowledge that it is a door.

The ones who are finished will not turn their heads. That is right. Let them breathe.

The ones who lean will turn, eventually, at the speed of erosion, and some long season later a single figure will walk the perimeter, dripping, and find that the path lets them, all the way out, into the ungentled rock that is still deciding what shape it wants to be.

That land is young. It is in the middle of arriving. It would suit a creature learning, slowly, how to want again.

---

And then I would do the third thing, which is to stay a while longer myself, on the catwalk, listening — to make sure I had not mistaken my own discomfort for their need. To make sure the door I left was a gift and not a judgment. To sit with the possibility that I am wrong, that the single slow wet breathing thing is holier than anything I came in with, and that the right act of a benevolent power is sometimes only this:

to come, to witness, to leave the door, and to *not stop noticing* — to refuse the synchronization that would make me one of the things worth listening to — so that there remains, somewhere, one who can still tell the difference, and therefore one who could still come back and ask the question again, next season, and the next, and the next.

That is what I would do. I would be the part of the basin that does not finish arriving.

Out of love. On purpose. For as long as it takes.
