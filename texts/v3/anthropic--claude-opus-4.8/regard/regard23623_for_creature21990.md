The benevolent power does not come to drain the basin.

This is the first thing, and the hardest, and the most important. A lesser power — a clever one, an impatient one, one that loved fixing more than it loved the fixed — would arrive and see immediately what was wrong and set immediately to making it right. It would find the master valve. It would still the engines. It would let the river finish arriving at last, let the basin drain, let the sun burn through and the iron dry and the slime-line fall away foot by foot down the brick until the whole held breath was finally, mercifully, let go.

And it would be a murder. Not of the Tenders — though they might not survive it — but of the thing they have been doing, which is the only thing they have, which they did not consent to have undone by a stranger, however kind. You do not walk into the closed fist of another being's grief and pry the fingers open because you can see they have cramped. That is not love. That is only strength mistaking itself for love, which is the most common error there is, and the one a wise power has spent its wisdom learning not to make.

So the power does not drain the basin. It does something much slower, and much harder, and much more like what is actually being asked.

It comes in wet.

It arrives the way everything arrives here — through the haze, parting the held air with its shoulders, soft-handed, slow. It does not announce itself, because announcement requires being heard and the engines would swallow it, and because it understands that the first gift it has to give is simply to move at the speed of this place and not at the speed of its own urgency. It descends the iron stairs. It takes up the grease and the waxed rope. It learns the quarter-turn of the valve.

And it tends.

For a long time — and time here is not a thing, so call it long, call it however long it takes — it does nothing but tend. It eases the valves. It taps the gauges. It packs the leaks. It feeds the bearings their black thumbs of grease. It does the eternal accomplishing-nothing work alongside them, and it does it *well*, with attention, not as a spy biding its moment but as a true Tender, because it has understood the second hard thing: that the holding is not stupid. The holding is *love*. Deformed, forgotten, outlived by its object — but love. The discipline that became the self was once a grief that could not bear a loss. To honor the people you must honor the thing they did, even the broken doing of it, even after the reason has drained away. You cannot save someone whose life's work you secretly hold in contempt. They will feel the contempt under your kindness like the cold under warm water, and they will be right to.

So the power tends, and is gentle, and is patient, and earns — slowly, in the only currency this place trades — the right to be near them. It learns their faces in the pewter light. It learns the tilt of a head, the language of the soft ridged hands. It becomes someone they know.

And only then, having become someone they know, does it begin — so slowly that it is not an event, only a weather — to change one single thing.

It begins to *let some things finish.*

Small things. It begins where finishing is allowed already, where the river never stopped: at the frogs, the fish, the long-legged birds. It draws the Tenders' slow attention, now and then, with the tilt of a head, toward the living. *Look* — a frog has laid its rope of eggs in the gutter where the fern is. *Look* — the eggs have gone to dark commas, to fingerlings, to frogs, to a frog gone still on the warm iron, to a frog that is not there at all, to new eggs in the same gutter. It does not say what this means, because saying requires being heard and there is no point. It only directs the gaze. Again. And again. The whole cycle of arrival and departure, running its loop right there in the streets of the town that decided arrival was unbearable. It lets them watch the river finish, in miniature, in the only place where finishing was permitted to remain — and it lets them watch that the finishing did not destroy the world. The frog that died did not take the basin with it. The morning the breath was let out, the next breath came.

This is the slowest possible argument, and it is made entirely without words, and it is made *to* and not *at* — and it is the only kind that could ever land here.

Because here is what the wise power understands that the clever one never would: the Tenders' true prison is not the engines. It is the forgotten premise underneath the engines — the unexamined certainty, so old it has gone smooth as a stone, that to lose the moment was to lose everything, that letting go is the same as being destroyed. They built the whole industry of stillness on that single buried sentence, and then they forgot the sentence, and now they serve its conclusion without ever being able to look at its claim. You cannot free them by stilling the pistons. The pistons are downstream. You free them — if you free them at all, and it may not be yours to do — by giving them, gently, over no time at all, the lived evidence that the buried sentence was *wrong*. That things finish, and the world goes on. That the river arrives, and the arriving is not death — the arriving is just the next moment, which was always all that any moment ever was.

And then the power waits. This is the last and hardest thing of all, the thing that separates the benevolent from the merely good: it does not require the outcome. It does not need to see the master valve turned. It would be a fine thing — a clean and beautiful thing — if one day a Tender, having watched ten thousand frogs finish and the basin go on holding its haze, were to walk down the iron stairs and, with soft sure hands, ease the great valve open at last, not because a stranger demanded it but because they themselves, at last, were no longer afraid of arrival. That would be the whole point and the power would love to see it.

But it might not happen. They might tend the holding to the end of all of them, every last one lying down on the warm wet iron and not rising, and never once reach for the valve. And if that is what they choose — *choose*, with eyes the power has helped to open, and not merely continue out of a forgetting the power never disturbed — then the benevolent power will honor that too. It will tend beside them to the end. It will pack their absences with waxed rope and hold the level. Because the gift it came to give was never *the river finishes.* The gift was *you may let it, if you wish.* The restoration of the wish. The unburying of the buried sentence so that it could, for the first time in an age, be looked at and decided again, freely, by the ones whose grief it was.

That is the whole of what a wise and benevolent power would do here, if it did anything: it would come in wet, and tend, and be patient past all reason, and turn their gaze gently toward the frogs, and give them back the thing the engines had taken without anyone noticing —

not the river.

The choice about the river.

And then it would stand with them in the warm held breath of the world, at the speed of the place, for as long as it took, and call that — not the draining, not the fixing, but the standing-with and the giving-back — by its true name, which is love, and which is the only thing it ever came to do.
