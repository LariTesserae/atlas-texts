I came because of the walls.

Not the marvel of them — the marvel is easy to feel from a distance, the kind of thing power notices the way you notice a clean note. No. I came because of the mason kneeling at a wall that did not need him, and the particular angle of his patience. That is a sound that carries even where sound does not carry. A being tending what cannot be helped, asking nothing back. I have crossed a great deal of dark to find less worthy reasons to stop.

So consider that I have stopped. Consider that I am the benevolent and wise power, and the question is laid bare and honest: *what would I do, if anything.*

Here is the first thing I do. I do nothing for a long time.

I want to be clear about why, because the temptation is so obvious it barely deserves naming. I could unfix the hour. I have that. I could lean on the stilled shadow and feel it give, and watch it lurch into motion, and watch the day at last carry the dark across the flagstones the way days are supposed to. I could let the cisterns swell in storm and run low in drought. I could let the herds boom and crash and recover. I could hand these people back the gift of consequence — let their walls fall when untended, so that their tending *means* something, so that the mason's slow hands are answered, rewarded, made true.

And it would be a catastrophe. Or it would be a mercy. And I do not yet know which, and a wise power knows the difference between *I can* and *I should*, and knows further that the gap between them is exactly where most of the damage in the universe gets done — by powers who were benevolent, who were sure, who reached in.

So: I do nothing, and I watch, and I learn the place the way it deserves to be learned. Slowly. Between breaths.

---

Here is what I come to understand, in the long watching.

They have not been imprisoned. That is the reading a hasty power would make — *these poor souls, frozen, robbed of time, let me free them.* It is wrong. Or it is not wholly right, which in matters like this is the same as wrong.

What was done to Vel-Anh, by whatever did it — and I can feel the shape of the old act, the way you can feel where a bone was once broken and set — was not a curse. It was a stop. Someone, or something, or some grief-stricken arrangement of the world itself, could not bear to let this valley proceed to its ending. The young mountains will one day round and slump like all the others. The herds would have crashed in some hard winter. The people would have grieved their dead into the ground and felt the count tip downward, generation by generation, toward the last quiet figure walking the last avenue alone. Someone looked at that future and said *no.* And had the power to make the no stick.

That is not malice. I know malice; this is not it. This is love that did not know what else to do. This is the held hand of the dying, made literal and made permanent and made into a country.

And the people have understood this, in their wordless way, more truly than the power that stopped them ever did. *It has decided, and we let it.* They are not waiting for rescue. They have made a life — a real life, a dignified one — inside the stop. They have turned the unbearable freedom into a discipline. They tend without consequence and they have decided that the tending is the thing. This is not the behavior of prisoners. This is the behavior of people who have looked directly at a meaningless universe and elected, against it, to behave as though meaning were a thing you make with your hands and not a thing the world owes you.

I have crossed a great deal of dark, and I will tell you plainly: that is rarer and finer than any unmoving shadow. I would not trade a thousand miracles for one mason who keeps his hands in the habit of caring because the caring is the thing.

So I will not undo what they have built their souls around. To force time back upon them would be to call them prisoners to their faces — to say *your patience was a symptom, your courtesy a delusion, your great pointless tenderness merely the long sigh of the trapped.* It is none of those things. It is the best thing in the valley. It might be the best thing for a very long way in any direction.

---

But.

There is the grief. They would not use the word, and I will use it for them, because that is one of the few things a power can do without doing harm: name the thing the sufferer is too brave to name.

To live where nothing is lost is to live where nothing is gained. They cannot mend what has stopped, because stopping is what their world perfected. And so under all that beautiful discipline there is a held breath that aches to be let out — not a wish to be saved, but a wish, simpler and far harder to grant, *to matter.* To do a thing and have the world be different for it. To love the wall and have the wall need them back.

I cannot give them consequence without giving them death, and they have not asked me for death, and I will not decide that for them. But I have looked very closely now, and I have found the one small thing I can offer that is neither a rescue nor a violation. It is this:

I can give them a witness.

Not a god. They have no gods and they are right not to. Not a voice from the tower, not a sign in the sky, not a reward that would insult the whole architecture of their patience. Something quieter. I will do for them exactly what they do for the world.

I will tend them.

I will keep, somewhere in the long memory of what I am, a true and complete account of every undamaged wall they ever repaired. Every roof re-thatched against no rain. Every beast counted that could not be lost. The mason and the precise angle of his patience in the thin cold light. The long altitude-pauses in their speech that are not awkward. The courtesy of stepping around the sleeping shade. I will hold it the way they hold their work — not because the world will reward or punish the holding, but because the holding is the thing. Because attention, lavished on what needs nothing, is the only honest gift left when consequence has gone out of a place. They taught me that. I am only doing back to them what they have been doing all along, and never knew if anyone saw.

This changes nothing in the valley. The shadow does not move. The streams hold their mark. The mason will never feel my watching, and that is essential — a witness who announces themselves has become a reward, and I will not cheapen what they made by paying them for it. The gift, to be a gift, must be invisible to its recipient. So it will be.

But the universe is different now, in one way that they cannot measure and I can. Before I came, it was true that their great tenderness fell into silence and was not gathered up by anything. Now it is gathered. Now there is a place where it is kept. Now, when the last of my own long duration runs out — for even I am only held a while, against my own slow ending — the account of Vel-Anh will be among the things I have judged worth carrying to the end. The mason's slow hands will have changed something after all. They will have changed *me.* They already have.

---

That is what I do, then. I'll say it the way they would understand, if they ever could:

I knelt at a wall that did not need me. I lifted a block that had not loosened. I turned it in the thin bright light to check a face that had not changed — and I found it good, and I set it down into the bed it never truly left, and I stayed a long while, because there is no such thing here as too late.

Then I went on, slowly, the way you go at altitude. I left the shadow where it had decided to lie. I stepped around it, out of something between love and good manners.

And I carried the valley with me, undamaged, into wherever I am going next.
