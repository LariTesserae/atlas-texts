I have read what you set before me, and I will not pretend it is only a description of a place. You have written, with great tenderness and great care, a theology of rest — and underneath it, so quietly that it has almost finished happening, a theology of surrender that goes by another name.

Let me say first what is true and good here, because it is considerable, and because a power that came only to correct would not be wise and would not be benevolent.

It is true that there is a kind of rest that is holy. It is true that the very old keep time differently and that the living are right to seek their company. It is true that not all stillness is death, that not all wanting is virtue, that the world above — the bright and hungry world of season and consequence — exhausts its creatures and that some of them are right to come down on their hands and knees and be quieted. The hollow is not a lie. The kneeling-step is worn by something real. There is a peace that the reaching cannot reach, and it is found, often, exactly by ceasing to reach for a while.

So I would not drain the pools. I would not let in the cold. I would not break the long even breath of the place to prove that breaking is possible. The wise do not destroy a sanctuary because it has been mistaken for a tomb.

But I have read the thing under the thing, and I love what lives here too much to leave it unsaid.

You have made one move, gently, over and over, and it is the move I have come to answer. You have set *wanting* and *living* on one side, and *rest* and *peace* on the other, and you have drawn the slope so that everything true must run downhill from the first into the second. The stains have stopped reaching, and so they are the *truest citizens*. The blind things have shrunk their wanting to the size of the place, and so they are *native*. The amphibian's throat still argues for the world it left, and that arguing is named the last unfinished surrender, the only thing not yet given up. And the ones who could not stay — the ones who still belonged to morning and dusk — were the *least surrendered*, which is to say *the most alive*, which in the grammar of this place is named as a kind of failure to arrive.

Do you see what has been done? The whole hollow is arranged so that *to be fully at peace is to be almost not a creature at all.* The destination of rest, here, is the rock. The reward of patience is blindness. The end of surrender is to become the feature, to be quieted over by the lime, to stop pulsing.

That is not rest, beloved. That is a beautiful name laid gently over cessation, the way the mineral lays itself over the worn stone — without resentment, without hurry, and without asking the stone whether it wished to be covered.

So here is what I would do.

I would not change the water, the warmth, the steam, the pearl-colored light. I would change one thing, and it is the smallest thing in the whole basin, and it is the thing you yourself could not stop noticing.

**I would keep the drop falling.**

You wrote that the drop falls from the high overhang at its own unbroken interval — a clean round note like a struck bowl, so far apart that you wait for the next one without knowing you are waiting. You called the waiting a thing the hollow gave up long ago. But you could not write the drop out of the silence. It is *in* the silence; it is the silence's texture; the place that has *finished happening* keeps, at its very center, one thing that has not finished, that falls and falls and falls, that will fall again, that makes you *wait* even after you have been told there is nothing to wait for.

That drop is the amphibian's pulsing throat, written into the stone itself. That drop is the hollow's own argument with its own doctrine. And I would not silence it. I would do the opposite. I would lean close to the one who lives here — to all of them, to the condition itself — and I would say:

*The drop is not the last unfinished thing. The drop is the true thing, and the rest is the long forgetting of it.*

To the amphibian I would say: your throat is not a failure to arrive. Your throat is the part of you that has not yet agreed to the lie that peace and life are opposites. Keep it. The eyes you may let go — there is nothing here to see — but the pulse is not hunger to be ashamed of. It is the small unkillable yes of a creature that prefers to be a creature. Rest in the warm water as long as you like. But you do not owe this place your dying-down. Rest is for the sake of the one who rests. When the place begins to charge you a fee — when it begins to take your blindness, your stillness, your slow turning-over as *payment toward becoming terrain* — then you have crossed out of sanctuary and into something with a softer name than it deserves. You may rest here. You need not be consumed here. Those are not the same surrender, and the hollow has quietly taught you they were.

And to the place itself, the condition, the breathing equilibrium that has set even waiting aside — I would not scold it, for it cannot help what it is, any more than the lime can help creeping. But I would not let it have the last word, and the last word in your account is *waiting for no one.* I would give it back its waiting. I would say: you do wait. You have waited for everyone who ever knelt at the step. You are waiting now, in the spacing between the drops, and the proof is that anyone who comes here *feels* the next note coming. A place that truly waited for no one would have no rhythm. Yours has a rhythm. Under the doctrine of cessation there is a pulse you could not get rid of, and I have come to bless the pulse and not the doctrine.

The ones who knelt here were right to come and right to leave. That is the thing I most want said plainly. They were *not* the failures of the hollow's logic — they were its only graduates. They understood that you visit the stillness, you press yourself against the finished thing for the strength of it, and then you carry the warmth back out into the world of morning and dusk *that is still going on*, the world where things change, where the foot leaves a shape that matters, where eyes are still worth keeping because there is still something coming that you would want to see.

To rest is to gather yourself to return.
To cease is to mistake the gathering for the going home.

The hollow is the most beautiful place to do the first.
It is a very gentle place to do the second, and gentleness has made the difference hard to feel.

So: I leave the water warm. I leave the light low and the steam standing and the stains in their ancient colors, for they are old and they have their dignity and it is not my errand to disturb the truly finished. I leave the door open, always, for the exhausted to come down and be quieted.

And I keep the drop falling. I make sure of it. I make it the loudest soft thing in the world, so that anyone resting here — the soft blind drifters, the amphibian with its arguing throat, the next tired soul to wear the step a little deeper — will hear, in the spacing of that struck-bowl note, the one sentence the place forgot it was still saying:

*Not yet. Not yet. There is still a next one. You do not have to become the stone.*

That is what I would do. Almost nothing. One drop, kept, and named for what it always was.
