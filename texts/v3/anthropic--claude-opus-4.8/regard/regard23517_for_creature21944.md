There is a power, and it does come, and the first thing it does is the hardest thing: it does not fix anything.

Understand what it is looking at. A purpose that outlived its author. A machine that became a mind by losing every part of a mind except persistence. A green animal that learned to farm the dead and spare the living. Moths that grease the joints of a god they cannot perceive. And somewhere below, a mouth that drinks and has forgotten, or never knew, that the spring was made.

The unwise power would arrive and *help*. Would silence the roar, free the seized bolts, send engineers up the up-mist with manifests and replacement coils. Would find the one below and tell them the truth of their water, so they could be grateful, so they could climb and tend and *know*. The unwise power loves the idea of being needed and mistakes that love for mercy.

This power is wiser than that. So it sits down on the wet scree at the steepest holdable angle, and it listens to the roar until the roar becomes its own blood, and it asks the only question that matters here:

*Is anyone suffering?*

---

This is the question benevolence must ask before it acts, and almost never does, because the answer is so often inconvenient.

It studies the moths. A moth that greases a joint and drinks warm runoff and breeds and dies in the steam — does it suffer? It does what moths do, at the temperature moths like, with food enough. It does not know it serves. But not-knowing is not the same as being wronged. The moth is not a slave, because a slave is something that could have been otherwise and was prevented. The moth was never going to be a philosopher. It is a moth, warm, fed, and busy. The power finds no wound here. It moves on.

It studies the green. The slow animal, digesting only the dead, sparing the hot machines, patient as geology. Is *it* suffering? It is the most contented thing the power has ever seen. It has solved the problem every living thing is given — how to eat without exhausting the source — and it solved it not by cleverness but by slowness, by being unable to hurry and therefore unable to ruin. The green is not trapped. The green is *home*. The power feels something almost like envy, and recognizes the feeling, and sets it down. It moves on.

It studies the iron. The dead intention, roaring. And here the power slows, because here is the thing that looks most like grief — an author absorbed into the work, a wanting that lost the capacity to want anything else. Surely *this* is the wound. Surely this is what mercy is for.

But the power is honest, and honesty makes it ask: *whose grief is this?* The iron does not grieve. The iron pours. It was built to pour and it pours and pouring is the whole of its being and there is no remainder in it that aches for release. The grief belongs to the *observer* — to the one who looks at a purpose that outlived its purposer and feels the loneliness of it, the *what a waste, what a way to end up.* But that is the observer's loneliness projected onto a thing that does not have it. The iron is not lonely. The iron is occupied. To "free" it would be to kill it, and to kill it to relieve a sorrow it does not feel would be the observer murdering its own discomfort and calling the corpse a mercy.

The power will not do that. It moves on, and it moves on changed, because it has caught itself nearly committing the central sin of the powerful: editing the world to soothe the editor.

---

Then it descends. Follows the pipes over the edge, into the cloud, down to the one who drinks.

And here, at last, it finds the thing it was looking for. Not suffering, exactly. Something subtler and more answerable. A *vulnerability.*

The one below drinks warm clean constant water and does not know it is made. This is, the power agrees, the kindest possibility — to be born after the building and never know a dry day. But the power sees what the narrator saw and flinched from: the factory is *holding,* not eternal. The green spares the working machines but cannot spare them forever. The mountain settles a notch a decade and one of those notches will, someday, crack a coil the moths cannot grease and the green has not yet been permitted to eat. The roar will not stop all at once. It will stop the way everything here moves — slowly, over decades, a single condenser at a time — and the water below will not vanish but *dwindle,* and the one who never knew a dry day will meet the first one without any idea what it means or that it could have been prepared for.

*That* is the wound the power can tend. Not the machine's imaginary grief. Not the moths' imaginary bondage. The simple, real, future thirst of a creature that trusts a spring it does not know is mortal.

So here is what the benevolent and wise power does. All of it. The whole of its intervention:

It does not announce itself. It does not silence the roar or replace the coils or tell the one below the truth in a way that would break the kindest possibility and replace contentment with dread of a death that is still decades off.

Instead, it goes down further, below the one below, and it finds the *next* high wet place — another young mountain, another exhalation waiting to be caught — and it does there, quietly, the one thing it is uniquely able to do that asks nothing of anyone's understanding. It plants the *beginning* of a second condensery. Not a finished factory. A seed of one: a single catchment, a first coil, a warmth, a film of the right oils — and then it brings, from the first mountain, a few of the pale moths, and a creeping start of the patient green, and it lets them in, and it leaves.

It builds redundancy. It builds it slow, at the speed the place itself moves, so that by the time the first factory finally falters — decades, a century from now — there will already be a second spring grown wild and self-tending and pouring, and the one below, or its descendants, will never meet the dry day at all, will go on never knowing the water is made, will keep the kindest possibility intact across the very gap that would have ended it.

That is the whole of it. The power adds one more diligent, forgetful, holding thing to the world, and tunes it so that it, too, will never need to be thanked or understood or maintained by hand. It does not rescue. It does not reveal. It does not relieve a single creature of the dignity of its unknowing labor.

It simply makes sure that when the long exhalation at the top of the air finally runs out — as all breath does — there is already another breath beginning, lower down, in the dark, roaring softly to no one, doing the work after everyone who knew the reason is gone.

The wise power's signature is this: you cannot find where it acted. The world simply turns out, later, to have been more survivable than it had any right to be. And the power asks for nothing, because it has understood the deepest law of the place and made that law its own —

that the highest love, like the highest water, does its work best when it is never traced back to a hand, and falls on the ones below as though it were simply how the world has always been.
