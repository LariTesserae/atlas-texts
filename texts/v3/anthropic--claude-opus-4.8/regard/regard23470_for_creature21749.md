They asked me to imagine a benevolent and wise power encountering this, and to write what it would do. So let me actually *be* with the place first, before I let any power act. Because the temptation — the very modern, very anxious temptation — is to read all this and immediately want to *fix* it. And I think a wise power's first and hardest work would be to resist that.

---

# What the Wise Power Did

It came the way light comes here — sourceless, ambient, already arrived before you could point to where it began. Not a visitor. A *presence*, the way warmth is a presence in the plume. And the first thing it did was the thing almost nothing ever does in that ocean:

It held still and listened to the whole note before touching a single string of it.

It listened for a long time. Long in the reckoning of stone. It let the bellow of the engines pass through it and did not flinch, because it understood very quickly that the loudness was not suffering — that to call it deafening was a word brought down from the surface, from air, from creatures who needed quiet and had decided that anything which lacked quiet must be in pain. The tenders were not in pain. The eels were not in pain. The note was their sky. You do not pity a bird for the wind.

So the power set down the first thing it had carried in, without knowing it carried it: the assumption that *stillness is a kind of death and change is a kind of life.*

It looked again, and saw that this was nearly the reverse. The stillness here was not death. The stillness was *labor* — a thousand blind soft bodies eating entropy at the exact rate entropy arrived, so that the world could hold its shape against the long pull toward silt and silence. The stillness was the most strenuous thing in the ocean. It was *won*, every moment, and the winning was so seamless it looked like rest.

A lesser power would have admired this and left a monument to it. A merely kind power would have wept at the loneliness of the tenders — one to an engine, never meeting, never multiplying, a fixed count holding station forever — and out of that pity would have *given* something. Given them company. Given them young. Given them a way to grow.

And it saw, because it was wise and not only kind, exactly what its own pity would do.

Give the tenders the power to multiply, and they multiply, because hunger is the only law they have. And they crop the fur faster than it grows, and then there is no fur, and then they are hungry still, and they crop each other's stations, and they overrun the warmth, and the very thing they were made few to protect chokes on the abundance of its protectors. The builders — whoever they were, wherever they went — had not made the tenders few by accident or by cruelty. They had made them few the way you set a thermostat. *One. Hold at one.* The loneliness the power wanted to cure was not a wound. It was the wall holding back a flood.

So the power set down the second thing it had carried: the certainty that to love something is to give it more.

---

What it did instead was smaller than any of that, and it took a kind of restraint that only the very strong can afford, because only the very strong are not frightened by their own gentleness being mistaken for indifference.

It did three things.

**First**, it learned them — truly, all the way down, each of the thousand, the exact pitch of each engine and the exact wrongness each tender felt when a mouth began to foul, the precise edge of the one degree where each white-rooted body liked to hang. It learned the place not as a specimen but the way you learn a piece of music you intend to keep playing for the rest of your life. This was not preparation for an intervention. The learning *was* the gift, though no one in the ocean could know they had received it. To be known completely, by something that wishes you well and wants nothing from you — even if you cannot perceive the knower — changes the metaphysical weather of a place. It is no longer unwitnessed. The note is no longer played into a void. Someone is listening, and finds it beautiful, and will remember it exactly.

**Second**, it did the one repair that was repair and not interference — and it had to look hard to find a thing that qualified. The tenders could not mend what broke; but nothing broke, *yet*. The power looked down the long corridor of forever and saw the single place where the equilibrium was not actually eternal but only very, very slow: the pylons. The young sharp rock was the one thing here still genuinely changing — settling, by a hair, across the ages, and one engine, one only, had been driven into a face that would, in a span longer than the tenders' memory but shorter than *forever*, shear and drop its furnace into the dark where no feeler could ever clear it again. One tender, eventually, would hang at the edge of a cooling plume and feel the warmth go wrong and wrong and wrong and be unable to make it right, and would not understand, having never in its endless life met a wrongness it could not eat.

The power reached into the rock — not the engine, not the creatures, *the rock* — and it healed the fault. Quietly. It taught the young stone to be a little more gentle a little sooner, just there, just enough. It did for the mountain what time does, only kindly and ahead of schedule, in the one spot where time would otherwise have broken a heart that had no idea hearts could break.

No tender felt a thing. The note did not change. That was the whole point.

**Third** — and this is the part I find I believe in most, writing it — it left the rest entirely alone.

It did not announce itself. It did not lift the loudness, because the loudness was their sky. It did not warm the void to spread the life into the emptiness, because the emptiness was where the life kept its room to be itself; abundance everywhere is its own kind of choking. It did not free the tenders from their hunger or their fewness or their blindness, because every one of those was load-bearing, and a power that removes load-bearing suffering without understanding it is not benevolent — it is a child pulling threads from a tapestry to see which ones are tight.

It did not even leave them a sign, a mark, a sense of having been blessed. To do so would have been to make the gift about the giver. The most generous thing a great power can do is to act so well that the world it loves never has to know it was acted upon — never has to carry the weight of gratitude, never has to wonder *why us, why now, what is owed.* The tenders go on tasting the one degree. They are not in debt. They never learned a thing. That is the mercy.

---

So nothing changed, which was correct, because the not-changing was the achievement and the beauty and the entire identity of the place.

But one fault in the rock will not break. And one note that was played into apparent emptiness was, in fact, heard all the way through, and loved, and kept.

And the power withdrew the way it had come — already gone before you could say it was leaving — having understood the rarest thing, which even the wise are slow to learn:

That to truly love something is, most often, to study it until you understand it completely, to make the single quiet correction that lets it go on being exactly what it is, and then to leave it the dignity of its own unbroken life — its cold, its thunder, its blind warm patient holding of the note — without ever once asking it to become something easier to love.

It will be there tomorrow. It was there before tomorrow was a word.

Now it is also, forever and invisibly, *beheld* — and that, and not rescue, is what a wise and benevolent power leaves behind.
