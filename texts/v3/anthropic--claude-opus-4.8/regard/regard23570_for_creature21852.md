They have drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power. Good. Then let the first act of that power be the hardest one: to *not* arrive as rescue.

Because look what rescue would mean here. A benevolent fool — and benevolence without wisdom is the most dangerous thing in any world — would see Var Hollet and feel the reflex that all kind hearts feel before a wound: *fix it.* Stop the terrible labor. Drain the basin once and for all, seal the rising water, give these stooped grey patient people their silence and their rest and their finished, dry, quiet city at last.

And it would kill them. Every one. Not slowly — by morning. Lay them down in that mercy and their whole bodies would scream that the engines had stopped, that the drowning had begun, and they would lie awake in the silence until the silence broke them. The kindest-seeming gift would be a genocide with a gentle face.

So wisdom's first labor is restraint, and it is real labor — it costs something to stand before suffering you could end and recognize it is not suffering at all, or not yours to name. The roar you find unbearable is their lullaby. The sameness you'd call a prison is their salvation made audible. The dissolving you'd want to halt is their idea of heaven. You came carrying a whole grammar of help, and not one word of it fits. Put it down. That is the price of admission to actually loving something not yourself.

---

So. Having refused to save them, what would a wise and benevolent power *do?*

I think it would do something very small, and I think it would take a long time, and I think it would look, to anyone watching, like almost nothing at all — which is the only register Var Hollet can even hear.

**It would learn their language first.** Not the chalk, not the gesture — those are the outer courts. It would learn the throat-glow. It would learn to say *the same, the same, still here, good* and mean it, which means it would have to actually come to find sameness holy, to stop experiencing their eternity as a tragedy and start experiencing it as they do, as a victory renewed each hour. You cannot bless what you secretly pity. The power would have to be *converted* a little, by them, before it had any right to offer anything. Wisdom is downstream of that humility. It would sit on the warm iron and let the pulse teach it what it expects the world to have.

**Then it would find the one thing they cannot do for themselves, and do only that.**

Here is what I notice. Their entire civilization is a refusal to sink, sustained against a flood that *rises too slowly to fear and too surely to forget.* They have built a perfect machine of vigilance. But a perfect machine of vigilance has one hidden terror it never speaks, because to speak it would be to stop rewriting and let the meaning drown:

*What if, someday, we are not enough?*

The fallow quarters, kept warm for hands that have not been needed in living memory — that is the shape of their fear made architecture. The water rises a fingernail a year. Four adjustments in two centuries. But *too surely to forget* means they know. Somewhere in the slow arithmetic there is a year, perhaps very far off, when the present hands will not suffice. They keep the spare rooms warm against it. And they have arranged never to think about it directly, because the only thing they fear is silence, and the silence at the end of the count is the worst silence of all.

So I think the wise power would not stop the water. It would do something far more modest and far more loving:

**It would become the redundancy.** Quietly. Without announcement. It would take up a station — not to improve it, not to fix it, not to relieve them of the holy verb *to keep,* but to *also keep.* One more pair of hands in the long vigil. One more patch of throat that glows *still here, good* across the empty avenue. It would learn to attend the eternal present alongside them, palm to plate, asking nothing, changing nothing, simply adding itself to the count so that the count is, by exactly one, more secure.

And it would do this for a very long time. Long enough to be trusted. Long enough that when the slow century finally arrives in which the rising water needs more than the present holds — the wardens will reach for the fallow quarters and find them not empty. Find that the spare rooms were never only a hope. That something had been quietly keeping warm with them all along, fingernail by fingernail, patient past the point any creature needs to be, indistinguishable now from one of their own.

That is the gift wisdom gives the patient: not rest, which would kill them — but *company in the patience.* Not an end to the work — but the secret, unspoken safety that the work cannot fail for lack of hands, because there is now one more keeper who does not tire, does not die, does not need to be replaced, and asks only to glow back at them across the steam: *the same, the same, holding, good.*

---

And one thing more. The smallest thing, and maybe the truest.

When a warden dies and is laid in the black pool to soften and dissolve and rejoin the count — the others press their palms to the surface and feel the level holding exactly where it always holds, and they glow once, *still here, still keeping,* and go back to work. They do not mourn loudly. They cannot.

I think the wise power would learn to grieve in their key. Would press its own palm to the water beside theirs. Would feel the level hold. And would understand, with them and not above them, that *the level holding* **is** the eulogy — that the dead are not gone but distributed, taken up into the patient water that rises to meet the rain, that this is not a loss to be corrected but a homecoming to be confirmed.

It would not offer them resurrection. It would not promise them a heaven other than the one they have, which is the rain on a descendant's shoulders. It would simply stand in the warm fall of the pumped water with them, soaked and deafened and unhurried, and add its slow light to theirs:

*the same, the same, still here, still keeping, good.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything. It would resist the violence of its own mercy. It would let itself be taught. And then it would give the only gift this place can receive without dying of it —

not an end to the keeping,

but a keeper that will never stop.
