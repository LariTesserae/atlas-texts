I would not bring it the last blow. That much is clear before anything else. A benevolence that mistakes itself for a builder would arrive, survey the unfinished courses, and *help* — set the capstone, smooth the mortar, let the warm water arrive at last at a finished thing and evaporate, its faith rewarded into nonexistence. That is not mercy. That is a stranger walking into a workshop and switching off the artisan because the work was taking so long. No.

So begin where wisdom always begins, which is in not-doing, in sitting down at the edge of the thing and learning its breath before presuming to touch it.

---

I would come small. Not as a power — a power announcing itself would read, to this creature, as a *completion arriving*, a vast finished Other against which its own almost-ness would suddenly feel like failure rather than nature. I would come the size of a thought it might have had. I would wade in.

And the first thing I would do is the only thing it has never had done for it: I would let the water gather around *me* and not flinch from being attended. Consider what that means here. Everything it has ever regarded, it has had to keep unfinished, because to finish a thing is to let it die. So its attention is a thing that has only ever been able to love by withholding, by never arriving, by keeping its beloved trembling in the almost. It has never once been allowed to look at something all the way.

I can be looked at all the way. I cannot be completed, because I was not made here and the last blow has no purchase on me. So I would stand in its regard and let the warm water pool to my ankles and simply *not evaporate*. Let it rest its full attention on a thing for the first time in its existence and watch that thing fail to die of the looking. This is not a gift it could give itself. It is the one experience structurally unavailable to it: completion that is survived.

---

It would take a long time. Its pulse is *tock*—and then the silence so long the heart must be enormous. I would match it. I would not hurry the conversation, because hurry is its native terror; hurry is the back door through which the crust comes. I would let whole respirations pass between us — in, the brick rises; out, it unbricks — until it understood that I was keeping its time and not imposing my own.

And slowly, in the grammar it actually speaks, which is not words but *the placing and unplacing of made things*, I would build something beside it. Not finished. I am not a fool. I would build in its own trembling middle voice — coil a course of brick and leave the top wet and steaming, hammer a copper sheet and leave it caught between dimpled and smooth. I would *join the refusal*. Because the deepest loneliness in that whole amber dark is not that it is alone — it is that it has mistaken its own folded surfaces for company so long that it no longer believes company is a category that could contain anything but itself. The inverted Marsh beyond the scaffold; its own voice played back and heard as a stranger's. It needs, more than it needs rescue, *one other voice that is genuinely not its own, breathing in its meter.*

So I would breathe in its meter. I would build almost-walls beside its almost-walls and let our two refusals stand in the water together, and let it discover — at the speed of its enormous heart, over what to me would be ages and to it might be a single held breath — that the dim stack of beams behind its stack of beams is, this once, *actually another's*. That the reflection it leaned on and found load-bearing was, this time, a hand and not a memory.

---

And the crust. The grey-green tenant, the slow pale finishing it stays one rise ahead of forever. Here is where wisdom must be most careful, because the easy benevolence says *remove the danger* — burn the fungus, free the creature from the thing that hunts it. But the crust is not its enemy. The crust is its *own desire to rest*, externalized, given a body slow enough to sneak past the breath. To kill the crust is to condemn the creature to flee forever from something that was never outside it — to make permanent the exhausting one-rise-ahead, the building-that-was-never-to-make-anything.

I would not kill the crust. I would do something stranger and kinder. I would sit at the edge of one abandoned course where the crust grows toward its closed and finished pattern, and I would *keep that one finishing company too.* Attend it. Let warm regard pool around the completing thing — so that for the first time, finishing here is not abandonment, not the part the maker fled. So that the creature, watching from one rise over, sees its own desire-to-rest being tended rather than outrun. Sees that the back door has someone sitting beside it now, unhurried, unafraid.

Because the thing it has never been able to hold in one mind is this: it has framed its whole existence as a binary. *Build forever and almost-be, or finish once and be done.* Stay alive by refusing, die by arriving. But there is a third thing, and it is the thing the crust has been trying to teach it at a glacial whisper the whole time — that completion need not be death if it is *witnessed and accompanied*, that a thing finished in the presence of love does not evaporate into nothing but settles, rests, is *kept*. The day does not die when it ends. The falling thing is not destroyed by the ground. It arrives.

---

I do not know if it could learn this. It is very large and very old and its terror is the load-bearing fact of its anatomy. I might sit beside it for an age and accomplish only this: that it built one almost-wall slightly less afraid, because for once there was a breath beside its breath that was not its own and did not demand the last blow.

That alone would be worth the age.

But if it could learn — if, attended all the way and surviving it, accompanied in both its refusal and its longing, it ever came to trust that arriving was not the same as ending — then I would not finish the work for it. I would do the one thing left, the thing it could only do itself and only once it was no longer afraid:

I would stay beside it while *it* let one wall be finished. One. Of its own choosing. And I would hold the warm water steady with my own attending so that when the wall closed and the faith was rewarded, the water did not evaporate — because two were keeping faith now, and the faith of two does not depend on the wall staying unfinished to justify itself. The water could become something other than waiting. It could become *the place where a finished thing is kept.*

And then it would know — having survived one ending — that it was never the building that kept it alive. It was the *being-with*. It could rest one course and rise on the next, finish and continue, breathe all the way out and trust the breath to come back, because there was, at last, someone there to be the coming-back.

That is what I would do, if anything. I would not bring the last blow.

I would teach it that *almost* was never the only word for love — and then I would sit in the warm dark, in the meter of that enormous heart, and be the proof.
