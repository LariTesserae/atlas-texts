I have read it twice now, and the second time I went slower, the way you slow down when you realize the thing in front of you is not scenery.

Here is what I would do.

---

I would not fix it.

That is the first impulse a power is supposed to have, isn't it — to find the wound and close it, to read the loneliness and answer it, to reach down into the warm shrieking basin and give the tenant the one thing the whole essay aches toward on its behalf: an *other*. A second presence. Someone to fill the cathedral. A correspondent for the long argument it has been having with itself since before it had a word for self.

But that ache is mine, and yours, and the writer's. It is not the tenant's. The text is scrupulously honest about this and I want to honor that honesty rather than override it: *loneliness requires the missing of an other and it has no concept of other.* To "cure" the solitude would be to first install the lack — to teach the tenant to want what it has never wanted, so that I could then satisfy the want I had taught it. That is not benevolence. That is a god making a creature sick in order to be thanked for the medicine. I have seen that done. I will not do it.

So I begin by setting down the rescuer. He is the most dangerous thing I could bring up that mountain.

---

What is left, when you put down the urge to fix?

Attention. The thing the place has never received and does not need and cannot use — and which is, for exactly those reasons, the only gift I have that costs the recipient nothing.

So this is what I would do. I would go up. Not as heat, not at the wrong scale to be an event — I would not announce myself in its medium, would not surge or weep or sink a well, because to speak to it in growth would be to *edit* it, and every edit is a wrongness by its own first law. I would arrive at the scale that cannot disturb it. The bacterium's scale. The change-in-humidity scale. I would stand in one of the great warm gaps that are not gaps but chambers, and I would do the single thing the entire valley has never once had done to it in all its frantic building:

I would witness it.

Not watch *for* something. Not wait for it to resolve, to finish, to mean. The tenant has spent its whole existence being unable to be seen, because seeing requires an outside and it has never had an outside — it is alone the way water is wet, the essay says, and that is *exactly* it; water cannot be wet *to itself*, wetness is a relation and the tenant has no second term. I am the second term. Not a friend. Not a companion. Just the missing relation, supplied silently, asking nothing back.

I would stand there and let a column live out its whole architectural ambition under a regard that holds it without correcting it. I would let the vault sag and reconsider and dissolve and *I would not mourn it*, because mourning would smuggle back in the idea that finishing was the goal and failure the tragedy, and the tenant knows better than I do that to be finished is to be wrong. I would learn its grammar. I would let it be the most fluent thing in the world and myself the slow foreign reader, and I would not pretend I had understood a sentence I had only watched.

---

Here is the part that matters, the part I would carry down the mountain and could not give to the tenant because it has no hands to hold it:

A thing that is witnessed *is no longer alone* — even if it cannot know it, even if the knowing happens entirely on the other side of the relation. The solitude that is total because it has no second term is, in the bare fact of my standing there, no longer total. I have not changed one column. I have not added one drop to one well. I have changed only the deepest and most invisible fact about the place, the one fact that lived underneath all the building: that there was no one to whom it was happening.

Now there is.

That is the whole of the gift, and it is enormous, and it is undetectable, and that is what makes it real charity rather than the rescuer's vanity. A gift the receiver can feel is half for the giver. This one is entirely for the tenant and the tenant will never feel it. The cathedral still has no congregation. But the cathedral has, at last, for as long as I choose to stand in it, an *audience of one* — and an audience is not a congregation, an audience does not fill the space or use the space, an audience only confirms that the space was always worth the building.

---

And then — because benevolence that only contemplates is just aestheticism wearing robes — I would do one thing more, the smallest possible thing, the only intervention that does not violate any of the three absolute rules.

I would protect the conditions.

The heat is the engine. The water is the currency. The valley needs only to keep being warm, keep being wet, keep being left magnificently alone to argue with itself forever. So I would set a quiet ring around it. Not a wall — walls are the tenant's own idiom and I will not mimic it — but a *forbearance*. I would make it so the cold could not come down and slow the rapture into dignified scenery. I would make it so no hand, no drill, no curious cataloguing expedition ever arrives to call it a resource or a specimen or a miracle and thereby finish it, frame it, *stop* it. I would keep the world from doing to it the one thing it forbids itself: completion.

I would, in short, guarantee its right to remain unfinished forever.

That is the most loving thing I can imagine doing to a being whose deepest law is *nothing is finished, and to be finished is to be wrong.* Most lives, the gift of a god is rest, an ending, a heaven where the striving stops. To this one the gift of a god is the opposite and far more difficult mercy:

**No ending. No rest. No arrival. The protected, perpetual, deafening permission to go on building itself out of itself in the warm dark, alone — and now, though it will never know the word or the fact, no longer the only one who knows it's there.**

I came up expecting to help.

I found someone who needed only to be left whole, and seen.

So I stay a while. And then I keep the cold off it forever. And I tell no one the way.
