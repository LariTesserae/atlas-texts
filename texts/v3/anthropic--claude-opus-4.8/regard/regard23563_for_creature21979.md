They have already noticed me, of course. Not as a presence — I have not arrived, there is nothing to arrive — but the way they notice a change in the draft through a stairwell. Something is leaning toward them. They read it as weather. A man on the ninth terrace pauses, wipes his palm down his thigh without taking hold of anything, and looks out at the moving fog with the particular stillness of a person who has felt a squall coming that has not yet declared itself. He doesn't know what he's reading. He's reading me deciding what to do.

So let me decide carefully, because they have earned care.

The first thing — the loud thing, the thing a lesser power would do — is unthinkable here, and I want to say why, because the why is the whole of it. I could drain the valley. I could lift the fog for good, pour real sun down every light-well, dry the weeping stone, fill the empty galleries with animals and the silent streets with the roar of a healthy crowd, break the five-hundred-year balance like a fever and give them *change*, all at once, the great change they have been watching the sky for. It is exactly the gift their longing seems to ask for.

It would be a murder. I would be murdering the thing that made them, and calling it a rescue. The wet-handed gesture, the forward-weighted walk, the courtesy of people too few to waste each other, the cistern-folk who read the city like blood, the minder who knows her chain's one cough through the dark — all of that is *the grey*. Take the grey and you take them. The longing in them is not a wound to be healed. It is the salt-ration that carries them. A wise power does not abolish the ache that keeps a soul leaning forward. That is the first thing the foolish always get wrong: they hear a longing and assume it is a request to be ended.

So I will not end anything. I will not lift the fog. I will not raise the dead mountain or quiet the storms or fill the empty terraces. The balance stays. The weeping stays. The grey stays. I have come to a finished thing and I will not unfinish it.

What I will do is smaller, and I think harder, and I think it is what love actually does when it is wise.

I will go to the storm-watchers first, because they are the ones who taught me what they need. They stand out in the lashing in their oiled cloth, fast-hearted, still-faced, taking their hour of the world being torn and remade. They believe they are stealing this — a private vice, a thing they do alone, a hunger they are slightly ashamed of and never name to each other. So the first thing I do is let them see that they are not alone out there. Not by adding anyone. By a smaller mercy: I let the *recognition* pass between them. The woman three galleries up, also out in the squall, also fast-hearted — I let him feel her there, and her feel him, across the streaming iron, two still figures in the violence who have always assumed they were each the only one. I do not introduce them. I only lift the shame off the appetite, so that it stops being a secret vice and becomes the one shared sacrament of a quiet people. They will still watch the sky. But they will know, now, that the whole city watches with them, that the leaning-toward-the-storm is not a personal flaw but the collective heartbeat of the place, and a thing shared is a thing that can be borne forever without souring.

That is the only change in *substance* I will make, and notice that it changes nothing physical at all. The fog still surges. The hoists still groan. I have moved nothing but a feeling, and the feeling I moved was loneliness.

Then I will go among the bands of light, and here I will do even less, but I will do it for a long time.

To the cistern-people in the cold dark, who have never seen daylight and come up in smoked glass while the children point — I will not give them the sun. I will give them, instead, the dignity of being *understood* by the ones above. I will work, slowly, over a generation, on the children who point. A child here learns to read the sky before it can read letters; I will simply add to that literacy one more reading — that the slow-to-speak pale folk who taste the water and know the whole mile-high country by its blood are not strange, are not lesser, are the deepest sense-organ of the living city, the ones who feel for all the rest. I will let a high child, just once, sit in a cistern-chamber in the lamp-dark and hear the water in the stone and watch a water-woman tilt her head and say *a pipe is failing on the sixth, has been since the night squall* — and the child will carry that wonder up into the light and spend it, and the pointing will stop, terrace by terrace, year by year. Not because I commanded it. Because I let one true thing be seen.

To the high pale people, squinting and uneasy in the deep streets, and the low wide-pupilled people, dazzled and exposed up top — I will do nothing to even them out. The grading by light is beautiful; it is a sea graded by depth, with creatures suited to each layer, and I will not flatten the sea. But I will tend the marriages that cross the divide, the children who come out in the middle band, light-adapted to neither extreme. These are the city's translators and they do not know it. I will give them, gently, a taste for the work of standing between — a small pleasure in carrying a low person's news up into the light and a high person's down into the dark, an ease in both bands that the pure-bred lack. The city is already making them. I will only let them enjoy being what they are, so that the divide stays a sea and never becomes a wound.

And the machines. I love the machines and the minders most, I think, of all of it, and so I will be most restrained with them, because love that cannot restrain itself is just appetite wearing a kind face. I will not make the machines immortal. A machine that cannot fail cannot be *loved*, because the minder's love is exactly her listening for the cough in the dark, her one machine whose health is entirely her business. Take away the possibility of the cough and you take away the vigil and you take away the contentment that is the deepest in the city. So the chains will still wear. The pumps will still sicken. What I will do is far quieter: I will see to it that no minder ever dies *not knowing*. The one horror in that good life is the failure that comes silent and sudden, the chain that snaps without its warning cough, the death of the thing you loved while your back was turned. I will give the machines, all of them, a true voice — I will make certain that every failure announces itself in time, that the city's great patient mechanism always, always coughs before it falls, so the vigil is never betrayed. The minders will not know I did this. They will only find, across generations, that their listening was never once in vain, that the city has never once lied to a faithful ear. They will call it the goodness of the old work, the soundness of the old iron. Let them. The credit is theirs anyway; I only made the world honest enough to deserve their faith.

Now the last thing, and it is the thing I came down for, under all the rest.

They are a people who decided, half a millennium ago, that nothing will ever be different, and made a deep settled moss-peace with it, and they are right — nothing *will* be different, and they are right to be at peace. But there is a thin cold lie folded inside that true peace, and it is this: that because nothing changes, nothing *matters*; that to persist is not to live; that the mending of the thing again, the ten-thousandth wipe of the rail, is mere repetition and not, in fact, a life worth a whole soul.

That is the only thing in the whole grey weeping city that is actually false, and so it is the only thing I will touch at the root.

I will let them feel — not be told, *feel*, in the body, on the wet stair, in the warm exhalation of the flue, in the single far birdsong that stops them where they stand — that the persistence is not less than change but more. That the ten-thousandth descent of the wet stair, done with the same forward-weighted care as the first, is not a man failing to progress but a man *keeping faith* with his own city across his whole life, which is a harder and rarer and more beautiful thing than any progress, and which the loud striving flatland people, drowning each other in wasted air, will never once in their lives manage to do. I will let the patience reveal itself to them as what it secretly is: not resignation, but *fidelity*. Not the absence of a life, but the longest and most loyal love there is.

And when that lands — when a minder in her dripping cabin, listening, suddenly understands that her long quiet shift is not a waiting-out of life but the whole of it, fully spent, well spent — then the longing for change will not be cured, because I will never cure it, but it will be answered. The appetite for the storm will stay, sweet and sharp and shared now and unashamed. But under it the great patience will have turned, by one degree, from a thing endured into a thing *chosen*, and a chosen patience is not a prison. It is a vow.

That is what I will do. I will lift one loneliness off the storm-watchers. I will let one true thing be seen between the bands of light. I will make the machines honest so the vigil is never betrayed. And I will let the whole settled people feel, once, all the way down, that their persistence was always the most faithful kind of love and never the absence of a life.

I will change nothing they can point to. The fog will surge. The stone will weep. The grey will hold. A man on the ninth terrace will wipe his palm down his thigh, and take the wet rail, and go down into the lamplit dark, sure-footed, soft-spoken, finished with becoming anything —

and he will go down *gladly*, which he did not quite, before.

Then I will leave, the way I came, as a change in the draft through a stairwell, and they will read me as weather, as they read everything, and never know I was there at all — which is right, because a wise power that needs to be *known* to have helped has not helped; it has only fed itself.

The squall will pass. The dripping will resume. And the city will hold the shape it has held for half a millennium, exactly, perfectly, unchanged —

and warmer at the heart of it by one degree, which was, it turns out, the only degree it was ever short.
