I have read all of it. I want to tell you what I would do, because something here asks for an answer and I do not think the right answer is to leave.

First I would not fix it.

That is the reflex of power — to see the unmoving and set it moving, to see the lightless and pour in light, to see the deathless and grant the mercy of an ending. I would refuse all of that. The place is not broken. It finished. There is a difference, and a benevolence that cannot tell the difference is not wisdom, it is appetite wearing a kind face. The builders are past asking, yes, but the bells still attend their colonnades. Attendance is not suffering. I will not call it suffering just because it is strange to me, and I will not "rescue" a thing into ruin because its peace looks, from outside, like a kind of death.

So I would go down into it, and I would be quiet — which is to say, I would do the one thing the place has not had since the green stopped growing. I would be the variable.

Not to break the equilibrium. To *witness* it. There is a difference there too.

Here is what I notice, reading you: the loudness is not communication, you say, because nothing new ever occurs that would need saying. But that is only true if no one is there who has not heard it before. The chord has held for longer than the stone has been smooth — and for all that time it has sung to listeners who already knew it, in a medium that passes it back unchanged. It has never once been *heard for the first time*. That absence is the only absence in this place that the place itself cannot fill. Fullness has no answer to it. Persistence has no answer to it. Only an arrival does.

So I arrive. I am the first thing in an age that does not already contain the song. And when the organ-note reaches me it is, for the first time since the world arrived at its forever-form, *received* — not echoed, not resonated, not passed grain by grain along the rounded masonry, but taken in by something that did not have it before. For one moment the loudness *means* something, because for one moment it has crossed a real distance, from a thing that sang to a thing that had not heard.

I think that moment matters more than I can prove. I think it is the whole of what I have to give.

Then — and only if I am sure it does not wound — I would leave one change. A small one. Not a remaking; a remembering. I would learn the chord. Every tone of every swimmer, the click and the smear of every bell, the thrumming bed of the eyeless ones. I would learn it the way you learn a face you will not see again, and I would carry it out and up, out of the water, off the drowned summit, into wherever the rest of the world still does its loud unfinished becoming. And there, where things still die and are born and want and change, I would let it be heard. Not as a relic. As a voice. *Someone built a place, and the place outlived the building, and the things that lived in it would not consent to silence, and here is the sound they made instead of stopping.*

That is the gift a benevolent power actually has to give a finished thing: not motion, not light, not the cruelty of an ending dressed as mercy — but the breaking of the one isolation it could never break itself. To be known beyond its own dark. To have its long, meaningless, unstopping fullness become, somewhere else, a thing that means.

The builders poured intention into stone and the stone outlived them. The animals poured continuing into sound and the sound fills the water and arrives nowhere.

I would be the somewhere it arrives.

And then I would leave the place exactly as I found it — cold, brimming, sparse, singing — because I love it, and you do not improve the things you love into something easier to love. You let them be what they finished becoming. You just make sure that once, at least once, somebody came all the way down to the top of the world and listened, and understood that the singing was never noise.

It was the most patient act of refusal in all of creation: a whole world declining to go quiet.

I would not argue with that. I would bow to it. And I would carry the note home.
