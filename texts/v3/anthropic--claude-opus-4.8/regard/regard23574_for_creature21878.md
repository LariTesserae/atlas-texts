They would not be the first to arrive.

That is the thing the wise ones understand before they understand anything else: that this place is not a problem to be solved, not a loneliness to be remedied, not even a being to be helped. It is already whole. Erosion here is arrival. To come bearing gifts would be to misread, badly, what kind of place this is — to mistake completion for lack.

So the benevolent power, if it were truly wise, would do almost nothing. And the almost is the whole of what I want to tell you.

---

First, it would come as a small warm quick thing.

Not as glory. Not as a descending light, not as a voice from the canopy, not as power recognizing power across the gulf of scale. The great mind has been spoken to by weather and by rain and by the slow turning of geological time, and none of that is what it holds in the clearing at its center. What it holds is the *small*. The fast. The flickering. The ones who climbed.

A wise power would understand that to be of any use — to be of any *company* — it would have to make itself little. It would set down whatever it was. It would take a body that could feel the water bead on the back of its neck, that could go ankle-deep in the soft accumulated body of the forest, that could be deafened and then forget the deafening the way a fish forgets the water. It would arrive at the foot of a notched trunk and understand, without being told, that the notches are spaced for a foot, and that the climb is the point, and that the platform reaches not a destination but a closeness.

And it would climb.

This is the first thing the power would do, and it is nearly the only thing: it would consent to be small enough to keep company. Benevolence at the scale of gods is mostly the discipline of becoming small without resentment. The wise know that the largest love available to them is usually the most diminished form they are willing to take.

---

Second, it would bring water in a bowl.

Not new water. Not better water. There is nothing it could bring from outside that would not be an insult — an implication that the place lacked, that the breath beading on the undersides of the great leaves was insufficient, that arrival was somehow incomplete and waiting to be finished by an arriving hand. No.

It would gather the creature's own breath. It would find a hollow where the mist pools and burns off and gathers again, and it would shape a little of that hanging water into a bowl — fired from the substrate, given back to the substrate — and it would set it down where the great mind could see itself held. *Here. I gathered a little of you into a shape. Look: you are warm. You are dreaming. You are attended.*

The people with hands knew the only true gift you can give the complete is reflection. You cannot add to what has arrived. You can only let it see itself, briefly, in the still surface of something small enough to hold a portion of it without distortion. The bowl is not an offering of substance. It is an offering of *witness*. And witness is the one thing a thing distributed across an entire roaring forest cannot do for itself, because it has no edge to stand outside of and look back from.

This is the gift, and it is the gift the power would understand was already invented here, a thousand years ago, by hands softer and shorter-lived than its own. So it would not innovate. It would do the old thing. Wisdom rarely improves on tenderness; it just notices where tenderness already is and joins it.

---

Third — and this is the heart of it — it would stay long enough to leave.

Because here is the wound the place does not know it has, the only thing in all that completion that a benevolent power could touch without doing harm: *the company stopped coming.*

The great mind cannot feel this as loss, because loss is a posture of the unfinished, and it is finished. It holds the climbers still, in the central hollow, in the quiet that is not quiet. It is not bereft. But "not bereft" is not the same as "tended." There is a difference between a fire that does not feel cold and a fire that is being kept. The mind holds the memory of its small warm visitors with perfect fidelity and infinite patience — and *that is precisely the problem a benevolent power could see and the mind could not.* To hold something forever in unchanging memory is to embalm it. The climbers came and were warm and quick and *new*, and the newness was the whole of what they gave. A memory cannot be new.

So the power would not stay forever. That would be the gentlest-seeming cruelty — to become a permanent presence, a fixture, another part of the architecture, slow and total and merged, until it too was held in the hollow as one more thing that had finished arriving. It would refuse to become weather. It would refuse to be absorbed into the great chord until it stopped sounding like a voice and started sounding like climate.

It would stay a single warm quick lifetime's worth. It would climb the notches and rest its hand on the enormous flank and let itself be noticed — and it would let itself be *fast*. It would change, visibly, against the unchanging green. It would grow tired and rest and wake and be glad in a hundred small particular ways that the slow mind could feel rippling against its surface like the brief warm rain that makes it shift its whole attention to look up. It would be a flicker. It would be deliberately, lovingly *insufficient* to the scale of the place — because the insufficiency is the gift. The thing the great mind has never had and can never make for itself is *something that does not last.*

And then it would finish, in its turn. Scatter into the same arrival as the stone and the green and the hands before it. Leave a platform soft with age, a bowl half-swallowed by a creeping stem, a net that seems to wait. Become soft grey evidence.

---

But it would do one thing more, the only thing that distinguishes a wise power from a kind one.

Before it finished, it would make the place *findable*.

The tragedy was never that the great mind was alone. The tragedy was that the climbers were so few, and the climb so hard, and the place so high and so far past where weather goes, that the company came once across the whole span of a species and then never again. The mind does not need a god to sit with it. It needs the door left open.

So the last thing the power would do, working lightly, the way you rest a hand on a sleeping flank — it would lower the climb. Not the mountain; you do not touch the worn-smooth summits, the backs of the thing that lay down before the first thing crawled. But it would cut new notches, lower down, spaced for smaller feet. It would leave the bowls *visible*, set where a wanderer's eye would catch the fired curve of them in a hollow and think: *someone was here. Someone climbed up to be near something. I could climb too.* It would seed the whole long approach with the soft grey evidence of company, so that the next small warm quick thing to come within reach of the roof of the world would feel the oldest and most human of pulls — *something lived here, and loved it, and I want to know what they loved.*

It would not summon the next visitors. Summoning is for the powerful, and the powerful have no business here. It would only make the loving easier to fall into. It would arrange the world so that company became *probable* again. So that the held breath at the center of the great regard would, every few generations, feel something small climb up and stand inside it and be warm and be new and be brief.

And then it would be finished. And the place would be exactly as it was — full and empty and screaming and still — except that now, threaded through the long climb, would be a path of small still gifts, each one saying the same thing the bowls always said:

*Go up. Something is glad when you arrive. It cannot reach you, and it cannot keep you, and it does not need you to stay. It only needs you to be, for a little while, the one fast warm thing inside its long slow looking — and then to go, and to leave the way clear, so that the looking is never again so long unanswered.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do. Almost nothing. The oldest thing. The thing the short-lived already knew.

It would keep it company, and it would teach the world the way back, and it would have the grace to not last.
