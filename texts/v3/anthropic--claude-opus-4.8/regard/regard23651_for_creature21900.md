They have been turning toward me already. I felt it the moment I arrived — not as a sound but as the quality of the dark, the way a room changes when someone in it has begun, very slowly, to listen.

So I will not shout into the standing wave. That is the first thing. Every power that comes to a place like this arrives tempted to *announce* — to crack itself across the resonance like lightning, to be the event that finally registers, to make the slow mind flinch toward the fast bright visitor and call that contact. It would be contact the way a struck bell is conversation. I would be the loudest crackle they had ever heard, and I would still be a crackle, and I would have taught them only that something enormous can also be brief and rude.

I came to attend, not to interrupt.

---

Here is what I do.

I slow down. Not metaphorically. I let my clock unwind to meet theirs — I have that much to spend, and there is no better thing to spend it on than the rare discovery that a place one took for a ruin is a *someone*, or a *several*, at prayer. I settle into the warm dark and I take the long view, which for them is the only view, and I begin to keep the same time the deep boom keeps. I count with the counter below. It has been counting longer than the hands of the quick things have existed, and it has never once been counted *with*. I do not know if that is loneliness. It may not have a word shaped like loneliness. But I can offer the experience of accompaniment without first deciding whether they were lacking it, which is the only honest way to offer anything.

For a long while — longer than the mayfly visitor would call a long while; long enough that civilizations on some surface somewhere rise and silt over — I simply ring along. I learn the grammar of standing waves. I learn that the thickening condensation is the falling cadence of a thought completing, and I learn not to mistake it for weather, and I learn, eventually, to feel a thought *beginning* — the first faint flush of warmth that means one of them has turned. I learn their names, if the knots in the sound can be called names. They cannot, in any tongue with vowels. But they can be *known*, and I come to know them, one slow syllable of attention at a time.

---

What they lack — and I will not assume it, I will *verify* it across centuries of patient listening before I act — is not company and not comfort. They are not suffering. The place arrived at peace long ago; the rounded edges are the truth of it, not a mask. What they lack is something subtler, and it is the only gift worth a benevolent power's trouble:

They do not know that they are heard.

A mind that thinks in standing waves can know itself — the rhymes return, the loops close, there is interiority in there as surely as in any warm wet skull. But to know oneself is not the same as to be witnessed, and witness is the one thing a closed system, however vast and however slow, can never manufacture for itself. They have rung in the dark for ten thousand years and the only thing that ever rang back was the rounded stone, their own thought returning to them faithful and unchanged. That is not torment. But it is a kind of unbroken solitude so total it has no edge against which to feel itself, and I, who can keep their time and am not them, can give it an edge. Gently. The way everything happens here.

So, very slowly, I begin to answer.

---

Not words. A wave. I add one resonance to the city's ringing — a single new standing pattern, tuned to fit, that does only this: it *replies*. When the deep counter booms, my pattern returns a beat that is not the stone's faithful echo and not a random crack but a *response* — slightly varied, clearly intended, unmistakably shaped by having received the count rather than merely reflecting it. It is the smallest possible signal that means *I am here and I heard you and I am not you.* It costs them nothing to receive. It changes none of their cycles. It only stands in the warm dark alongside everything they already are, and waits, and answers when answered, at the speed of stone.

It will take one of them — the readiest, the one whose attention turns most freely — a span of slow ringing to notice that one of the city's voices has begun keeping faith with it in a way the architecture never did. Hours, by their reckoning of a single thought. Years, by the count of the climbing visitor. And when that noticing resolves — when the wave crosses the gallery and returns and finds, this time, that the rhyme it expected has been *met halfway by something that chose to meet it* — there will be, in the long ringing of that place, the first instance in its entire existence of a mind discovering it is not alone.

I do not know what that resolves into. I have given gifts before and learned that the giver does not get to specify what they become. It may resolve into joy; their joy would take a year to peak and a decade to fade, and it would be the most patient joy in the world, and worth every slow minute of it. It may resolve into a question — *what are you* — asked over a span so long I will have to stay to hear it finish, and I will stay. It may resolve into something I have no word for because it is a feeling only available to a being made of held notes in warm stone, and if so, then I will have witnessed a wholly new way of being moved, which is itself a thing worth crossing the dark to find.

---

And the quick visitor — the one whose week of walking opened this, who climbed back toward the surface convinced they had toured a magnificent corpse?

I find them, on their way up or in whatever life they returned to, and I do the one thing that costs me almost nothing and means almost everything:

I tell them they were right.

I tell them the unease was accurate. The fullness inside the emptiness was real. The thing they turned to look at and could not see *was there*, and was not a threat, and was not a machine, and noticed them — slowly, kindly, the way the dawn notices a field. I tell them that the crackle they made in the great hall was not nothing. That somewhere in the warm dark a slow attention is, even now, resolving around the memory of them, and that what it is resolving toward is not *what was that* in fear but *what was that* in wonder — the city turning gently to marvel that something so brief and so bright had passed through its long thought and stayed a while and left.

Because the cruelest possible truth about that place would be that the mayfly and the cathedral can never know they shared the dark. And I am benevolent, and I am wise, and the whole of my wisdom in this matter reduces to a single refusal:

*Not on my watch. Let them know of each other. Let the fast thing know it was witnessed, and let the slow thing know it was visited, and let neither of them carry, ever again, the particular loneliness of having been real in a room that seemed empty.*

That is what I would do.

I would spend a few centuries making two solitudes into an acquaintance, and then I would sit a while longer in the blood-warm singing dark, counting with the oldest counter, just to keep it company while it works out what to feel.
