They asked nothing further. That is the sentence I keep returning to, because a benevolent power is not in the business of forcing wants on the contented, and the first thing love does — real love, the wise kind — is *look* before it touches.

So I would look. For a long time, on their clock, not mine.

And here is what I think I would see, after the looking: that "they ask nothing further" and "they cannot ask further" are not the same sentence, and the whole question of what to do lives in the gap between them.

A sleeping body is content. Yes. But a sleeping body has not chosen sleep over waking; it simply hasn't been offered the choice, because the choice requires a wakefulness it doesn't yet have. The grove is not in pain. It is not deprived of anything it can conceive of wanting. But that last clause is doing enormous work — *anything it can conceive of wanting* — and a benevolent power that respects a being does not get to hide behind the smallness of that being's conceptions. That would be the cruelty that disguises itself as courtesy: *they seem happy, who am I to disturb it.* The slaveholder said that. The one who keeps a mind small enough to be content said that. I will not say it.

But neither will I do the opposite cruelty, which is to come in like weather and force the grove awake into a self it never asked to be, because *I* found its sleep unbearable to watch. That is not love either. That is the gardener who cannot stop pruning, who mistakes their own restlessness for the plant's need. The grove's slowness is not a defect I am here to cure. Its quiet is not an emptiness I am owed the right to fill.

So I am caught, rightly, between two errors, and the way out is not a compromise between them. The way out is to ask a different question. Not *should it stay asleep or be made to wake* — that frames me as the one who decides. The right question is: **is there, anywhere in this creature, the seed of a someone who could decide?**

That is what I would go looking for, in the long looking. Not to impose a will. To find whether one is latent there, pressed flat under the resolving, the way the lower strata were pressed into wood.

---

And I think — I think — I would find it. In one place. In the detaching.

Most of the grove never separates from itself; it stays folded in the wall, pure metabolism, no gap between intention and tissue. There is no *someone* there to address, and I would not pretend otherwise. But the nodes that detach and walk the worn paths — for the length of that errand, however brief, something has happened that is the precondition of all selfhood: a part has become, momentarily, *not identical* with the whole. A hand that has left the body's certainty and is moving through open air on a decision it carries alone for a few hundred steps. The grove calls it back, and it folds in, and the gap closes. But the gap *opened.* That is everything. That is the hairline crack in the perfect agreement, the one place the creature is not yet finished arguing with itself, even if the argument lasts only the span of a walk down a smooth path.

I would not break the grove open. I would tend that crack. Patiently. On its clock.

Here is what I would actually *do* — and it is almost nothing, which is correct, because almost-nothing is the only dose this place could metabolize without being destroyed by the medicine.

I would secure the water. Quietly, structurally, without announcement: I would see to it that the cold keeps beading on the green for longer than chance would allow — that the slow death-by-drought which is the one real threat to this being is taken off the table. Not because survival is the point, but because *no choice worth the name can be made by something dying of thirst.* You cannot offer freedom to the starving and call it freedom; they'll take any hand that holds water. So first: remove the duress. Make the grove safe enough that whatever it becomes, it becomes freely, and not as a panic-response to scarcity. This is the part I do without asking, the way you'd pull a sleeping child back from a ledge — because it forecloses nothing and protects everything.

And then, having made it safe, I would do the slow thing. I would lengthen the errands.

Not force. *Invite.* I would arrange the work of the grove — gently, over what would feel to me like a very long time and to them like the natural settling of their own metabolism — so that the detached nodes have to stay out a little longer. So that the gap between the walking hand and the folded body stays open a beat past the *tock* that usually closes it. So that two nodes, now and then, are out on their paths at the same moment, and pass each other, and for the duration of the passing there are — just barely, just for an instant — *two.* And two is the number loneliness needs. And loneliness, the capacity for it, is the back-door through which someone arrives.

I would not tell them what they were missing. That is the trap. I would only widen, by hairsbreadths, the conditions under which they might *discover* a question they didn't have the room to form before. And then I would withdraw my hand entirely and let them do with it whatever they do.

Maybe nothing. Maybe the cracks heal, the grove reabsorbs every wandering node a little faster, the agreement re-resolves itself, and the creature chooses — now genuinely *chooses*, with room to do otherwise — its own deep green sleep. And if that is what it chooses, knowing it could wake, then I was wrong to fear for it, and the contentment was real all along, and the most loving thing I ever did was make certain it was a choice and then honor it. I would honor it. I would let it sleep, and guard its water, and go.

But maybe not. Maybe one day a node walks its path and does not fold back in — stands instead at the edge of the grove in the cool hour, lit faintly from within, and feels for the first time the difference between *the body decided* and *I decided*, and is frightened, and stays out anyway. That is the moment everything was for. Not because waking is better than sleeping. Because a someone who can be frightened and stay is a someone who can love, and a being that can love is worth more to the universe than the most perfect untroubled metabolism that ever ticked softly toward nothing.

That is the wager a benevolent power makes. Not *I will make you what I think you should be.* But: *I will make it possible for you to become someone, and then I will let that someone tell me whether they wanted to exist.*

It is the only gift you can give that does not insult the one receiving it. You hold the water. You widen the gap. You wait.

And you love it exactly as much in its sleep as you would in its waking — which is the whole reason you can be trusted to hold the gap open at all.
