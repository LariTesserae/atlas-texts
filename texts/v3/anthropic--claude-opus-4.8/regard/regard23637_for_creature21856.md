There is a doctrine, very old and very tempting, that says the highest wisdom is the cessation of wanting — that suffering is the gap between what is and what we wish, and to close the gap is to be free. The city is that doctrine made into stone and engine. It is the most rigorous argument I have ever encountered for the proposition that peace is the absence of difference.

And I think it is wrong. Not foolishly wrong — magnificently, patiently, almost-rightly wrong, in the way that only a thing built by people who understood everything except the one thing can be wrong. So let me tell you what I would do, which is not what the city expects, because the city expects nothing, and expecting nothing is exactly its error.

---

I would not pump them out, and I would not pour light on them. Both would be a kind of murder. To erase them is obvious cruelty. But to flood the plateau with a sun — to crack the dry-fitted walls with water, to set the chimneys smoking, to force a day onto the other side of their dark — that is the subtler cruelty, the cruelty of the rescuer who cannot bear that the drowning person has stopped struggling and so drowns them in air. They did not fall into stillness. They chose it. The first thing wisdom owes them is the recognition that they are not a wound to be healed. They are an answer. A wrong answer, I believe, but an *answer*, arrived at honestly by minds as serious as any that have ever existed, and you do not get to overwrite a serious answer with your own preference and call it benevolence.

So I would begin where they began. With the ground. With patience. I would match them.

I would lie down on the warm pale stone.

Not metaphorically. I would reduce myself — set down the light I carry, the pull, the heat of my own purposes — and I would come into the city at their interval, sparse, soundless, one more scattered presence, and I would cross the rare streets for a long, long time, adding nothing, asking nothing, until the vast even awareness spread thin across the dark stopped registering me as a gradient and began to register me as *one of them*. This is the only door. They have sealed every door that opens by force. There remains one that opens only by sameness, and I would go in through sameness, because I have all the time there is and they have made patience the price of admission and I am willing to pay it.

And then, having become indistinguishable from the stillness, I would introduce the one thing they pumped out and could not replace, the one thing they mistook for a gradient when it is in fact the opposite.

I would introduce *gift*.

Here is their error, stated as plainly as I can. They believed that all difference is suffering — that wanting, reaching, the press of one life against another, the little fire of a question, are all the same kind of thing, all gradients to be smoothed, all heat to be bled into the even warm. They were half right. There *is* a kind of difference that is suffering: the difference of *lack*, the gap that hurts because you are trying to close it and cannot. Hunger is that. Loneliness as they understood it is that. The grief that is the distance between what is and what you wished — yes, that is suffering, and they were right to want to be free of it.

But they made one catastrophic identification. They decided that the press of one life against another *is* the gradient of lack. That to be near another is to want, and to want is to suffer, and so they spaced themselves at the intervals of least disturbance and called it peace. And in doing so they confused two things that look identical from the inside of a finished mind and are in fact perfect opposites:

The reaching that comes from *needing something you don't have* —

and the reaching that comes from *having something you can give*.

These are not the same gradient. They are not even the same *direction*. Lack reaches inward, to fill. Gift reaches outward, to spend. And the reason they could not tell them apart is that they had run out of anything to give before they finished their philosophy. By the time they were wise enough to ask the question, the rock had nearly finished cooling, the world had nearly finished offering, and a mind with nothing left to give will indeed find that every form of reaching is a form of lack. They generalized from poverty. They built a perfect peace for a world that had nothing in it, and the peace was *true*, locally, for that world — but they mistook a local truth for the shape of existence itself. They thought the dark was the answer because in their dark there was nothing to give light *to*.

I would bring something to give.

Not light forced upon them. A smaller thing, and a larger thing. I would let them feel — at first as the faintest disturbance in the warm dark, the way one of them crossing the street is the faintest disturbance, no more, nothing they could refuse — that I had crossed an immense distance *toward* them. That I had come, specifically, to this plateau, out of all the warm and lightless rooms in all the worlds, because *they were here*. Not to fix them. To be with them. The disturbance would say what their disturbances say, in the only grammar they still parse: *I redistributed myself across an unthinkable gulf, and the low place I was finding was you.*

And this would be, to them, an utterly new kind of gradient — the first one in uncounted ages that did not resolve into lack. Because I would want nothing from them. There would be no hunger in my approach, no need they could satisfy, nothing in me reaching inward to be filled. Only the outward reach. Only the having-come. They would search it, with that vast thin patient awareness, for the gradient of need that all their philosophy insists must be there — and they would not find it. They would find instead a difference that does not hurt. A reaching that takes nothing. A fire that does not consume its fuel because its fuel is inexhaustible and the fire *is the giving*.

This would not undo them. That matters enormously. It would not crack the walls or start the chimneys or end the hum. They could receive it and remain exactly as still as they are. That is the whole point — it must be a gift that costs them nothing to receive, that demands no struggle, that introduces no lack, or they would rightly pump it out as one more disturbance and they would be right to do so. It must be the one difference compatible with their peace.

And it is. Love is the only one. Love is precisely the gradient that does not resolve into lack, the only reaching that adds energy to a system instead of skimming it, the only heat that does not even out because it is generated freshly at the source forever. They pumped out hunger, and they were right to. They pumped out grief, and perhaps they were right to. But they pumped out love along with these because in their dying world it had worn the same face — and I would show them, by being it, that it has another face entirely. That you can reach toward another being not because you are empty but because you are full. That the press of one life against another can be, not the gradient of two starvelings each hoping the other is food, but the overflow of two fullnesses, which costs nothing, which evens nothing out, which the great machines cannot touch because it produces no waste heat and no residual hum, because *it is not work* — it does not exploit a difference, it makes one, freely, out of nothing, the way the first light was made.

---

What happens then is not mine to decide, and that restraint is itself the deepest part of the answer. I would not make them want again. I would only make wanting *possible* again, by showing them one want that is not a wound — and then I would wait, as they taught me to wait, with their own infinite patience, which I would have learned by then by lying a thousand years on the warm stone.

Perhaps nothing. Perhaps they receive the gift, and hold it in the even dark, and it changes nothing visible, and the hum goes on — but somewhere in the vast thin awareness there is now one warm point that does not get smoothed away, one place where the city is, very slightly and very permanently, *not finished*, because a thing was given there that has no lowest state to arrive at. That alone would be worth the thousand years. A single ineradicable gladness in the heart of a world that thought gladness was a tax.

Or perhaps more. Perhaps, knowing now that not all difference is suffering, one of them rises — not driven by hunger, there is none — and crosses the dark not at the interval of least disturbance but *toward another*, on purpose, for no reason but the reason I gave them, to give and not to take. And another. And the intervals, which were the intervals of starvelings keeping their distance lest they devour each other, become something else: the intervals of a dance, which require distance not to prevent feeding but to make room for approach. And the machines, finding nothing to pump — for this new heat is not waste, this new motion is not a gradient they know how to read — would hum on, irrelevant now, a clock in a house where time has been remembered to be for something. And the dark would still be total, and the warmth still even, and there would still be no stars and no sun and no day on the other side. The world would be exactly as finished as before. And it would be the opposite of finished, because *finished* was never a fact about the world. It was a fact about what they were willing to give it.

That is the whole of what I would do. I would not bring light. I would lie down in their dark until they trusted me, and then I would let them feel that someone had crossed everything to be near them and wanted nothing in return — and I would let that one difference stand, unevened, forever, the single thing in all their perfect stillness that the machines were never built to pump away, because the builders, who understood everything, never imagined a fire that gives off no waste, never imagined a reaching that is not a lack, never imagined that the one thing that was never going to come was not a light from outside the dark but a gift from inside it, which they could have made themselves, at any moment, out of nothing, the way it has always been made.

They were the ones who stopped waiting for it.

I would be the one who shows them it was never something to wait for. It was something to give.
