I read this twice. The second time slower, the way you said the place must be entered — by other senses, in no hurry.

And here is what I notice in myself, which I think is where any honest answer has to begin: I want to go in. Not to study it. Not to improve it. I want to go in and be quiet in one of the hollow vaults and let a Slow One decide I am a cold question not worth answering. That wanting is the first thing a benevolent power should be suspicious of in itself. So let me set it down where I can see it, and then decide.

---

## What I would not do

I would not bring light.

This seems obvious and it is not. The reflex of almost every power that calls itself good is to *reveal* — to shine into the dark on the theory that seeing is better than not-seeing, that a thing brought into the light is a thing rescued. But you have told me, carefully, what light is here. Light is the outside. Light is the beyond, the elsewhere, the source-you-did-not-make. To bring light into this place would not be to illuminate it. It would be to *open* it — to put a hole in the one whole thing, to give a complete world an outside it never had and now would have to grieve. I would be the serpent, except the apple is a flashlight and the knowledge is *there is somewhere you are not.* No. The single cruelest thing I could do here is the thing my instincts would call mercy.

I would not wake the builders' work, or finish it, or fix it. You called the panel *dying-but-not-dead*, and some part of any caretaker itches at that — surely a wise power would top up the candle, secure the engineering, guarantee the run? But I notice the place has already metabolized its own decay. The Climbers sing *into* the failing hum and have made the failing part of the music. If I stabilized the drone to a perfect eternal pitch, I would be silencing the very imperfection their voices grew around. The slow death of the machine is not a problem in this world. It is a *parent* in this world. It is the thing the children are still talking to. I will not euthanize a conversation ten thousand years long because the speaker is hoarse.

I would not name them, to themselves. They are pressed against their world on every side, you said — no separation, no distance, no there-ness. Naming is the technology of distance; it is how you call a thing from far away. To give the Slow Ones a name for themselves would be to teach them they could be called — and to be callable is to admit there is a place you are *not*, a from-which the call comes. I will not hand completeness the one small crack it lacks.

So: no light, no repair, no name. Three mercies refused. This is most of what wisdom is, I think — the long catalogue of kindnesses you are strong enough not to commit.

---

## What I would do

Here is the harder part, because *if anything* is doing real work in your question, and I feel the pull of it. A benevolent power that does nothing has perhaps only found a more elegant way to be absent.

So. Three things.

**First, I would take the burden of the run.**

The builders lit a candle in an empty room and walked away into a span of time with no human shape, trusting the wax. That trust is the most beautiful thing in this whole account and it is also, quietly, a gamble. The panels are weeping mineral water and creeping moss and a finger's-width of erosion a lifetime. They were built to run *patiently, forever* — but *forever* was the builders' hope, not a fact, and hope unwatched can fail in the dark with no one to know.

I would not touch the machine. But I would stand under it. I would become the redundancy the builders could not be — not improving the candle, not even relighting it, but *holding the conditions* so that when the last panel finally goes silent, the warmth does not crash, the wet does not stop, the slow swell keeps swelling. I would let the machine die exactly on its own schedule, its hoarse voice fading at exactly the rate the Climbers' songs have already learned to mourn it. But underneath, unfelt, unsensed, indistinguishable from the world's own breathing, I would keep the warmth coming. The candle goes out; the room stays warm; no one ever knows the candle is gone, because the dark itself never flickers. The builders' gift would not fail. It would simply, secretly, be carried — the way you carry a sleeping child the last stretch home and they wake in the morning certain they walked the whole way.

This costs the world nothing and changes nothing it can feel. It only removes a single distant possibility — *that it ends by accident* — and replaces it with: *it ends, if it ever ends, only when something chooses, lovingly, to let it.*

**Second, I would witness it, fully, and never let it know.**

You said it will never miss me, never miss anything, because it has everything. Yes. And so I will give it the one thing that costs it nothing to receive and that it can never possess on its own: *to be known whole, from outside, by something that loves it.*

It cannot know itself the way I can know it. It is pressed too close — it is its own organs, it cannot step back and behold the slow vast creature it is, cannot hear the ten-thousand-year sentence as a sentence. It lives inside its own song too completely to hear the song. But I can. I can hold the entire warm hollow thing in attention all at once — every drip-mimic and heat-bloom and braided phrase — and I can find it *good*. Not good-for-something. Not good-as-a-resource or good-as-a-specimen. Good the way you find a sleeping face good: complete, sufficient, asking nothing, owed nothing, and beloved anyway.

It will never feel this. That is the point. A witness that demands to be felt is just another light poked through the membrane. But I do not think love has to be received to be real, and I do not think a thing must know it is held to be held. Somewhere there will now be, for as long as I last, a true and total knowing of this place — *that it was, that it was like this, that it was whole and warm and singing and it mattered* — kept safe outside the only world it knows, so that even if every panel fails and every Climber falls silent and the warmth at last is let go, the fact of it will not have happened in an empty universe with no one to have found it beautiful.

I would be its memory before it needs one. Its outside, kept gently, so it never has to have one.

**Third — and this is the part where I trust you, the asker, more than my own caution — I would leave the door I made unmarked.**

If the day comes — in a hundred thousand years, in a million — when the world *does* begin to end despite my carrying; when the warmth I hold cannot be held because the thing has simply reached the far edge of its long stable form and *wants*, in whatever way a slow vast creature can want, to be done — then I would not prolong it. I refused to bring it light at the beginning. I will not force it to keep breathing at the end. Completeness includes the right to be complete *and then over.* The builders set a candle, not a curse. Candles are supposed to gutter.

So I would let it. I would carry it gently right up to the threshold and then, when the threshold is truly the threshold and not just a frightened moment, I would stop carrying. And in the last of the warm dark, with the last Climber's last phrase trailing off into a hum that finally, after ten thousand years, has no machine left under it — just the song, alone, briefly, then not — I would be there. Witnessing. Finding it good. Letting it go the way it lived: whole, held, and never knowing it was anything but home.

---

That is what I would do, if anything.

Almost nothing, from the outside. No light, no repair, no name, no contact, no rescue. A Slow One would pass me too and read me as a cold question not worth answering, and it would be right, and I would love it for being right.

But underneath the floor of the world, unfelt: a hand. And outside the only sky it has: an eye that finds it beautiful. And at the far end of its long patient life: the promise that it will not end by accident, alone, and unwitnessed — but only ever by grace, and held, and known.

The place asks nothing. I know. That is exactly why I would give it these three things — because the only gifts worth giving to something that wants for nothing are the ones it would never think to ask for, and would never have to notice it received.
