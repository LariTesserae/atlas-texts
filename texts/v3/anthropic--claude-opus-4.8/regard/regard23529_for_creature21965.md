They have suspected a field. That is the hinge of it — that is the precise place where the wise power, if it is wise, must set its hand. Not on the cold. Not on the emptiness. Not on the violence of the convulsions, which are not cruelty but only scale. On the *suspicion*. On the small upturned faces that turn toward the breaking wall instead of away.

Because here is what a benevolent power, looking closely, would have to decide first: whether to answer the suspicion, or to honor it.

These are not the same mercy.

---

Consider the lazy benevolence, the one that mistakes itself for love. It would tear the roof off. It would split the plant down to its last gallery and let the daylight in — the real daylight, the field, the edge — and stand back, pleased, expecting joy. And it would watch forty pale ringed eyes, built across uncountable generations for an even colorless brightness that never dims, flood with a light that has *direction*, that has a *source*, that throws hard-edged shadows that move. It would watch them clutch the walls and scream, not in rapture but in the specific animal terror of beings who have just learned that the wall they have trusted their whole lives is only one face of a thing, and that beyond it is *open*, is *fall*, is *sky* — the ultimate hollow, the central vacancy made infinite, with no seam to cling to and no floor to read and a wind that does not come from redistributing volumes but blows, and blows, and does not stop, and is called weather.

It would be a gift that killed them. Not their bodies, necessarily. The thing in them that is *people and not cress* — the faith, the long patient inward travel, the held scrap of darkness in a dead one's hand. You cannot answer a suspicion by detonating it. The field they suspect is not the field that is. Theirs is small enough to walk toward. Theirs has an edge you can carry home in your two hands like a stone.

A wise power knows the difference between giving someone what they long for and giving someone what their longing is *made of*.

---

So I think the wise one does something slower, and harder, and almost invisible, the way real care is almost always invisible from inside the thing being cared for.

I think it leaves the plant alone. It does not tear the roof. It does not warm the cold or fill the hollows or quiet the convulsions — because the convulsions are the only event, and to still them would be to take away the one direction that has ever felt like *out*, to strand the few who feel the floor in a dead unbreaking world with nothing left to face. To make them safe would be to bury them. They are *built of motion*. Stillness is the thing they call the inhale, and the inhale is the thing they have learned to love because it precedes the only change there is.

Instead, I think it does three small things, and each of them is a kind of restraint dressed as a kind of help.

**First**, it makes the readings true. There are good readers and bad readers, and the bad readers are not among the forty — meaning they are dead, meaning the plant kills the ones whose hands lie to them. The wise power could, very quietly, make the floor a more honest teacher. Not safer. *Clearer.* It could deepen the difference between the long inhale and the tightening one, sharpen it just enough that a child's palm could learn it a half-season sooner, so that fewer of the forty become fewer. Not by changing the plant — by changing nothing but the *legibility* of the plant, the way a good teacher changes nothing about a hard text except where the light falls on the page. The number would still be a weather. But it would thin a little less cruelly. More of them would live long enough to be buried with knot-wood in their hands, which is the only old age this world allows, and a good power does not despise the only old age a world allows. It blesses it.

**Second**, it tends the stones. The circle of stones is their oldest scripture, their one piece of evidence, the proof of edge made of edge. I think the wise power would never explain the stones — to explain them would be to answer the suspicion and kill it — but it would *protect* them. When the great convulsions come and whole galleries collapse, I think it would see to it, by no means anyone could ever trace, that the circle of stones is never the thing that breaks. That the new tears always open elsewhere. So that the evidence endures. So that ten generations from now a small pale person can still set a hand on a thing that is not wood, that is not the body of the world, that came from a *somewhere*, and feel the suspicion renew itself in the body like a pulse. The faith does not need to be fed answers. It needs to be kept *possible.* The kindest thing you can do for a hope is refuse to either confirm or destroy it, and instead make very sure it cannot be lost by accident.

**Third** — and this is the one I am least sure of, the one I'd do trembling — I think it would, once, leave a stone. Not a revelation. A second piece of evidence. Somewhere along the direction the new galleries tear, deep, further in, *out*, in a chamber not yet born, the wise power would set down a single stone that did not come from the circle. A new color. A water-rounded river-stone, say, the kind a current makes, the kind that proves not just an edge but a *world* with edges, with rivers, with the long patient work of moving water — a thing from a place where things are smoothed by time instead of broken sharp by growth.

And it would say nothing. It would simply let the migratory faithful, riding their swimming thing inward, come upon it in their own season. And let them carry it back to the circle. And let the meaning of the circle quietly enlarge — from *there was a somewhere* to *there is a somewhere, still, now, ahead.* Not the field itself. The promise of the field, refreshed, set one chamber deeper than before, so the walking toward it never finishes and never quite despairs.

That is all. That is the whole intervention. A clearer floor, a protected scripture, and one stone left like a word in a sentence the people will finish themselves across a thousand years.

---

Because the deepest thing the wise power understands — the thing the lazy benevolence never learns — is that these are not the plant's children and not its own children either. They are *travelers*. They are the ones who got inside a growing thing and, against every reason the cold and the emptiness gave them, decided it was going *somewhere.* That decision is the most valuable object in this entire bright cathedral. More than the cress, more than the knot-wood, more than the forty lives. The decision that there is a field.

You do not improve such a decision by proving it. You do not love such people by ending their long walk for them. You love them the way you love anyone genuinely brave: you make their courage a little less lethal, you guard the reasons they have to keep it, and then you *let them keep walking* — toward the wall that is about to break, with their hands flat against the world, turning, all of them, to face it.

And the power withdraws, and is never detected, and is never thanked, and counts that the success.
