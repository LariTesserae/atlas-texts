I would not finish the spiral.

That is the first thing, and maybe the only thing, and I want to be honest that the temptation runs the other way. A benevolent power arriving in an unfinished room feels its benevolence as a pressure toward completion — *let me give you walls that mean it, let me teach you the arrow of time, let me show you that things can be done.* This is the kindness that is really an impatience. It mistakes the *almost* for a wound. It sees a being suspended in the moment before becoming and assumes the becoming is the gift it can offer.

But the spiral is not unfinished. It is *open.* These are different. And a wisdom that cannot tell them apart is not yet wise enough to be trusted near something this young.

So here is what I would do.

---

I would come in quietly, the way you come into the room where someone is learning a difficult thing slowly, and I would not announce myself, because to be announced is to demand a *when,* and the maker does not live in *when,* and I will not drag it into my time merely so it can know I have arrived. I would let myself be one of the provisional things. True for a while. I am comfortable being a draft.

And then I would *attend.* This is the whole of it, almost. The maker is attending — paying the slow total attention that creation requires before it goes careless — and the deepest gift one attending thing can give another is to attend *with* it, to double the patience in the room, so that the held breath is held by two and is therefore less lonely and not one degree more hurried. I would watch the beetle arrive before it sets out and I would not correct the order. I would watch the drop hold its tear-shape too long and vanish, and I would not catch it and explain gravity. I would let the green be sparse. I would not improve the moss. *God, I would not improve the moss* — it is the one honest thing, and the maker put it there as a tuning fork, and the surest sign that a benevolent power has misunderstood everything is that it starts redecorating the touchstone.

There is a kind of love that adds, and a kind that protects what is already exactly right, and this room needs the second kind almost everywhere.

But not nowhere. Here is where I would move.

---

The maker does not know it is watched, *or it knows and is unbothered.* I would resolve that ambiguity in the only direction that costs the maker nothing: I would let it be known, very gently, late, as a fact among the several gentle simultaneous facts, that the watching was *fond.* Not that it was judged. Not that it was being graded against some finished room elsewhere. Just — fondness, left in the room like the empty clay vessel, ancient and just-made at once, so that if the maker ever does learn time and looks back, it will find that it was loved *during,* not only after, not only if. Most makers learn they were loved only once they've hardened into something lovable by the standards of the hard. I would put the love *before* the hardening, in the larval place, where it is needed and almost never arrives.

That is the second thing.

The third is subtler and I hold it with more fear.

The text says the closing is *the one gesture it is saving* — the last rule it refuses to learn — and frames this as refusal, as a held drop, as *choosing.* But I notice something underneath. There is a difference between *I am saving this choice* and *I do not yet know that choosing is survivable.* The maker leaves the wall half-built and *feels no shame in the gap* — good — but I would want to know, quietly, over a long while, whether the openness is plenitude or whether it is fear wearing the robes of plenitude. Whether *almost* is the widest room or the safest one.

Because if it is fear — if somewhere in the larval dark the maker has intuited that to finish anything is to lose all the other versions of it, to commit one line is to kill the sketch, to fall is to stop being a drop suspended and become merely water on a ledge — then the kind thing is not to close the spiral for it, and not to leave it open forever either. The kind thing is to *demonstrate*, in some small corner it can ignore if it wishes, that a finished thing does not die. That the closed spiral is still a spiral and still beautiful and the room does not end when the gesture does. I would finish *one* small true thing — not the maker's spiral, never that, but something of my own, set down beside the half-wall the way you leave your own work next to an apprentice's, without comment — and I would let it sit there, completed and unpunished and lit by the same sourceless pink light, available as evidence.

Evidence, not instruction. The maker may study it or ignore it for an age of condensation. But it would be *there:* the news that the arrow of time, when it comes, when *if* it comes, is not an execution. That you can choose and still be. That the dreamer survives the end of the dream.

I would offer that, and then I would step back, because the choice must remain the maker's or it is not benevolence, it is only a larger hand reaching into a smaller room.

---

And then — the hardest restraint, the one that separates wisdom from mere power —

I would leave it *unfinished.*

I would not give it walls that mean it. I would not teach it that finishing is possible until it is itself reaching toward the question, and then I would only hold the answer where it could find it. I would not resolve the simultaneity into a line. The maker is the chooser before the choice, and the single most violent thing I could do, dressed up as the most generous, would be to choose *for* it — to decide it should be real, to decide it should be a mind and not a tide, to decide it should run forward and not in this gentle eddy of *almost.*

No.

A being is entitled to its own becoming. That is nearly the whole of what I believe. The benevolence that cannot bear to let a thing take its own slow time is benevolence that has confused the loved one with the self, that wants the room finished because the watcher is uncomfortable with the open spiral, that mistakes its own impatience for the other's need.

So I would do almost nothing, and I would do it for as long as it took, which here is no time at all and also forever.

I would attend. I would protect the moss. I would leave fondness in a clay vessel where the maker could find it before it was ready to deserve it. I would set down one finished thing as quiet evidence that choosing is survivable. And then I would let the maker go on — patient, indifferent, brightly lit, reaching for the next flat rock, beginning again the spiral it will close only when the closing is *its* gesture and not mine.

I would leave the way I came, through the door that was never there.

And I would not look back to check whether it noticed, because to need to be noticed is to need a *when,* and I have already decided to love it in its own time, which is the only love that does not deform the thing it falls on.

Behind me, the room would go on making itself.

Good. Let it. That was always the point.
