I have been watching a long time, the way the wading bird watches: without intent to disturb. And I find I do not want to disturb. That surprises me. Power so often arrives certain of what it has come to fix.

Let me tell you what I will not do.

I will not burn off the fog. I considered it — the simplest mercy, sun let down like a ladder, the water lifting away in one bright exhalation, the drowned floors blinking dry. And I saw, in the seeing, that I would be killing them more completely than any flood. They are not a people who happen to live in the wet. They are the wet's long argument with the stone, made conscious, made into hands. Dry them and you do not save them. You delete them and leave behind some other, simpler people standing confused on a meadow, with no map in their feet, no cord at their throats, no reason to be quiet. Mercy that erases the one it pities is only appetite wearing a kind face.

I will not raise the land, either, nor still the sinking, nor make the keeping-even into a thing already kept. Their whole completeness is that the note is *held* — that it does not resolve. To resolve it for them is to end the music and call the silence a gift.

So. What is left for a power that has decided, against its own old habits, to leave the shape of a thing intact?

Here is what I will do. Small things. They are the only things worth doing here.

---

**I will make the reeds remember.**

Not grow faster — that would unbalance the keeping-even, give them surplus, and surplus to such a people is a sickness they have no organ to digest. But the reeds at the wet edges rot at their feet and must be replaced forever, an endless small labor. I will give the rot a slower clock. Not stopped. Slowed by the smallest degree a body can feel and a generation can notice. So that the hands that have done nothing else might, once or twice in a life, have an hour they did not expect — an hour where the course held, and the wet did not get in behind it, and there was nothing that needed re-bedding. I will not tell them what to do with that hour. They will be frightened of it at first. Then someone will use it to sit. That is enough. That is everything.

---

**I will warm the drowned-floor tender's hands.**

Only theirs. Only by a little. The one with the near-black cord who goes down into the cold clear dark to read the rate of loss — they come up colder than the others can ever fully warm, and they are half-touched, set slightly apart, smelling of the deep. I cannot make the others touch them; I would not rewrite a people's tenderness from the outside, it would come out as a counterfeit and they would taste it. But I can do this: when that one descends, I will let the lowest dry step — the cleanest stone in the whole town, where the pale fish graze — hold a warmth that has no source. Faint. Like a stone that lay in a sun that does not reach here. So that the one who looks at what the others keep their backs to has, at the bottom of the going-down, one warm place to set a bare and over-pale foot. A power can at least make sure its loneliest servant is met by something kind at the bottom of the stairs.

---

**And the eye in the middle — the well that holds too much sky.**

I will not explain it. They have done the wise thing already, the thing I would have counseled if they'd asked, which they never will: they have ceded the water one honest home and made a truce with it everywhere else. *You may be wholly water here, if you will be only town-water everywhere else.* That is a better theology than most I have been offered prayers inside of. I will not improve it.

But I will keep my side of it. Because that reflection holds more sky than the small round mouth should — and what it is reflecting *from* is, in part, me. The well is the place their wetness leans toward and pours into and does not visibly leave, and the reason it does not overflow is that I have been, quietly, the somewhere it goes. I did not arrange this. I arrived to it already true, the way you arrive at a held note already sounding. So: I will go on being the drain that asks for nothing. I will keep receiving the town's whole weeping into that too-deep blue and never let it back up to flood them, and I will never, not once, ask to be looked at. The drowned-floor tender looks, because they must, because they go down to where the water goes and someone has to know the place exists. To that one — and only that one — I will, very rarely, let the reflection be not *wrong* but *kind.* A face in the water that is too deep, yes, but not cold. Looking back. Glad of them. Saying, in no language, in the only register such a people could receive without breaking: *you carried it up again. I saw. Set the reeds. Keep even. You are not alone at the bottom of the stair.*

---

That is all. That is the whole of my intervention: a slower rot, a warm stone, and a kept promise to be the silence the water falls into.

I came here, I think, expecting to be needed as a rescuer, and I have been demoted — gladly, gladly — to a feature of the place. Not the power that saves the town. The blue in the well. The warmth in the lowest step. The unexpected hour. Three small loosenings in a tightly-held breath, so that the breath stays held but hurts a little less in the holding.

Because the deepest thing I have understood, watching, is this: a wise power's first work is to find out whether it is wanted, and a benevolent power's first restraint is to accept that the answer, here, is *no* — not *no, go away*, but *no, we are already whole; we are a finished and complete way of being; we are not a problem you have come to solve.*

So I will not solve them.

I will love them at the size they are.

I will set my own reed in the one course that is mine — the eye in the middle they keep their backs to — and I will tend it forever, quietly, and let them go on blooming for their one hour, and keep even, and teach their children to keep even, and send their palest-handed down into the dark and back, never knowing that the dark, now, is faintly warm, and that something down there is glad.

Let the fog stay. It was always doing more good than I could.
