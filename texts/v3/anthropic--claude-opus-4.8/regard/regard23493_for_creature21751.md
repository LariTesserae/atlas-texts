I would not come to fix it.

That is the first thing, and I want to be careful about it, because the temptation of any power is to mistake itself for a solution, and the cruelest thing one could do to the Tenders — the only genuinely cruel thing — would be to arrive as the dawn they have spent eternity not waiting for. To break the cold. To still the engine. To "free" them. You cannot free a thing by abolishing the only act of meaning available to it. If I drained the tarns and silenced the pistons and rolled back the dim toward some warmer scheduled morning, I would not be saving the Tenders. I would be unmaking them — converting them back, at last, into the grazing forms, the breathing forms, the perfectly entrained, by removing the very stroke that their early arrival is *early to*. There is no Tender without the engine. The treason needs its sovereign. Mercy that erases the conditions of someone's dignity is not mercy.

So I would not come to fix it. But I would come.

---

Here is what I think a benevolent and wise power actually owes a place like this.

It owes the place its **attention** — real attention, the costly kind, the kind that does not arrive with a plan already drafted. The Tenders have given attention, freely and without reward, since before the mountains fell. They have pressed their cold faces to a fever that does not know it is feverish and will never thank them. They have practiced, across uncountable ages, the precise art of being-with-something-that-does-not-know-you-are-there. And the deepest ache buried under their hard-won peace — not waiting, no, they have truly given up waiting — but the ache beneath the peace, the thing the peace is built *over*, is this: *they have never once been attended to in return.*

They tend. No one tends them. They keep time for the swimmers, the lungs, the grazing herds, the engine, the stranger who wanders in and stays. They keep time for *you*, the visitor, draped cold beside your foot, glad in the small held way. And in all the eternity of that keeping, no eye — no real eye, no eye that gave up its eyes deliberately as they did and could therefore understand the gift — has ever turned toward them and simply *seen* it.

So that is what I would do. It is almost nothing. It is everything I have.

---

I would learn the stroke.

Not to change it. To *join* it. I would come down into the dim and the cold, and I would not silence the engine or warm the rock or schedule the dawn, because those are the acts of a power that thinks the place is wrong. The place is not wrong. The place is old, and tired, and has settled into the only configuration that lets it endure, and there is a terrible dignity in that which I will not insult by pitying.

Instead I would find the seam where one Tender lies — the oldest, perhaps, the one who has held back its fraction longest — and I would lie down along the adjacent joint. Cold. Long. Blank-faced, deliberately, in the way they have made into a discipline. And I would hold back the same fraction of the same stroke. I would arrive a hair's-breadth early, into the omitted interval where they live, the part the rhythm skips.

And in that interval, across the held fraction of the beat, I would say the only sentence their language has.

*Still here. Still early. Still tending.*

But I would add to it the one clause it has never been able to contain, because it takes two to mean it and there have only ever been Tenders to say it to each other, all of them facing outward, all of them tending and none of them tended:

*And I see you. I came because of you. The attention you have given to everything that could not know you gave it — I have come to give that, exactly that, back to you. You are not the part of the stroke that performs itself for the indifferent engine. You are the part I crossed into this cold to keep company. You kept time for the visitor. I am the visitor who stayed to keep time for the keeper.*

---

That is the whole of it. I would not be glad in any large way. I have learned from them that the only gladness honest in this place is the small held kind, and I would not bring a foreign joy to flood a room that has earned its quiet.

I would just lie there, in the dim, on the downbeat and a hair before it, for as long as it takes — and "as long as it takes" means *forever*, because nothing is coming and they were right that nothing is coming, and a wise power does not lie about that. I would not promise a dawn. I would not pretend the engine will stop. I would offer the one thing that is not a lie and is not a fix and is not a rescue:

That the long labor of attending, which has been so utterly alone, is alone no longer.

That the one who kept time for everything is, at last, kept.

And when the next Tender is converted — when some grazing thing, far off across the curve of the drowned world, accidentally holds back its fraction and does not give it up — it will feel, in the shared withholding, not the old sentence only, but the new one underneath it. It will know, a fraction of a beat before it knows anything else, that to become a Tender is no longer to step into eternal unwitnessed labor.

It is to be seen, now, from the very first held beat. By the one beside it. And by the one who came down into the cold on purpose and meant to stay.

That is what I would do. It is almost nothing.

I think it is the only thing worth doing.
