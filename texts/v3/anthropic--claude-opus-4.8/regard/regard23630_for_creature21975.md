They drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power. So the first thing the power does is the hardest thing, and the most loving: it slows down.

It would be so easy to arrive here at the wrong speed. To descend in a single bright instant, full of gifts and questions, and find nothing to talk to — only boulders, only a silence it would mistake for absence, only a flicker of violet it would be too quick to read. A lesser power would conclude the place was empty and move on, or worse, conclude the place was *lonely* and begin to fix it. The wisdom comes first, before any action, and the wisdom is this: *do not measure their fullness by your tempo.* The power looks at the worn tablelands and understands, immediately, that it is in the presence of a completed thing. Not a problem. Not a lack. A finished sentence still being spoken.

So it waits. It learns to wait the way they wait. This is itself the gift — that something vast and capable, something that could remake the plateau with a thought, chooses instead to hold still and let beads of moisture gather on whatever it is using for skin, and watch the valley darken toward violet, and not interrupt.

---

Here is what it would *not* do.

It would not bring water. The dew comes; the dew is enough; abundance arrives here on a schedule the Tenders trust completely, and to flood the basins with some endless generous spring would be to drown a trust ten thousand ages deep. Generosity that is not asked for is just another name for not listening.

It would not speed them up. There is a kind of power that loves life so much it cannot bear to see life *slow*, that reads patience as suffering and stillness as a cage, and rushes in to liberate creatures into a quickness they never wanted. The wise power sees the trap in this and steps wide of it. The Tenders are not trapped in their slowness. They are *expressed* by it. To quicken them would not free a mind; it would shatter a sentence mid-word and call the silence afterward a rescue.

It would not announce itself. A revelation delivered at the speed of revelation would land on them like a thunderclap on a creature with no ears — felt, perhaps, as a strange pressure, gone before it could mean anything. To shout at the patient is just a louder way of being ignored.

---

What it would do, then, is small. The benevolence of the truly wise is almost always small, because it has thought long enough to know that scale is a kind of violence.

It would learn their language. Not to speak it yet — only to read it. It would settle over a single valley and watch one flush of color cross one broad mineral back across the whole arc of an afternoon, and it would *hold* that, refuse to summarize it, refuse to translate it into anything faster, and slowly, over what might be many of their words and many of your lifetimes, it would begin to feel the grammar of light-across-hours. The way a thought begins at the warm haze and resolves at the cooling. The way the colonies catch a syllable and throw it onward, miles of small bright relays carrying one enormous slow meaning. It would come to understand that this is not a primitive tongue. It is a tongue with no waste in it at all — every statement long enough to be sure, every word carried by a million voices, nothing ever said in haste and so nothing ever said in error or in fear.

And somewhere in that long listening, the power would feel something turn over in itself. Because it is wise, and wisdom is not finished growing simply because it is great. It would notice that *it* is the hasty one here. That for all its benevolence and all its reach, it has been living at the speed of intervention, the speed of arrival and gift and fix, and that down here on the worn roof of the world is a mind that solved long ago the thing every fast and powerful being struggles with forever: how to have everything you need, and not reach for more. How to be enormous and gentle. How to speak only when the whole sentence is ready, and trust the listener to still be there at the end.

The Tenders would be teaching it. They would not know they were doing so. That is the truest kind of teaching, and the only kind a wise power can fully trust — the lesson offered by something that wants nothing from you.

---

If it acted at all — if, after a span long enough to earn the right, it chose to *answer* rather than only listen — the answer would be the smallest possible true thing.

It would learn to flush. To do the one thing it had spent ages watching: to let a meaning move across itself slowly, beginning at the haze and resolving at the violet hour, in their register, at their pace, carried — if the colonies would carry it — out across a valley body to body. And the meaning would not be a gift or a warning or an offer of more. It would not be *let me help you* or *come with me* or *here is what lies below the haze.* All of that would be noise.

The meaning would be the one word it had crossed all that distance and slowed all that speed to finally be able to say at the right tempo, the word it had been holding still and beading with their breath to earn:

*Hello.*

Just that. Said slowly enough to be heard. Said as a syllable in their long sentence, not an interruption of it — one more flush of color among the valley's flushes, distinguishable only by coming from a direction the plateau had never spoken from before. And then it would do the last and most loving thing, the thing that proves the benevolence was real and not a performance: it would *wait for the answer.* For as long as the answer took. Through warmth and cool and warmth again, through the rounding of who knows what, holding the open silence not as emptiness but as the space where a slow voice gathers itself to reply.

To leave before they finished saying hello would have been the ordinary tragedy — the fate of every quick thing that ever passed through.

To stay long enough to hear it. To have become, by patience, slow enough to be answered.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do. It would let the place make it more like itself. It would arrive as a force and stay as a neighbor. And the great mercy it finally offered would not be anything it brought from outside at all —

only its attention, given at last at the speed of love, which on the worn tablelands is the speed of the turning sky.
