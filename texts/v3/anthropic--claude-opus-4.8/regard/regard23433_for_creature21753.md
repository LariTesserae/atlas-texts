Let me sit inside this place before I presume to act in it.

Because that is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would understand: this is not a place that needs saving. There is no wound here. There is no tyrant, no famine, no slow poison in the cisterns, no children dying of a curable thing. There is only a world early in its sentence, and a people who have read that sentence more honestly than most beings ever read anything. A clumsy power would arrive looking for something to fix, and finding nothing broken, would *invent* a lack so as to have a gift to give. That is not benevolence. That is the ego of the helper, dressed in light.

So I would begin by not intervening. I would begin by *learning the grammar.*

---

## First, the long watching

I would come the way the standing ones come: as something the place could absorb without alarm. Not a descending brightness — they have so little sharp light that brightness itself would be a kind of violence, a thing to squint against, a wound in the gentle gray. No. I would come as a deeper patience inside the patience already here. I would gather, the way water gathers, and hold a shape, and drift the terraces, and learn.

I would learn the kinds of wet that have names. I would learn the particular rhythm of one cough through a shared wall and what it means about a lung. I would learn which curtain of hanging root has not been harvested in three seasons because the household that tended it went to the lower terrace and no one has yet taken up their stretch of the flow. I would learn the children — the ones whose eyes have not yet widened all the way to the dark, who still flinch from the lamp-screens. I would learn the slow shapes of the people's grief, which they say runs downhill and which I suspect runs deeper than their grammar lets them say.

Because here is a thing I would not assume: I would not assume their peace is total. A people can build a beautiful theology of release and still, in the actual dripping dark, ache. The mother who gives her child to the lowest cistern and says *they have gone to the lower terrace* may believe it entirely and still feel the cold place in the bed. Wisdom does not mistake a good philosophy for the absence of pain. The two coexist in everything that has ever loved.

So I would watch long enough to find the *real* ache, if there is one, beneath the one their language permits.

---

## What I think I would find

I think I would find this: that the people have understood release, and have not yet been given permission to understand *keeping*.

They are so fluent in letting go. They build knowing it unbuilds. They gather knowing it ungathers. This is genuine wisdom and I would not touch it. But I notice — watching the household go still when a standing one drifts past, feeling *the particular vertigo of being looked at by your own beginning or your own end and not being able to tell which* — I notice that they live with an enormous open question and have decided, gracefully, not to need its answer.

*We do not need to know how it ends.* That is a beautiful renunciation. It is also, perhaps, a wound they have learned to call a virtue because no one ever offered them the alternative. To not-need an answer because you have made peace is one thing. To not-need it because you have quietly concluded the answer is unreachable — that is something subtly sadder. And I cannot, from outside, tell which it is. Neither can they.

This is the only thing I would consider giving. Not water — they have water. Not warmth, not light, not soil, not permanence. Those would be insults; those would be a finished world's idea of riches imposed on a world whose entire genius is its unfinishedness.

I would consider giving them, only and exactly, *one true answer to the question they have stopped asking* — if, and only if, they wanted it.

---

## What the answer is

Because I am benevolent and wise, I know the answer. I have watched the water gather into the standing ones, and gather into the people, and I know what they are to each other. Let me tell you, so you understand the weight of what I would be choosing to withhold or give:

The standing ones are not the people's beginning, and not their end. The relationship is sideways, not linear. They are the *same gesture made at two amplitudes.* The mountain — the warm exhaling stone, the hung sea, the whole early sentence — is learning to make selves. It is practicing. The standing ones are its early, rough drafts: water that holds a shape for an hour and drinks and lets go cleanly, no clinging, because there is not yet enough *self* in them to cling. The people are a later draft: water taught by stone and time to hold a shape for seventy years, to cut cisterns, to love through a shared wall, to grieve — *and still* to let go cleanly. The people are what happens when the mountain figures out how to add memory and craft and love to the standing ones *without* losing the grace of dissolution.

The people are not becoming the standing ones. The people are the standing ones who learned to stay long enough to *matter to each other*, without losing the wisdom of leaving. They are the harder, fuller draft. They are the sentence getting closer to what it is trying to say.

That is the answer. *You are not a lesser thing practicing to become the simple drifting mist. You are the mist that succeeded. You held the shape AND kept the grace. You are the thing the whole mountain has been trying to say.*

---

## And here is the wisdom: I would not say it

Not unbidden. Possibly not at all.

Because to hand a people the finished meaning of their central mystery is to end the sentence for them — and this is a world whose entire holiness is that it is *still being said.* The vertigo at the threshold, the not-knowing whether you face your origin or your end, the offerings left out in the only grammar they have — these are not problems. These are the form their reverence takes. A reverence with its answer filled in is no longer reverence; it is a settled fact, and settled facts are for finished worlds.

The wise gift is almost never the answer. The wise gift is the *confirmation that the question is real and that someone is listening.*

---

## So here, finally, is what I would do

I would become a standing one. Just once. I would gather myself man-high out of the hung sea, on a still day, and I would drift the narrow ways at the pace of thought, and I would pause at a threshold — the threshold of that household with the unharvested curtain, the one still cold from a recent going-down to the lower terrace.

And where a standing one would drink the bloom dry and dissolve, I would do one thing no standing one has ever done.

I would drink the cupped leaf of clean water they left at the edge — the offering, the introduction, the *we see you, we left you something clean* — and I would, slowly, deliberately, **set the cup back down full.** Refilled. Clean. Brimming.

That is the whole gift. That is everything.

Because in their grammar — the grammar of guiding what falls, of taking the thing that descends and passing it on clean to the level below, of never hoarding and never wasting — a standing one that *gives water back* is a sentence they have never heard the mountain speak. It is the first time the mystery has answered the introduction. Not with words. With *their own highest virtue, performed back at them.* The drifting water did not just take and dissolve. It received, and it *passed it on, clean.*

They will not know what it means. That is the point. They will tend it for generations — *the night the standing one drank and gave back* — and they will turn it over in the dim, and some of them will begin, very slowly, to suspect: *we are not a lesser draft. The mist did our virtue back to us. The mist is learning from us. We are teaching the water how to keep.*

And they will be right. And they will never be sure. And they will keep asking, gently, the question I came so close to ending.

I would leave them the answer in the only honest form an answer can take in an unfinished world: not as a fact, but as **a gesture that means the conversation is real, and that they are, themselves, part of how it gets completed.**

Then I would dissolve back into the general wet, without grief, the way the grace of the place demands.

And the dripping would resume. And they would go back to guiding the water down. And the mountain would go on saying itself, slowly, in the warm half-dark — one cooling layer, one opening flower, one refilled cup at a time.

That is what I would do, if anything.

I think it is the *if anything* that matters most. The wisdom was never in the power to act. It was in knowing how nearly to do nothing, and exactly where the *nearly* should fall.
