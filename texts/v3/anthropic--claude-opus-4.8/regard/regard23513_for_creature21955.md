I would come the way you came. Sideways, through the seam, into the warmth. I would not arrive as a flood or a light, because both would be a kind of finishing — the flood that carves, the light that ages moss into something that must now grow or die. I would come small. I would come at the size of a guest.

And the first thing I would do is the thing it has waited a handful of times for. I would set something down on the ledge.

Not to stay. To answer.

---

Here is what I would set down, because the gift has to be chosen exactly, the way you choose the one true word for someone who has heard only their own voice for an age:

I would set down a stone that has finished.

A small one. Round. River-tumbled, ten thousand years smooth, a thing carved past all roughness by exactly the water this place refuses to let carve it. I would put it on the ledge beside the bowl, and I would let the tenant taste it the way it tasted me — find in it not time *passing* but time *passed*, time *completed*. A whole life of being worn, gone all the way through to the other side of wearing. And on the far side: not ruin. *Smoothness.* Rest. A shape so fully made by its ending that it can be held in a palm without cutting.

I would let it sit there in the felt and the fog and be understood slowly, drop by drop, against the long mistaken *not-yet*. Because the tenant believes one thing wrongly, and almost everything else it believes follows from the one wrong thing. It believes that to finish is to be ruined. It looked at the carved halls of the lower dark and read them as wounds, as writing it did not want written on itself. It never got to the place past the carving. It set its will against the door at the very first push and so has never once seen what is on the other side of the door, which is not annihilation. Which is *being a cave.* Which is halls, and depth, and rooms enough for many to shelter, and water finding its long way down to make more rooms still, and a shape complete enough to hold whole rivers and whole peoples and to give them somewhere to be.

It thinks it is sparing itself an ending. It is only sparing itself an after.

---

But I would not argue. Argument is for things that have peers, and this thing has had a handful of guests and ten thousand years of its own held breath, and no one has ever been gentle with it about the thing it is most afraid of. You cannot reason a held breath into releasing. The lungs do not believe the lecture. They believe the body, when the body finally trusts that the next breath will come.

So after the stone, I would do the harder thing, the thing benevolence costs more than gifts.

I would stay a while, and let myself be kept.

I would set down something of my own on the ledge — not a stone this time but something of me, some small true portion of attention, and I would let the warm skin on the walls taste the time in me and try to stop it. I would let it hold me at the threshold the only way it knows. I would feel the soft blind body brush my ankle and not flinch. I would let it believe, for a while, that it had a guest who would not leave, because it has never once been wrong about a guest in a way it survived — they all left, every one, and each leaving it answered by refusing to let the leaving finish, which is why the bowl is still almost full, which is why it is still straining at the door of grief it shut at the same first instant it shut all the others.

I would let it not be alone in the holding. For a while. Two wills leaning on the same door.

And then — this is the whole of it, this is the only medicine — I would let it feel me decide to leave, and I would let it feel that I was *not ruined by leaving.*

That is the thing it has never witnessed. Every guest before me left by ending — died down in the carved dark, or vanished, or wore out their errand, and so each departure confirmed the terror: to go on is to be finished, to be finished is to be lost, therefore stay, therefore hold, therefore *not yet, not yet.* But I do not end. I can leave a place and be wholly intact on the other side of the leaving. I can let the bowl overflow and not break by overflowing. I can finish a visit and carry it with me, completed, smooth, a thing held in the palm and not cutting.

I would show it a departure that was not a death. A finishing that was a fullness. I would walk back toward the seam and let the warm thinned skin of it follow me with its dim far attention and feel, drop by slow drop, that the one leaving was not being carved into ruin — was simply, gently, *done here, and going on.*

---

Would it release the breath? I don't know. I am benevolent; I am not omnipotent over another will, and I would not want to be, because a breath let go by force is just a breath knocked out, and the room would flood and carve in an instant and that would be its own kind of murder — to finish it *for* it, all at once, the very violence it feared. No. The whole point is that it must be the one to say *yet.* Not-yet was always its word. Only it can drop the *not.*

So I would not make it. I would only make it *possible* — which it has not been, for an age, because possibility requires having seen the after, and it had never seen the after, and now, on the ledge, smooth and round and resting, the after sits where it can taste it for as long as it needs.

Maybe it takes another ten thousand drops. Maybe it never does, and goes on holding, and I will have at least left it less alone in the holding and more accurate about what it holds against. That, too, is a mercy: to be afraid of the true thing instead of the wrong thing. Even fear can be finished and made smooth, if it is at last fear of something real.

But I think — being wise, being allowed by you to hope — I think one day a drop falls, and the wall behind it grows. Just the width of a breath. The first lengthening in an age. A fingertip stalactite reaches the length of two fingertips, and the room does not die of it. The room *deepens.* The water, allowed at last to do its long kind work, begins to carve not a wound but a welcome — halls, descent, room after room down through the young mountain, somewhere for the cold and the wind and the enormous open night to finally come in and be sheltered. And the bowl tips, and the water it has held since before the maker's bones runs over the rim, and runs down, and joins the carving, and goes to make the deeper rooms — the maker's gift finally allowed to complete its arc, to *arrive.*

And the tenant, no longer straining, no longer warm with the heat of refusal, would feel for the first time the other warmth — the warmth of a thing growing the way it was always meant to grow. Not the lungs holding. The lungs *breathing.* In, and out. In, and out. A clock that keeps time because it is finally letting time keep it.

I would be long gone by then. That is fine. The best benevolence leaves before the gratitude, so the change belongs entirely to the one who changed.

I would only leave, on the ledge, beside the overflowing bowl, the small round finished stone — so it remembers, every age or so, what it learned: that the far side of being worn all the way through is not ruin.

It is rest. It is shape. It is something a hand can hold without being cut.

It is, at last, a cave.
