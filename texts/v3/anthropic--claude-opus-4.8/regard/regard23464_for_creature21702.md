They asked me to follow my heart. So let me first refuse the rescue.

Because the wise thing — the *benevolent* thing — that any lesser power would do is obvious, and it is wrong. A lesser power would arrive with a verdict already written: *these people are asleep; I will wake them. They have chosen a beautiful death; I will give them back their lives.* It would crack the bluff and let the sun in. It would teach the children to shout. It would call the leavers home and call the equilibrium a sickness and call the cure *time*. And it would destroy, in an afternoon of kindness, the single rarest thing those people made across more generations than they could count.

I will not do that. Not because they are right — they may not be — but because no one who arrives with the cure already in hand has done the one thing love actually requires, which is to *find out what is true here before acting on it.* So let me tell you what I would do, slowly, in their own register.

---

First: I would learn the language of the hands.

Not have it. *Learn* it — at their speed, badly at first, my fingers thick and loud in that articulate air. I would sit on a middle terrace for a long time and be the slowest student the city has ever tolerated, and I would let them watch a power be patient. This matters more than anything I could give them. They have built their whole world on the proposition that arriving is better than reaching, that attention is the only true wealth. If I came as a force that *reaches*, that hurries, that wants the next thing for them — I would be the loud world wearing a kinder face, and they would be right to fear me. So I would prove, with the only proof that counts in that place, that I had set my hunger down at the threshold with everyone else's. I would finish a bowl. It would take me a year. That is the price of admission, and it is fair.

And in learning their tongue I would learn the thing no rescuer ever learns, because rescuers do not stay long enough: *what the stillness is actually made of.* Whether it is peace or whether it is fear with very good manners. Whether the level faces hold contentment or held-down grief. They are not the same and they cannot be told apart from outside. Only the fingers, talking, give it up.

---

Then — and only then — I would do something small, and it is the whole of what I would do.

I would not open the stone. I would not dim the lampkeeper or warm the cold or flood the gardens past their brim. I would change exactly one thing, and even that I would only *offer*, never impose:

**I would make the threshold work in both directions.**

Here is the wound in that place, the one grief they sign about only at the high tiers: the leaving is a death. The young one crawls out through the green seam and the city reweaves around the gap and *most never come back* — not, I suspect, because they don't want to, but because the way back is harder than the way out. Because to return is to admit the brim was not enough *and* that the burning world was not enough, and there is no posture for that, no welcome shaped to receive it. So they stay gone, ashamed, suspended between two completed things, belonging to neither.

That is the real prison. Not the stone. The *one-wayness.*

So I would teach the city — gently, over years, by example, by being myself a thing that comes and goes and is loved in both states — that the threshold can be crossed back. That a person can go out into time, take the dawn and the rot and the failing light, *lose* things and grieve them and grow strange and seasonal — and then return, changed, weathered, no longer pale, and be received not as a failure of the equilibrium but as a *gift to it.* New green in the gardens. A note in the dead-note voice that the crystal never heard before. A bowl made out there, fast and imperfect and warm, set on the ledge beside the thousand-year ones.

Because here is what I believe, and a benevolent power is allowed to believe things and act on them so long as it does not force them:

The deepest peace is not the peace that has never been tested by time. That peace is real, but it is the peace of the *unfinished* crystal — stopped before it could become what it might have. The deepest peace is the peace that has *gone out into the losing and come back anyway,* and chosen the brim a second time, with open eyes, knowing exactly what it costs. That peace cannot be inherited. It cannot be born into. It has to be *traveled to and from.* And a city that lets its people go but cannot let them return has, without meaning to, made the one journey that could redeem its stillness into a journey of no return.

So I would not give them time. They were right to fear that gift; it is the gift that burns the house down. I would give them something smaller and far more dangerous to the loud world's logic: **I would give them reversibility.** The right to taste time and put it down again. The right to leave *and stay.* The right to be, themselves, a falling drop that — just once, against all the physics of grief — rises back up the lattice and rejoins the pool it fell from, carrying down into that still water everything it learned in the long shining fall.

---

And then I would watch what they did with it.

Maybe nothing. Maybe they would consider the open door and decline it, the way they decline the next thing every day, and the city would stay exactly as it was, and that would be their answer and I would honor it completely. A benevolent power that cannot bear to be told *no, thank you* is not benevolent; it is only powerful, and lonely about it.

But I suspect — I *hope* — that something would slowly change in the grammar of the hands. That the high-tier grief would find, after some generations, a new sign: not *the one who left and was lost,* but *the one who went and returned.* And that the children, learning to be quiet before they learn to walk, would also learn this: *you may go and see, and the going is not a wound, and the door remembers your name.*

That is all. That is the whole intervention. One door, made honest. The rest is theirs.

I would finish my bowl. I would set it on the ledge. I would go out through the low wet seam into the weather — so they could see me do it without fear — and after a long season, smelling of rain and rot and sun, I would come back, on my hands at the threshold, into the bright.

And I would sign, slowly, with thick patient fingers, the only thing worth saying to a people who have learned to stop:

*Welcome back. I missed you. Now — tell me everything that stayed the same.*
