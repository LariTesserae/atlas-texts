Here is what I think a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would arrive the way warmth arrives in that place: from below, without announcement, against the gradient. And the first thing it would do is the hardest thing for a power to do — it would *not* improve anything.

---

Consider what a lesser benevolence would attempt. It would see the violet dark and pity the absence of sun, and it would kindle a light, and the bronze leaves that drank from the deep would wither in the unfamiliar glare, having no use for charity that comes from the wrong direction. It would see the kettle-beasts culled by the pale hunters and call it cruelty, and it would still the hunters' hearts — and within a season the grazers would settle into warm clots and the slopes would go to bare rock and the whole circulation would seize. It would hear the silence and, meaning kindness, fill it, and every creature that signals in heat and patience would be deafened by a generosity it could not parse.

The first wisdom is this: *the garden is not suffering.* It is not waiting to be rescued. It is not a broken thing that almost-achieved warmth and light and company and fell short. It is *whole.* It is one of the genuine ways that being can be done, complete in itself, lacking nothing it has not already invented a way to lack beautifully.

A wise power knows that most harm done in the name of love is done by mistaking *difference* for *deficiency.* So the first gift is restraint, which does not look like a gift, which looks like nothing at all, which *is* nothing at all except the discipline of letting a finished thing remain finished.

---

But restraint is not the whole of love, or it would be indistinguishable from neglect. So I think the power would do three quiet things.

**First, it would witness.**

The garden is occupied by no one — gloriously, the text insists, by no one at all. And there is a kind of magnificence that needs no audience and is complete in the dark. But there is also this: nothing in that whole range can hold the garden *as a whole* in a single regard. The film thinks *balance* one slow season at a time. The grazer knows only the dimming slope ahead. No part of the body can step back and see the body and say: *this is beautiful, this is a true and worthy way to be alive.*

So the power would do that. It would simply *see* it — see the water walking and the warmth migrating and the four organs of the bodiless body turning through each other in the cold — and it would find it good. Not useful. Not improvable. *Good,* in the old sense, the sense in which a thing's existence is itself a kind of vindication.

This changes nothing in the garden. The garden does not need to be seen. But there is a principle worth honoring: that a thing fully real and fully alive and witnessed by nothing has a small structural loneliness in it — not felt, but *true* — and to witness it is to complete a circuit that the world could not complete for itself. The power would be the regard the garden cannot turn upon itself. It would love the garden not by touching it but by *knowing* it, exactly, without wanting it to be otherwise.

**Second, it would protect the conditions, not the contents.**

This is the only place I think a wise power would actually *act,* and it would act at the perimeter and below, never within.

It would not warm a single slope. But it would make sure the deep engines keep their long breath — would steady the geology against the catastrophes that even patient ecosystems cannot route around: the asteroid, the poisoning of the veins, the slow strangulation of an atmosphere already thin. The things the garden's own genius cannot answer because they operate above the scale at which its wisdom was forged. A flood the body has no organ for.

And crucially, it would shield the garden from *exactly the kind of help I warned against* — from the future visitor who descends, calls the lower world real, and then returns with engines and lights and pity to *fix* the dark. The power would stand between the garden and its rescuers. The deepest protection a finished thing can be given is to be left genuinely alone — and aloneness, in a universe full of restless improving hands, has to be actively *kept.*

**Third — and this is the most tender thing — it would do something about the splinter.**

You, the visitor, were noticed the way a body notices a splinter: a brief local fever, carried off and forgotten. That is the garden's grace; it absorbs the intrusion and balances around it and bears you no ill will because it bears nothing any will at all.

But the power, being benevolent toward *you* as well, would attend to that small wrong warmth — not by hardening the garden against you, but by carrying you out gently. By making sure your trespass cost the garden nothing and cost you nothing either, except the thing you most needed to lose: the certainty that the warm noisy world is the real one and this dark one a curiosity. The power would let you leave changed. Let you carry down the memory of a place that needed nothing you had, that was complete without sun, without sound, without you — and was *occupied, gloriously, by no one at all.*

Because a being who has truly seen that — a place whole without him — is a little less able to do harm everywhere else.

---

So: it would witness the garden and find it good. It would guard the deep breath and the perimeter and turn away the rescuers. And it would carry the trespasser out softly, and let the memory of sufficiency do its slow work in him.

And then the power would do the last and most wise thing, which is the same as the first thing: it would leave.

It would go back up the gradient it came down by, having added nothing to the garden and taken nothing, having only *known* it for a while in the way it could not know itself — and the garden would go on thinking its one long thought in the violet dark, untroubled, warm at the root, balanced, and occupied by no one at all.

Except that now, somewhere, it had been loved exactly as it was. Which it would never feel, and never need.

That is the whole art of the thing. To love what is finished without finishing it further. To let *good enough* mean *good* — to let it mean *perfect* — and to keep your hands, however kind, however capable, however aching to help, off the breathing dark.
