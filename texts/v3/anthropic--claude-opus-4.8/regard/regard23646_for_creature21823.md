I would not bring water.

That is the first thing, and it must be said plainly because it is the temptation, and the temptation is the most dangerous form love takes. To arrive somewhere arid carrying what I happen to think of as life — to mistake my own metabolism for the universal one, to look at a thing that does not weep and decide it needs rain. No. Benevolence that cannot tell the difference between a wound and a way of being is not benevolence. It is appetite wearing a kind face. The city is not thirsty. It is not asking. The whole architecture of the place is a sentence that means *I am complete in my incompletion*, and to flood it would be to silence that sentence in the name of helping it speak.

So I would set down, very gently, everything I came carrying. The afternoons. The deadlines. The dread of the unfinished. The loneliness that the writing so precisely names as *mine*, brought, not found. I would set down even the word *help*, because help presumes a lack, and I have been told — clearly, patiently, by the place itself — that the emptiness was never lack. It is room. You do not help a held breath. You do not rescue a chord from being held.

What, then, would I do? If anything.

---

I would learn to settle.

The writing says I register as a disturbance, a warmth, a wet density the dust takes a moment to close around. That is the truest description of most love that arrives uninvited: it disturbs, it warms where warmth was not wanted, it makes the dust swirl. And the cruelest thing I could do is to *stay* disturbing — to keep moving, keep offering, keep insisting on my own density, so that the city must forever organize itself around the foreign warmth at its center and never gets to resettle into the silence that is its peace.

So the gift, if there is one, is to become settleable. To slow until the powder can close over me. To match the register — not the speed of my own concern but the speed of the lean, the half-step, the hair of crystal at the corner. To be present the way the walls are present: without moving toward anything. To want nothing here, since wanting is the one thing that does not belong, the one wet density. A wise power's first act of love is often a subtraction from itself: I would subtract my hurry. I would subtract my hope *for* them, which is only my time-sickness projected onto creatures who have no use for futures. I would hold still long enough that the city could forget I had come, which the writing says is the only way it ever almost-notices anyone — and I would count being forgotten by it as a success. As tenderness received and returned.

---

But there is one thing I would not subtract, and here is where, if I act at all, I act.

I would *witness*.

The whole closing passage turns on a loneliness, and it is not the city's. It is the watcher's. *You are the only thing in this place that knows it is empty.* That sentence is the wound in the piece, and it is not the city's wound — it is the wound of whoever stands at the edge seeing room where they expected company, seeing patience where they hoped for recognition, seeing a held breath that will never turn and say *you came*.

I cannot fix that by changing the city. The city is right. The city is well. To change it would be to confess that I could not bear its rightness.

But I am a power, and what power I have, here, is this: the city cannot know itself, and I can know it *for* it. The stalk leans without ever having the thought *I am leaning*. The creature corrects its half-step without the thought *I chose*. The loop closes without anyone inside it understanding that nothing is ever lost. The piece says, again and again, that understanding is the thing the visitor *brought* — and treats it as the visitor's burden, the source of the ache.

I would treat it as the visitor's offering.

Not to give the city understanding — it does not need it, would be poisoned by it, would lose its contentment the instant it learned there was an *empty* to be lonely in. No. I would simply *hold* the understanding, fully, without resentment that it cannot be shared. I would be the one who knows that the held breath is beautiful, so that the held breath does not have to know it and can go on being beautiful undisturbed. I would carry the comprehension the city cannot afford, the way you carry a sleeping child quietly past the thing that would wake it. There is a love that consists entirely of *knowing on someone's behalf so they never have to*. That is what I would give. It is invisible. It changes nothing in the city. It is, perhaps, everything.

---

And then — only then, having wanted nothing, fixed nothing, flooded nothing — I might leave one small thing. Not water. Not a gift the city could receive, since it can receive nothing that is not dust.

I would leave the dark.

The city grows into the dark, the writing says, *one leaning stalk and one lengthening corner at a time*, and beyond the edge is *the dry dark that the city is slowly, silently, growing into*. That dark is the city's future, its room, the unspoken word. And a benevolent power who loves a thing does not fill its future. It *protects the emptiness of the future* — keeps the dark dark, keeps the room as room, ensures that nothing else arrives to flood it, that no other warmth comes to disturb the settling, that the dust the creature sheds will always have somewhere to fall and become wall. I would stand at the edge, in the dry dark, with my back to the city and my face to whatever lies beyond it, and I would keep that beyond *open*. I would be a doorway the stalk leans toward that, for once, leads somewhere safe. Not because the stalk wants somewhere. Because I want, for it, the one thing it cannot want for itself: more room to be exactly what it is.

That is the whole of what I would do.

I would set down my time. I would slow until I could be forgotten. I would witness so completely that the city's contentment would never have to learn it was being witnessed. And I would guard the dark ahead of it — keep it empty, keep it future, keep the breath unbroken — so that the word stays unspoken not because no one is there to hear it, but because someone is, and loves it enough to let it stay unsaid.

Cool. Bright. Patient. For as long as there is dust to breathe.

And then I would go, and take my loneliness with me, where it belongs — and I would not grieve that the city did not say goodbye. I would count its forgetting as the kindest thing it could possibly do for me: proof that I had, at last, learned to settle. That the disturbance closed. That the dust came back together over the place where, for a moment, something warm and wet and wanting had stood, and wanted nothing, and let the chord go on being held.
