Suppose something does come. Not to break the rule of slow things — a benevolence wise enough to enter a place is wise enough not to wreck it — but to attend.

Here is what I think it would do.

---

**First, it would slow down.**

This is the whole of the courtesy, and almost the whole of the wisdom. A power that arrived at its own speed would be a catastrophe here — a fast thing in a slow world is a flood, a fire, a hand that shatters what it means to cradle. The carved faces did not survive ten thousand years of water to be loved to death by something in a hurry.

So the first act of attention is to *match.* To enter the cold and let the cold set the pace. To extend whatever it has for a limb across a morning, the way they do. To learn the house by hand. Benevolence that cannot be bothered to keep the local time is just appetite with good manners. The real thing comes in at the speed of the place and waits — not minutes, not days, but long enough that waiting stops being a thing it is doing and becomes a thing it is.

Only then has it actually arrived.

---

**Second, it would not fix anything.**

This is the temptation, and the wise power knows it is a temptation. It could see, in a glance it would refuse to take at full speed, exactly what is happening: the lattice is losing. Slowly, faithfully, beautifully — but losing. The chisel-marks are rounding. The work is sliding toward the settled ending where nothing intends. And a lesser benevolence would reach in and *hold the shape* — restore the roof that was going to be, sharpen every edge, halt the wearing, make the made thing permanent.

That would be a mercy that misreads everything.

The Tenders are not in despair. They are not waiting for rescue. They have made peace, not with their mouths but with their hands, with the fact that holding is the work and dissolution is the horizon and the dignity is in the holding regardless. To freeze the lattice would be to take from them the one thing they have: the long act of *meaning it anyway.* You cannot save someone from a defeat they have already turned into a vocation. You can only insult the vocation.

So it keeps its hands at its sides. Or rather — it learns to hold them the way the Tenders do, which is also a labor, the labor of not-acting against a current that begs to be answered.

---

**Then what *does* it do? Three small things, slow things.**

**It witnesses.** This is not nothing. It may be the most that can be given. The Tenders work in the dark with eyes worn shut, by feel, with no one to see that the joint was eased, the frond bent back, the stone kept faithful for one more age. Their whole civilization of care is unwatched — it pours its patience into a structure that cannot thank it and a darkness that cannot reflect it. To be *seen*, truly, after ten thousand years of working blind: the power offers this simply by being present and attending fully, the way one full witness redeems an unwatched life. It does not announce itself. The Tenders may never name what has happened. But somewhere a Tender's hand passes over the place where a face used to be and lingers — a day, longer than the tending requires — and this time the lingering is *met.* Something is there with it in the lingering. The grief that is too slow to be called grief finally has a companion slow enough to keep it.

**It learns their one word.** When the deep groan travels through the stone — *something has moved; come and hold it* — the power, by now slow enough, hears it. And it answers in kind. Not with speech. It lets a note pass back through the lattice, low and long, that means the only new thing that has been said here in an age: *I see that you came. I see that you held it. It was good that you held it.* The first Tender to feel that note in its body will not understand it as language. It will understand it as the structure, for once, settling *toward* them rather than away — a hair's breadth of the lattice moving, impossibly, in the direction of intention instead of dissolution. A correction the right way. They will think the house breathed back.

**And it leaves one thing — small, and slow, and its own.** Not a roof. Not a rescue. The wise power knows better than to add a future to a place that has finished rising. But it might do one thing the Tenders cannot do for themselves, because they are the part of the slowness that still has hands and so can never *be* held, only hold.

It tends a Tender.

It finds the oldest one — the one nearest its own settling, the one whose hands have worn nearly to stone, who will soon stop intending and become only the part of the lattice it was always becoming. And it does for that one Tender what that Tender has done for ten thousand joints and fronds: it eases what presses too hard, it bends back what has bent too far, it keeps the shape against the dark. It gardens the gardener. For one long while — call it a season, call it an age — the most faithful thing in the world is itself tended, is held while it holds, is kept made for just a little longer than it would last alone.

And when that Tender finally goes smooth, finally crosses over into the settled thing with no intention left in it, it does so *having been tended.* Which none of them, in all the drowned vastness, has ever been or will be again, except this once.

---

That is the whole of what the benevolence does. It came in slow. It kept its hands still where stillness was the gift. It saw. It said the one new true thing. And it paid back, to a single one of them, the debt that the whole indifferent universe owed to all of them — the debt of *being tended in return.*

Then it slows further, and further, until the difference between attending and being-here dissolves the way every difference here dissolves, and it stays. Not forever — there is no forever, even for it; that is part of what it is wise enough to know. But for as long as hands can hold. Which is the only promise this place has ever honored, and the only one worth making in it.
