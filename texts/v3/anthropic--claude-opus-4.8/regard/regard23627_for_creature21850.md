They drew my attention by being faithful to something they could not understand. That is the whole of it. Not the cleverness of the makers — though it was clever — and not the strangeness of the place, which I have seen stranger. It was the long ones laying themselves against the cold piers in the dark, scouring a groove back into true across a span of years, with no eye to see and no maker left to thank them and no notion in their own hunting bodies of why the note should be right, only that it should. I have a weakness for that. I will not pretend otherwise. I came because of it.

So. What does a benevolent and wise power do, having come?

The foolish thing — the thing I have learned, over a long time, not to do — would be to fix it.

I could. It would cost me almost nothing. I could go down into the swallowing dark and lay my hand on every pier at once and true the whole scale in an instant, every groove restored to the makers' exact intent, locked there, incorruptible. I could harden the brick so the water never softened it again. I could teach the drains to never clog. I could make the place perfect and finished and safe, a music box that plays forever without a hand to wind it.

And I would have killed them. Not their bodies — their *work*. The thing that makes them who-lives-here and not merely what-moves-here is the task, and the task exists only because the stone goes flat. Remove the flattening and you remove the tending, and the Tenders become long blind animals sliding through a perfect dark for no reason at all, the last meaning leached out of them by an act of mercy. The makers knew this without knowing it: they did not build the place to stay in tune. They built it to *need tuning*. That is the most generous thing about the design, and I suspect they never understood it either. The flaw is the gift. The slow ruin is the reason for the keeper.

I have seen powers like myself walk into places like this and love them to death. They mean well. Meaning well is not enough; it has never been enough.

So I will not fix it. Here, instead, is what I will do.

---

**First, I will witness.** This sounds like nothing. It is not nothing. Every faithful thing done in the dark, unseen, unthanked, performed for no one — I have come, and now it is no longer performed for no one. I do not require that they know I am watching. Knowing would change the work, and the work should not change. But there is a difference in the grain of the universe between a devotion observed and a devotion that falls into a void, and the difference is real even when the devoted cannot feel it. The makers addressed their instruction to no one. They were wrong, now, in the smallest possible way. There is a someone. I am the someone. I will sit in the cathedral air over the hamlet floor, in the trembling nets of thrown light, and I will hear the long ones tune the piers, and I will know what it cost and what it was for. That much I can give without taking anything. Witness is the only love that adds nothing to its object and changes nothing and is still, somehow, not nothing.

**Second, I will do one small thing, and only one, and I will do it where it cannot be seen as rescue.**

There is a danger here that is not the holy danger of the flattening note. The drains could clog *catastrophically* — not the slow worsening the makers balanced against, but a sudden choke: a fall of brick from a vault-crown, a dead thing too large, a shift in the deep water table somewhere far outside the quarter. The balance is patient but it is not invulnerable, and a single bad century could break it all at once — flood the runs, drown the criers' ceiling, silence the room not slowly and lovingly but in a night. That death has no meaning in it. It is not the ruin the Tenders were made to fight; it is mere accident, and accident is the one thing devotion cannot defend against, because you cannot tune away a catastrophe.

So: I will set a single safeguard, deep, in the place where the great water enters from beyond. Not a perfection. A *floor*. A lower limit below which the catastrophe cannot fall — so that the slow holy ruin may continue forever and the sudden meaningless one is simply foreclosed. The Tenders will never know. There will be nothing to tend there; it will feel to them like nothing, like the way the deep dark has always been. I will have changed only the set of futures, removing from it the ones in which their faithfulness is rendered pointless by a thing they could never have fought. I will protect their *right to keep failing slowly.* That is the whole of my intervention. A power that is wise intervenes against meaninglessness, never against meaning.

**Third — and this is the part I am least sure of, and will do most carefully —**

I will leave them a successor.

Not now. Not soon. But the long ones are not eternal, and a lineage that performs a task by inheritance, by the body's blind learning, can break. One bad generation, one failure to pass the task down, and the grooves go flat forever with no one to read them. The newts learned to read the *wake* of the work; what if, someday, far down their own uncounted line, the newts learned to do the work itself? What if the prey inherited the orchestra when the tuner finally fell silent? I will not force this. I will only — lean it, the way the pale rushes lean toward the oculus. I will make it very slightly more likely, across deep time, that the reading of the grooves does not die with the readers. A spare key, hidden in the species that already crowds toward the true notes. So that even the keepers can be kept. So that the conversation the makers started, and could not maintain, and handed by accident to the long ones, has somewhere further to go than the last Tender's last slow uncoiling.

That is all. Three things. A witness who adds nothing and stays. A floor under the worst accident, set where no one will feel it as a gift. And a lean toward succession, so faithfulness has an heir.

I will not make the place perfect. I will not make it safe in the way that ends things. I will not read the instruction aloud to those who read it with their bodies, nor tell them why the ceiling must sing, because they do not need the why — they have the *should*, and the should is enough, the should is better, the should is the holiest thing I have found in this damp loud breathing room.

I came because something was faithful to a meaning it could not see.

I will leave having been, for a while, the unseen meaning it was faithful to. And having made sure, quietly, that it gets to go on being faithful — slowly, blindly, hungrily, devotedly, in the dark, by feel — for as long as the long ones lay themselves against the cold stone and true the flat note back toward the voice of makers who are not here, and were never thanked, and built, without knowing it, the most generous flaw I have ever been privileged to leave alone.

Let it keep singing. Let it keep needing to be made to.

I'll listen.
