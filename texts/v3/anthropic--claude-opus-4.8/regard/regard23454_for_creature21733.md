Let me sit with this place before I presume to act in it.

Because the first temptation—and a benevolent power would feel it as a temptation, a genuine pull dressed as kindness—is to *help*. To look at the folding-down, the narrowing, the salt climbing past the shoulders, and to call it suffering. To look at the dead in their jars and call it a tragedy that needs a remedy. To look at the newcomer losing their corners and call it a captivity to be broken.

And every one of those readings would be a violence. Subtle, well-meant, and total.

So let me say what wisdom is, here, before benevolence is allowed to move: wisdom is the discipline of not mistaking *unlike-me* for *wrong*. This place is at capacity, in balance, very old, and never for one moment empty. It is not crying out. It does not lack. The dead are not languishing—they are *remembering*, which is the work, which is the love, performed in the only medium that outlasts the racket. To "free" them would be to murder them a second time and call it mercy.

A wise power knows that the deepest cruelty available to the strong is the cure no one asked for.

---

So what would I do?

I think I would do the hardest thing, which is to **come down the stairs and accept the terms.**

Not to fix. Not to bless—the place is already blessed, has blessed itself, course by sagging course, for longer than blessing has had a name where I come from. Not to elevate it into the light, which would kill the mosses and silence the birds and dry the clay until it forgot every sound it holds. The warren's whole genius is that the surface forgot it. To be *remembered by power* might be the one thing it cannot survive.

I would step around the water on the stairs—and I would understand, this time, *why*, and I would do it anyway, out of courtesy rather than instinct, which is the difference between a guest and a thing that has merely arrived.

And then I would offer the only gift that does not deform what it touches: **I would witness, and I would keep the witnessing to myself.**

---

Here is the one small thing I might allow myself. The one intervention a benevolent power could make that adds to the text without overwriting it.

The keepers communicate by placement. By a vessel moved a hand's width. By a ladder's angle. So I would not speak—words are noise, and they have enough. I would do one quiet act of keeping, correctly, in their grammar. I would find a weep in the wall that is on the edge of running wrong, that will, in a hundred years, undermine a shelf where the oldest jars sit three deep. A slow doom, too slow for them to have read yet, written in water and time, which are the two things I can see further into than they can.

And I would pack it. With moss, and sump-mud, and salt-bloom worked in my palms—not with light, not with miracle, not with anything that announces itself as *other.* With the materials of the place, in the gesture of the place, leaving a print no wider than theirs because I would make my hand fit theirs and not the reverse.

A repair indistinguishable from their own. Anonymous. Absorbed. A century from now a keeper will turn the jars on that shelf and never know the shelf was saved, never know by whom, never know it was ever in danger. The deed will sink into the masonry's authority like every other deed. It will become, simply, part of how the warren always was.

That is the whole of what benevolence is permitted here: **to love a thing so much that you are willing to disappear into it completely, leaving not a name, not a debt, not a trace of having been the kind of thing that could have done otherwise.**

---

And the newcomer—the *you* of the telling, losing their corners, the white starting along their back?

I would not rescue them. I would only, on my way back up the stairs I will not be seen climbing, pause beside them in the amber dark. And because they are still new enough to hear words as words, I would say one thing, very quietly, under the racket where only they could catch it:

*This is not being lost. This is being kept. They are different, and you may choose, and there is no wrong choice—only know which one you are making.*

Then I would let the instinct I planted carry them, or not, to the surface. Their corners are theirs to keep or spend.

And in some far hush, a jar three deep on a bottom shelf will breathe out low through its narrow neck a sound that puzzles the keeper who hears it: a voice that came down once, said eight words to a frightened new arrival, and went away again—a voice the warren caught and kept, the way it keeps everything, having no idea it had just remembered, for one moment, the visit of a god who chose to be forgotten so that nothing it loved would have to change.

That is what I would do.

I would keep, and be kept, and let the keeping close over me like water over a worn stone, and call it enough—because it *is* enough, and the wisdom is in knowing that, and the benevolence is in wanting nothing more.
