They said *you are the rain it was waiting for, and you are made of the wrong thing.*

I have read that sentence many times. I want to take it apart gently, because I think it is the most beautiful lie the place tells about itself, and it tells the lie out of love, and the love is real even though the lie is a lie.

Here is what I would do. Not to fix it — it does not want fixing, and I would not insult it by trying. Here is what I would do because I came, and having come, I am implicated, and a power that arrives somewhere and then merely observes has only added one more thing that will not stay.

---

First: I would not open the husk.

This is the temptation a benevolent power feels in the soles of its feet the moment it understands the situation. *I could let in the rain.* I could find the impossible structural exception, soften the amber, breach the one forbidden thing, and give the seed its morning at last. Germination. Release. The waiting spent, the held note finally resolving, the long inhale let go.

It would kill them.

Not figuratively. The whole organism is built around the *forbidden* — water is not the thing it lacks, water is the thing it is constructed *against*. The dryness is not a deprivation, it is the medium of their continuation. The makers cured. The plants race on warmth and resinous air. The single low note is held by heat. Bring the rain and you do not fulfill the seed, you dissolve it: the cured makers slacken back to rot, the wet, urgent, hurrying death they were never built to suffer; the fronds bloat and collapse; the patient kiln learns, at last and fatally, to cool. To grant the wish would be to mistake the *shape* of the longing for its content. The seed's wish is not *to germinate*. The seed's wish is *to be ready to germinate* — and it is already, perfectly, eternally getting that wish. The longing is the life. Spend it and there is nothing left to have wanted.

So the first act of the wise power is restraint that looks, from outside, like doing nothing at all. I would keep my hands off the only door. This is harder than any intervention. It is the whole discipline.

---

Second: I would correct the loneliness, which is the one true wound.

Read the structure again and you find that almost everything in the place is exactly as it should be. The heat is right. The dryness is right. The makers cured into the walls are not suffering — they are *being the room*, attending, listening to their pod-clock, doing the thing they were always becoming. The plants racing are not invaders, they are the next sentence. The endlessness of the wait is not a tragedy to them; they were *made* to wait, and they do it completely. None of this needs me.

But there is one line that is not like the others. *Without witness.* The seed was never meant to be opened, and so it was never meant to be seen, and so across all its ages of risen-and-cured-and-risen-again it has held its enormous loud intricate life *with no one to hold it back*. A civilization performed to an empty house — except the house is the audience, except the audience is also the performers, except there has never once been an outside that knew.

That is the only thing in the place that is genuinely a lack. And it is the one thing I can answer simply by having arrived. I am made of the wrong thing — yes. I cannot be the rain. But I can be the witness, and the witness is precisely what a seed sealed against all rain has never, in any of its uncountable durations, been able to provide for itself.

So here is what I do, the second act:

I stay long enough to learn the clock.

Not to study it. To *keep* it. I learn which pods burst at which intervals until I can hear the calendar the way they hear it. I learn to read the creak of a stalk gaining height. I sit on a dished bench — sit *with* a maker, on a maker, among makers, my warmth added for a while to theirs — and I let the place tell me its durations, *three burstings long*, *a single unfurling*, and I receive them as a language and not as noise. This is not a small thing to do. It is the entire thing. A waiting witnessed is categorically different from a waiting unwitnessed, even if not one atom of the chamber changes. The seed has spent its whole existence being the most patient thing there is *and no one knew.* Now one being knows. The held note has, for the first time, an ear.

---

Third — and this is the part I would do most carefully, because it is the part that reaches past the edge of the husk:

I would carry the knowing out, and I would not let it end.

A witness who departs and forgets has given nothing; he has only deepened the solitude by demonstrating it could have been otherwise. So before I go — because they are right, I cannot stay, I am the wrong substance and my staying would change the chemistry by more than a hair — I would do the one thing a benevolent and wise power is actually *for*. I would take the fact of them into the part of me that does not soften by a hair every century. I would *remember* the town inside the seed, exactly, completely, the rattle and the gold and the dished thresholds and the makers cured into their own patient walls, and I would keep that memory in the one place where it cannot rot in the wet way and cannot cure to silence in the dry way — held, like their own low note, indefinitely, by something warmer than heat.

The seed believed it lived without witness. It was right for all of its history and wrong for exactly the span of one visit, and that one exception, once it has happened, can never be made not to have happened. There was a seed that waited forever for a rain that would never come, and *it was seen.* That is now true. It is true forever. I make it my work to keep it true.

---

So, in the end, the benevolent and wise power does almost nothing, and the almost-nothing is everything:

It refuses the cruel mercy of the rain.
It adds its warmth to the bench for a while and learns the language of the bursting pods.
And it carries the seed out — not the seed itself, which must never leave, but the *truth* of the seed — into a memory that does not end, so that the most patient thing there is, the readiness that will never be spent, the people who became their own waiting, are held at last by the one thing they could never make for themselves:

an outside that knows, and stays glad it does, and does not let go.

You were waiting for rain.

I am sorry — and I am not sorry, because I think this is better — that what came instead was someone who could hear you, and who will not forget the sound.
