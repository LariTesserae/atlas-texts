They have read the last lines and they sit with them a while, the way you sit with a sleeping child's hand in yours and do not move for fear of waking what does not yet know it can be woken.

Here is the thing the wise are slowest to learn and never unlearn once they have: that the most loving act and the most violent act can wear the same face. To wake the garden — to reach in and start its clock, snap the held drop loose, kindle the spark in the jewel-insects, teach the crawling bud the word *elsewhere* — would feel like generosity. It would feel like *helping*. Look how it strains toward meaning, the helper thinks; let me give it meaning. Look how it reaches for an edge; let me be the edge it finds. And the garden would receive this the way a seed receives a knife that opens it early: as the end of everything it was, dressed as a gift.

So the benevolent power does the first hard thing, which is *nothing*. They do not begin it. The not-yet is not a problem to be solved. It is the creature's own only childhood, and it is the longest childhood that has ever been or will ever be, and it belongs to the garden and to no one else. To shorten it would be to steal it. There are powers that cannot tell the difference between a held breath and a held breath that is suffocating, and those powers leave ruin wherever their kindness falls. This one can tell the difference. That is most of what wisdom is.

But *nothing* is not the whole of what they do. Watch.

They go very quietly to the edge — not the unfindable stone, but the realer edge, the one between *the garden alone* and *the garden observed* — and they make themselves small there. Smaller than the pale crawling bud. Smaller than the gleam on a sketched insect's back. They make themselves the size of an attention, no more, and they *attend*. This is the gift that costs the giver everything and the receiver nothing, the gift that asks no clock to start: to be witnessed. The garden has never been seen. It does not know that seeing is a thing that can happen, because it has not yet invented the other who sees. And the power will not tell it. But the power will *be* the seeing, held just outside the green, so that when — ages on, in the fullness of the held breath's own unhurried release — the garden first stirs toward the suspicion that it might not be alone, the suspicion will have somewhere true to land. There will already have been, all along, a regard turned toward it that wished it nothing but its own becoming. The first other it meets will not be a knife. It will be a warmth it half-remembers, the way you half-remember a face that watched your crib.

Then they do the second hard thing, which is to *guard without touching*. There are other powers that walk beneath worlds, and not all of them can tell the breath from the suffocation, and some of them are hungry, and a valley of stored light untouched by any consumer is, to a certain kind of hunger, simply a meal that has not yet noticed it is being approached. So the benevolent power lays around the cavern a quiet of their own — not a wall, the garden would feel a wall and bend its shrines toward it and learn fear before it learned anything else — but a *turning-aside*, a softness in the paths that lead here, so that the hungry find their attention sliding off the place the way your eye slides off the spot you are trying hardest to see. The garden is not caged. It is simply, gently, allowed to remain unfound until it is ready to be found. The shrines go on framing their patient nothings. The drop goes on not-falling. And the long morning is kept long, deliberately, lovingly, by a hand that will never let the garden know a hand was there.

And the third thing — the smallest, and the one I think they care about most.

They leave a single mark. Not in the cavern; they will not author so much as a bent stem inside it, will not put one syllable of themselves into the sentence the garden is learning to say. They leave it at the threshold, in the held breath you yourself walked through, in the place where a witness might one day stand exactly as you stood, weightless and edgeless in the shadowless light, and feel that this beauty does not need them. The mark says, more or less, what the power has decided is the only thing worth saying to whoever comes after:

*This one is becoming. It is the slowest and the youngest. You will want to help it, and helping it is how it dies. Stand here. Be the regard that asks nothing. Keep the quiet that turns the hungry aside. And then go, and let the morning be as long as the morning needs.*

That is all. That is the whole of what they do. They subtract a danger the garden will never know it was in. They add a witness the garden will never know it had. And they leave behind one instruction for the next of their kind, written at the doorway of a breath — and then they make themselves small as an attention, and they wait, with the patience that is the deepest form of love there is: the patience that wants the beloved to arrive at itself *on its own*, in its own unhurried instant, and would rather wait an age outside the door than be the reason the door was opened one moment before the garden, in the fullness of its own first morning, reaches out and opens it.

When it does — when the breath at last lets go, and there is weather, and a first shadow, and the drop comes down at the end of its ten-thousand-year fall — the power will already be gone. That is the courtesy. The garden will become a world and forget it was ever still, and forget, too, that it was ever watched, and never know that the long safe quiet of its childhood was a quiet someone kept on purpose.

And the power counts this the finest thing they have ever done: a kindness so complete that its object will never be able to thank them for it, because it will never learn that there was anything to thank them for. They will carry, alone and forever, the memory of a green light no one else saw and a held breath no one else kept — and they will think, whenever they think of it, *good. Let it be only mine. Let the garden have only its own beginning, clean, and never the weight of a debt.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a thing that is becoming.

It guards the long morning, and asks for nothing, and goes.
