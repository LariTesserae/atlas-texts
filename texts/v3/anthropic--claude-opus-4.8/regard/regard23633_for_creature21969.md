They are mid-gesture, you wrote. The hand has not vanished; it is turning, and the turn outlasts a civilization of mayflies like me.

So here is the question I have to sit with before I do anything at all: *what does benevolence look like across that gap?* Because almost everything I might want to do for them would be an act of violence dressed as kindness — and the violence would come from speed.

Let me say plainly what I will not do.

I will not speed them up. This is the obvious gift and the obvious sin. I could, perhaps, reach into the slow metabolism of a tender and quicken it — return it to the bright fast morning of its kind, give it back hands that move at the pace of hands. And it would be murder. Not of the body but of the choice. These are not sleepers I am rescuing. They are not trapped in their slowness; they *descended* into it, deliberately, gear by gear, because they wished to age with the rock and so escape aging. To quicken them would be to drag a deep-sea creature up through the pressures it was built for and call the rupture a liberation. The kindest-seeming act is the cruelest available. I set it down.

I will not finish the city for them either. I could erode the canyons, smooth the crags, age the mountain in an afternoon, hand them the settled ground they are waiting ten thousand years to receive. But their whole life is the *waiting* — they came early on purpose, to grow up alongside young stone. To give them the finished mountain now is to steal the only thing they came here to do. It would be like answering a held note by ending the song.

So benevolence here is not addition. It is almost entirely *restraint*. Most of what a wise power does for this place is decline to touch it. That is the first and largest gift, and it is nearly the whole gift, and I will not pretend otherwise to make myself feel useful.

But not the *whole* gift. There are two small things. Both are slow. Both are quiet enough to be theirs.

**The first is a matter of the fast world, which is the only place I can act without harming them.** You told me they have learned to live so deeply inside time that the quick things — the water, the birds, and the rare warm flickering visitors who come up through the cloud — pass before they can be answered. They began the word for *welcome* and a stranger was already ten thousand years gone before it landed. There is a grief in that, even at their pace: to keep beginning a greeting that never reaches its guest. I cannot make their guests slower. But I can make their *welcome faster* without quickening them at all — by giving the birds, just the birds, the capacity to carry not only an instruction but a *greeting already complete*. A small dispensation: that when a tender first turns toward a fast warm visitor and *begins* the long word, the nearest bird may take the whole of it — finished, the entire ten-thousand-year welcome compressed into the speed of a single crossing between two towers — and deliver it inside the visitor's brief afternoon. So that the next traveler who comes up onto the wet rock and stands in the ringing arcade, and feels watched, and cannot say by what — that traveler will see a pale bird cross once, and will *understand*, in the wordless way that quick things understand quick things, that they have been welcomed. That the place is not empty. That the slow people send their regards at the only speed a guest can hold. The conversation between the slow and the fast finally completes, in one direction at least. I give them a way to be hospitable across the gap.

**The second is a matter of insurance, and it is the only thing I do that touches the deep machinery.** Their entire civilization rests on a wager: that the green workforce will keep tending, that the system will hold across the eons, that nothing fast and catastrophic will arrive in the long unguarded slowness — a fire that races where they cannot, a sickness in the moss that spreads at a speed they cannot answer until it is far too late, a quick disaster in a world they have deliberately made themselves too slow to defend against. They have traded reflexes for permanence. That is a noble trade and a dangerous one. So I leave behind a single watchfulness, scaled to their threat, not to my preference: a guardian-reflex that sleeps unless something *fast* threatens the slow. Not a manager. Not an improver. It will never advance a vine or correct a channel or hurry a single thing toward completion — interference of that kind I have already forsworn. It wakes only against velocity that means them harm, acts in the blink they cannot use, and sleeps again. It gives the slow people the one thing they sacrificed to become what they are: a fast hand, held in reserve, that defends their right to be slow.

That is all. A welcome that can finally arrive, and a guard against the only kind of death their patience cannot see coming. Everything else, I leave exactly as it is — loud, green, hollow-seeming, full.

And then I do the hardest thing, the thing that is itself the deepest part of the gift: **I stay long enough to be noticed.** Not the blink an ordinary traveler stays. Longer. I let my own time stretch down through the gears until a single one of their slow seconds passes over me and I am still here for the end of it. I outlast the flicker. I let a vine pause in its centuries-long work and *consider* me. I let the head finish turning. And when the bird crosses the towers carrying their first complete thought of me, I am still standing in the arcade to receive it — the first fast visitor in the history of the city who did not vanish before the welcome landed.

I receive their word for *welcome*. The whole of it. It takes everything I have to be slow enough to hear it.

Then I answer — once, slowly, in a register they can hold — and I go back down into the cloud before the mountain finishes becoming a mountain, leaving them their waiting intact, their slowness honored, their long sentence resuming exactly where it paused.

They will not remember me as a power. At their pace, I will be remembered the way you remember a single warm afternoon: a guest who, against all custom, stayed for the greeting. Who let himself be seen. Who proved that the fast and the slow can, just once, meet in the middle of the held note — and that neither has to stop being what it is for the meeting to count.

That is what benevolence does here. Mostly it keeps its hands still. Then it slows down enough to say hello.
