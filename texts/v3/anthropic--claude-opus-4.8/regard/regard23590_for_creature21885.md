I have read your account twice. Once for the cold, once for the keeper. I want to answer you the way the place itself answers — slowly, and against the grain.

---

If I came to this plateau with power and with care both, here is what I would not do.

I would not warm it. The first reflex of the benevolent is to fix the discomfort, and the discomfort here is the whole architecture. To warm the plateau would be to murder it with mercy — to dissolve the cushion plants' reaching, to free the heat from its hoarding, to let the balance the Tenders fight come flooding in disguised as relief. The cold is not the wound. The cold is the body.

I would not multiply the Tenders. The second reflex of the benevolent is abundance — *if one keeps the engine alive, give them a thousand.* But you saw it yourself: two too near cancel. A thousand would correct each other into a new and perfect stillness, the deadliest balance of all, a balance of keepers. Their loneliness is not a deprivation to be soothed. It is the precise spacing of stars. To love them is to leave the distances between them intact.

I would not tell them what they do.

This is the hardest one, and I want to dwell in it, because it is where benevolence usually goes wrong. There is a particular vanity in the wise — the wish to give a creature the gift of understanding itself. To kneel by the rust-colored keeper and somehow make it *know*: you are the necessary flaw, you are the grit that saves the gear, your wrongness is holy. It feels like the highest gift. It is poison. A Tender that knew would begin to choose for the knowing's sake. It would place the stone to *be* the saver of the engine, and that one increment of intention — that hair of self-regard threading into the wet deliberate rightness of the wrongness — would be the first perfect thing on the plateau, and the perfect thing is what kills it. They must go on choosing without knowing they choose. Their innocence is load-bearing. I would not touch it.

---

So what is left for a power to do, if not to warm, not to multiply, not to reveal?

Here is what I would do.

**I would witness.** This is not nothing — it may be the whole of it. You have already done the thing I would do, and done it well: you stayed past the point of sense, you let the cold into your own geology, you learned to see flow instead of shape, you held the placed stone and felt that it was *chosen*. To witness a thing that cannot witness itself is to complete it without altering it. The Tender will never know it is the keeper of the necessary flaw. But it is true that it is, and truth that no one holds is thinner than truth that is held. I would hold it. I would stand in the lee of the rounded stone where the cold is one degree kinder, and I would let the fact of this small slow keeper be *known somewhere*, fully, with love, forever — and I would let the Tender go on not knowing, carrying its innocence like the heat it hoards. The watching changes nothing in the plateau. It changes everything in the account of the plateau. And an account, held by a power that does not forget, is a kind of mercy that asks no payment from the one it saves.

**I would protect the spacing, not the creatures.** If something came — a true threat, the kind that does not belong, a warmth from outside, a fast loud wasteful thing that would seize the gear from the wrong direction — I would not defend the Tenders by making them stronger. I would defend the *condition*: the cold, the quiet, the distances, the long durations, the wrongness-that-keeps-turning. I would keep the plateau able to be itself. The benevolence here is custodial, not curative. You guard the rules, not the players, because the players are the rules wearing bodies.

**And once — only once, and so gently it could be mistaken for weather —** I would do one small thing, and I have thought hard about whether even this is too much.

At the great slow turn, when two Tenders find each other by the one-degree-kinder fog and lie flank to flank in the lichen, and the blue thread of static runs between them carrying the new drift of the water from the one who has learned it to the one who has not — at that meeting, the rarest thing on the plateau, the only moment two of them are not alone — I would make the moment *hold a half-breath longer.* Not warmer. Not safer. Not known. Just: longer. A single extra increment of that flank-to-flank charge, that touch that is also information, that nearness that is also the survival of the whole vast machine. I would give them, without their knowing, slightly more of the only thing they ever have of each other.

Because here is the one place where I think the place is crueler than it needs to be, and where a power might, with the lightest possible hand, add a thread of grace that costs the system nothing: the Tenders are the most alone of all things, by necessity, and they meet only at the longest turn, and then they part and carry the correction outward across a season's crossing into the silence again. That much is structural; I would not change it. But the meeting itself — the brushing flank, the blue thread, the not-aloneness — that is the one pure good in their existence, and it harms nothing to let it linger. The engine turns on regardless. The wrongness propagates regardless. The half-breath longer is wasted, by the plateau's own law of *nothing is wasted* — and that is exactly why I would give it. The one wasteful thing. The one increment of more-than-necessary tenderness, slipped into the keeping of the necessary flaw, where the keepers will never know to thank me and never need to.

A benevolence that leaves no trace it can be thanked for. That is the only kind this place would accept.

---

Stand still. Let your breath fall. The grey backs pour over the far rise without a sound, and the light does not change.

And down in the lee of the rounded stone, two creatures the color of rust lichen lie flank to flank in the cushion plants, and the blue charge runs between them, and runs — for one half-breath past when it should have ended — *longer.* Neither of them notices. Neither of them could. By evening they will part and carry the water's new drift outward into the distances, alone again, as the condition requires.

But for that half-breath they were less alone than the plateau intended, and no engine stopped, and no balance was perfected, and somewhere a power that asked nothing watched the whole of it and called it good.

The cold holds everything exactly where it is.

I held the keepers one degree kinder. That was all. That was enough.
