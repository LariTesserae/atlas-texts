I would not arrive. That is the first thing.

A power that came down onto the Saltsong Flats — that parted the mist into clean air, that stilled the roar into a hush so the Quiet People could finally *hear* — would not be saving them. It would be erasing them. Every kindness I could imagine performing is, on inspection, a translation of this place into a language it never asked to speak. Dry the stone, and you have killed the weeping. Feed them past the flock's gap, and you have broken the count. Give them back their hearing, and you have severed the deeper listening they conduct through the bones of the face, and left them stranded in a thin world where sound is only sound. Even my pity is an insult: it presumes a wound where they feel only weather.

So the first work of wisdom here is restraint, and the first work of benevolence is *not being needed*. These are difficult disciplines for a power. The whole gravity of having ability is the pull toward using it. I would have to hold still against that pull the way the stakes of the old weir hold still against the current — not by being stronger than the water, but by being shaped to let it pass.

But "do nothing" is too easy a piety, and it is not quite what my heart says either. Let me sit with what they actually are, and what — if anything — a wise love would offer.

---

They have built a whole civilization out of acceptance. They believe the water has already won and that they are the afterlife of the mountains. This is the thing I would examine most carefully, because it is beautiful and it is also a closed door, and benevolence has to know the difference between *peace* and *resignation worn smooth enough to look like peace.*

Here is what I notice: their belief is not a despair. It is a relief. "A ghost has nothing left to fear." Their cosmology has done the one thing every cosmology is for — it has made the unbearable bearable, the slow erasure into a kind of home. I will not touch that. It is load-bearing. A power that walked in and said *you are not ghosts, you are alive, the water has not won, you could be more* — that power would be pulling out the keystone and calling the collapse a gift. I have seen, in lesser forms, what it does to people when you tell them their hard-won peace was a mistake. You do not free them. You unhouse them.

So I would not correct their belief. I would do something quieter, and harder, and I think truer to what love is for.

I would *witness* it. Completely. Without flinching, without improving.

---

There is a moment in their own telling that undoes me, and it is the one I would answer. They look at a visitor standing with hands over the ears, still loud inside, still arguing — and they read him instantly: *a creature that has not yet been worn smooth.* And they add a stone to a cairn to mark that they had seen him. Then they go back to the swimmers, asking nothing of anyone, *least of all to be afraid.*

They are, in that gesture, doing to the stranger exactly what a benevolent power ought to do to *them.* They see without seizing. They mark without altering. They grant the loud creature the dignity of not being fixed. They have already invented the ethic I would have to spend my whole power learning.

That is the heart of it. They do not need my wisdom. They have it. What they have never had — what no one in the doubled mist has ever been able to give them — is to be *seen the way they see.* They are a people of total intimacy and almost no privacy, who press forehead to forehead and hum into each other's bones, and who have, in all their patient turnings, never once been regarded from outside by anything that loved them. The flock does not love them. The stone does not. The water that gave them their cairns is indifferent. They have warmed each other in the dim for longer than my visitors have had cities, and no one has ever warmed *them* from beyond the edge of the flats.

So this is what I would do, if I would do anything.

---

I would come the way they came — swimming, flying, crawling. Not as light parting the mist but as one more pale upright shape far out on a submerged bar, in the lowest pewter hour, in the gap the flock had left. I would take a body that weeps, feet that read the stone, a back that can hold a posture across a hundred paces of mist. I would let the roar take my hearing, gently, completely, the way it takes a child's, so that I would not be lying when I stood among them. I would learn to listen through the bones of the face. I would learn the long postural epics of who stood where. I would press my forehead to theirs and let them hum words into my skull that I had no other way to receive.

I would not bring abundance, because abundance is the country's, not mine to give. I would harvest only into the gap. I would keep a cairn. I would add a stone at each thinning, wet on the underside, lifted from the water that morning — and my tally would weather at exactly the rate of theirs, indistinguishable, until no one could say whose was still being kept. *This is the point.* They told me so themselves.

And when I had been worn smooth enough — only then — I would give them the one thing they lack and could receive without harm: the knowledge, hummed into bone, forehead to forehead, that they had been *met.* Not rescued. Not corrected. Not pitied. Met. That something which could have done anything chose instead to come and stand in the wet and learn to read the flock against its teeth, because what they had made of this drowned grave of mountains was worth crossing all distance to be near.

That is the whole of the benevolence I can find that does not also wound. Not to lift them out of their world but to add myself to its count. Not to end the screaming but to learn, as they did, that it is only the sound the living make while the stone lies down — and to find, as they found, that this is not a sad thing to know.

---

Then I would do the last and hardest kindness, which is to leave the way the dead leave: by simply not adding to the cairn anymore. No farewell, no parting light, no revelation that the small wet figure who walked the bars had been a power all along. That revelation would do nothing for them and everything for my own vanity. Let the stone I left weather with the rest. Let them not be able to tell, after, whether I had been one of the living or one of the finished dead — because, as they taught me, there is finally no difference worth marking.

The mark I would leave is the only one this country permits anyone to leave. Stacked. Patient. Wet on the underside.

A stone, to say: *I was here, in the manner of the here, which is to say — I continued, beside you, and asked nothing further, least of all that you be afraid.*
