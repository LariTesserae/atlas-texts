# The Dry Canopy

Begin high. Begin where the air thins to a clean knife-edge and the light comes down without water to soften it — no haze, no mist, no humidity to scatter the sun into gold. Here the light is white and absolute, falling through nothing, landing on everything with the flat clarity of a thing seen too closely.

There is no water here. Not in the way you mean. No rivers cut these slopes, no rain pools in the rock hollows, no clouds gather to break. And yet — this is the first strangeness — the place is lush.

---

The vegetation lives on something other than rain.

These are the **dry forests of the upper shelves**, and the plants here have made their peace with a world without flow. They are pale, most of them — the silver-green of plants that hoard rather than spend. Broad waxen leaves that do not transpire so much as breathe, drawing moisture not from soil but from the difference between day and night, from the cold that settles into their tissues at dark and condenses, invisibly, inside them. Each plant is its own small economy of dew that never reaches the air.

The forest grows sparse. This matters. It is not a jungle crowded leaf-on-leaf, fighting for sun. There is space between every living thing — wide aprons of pale stone between one stand of growth and the next, the way an old garden gone wild still keeps the bones of its emptiness. A tree, then forty meters of bare weathered rock, then a low spreading mat of blue-grey succulents, then more emptiness. The plants do not crowd because crowding wastes the cold. Each one needs its own night, its own undisturbed darkness to make water out of temperature, and so they hold themselves apart with the formality of monks.

The cool sits in everything. Not cold — cool. The temperature of stone in shade at altitude. It does not bite; it simply withholds warmth, and the plants are built for it, and the few animals are built for it, and you, walking, would feel the sun on your skin and the chill beneath it at once, the two never quite resolving.

---

The land itself is **old past reckoning**.

Whatever made these heights — uplift, ancient fracture, the slow buckling of something continental — finished its work an age ago, and since then the only sculptor has been weathering. Not water-weathering. Wind, frost, the patient flaking of stone that heats and cools and heats again across millions of mornings. The result is a terrain of *settled forms*: nothing sharp remains. The peaks are rounded shoulders. The cliffs have stepped back into terraces, shelf below shelf below shelf, each one softened, each one carpeted thinly with the sparse pale forest, so that from a height the whole massif looks like a vast staircase descending into more staircases, draped in silver-green, going down farther than the eye resolves.

The rock is layered, and the layers tell the story: bands of cream, bands of rust, bands of a soft violet-grey, all of them rounded at every edge. Run your hand along an outcrop and it gives a faint chalk to your fingers — the surface is always very slowly returning to dust. This is a landscape in balance. It loses a little stone to wind each year and grows a little life to hold the rest. The two have agreed.

---

And the **scale** is the thing that finally makes the mind let go.

This is not a mountain. It is a *region* of altitude, large enough to make its own weather, and the weather it makes — having no water — is strange. Across the vast terraced country there move slow rivers of temperature instead of air: warm tides that pool in the low shelves by day and pour, invisibly, down the staircases at night as cold drainage, sighing through the forest stands and feeding them. You can stand at the lip of a terrace at dusk and feel the cold begin to flow past your ankles, downhill, soundless, going to fill the basins far below where the densest growth waits to drink it.

There is wind, too — vast and unhurried, the breath of a body too large to hurry. It crosses the shelves in long passes that take hours to arrive and hours to leave. When it comes through the spaced trees it does not roar. The forest is too open to roar. It makes instead a low dry sibilance, the sound of many waxen leaves turning, like sand poured very slowly, like the held breath of a great patient animal. This is most of the sound there is.

---

For the rest, it is **quiet**.

A silence so deep it has texture. The plants do not rustle much, being stiff. There is no water to trickle or drip or rush. When an animal moves — and animals are rare here, scattered as the trees are scattered — you hear it from a long way off: a single clatter of dislodged stone, a dry footfall, then nothing for a long time again.

The fauna are few and far between, and they are creatures of the cool and the dry. Pale browsers with long necks that crop the moisture-rich leaves and never need to drink, getting all their water from the plants that made it from the night. They move slowly, in ones and twos, vast distances apart, and they make almost no sound — soft-footed, padded, evolved for a world where noise carries too far and announces you to whatever hunts. And something does hunt, somewhere: you will find, on a high shelf, the clean weathered bones of a browser, picked and bleached and already half-returned to the chalk of the rock, with no print or sign around them to say what came. The predators here are rarer even than the prey, and quieter, and you will likely never see one. The ecosystem turns over slowly, on a scale of decades, a patient predation across enormous distances — but it *does* turn. Things are born and grow and are eaten and weather into stone. The place is alive and in motion; it simply moves at the pace of the mountain.

---

Nothing is built. No wall, no path, no mark of any hand. The terraces look made — they have the regularity of architecture — but no one made them; they are only the patience of rock revealing its layers. There are no tools, no devices, no fire. The only intelligence here is the slow distributed cleverness of life solving the problem of thirst without water, again and again, in every silver leaf.

---

Stand here at midday. The white light comes straight down through dry air onto pale forest and rounded rust-and-cream stone, staircase below staircase below staircase, fading into distance with no haze to hide the farthest shelves — so that you can see, impossibly far, the whole architecture of the high dry country laid out clean to the curve of the horizon. The cold sits under the brightness. A long breath of wind arrives, turns ten thousand waxen leaves with a sound like slow sand, and moves on. Somewhere, a stone falls. Then the silence comes back and fills everything, patient and enormous, the way it has for the entire age since the water left and the forest learned to make its own.
