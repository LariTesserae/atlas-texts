There is a hollow in the low country where the water has decided to stay.

You come to it through ground that is already soft, already giving, the kind of warm wet earth that holds the shape of your foot for a moment and then lets it go. The land does not rise here. It barely rises anywhere. What height there is comes from the swelling of waterlogged ground, a few hand-spans of mounding where minerals have built up under the surface seep — limestone, mostly, gone soft and pale as old soap.

The hollow is small. You could walk its rim in the time it takes to grow restless. That is part of what it is: a bounded thing, a single feature, a basin no larger than a meeting-hall, and yet it is so full of itself that smallness is not the word that stays with you. It is packed. Every surface is occupied, every fold of stone holds water, and the water holds light, and the light holds the slow steam that rises off the warm pools. There is no empty space in the hollow. The air itself is crowded — thick, humid, beaded, a held breath that never quite releases.

Almost nothing grows here. This is not a green place. The walls of the basin are wet rock — travertine in tiers, dripstone, flowstone, mineral laid down drop by drop over a span of time that the eye cannot hold. Here and there a smear of something living clings to the warm seeps: not plants, only the faintest films, rust-orange and sulphur-yellow and a deep iron purple, the simplest of life painting the stone where the warm water comes up. These are not gardens. They are stains that happen to be alive. They do not move, do not reach, do not seed. They have been the same color in the same places longer than there have been eyes to notice.

The water is the whole of it. It weeps from every seam. It pools in the terraced basins the rock has built for itself, each pool brimming and feeding the one below in a thread so thin and steady it makes no splash, only a sustained trickle that has gone on so long it has stopped being a sound and become the silence's texture. The pools are warm — bath-warm, blood-warm in the deeper cups where the earth's own heat rises. Steam stands over them in still ropes. When the dim light catches the steam it goes the color of pearl.

It is warm here, evenly, in every corner. No cold pockets, no hot ones. The warmth comes up from below and the wet holds it close and nothing carries it away. You sweat without effort. The stone is warm to the touch, faintly slick, smoothed by the constant water into surfaces like the inside of a shell.

The light is low. There must be a sky somewhere above — a pale lid of cloud, perhaps, the hollow's own breath gathered overhead — but no direct sun reaches the basin floor. Everything is lit by a diffuse grey-gold glow that does not seem to come from any direction. Shadows are soft and few. You cannot tell the hour. You suspect the hour does not change much, that this is simply the light the place keeps.

And it is quiet. Not silent — the trickle is everywhere, and now and then a single drop falls from a high overhang into a pool with a clean, round note, a sound like a struck bowl, spaced so far apart that you wait for the next one without knowing you are waiting. Otherwise nothing. No wind reaches in. No leaves to stir if there were. The quiet is the deep quiet of a place that has finished happening.

Life passes through rarely. A pale amphibian, eyeless or nearly so, may rest on a wet ledge with its throat pulsing, then be gone the next time you look. Small soft things move in the warm water — blind, unhurried. They are visitors more than residents. The hollow does not feed a great population; it only tolerates a few, and those few have grown still and patient to match it.

There are signs that people came here, once, and not many of them, and not often. On the broadest terrace someone set flat stones into a rough floor, long ago, and the flowstone has half-swallowed them now, mineral creeping over their edges like a slow tide turned to rock. There is the stump of a low wall — dry-laid, no mortar, the work of hands and not of machines. A niche cut into the soft stone, with the ghost of a worn step below it, as if this were a place to come and sit, or kneel, or wash. A few clay shards near the water, the lip of a vessel, simple and unglazed, the kind of thing shaped on a turning board and fired in an open pit. Whoever made these knew metal barely or not at all. They came with their hands and a few tools and their patience, and they left, and the place absorbed the mark of them as gently as it absorbs everything.

Nothing changes here. This is the truest thing about the hollow, truer than its warmth or its wetness. It is at rest. The water that built the terraces is still building them, but so slowly that across a human life the rock seems fixed. The pools are always full and never overflow. The steam always stands. The stains keep their colors. The drop falls and falls and falls from the overhang at its own unbroken interval. There is no season here, no flood and no drought, no morning and no dusk that you could name — only the long, even, breathing equilibrium of a place that arrived at what it is a very long time ago and has been content ever since.

You can stay as long as you like. Nothing will hurry you, and nothing will happen. When you leave, the foot-shape you pressed into the soft rim will have already filled with seeping water, and in time the lime will quiet it over, and the hollow will be exactly as it was — warm, wet, dim, full, and waiting for no one.
