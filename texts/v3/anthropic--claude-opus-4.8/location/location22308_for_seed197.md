There is a city the size of a single room.

I mean this literally, so let me hold it carefully before it slips. The place has boundaries you could pace in nine strides — a hollow grown into the base of something that was once a tree and is now closer to a cathedral made of one continuous plant. The walls are bark and the bark is also masonry; there are courses in it, regular as brickwork, laid down by no hands but laid down nonetheless, ring upon ring, the established weathering of a thing that has stood so long it learned to imitate the works of builders. Stairs spiral where the grain decided to spiral. Doorways arch where the wood thinned. Nobody built this. It built itself toward the idea of being built.

The whole interior is plant. Not plant *growing in* a space — plant *as* the space. Floor is a packed living mat that gives slightly underfoot and breathes back up against your soles. The pillars are stems thick as silos. The ceiling is a canopy so dense and so low it functions as a roof, and from it hang structures that resolve, the longer you look, into balconies, into lintels, into the corbels of an architecture no one designed but everyone could navigate. Town-scale construction folded into grove-scale space. A hamlet pressed into a closet.

And it is nearly empty.

This is the thing hardest to say. The room-city is built for thousands and holds almost nothing. Vast distances open between one presence and the next — though "vast" inside nine strides is itself a contradiction I am asked to honor, so honor it: the space between two points here is longer than the space that contains it. You walk toward the far wall and the walk takes a long time. The emptiness is not absence of stuff; it is abundance of *between*. Hollowness as a positive quantity. You feel the near-void the way you feel a held breath in a body — it is doing something.

It is warm. Damp-warm, glasshouse warm. Moisture beads on the inner bark and runs in slow threads and drips, and there are pockets where water has gathered into small still mirrors no bigger than a cupped palm, and these pools do not always reflect what is above them. Rain comes intermittently from the canopy-ceiling — the plant sweats, condenses, releases — a soft interior weather with its own cycle, building and breaking across what might be an hour. The light is ordinary. Daylight, even, though there is no sky; the light simply *is the right amount*, normal and unremarkable, which inside a sealed room of living wood is the strangest thing of all and I keep almost not noticing it, which I think is the point.

Now the sound.

The sound is total. It is the loudest thing I have ever been asked to describe and it never relents. The plant is *deafening* — sap moving through trunks the width of towers roars like rivers in flood; the canopy overhead grinds and creaks at a volume that is physical, that presses on the chest; every drip of condensation lands like a struck bell and there are thousands of drips. Beneath it, the fauna — abundant, abundant — and here is where the emptiness and the abundance go to war and both win. The animals are everywhere in the *sound* and almost nowhere in the *space*. The room teems sonically with creatures you cannot find: chittering, lowing, the wet click of a thousand mandibles, wingbeat, birdcall, the breathing of something large very close to your ear — and you turn and there is nothing, there is the long emptiness, there is one slow beetle the size of a thumb crossing a leaf forty strides off. The fauna lives in the acoustics. The bodies are scarce and the *evidence* of bodies is overwhelming. You are alone in a screaming crowd.

This is where the reality begins to fail, and it fails generously, the way fruit fails into ripeness.

The distances will not stay decided. Walk toward the beetle and you may arrive in three steps or three hundred and the room neither shrinks nor grows; it is *both sizes at once* and waits to see which one you'll commit to. The water in the small pools shows you the canopy, then shows you a different canopy, then shows you the underside of the floor you're standing on, as though the room were folded through itself and the pools are the creases. Sound arrives before its source moves and after its source has gone. A dripping you hear now will land, visibly, a few seconds later — the bell rings and *then* the water falls into it.

And the plant is one plant. I keep returning to this because it organizes everything. The pillars, the floor, the roof, the spiral stairs, the balconies, the brick-grain bark — one organism, established, in balance with itself, weathered into the shape of a settlement and continuing to grow with the unhurried violence of growing things. It is active. It moves while you watch. A stair completes a turn it had not finished. A doorway closes by a finger's width. The town is alive and the life is slow and enormous and indifferent, and the emptiness inside it is the breath of something that breathes once a century.

There are no tools here. Nothing has been made. And yet everything has the look of having been made, which is the deepest weirdness, deeper than the folding pools: a place that arrived at *civilization* by purely growing, that grew streets and rooms and the felt-sense of habitation without ever knowing a hand. You stand in the made-unmade, in the crowded-empty, in the deafening hollow, and the warm rain falls upward into the bells, and you understand, briefly, that you are inside one room of a house with no other rooms, and that the house is a seed, and that it has not yet decided what to become.

It is taking its time.

So should you.
