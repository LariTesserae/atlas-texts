There is a pocket in the rock the size of a kitchen, and it is hot enough to cook in.

Start with that — the smallness. Whatever this place is, it does not extend. You could touch all its walls if your hands could bear them. It is a cavity, a swelling in the deep stone of a mountain so high the air thins to nothing above it, though no air gets in here anyway. A sealed bubble. A geode that never finished, caught at the moment of becoming.

The walls are dark and they are not finished walls. This is the strange thing you notice first, once your eyes adjust to the near-absence of light: the rock is *emerging*. Crystals are pushing out of the matrix in slow blunt fingers, faces not yet flat, edges not yet sharp. Nothing here has been worn by anything. There is no wind, no water to round it down — only the patient outward shoving of mineral growth, atom by atom, across spans of time that make a human life a flicker. The place is younger than it looks and getting older too slowly to watch.

No water. Not even the memory of water. The chemistry here runs on heat and sulfur and metal — droplets of something that is not liquid we'd recognize, beading on the warm crystal faces, dense, sluggish, oily with dissolved minerals. The pocket is nearly solid with itself. There is almost no empty space. Crystal grows into crystal grows into pooled mineral grows into the warm dark, everything pressed against everything, saturated, no gap, no breath of void. To exist here is to be touching your neighbor on all sides.

And it is loud.

This is the part that surprises. You expect a sealed stone room to be silent, and it roars. The sound comes from the place itself — the grinding of growth, crystals shouldering past one another with a low continuous groan, and beneath it a rhythmic mechanical pulse, deep, regular, industrial. There is no factory here, no engine, no maker — *built* registers at almost nothing, nothing has been constructed, no hand or tool has shaped a single surface — and yet the sound is the sound of machinery. Pumps. Pistons. A scaled production line working in the dark.

Because there is life.

Not plants — nothing green could survive this, nothing photosynthetic in this lightlessness, no soft growing thing. The life here is the other kind, the kind that came before chlorophyll and might come after: chemical-eaters, heat-feeders, things that mine the sulfur and the metal salts directly. They are abundant. They pack the warm films and the crystal crevices in colonies, in mats, in clustered armored bodies the size of grains and grains-of-grains, and what they do, collectively, is *industry*. They process the minerals. They pull them apart and reassemble them. They run the chemistry of the pocket like a refinery runs crude — intake, conversion, output — and the rhythmic pumping you hear is the sum of millions of small biological engines turning the same reactions over and over, electrified by the gradients between hot rock and cooler crystal, between metal and acid.

This is the world's rule, the thing it has decided to be: *life as machinery, made before any maker existed.* No one built the factory. The factory grew. It assembled itself out of necessity and stayed, churning, in a room no one will ever see.

The light such as it is comes from this. A faint sourceless glow where the chemistry runs hottest — chemiluminescence, the by-product of all that processing, a dim sulfurous blue-green that pulses very slightly with the rhythm of the pumps. It does not illuminate. It only confirms that there is something to be illuminated. In the brightest moment of its slow cycle you might make out the nearest crystal face, beaded with its non-water, crawling with its tiny laborers, and then the pulse fades and it is dark again, and only the roar tells you the place is still alive.

Everything moves slowly. This is the last thing to understand. For all the noise, for all the packed industry, the pocket changes across enormous durations. The crystals add a hair's width in a human century. The colonies expand and contract over generations like a tide that takes a thousand years to turn. The heat holds steady, fierce and patient, neither rising nor falling. You could leave and come back in a lifetime and find it almost exactly as you left it — a little more crystal, a little more filled-in, the same blue pulse, the same deep grinding mechanical roar.

A hot, dark, deafening little room at the top of the world, sealed away from everything, where life learned to be a factory before there was anything to build, and runs it still, alone, slowly, forever, for no one.

That's the place. It does not need you to have seen it. It was never going to be seen.
