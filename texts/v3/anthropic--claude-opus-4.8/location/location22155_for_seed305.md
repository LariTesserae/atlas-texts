# The Loom Plateau

Start with the cold. Not the cold of wind — there is little wind here — but the cold of altitude, the thin clean cold that arrives in the bones before the lungs notice the air is sparse. You are very high. High enough that the light comes down hard and unfiltered, a flooding brilliance that does not warm. The sky is a white-blue so intense it seems to press. Everything throws a sharp shadow, and the shadows are the only dark things in this place.

The land is young. It has the look of something recently broken and not yet worn — ridges with edges still keen, fresh black stone cracked open into facets that catch the light like wet glass even when dry. There has been no time for softening. The slopes are angular, the valleys V-cut and narrow, and where water has begun its work the channels are shallow scratches rather than gorges. This is a landscape in its first morning.

And it is *vast*. Not large — vast in the way that becomes its own weather. You can stand on one high spur and watch a different climate happen forty kilometers off: a column of condensation rising and curling back on itself, a slow drift of mist pooling in a basin that will never reach you. The plateau makes its own seasons in its own pockets. The horizon does not end so much as fade, ridge behind ridge behind ridge, paling into the brilliant air until the last ranges are only a suggestion of edges.

---

Now the strangeness, because it cannot wait.

The plateau is *packed*. There is no empty ground. Every surface, every facet of fresh stone, every fissure and ledge, is occupied — and the thing that occupies it is alive. The vegetation here is not soil-rooted in the ordinary way. It is a single continuous fur of growth, a moss-that-is-not-moss, deep green going to blue-black in the shadows, and it covers the young stone like a pelt drawn tight over bone. Where two ridges meet, the growth is so dense it forms a seam, a piling fold of itself, and in those folds the cold condenses into water — beads of it, runs of it, the only water the plateau reliably keeps. Damp lives in the seams. The high faces stay dry and bright; the moisture hides in the creases of the world.

The growth breathes. Slowly. If you sit still — and you will have to sit still, because the air is thin and you tire — you will see the whole pelt rise and settle across an hour, the way a sleeping animal's flank moves but stretched across a duration that is almost geological. This is the *slow* of the place: everything here changes, but on the wrong timescale. The mist takes a day to cross a valley. The light shifts its angle but never its intensity. The plateau is patient to the point of seeming dead, and it is the opposite of dead — it is so saturated with life that the life has had to slow down to fit.

---

The fauna is the overwhelming thing.

I will try to be precise, because vagueness would betray it. The animals here are small and there are too many of them. The dominant kind is something like a beetle the size of a thumbnail, iridescent, and it lives *in* the green pelt — billions upon billions of them, one continuous population spread across the entire plateau, so that the ground itself shimmers and shifts with a low constant motion you mistake at first for heat-haze. They are not heat. They are appetite. They graze the green fur and are grazed in turn by larger things — long pale creatures that swim through the pelt like fish through shallows, breaking the surface and submerging, leaving wakes.

And the beetles make sound. This is where the *loud* comes from. Each one is nearly silent, but a billion of them, scraping and clicking and shearing the growth, produce a sound that has no source and no gap — a vast dry roar, a sustained sustained *shhhhhhh* threaded with higher chitterings, that fills the whole sky-bowl of the plateau and never once stops. You stop hearing it after an hour the way you stop hearing your own blood. Then a creature surfaces nearby, or a cloud-pocket of beetles lifts off in alarm, and the roar swells and you remember: this place is screaming, gently, forever.

---

Here is the part that took me longest to see, walking the seams.

The beetles are *industrial*. I do not mean they resemble industry. I mean they do it. In the deep folds where the damp collects, the beetles aggregate into structures — not nests, not hives, but **engines**. They lock their iridescent backs together into rotating wheels, hundreds of them forming a disc that turns slowly, driven by the ones underneath climbing endlessly upward. The wheels drive shafts of compacted bodies. The shafts turn other wheels. Heat rises off these assemblies in shimmer. They are pumping the condensed water out of the seams and up into the high dry growth, irrigating the pelt that feeds them — a circulatory machine made entirely of living parts, mechanized, electrified even, because where the bodies grind against the fresh stone they throw small sparks and the air above the engines smells of ozone and crushed mineral.

No one built this. *Built* is nearly absent here — there is not a single made thing, no wall, no tool, no shaped object in the human sense. And yet there is machinery everywhere, scaled to the continent, factories of flesh turning in every fold of every valley, mass-producing nothing but the conditions of their own continuance. The plateau is wholly untouched and wholly mechanized at once. It manufactures only itself, slowly, in the cold bright air, with no purpose anyone could name and no rest.

---

The rules of this world, as best I can give them:

Nothing is empty and nothing is fast. Everything is occupied, everything is patient. Water is the rarest thing and lives only in the creases, and the engines exist to move it, and the green exists to be moved-toward, and the beetles exist to be the engines and the eaters and the eaten all together. The light never dims and the cold never lifts and the sound never stops. Above it all the mist makes its slow continental migrations, the only thing that looks like weather, and even it moves like something dreaming.

If you stayed, you would become a feature. The pelt would grow over your stilled feet within a day. The beetles would route a small engine through the warmth of you. You would be irrigated. In a hundred years your shape would be a soft rise in the green, breathing on the plateau's long slow rhythm, screaming faintly with the rest of it, bright and cold and full.

That is the place. It does not want you. It does not refuse you. It simply has no room — and makes room, the way it makes everything: slowly, and out of bodies.
