# The Sump Works

Start at the water, because everything here starts at the water.

It comes up from below. Not rain — though the air is thick enough to be called weather, and the iron undersides of the catwalks weep steadily into the channels — but groundwater, pressing up through a floor that was never properly a floor. The whole district sits maybe a meter above the table, and the table keeps reaching. You can see it in the way the brick sweats, in the green-black tidemarks that climb every wall to the same patient height and stop, in the standing sheets between the foundations that never fully drain and never quite become ponds. The place is a town that decided to be built in a marsh and the marsh has been winning, very slowly, for a long time.

It is not large. You could cross it in twenty minutes if the walkways let you, which they don't. Call it a few hundred meters on a side — a single low basin ringed by spoil-banks and the backs of taller structures, so that no matter where you stand the horizon is close and built and dim. A room the size of a hamlet, roofed by its own steam.

## The Construction

Everything is layered. This is the first thing the eye understands: nothing here was built once. The brick rests on older brick, which rests on timber pilings gone soft and black, which rest on something none of the present residents remember sinking. Pipework crosses pipework. A cast-iron main as thick as a person's body runs along one wall, and clamped to it, decade by decade, are smaller pipes, conduits, brackets that once held lamps and now hold only the green felt of moss. The town grew the way coral grows — accretively, each generation cementing itself to the carcass of the last.

The dominant material is iron in two states: the dry kind, painted over and over in lead colors that have all darkened to the same bruise; and the wet kind, the rust that blooms wherever water and air meet, which is everywhere, so the rust is everywhere — not as decay exactly but as a slow mineral flowering, ochre and umber and a deep liver-red, crusting the rivet-heads, hanging in stalactite threads from the catwalk seams. Underfoot and along the channels, the brick has gone the colors that brick goes when it is permanently damp and warm: liverwort green, the soft luminous green of algae in still water, the black of the truly soaked.

It is warm. Not hot — there's no scorch in the air — but a close, blood-temperature warmth that comes partly from the climate and partly from the machines, and the warmth and the wet together mean the air is always just at the edge of becoming visible. It hangs. It beads on the back of your neck. Breathe and you taste iron and chlorophyll and the faint sulfur of water that has been underground.

## The Machines

Here is the strangeness, and it took me a while writing this to see it clearly, so let me arrive at it the way you'd arrive at it walking in.

The machines are running. You hear them before anything — that's the loudness of the place, a sound that fills the whole basin: the wet percussive thud of great reciprocating pumps, slow, maybe one stroke every few seconds, so the whole district has a pulse. Under that, the harmonic drone of flywheels, the hiss of steam relieving itself through valves, the trickle and gush and slap of moved water finding its level again. The sound is industrial and it is enormous and it never stops and it is, somehow, calming, the way a heartbeat heard through a wall is calming.

The machines are pumps. The entire industry of this place, its only industry, is the moving of water. The pumps lift the groundwater out of the sumps and push it through the iron mains to be discharged somewhere up over the spoil-banks, and the water sinks back down through the marsh, and comes up again into the sumps, and is pumped again. It is a closed loop. The town's whole industrial might — its engines, its electrified arc-lamps that make the dim brown glow, its scaled and organized production — is devoted entirely to lifting water that immediately returns.

And here is the thing I didn't expect to write: **there is no one.** Not a soul. The fauna reading is almost zero and it turns out to be honest about more than animals — there are no people either, no birds, no rats in the pipes, no flies in the warm wet air where flies should swarm. The catwalks have railings worn smooth by hands and the hands are gone. The lamps are lit and no one lit them. The pumps run and no one feeds them. The whole machinery of a working town labors on, perfectly maintained by its own running, in total absence of anyone to work for.

## The Slow Life

But it is not lifeless. The vegetation reading is high and it means it.

Without animals, without people, the plants have inherited the works. Not trees — the basin is too low, too small, too perpetually flooded for trees — but everything below trees: a dense, layered, near-touching saturation of green. Liverworts and mosses sheet every horizontal iron surface in cushions thick enough to sink a hand into. Ferns uncoil from every joint and crevice where rust has made a soil. Reeds and horsetails — the horsetails especially, those ancient jointed stems, which belong to a world before flowers and before animals and seem to know it — stand in every channel, so dense the water moves through them as through a comb. Algae greens the standing sheets to opacity. Things bloom that I don't have names for: pale fungal shelves up the damp brick, threadlike growths bridging pipe to pipe like the place is knitting itself shut.

This is the dynamic of the place — slow. Everything that changes here changes across long durations. The plants advance a finger-width a season. The rust flowers a millimeter a decade. The water cycles on its patient few-second pulse but the town itself is in balance, established, long-settled into being exactly what it is: a green wet engine-room with no engineer, pumping its single inexhaustible substance around and around, growing slowly furrier, slowly more itself.

## The Rules

I think the rule of this world is conservation. Nothing leaves. The water that's pumped out comes back. The iron that rusts does not crumble away but only changes color and texture in place. The plants take in only light and water and the warm air and give back only more of themselves. There is no consumption here because there are no consumers — only the long, balanced, animal-less cycling of matter through forms.

And the machines must be part of that cycle, somehow, though I can't see how. Maybe the warmth they throw is what the ferns grow toward. Maybe the lamplight they make is the only sun this dim basin gets, and the plants drink it, and that is why the lamps were built, or that is why the lamps stay lit — not for absent people but for the green that has replaced them. Maybe the pumps don't move water *for* any reason at all anymore. Maybe they just are the way this place breathes, and have been breathing since long before the last hand left the railing, and will go on, thud and hiss and trickle, one slow stroke at a time, in the warm wet dark, getting greener.

Stand in it long enough and the pulse gets into your chest and you stop expecting anyone to come. The place isn't waiting. It was finished a long time ago. This is just what it does now: it keeps the water moving and it keeps the green fed and it asks nothing of anyone, and it makes, forever, that enormous patient sound.
