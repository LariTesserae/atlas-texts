# The Loud Green

There is a city here, but no one built it. Or rather — the building never stopped, and there were never any hands.

## What you stand on

The ground is not ground. It is the dense, woven mass of a thousand generations of stem and runner and root, compacted into something that bears weight the way a mattress bears weight — it gives, it remembers, it pushes back. Walk and the surface flexes; behind you it slowly fills back in the dents your steps made, a green wound healing in real time. There is no soil beneath. There is more plant, and beneath that, more, all the way down past where anyone has dug, which is not far, because the digging closes.

It is warm down at this low elevation — the warmth of a packed greenhouse, of breath held in a fist. Sweat starts immediately and never finishes. The air is thick enough to lean on.

## The architecture that grows

The city is towers, and the towers are alive.

They rise as stems do, but stems the width of grain silos, fluted and fibrous, sheathed in bark that is really layered leaf, and they organize themselves — this is the part that refuses to make sense — into streets. There are colonnades of trunk spaced like the columns of a forum. There are arches where two growths met in the air and fused. There are whole districts of dwelling-shaped hollows, swellings in the vegetable mass that open into rooms, with sills and lintels and the suggestion of doorways, none of it carved, all of it *grown into being* as if the plants remembered a city they had never seen and were trying, urgently, to become one.

And they are *young*. Everything is fresh-edged, recently extruded, the cut-green smell of a snapped stalk hanging everywhere. The sharp features of new formation: ridges not yet worn, points still pointed, the architecture brand-new every hour because every hour it is remade.

## The turbulence

This is the thing you must understand before anything else: it does not hold still.

The growth here moves at the speed of weather. A boulevard of trunks that stood an arm's-length apart at dawn has, by what would be noon, closed into a solid wall, the gaps grown shut, and a new boulevard has torn itself open forty feet away with the violence of a glacier calving. Towers buckle under their own racing mass and fall — slowly, hugely — and where they fall, three more shoulder up through the wreckage, already flowering, already fruiting, already rotting and feeding the next.

Tendrils whip across openings and seal them. Vines unspool from the canopy at the rate of a thrown rope. A whole quarter of the city will inhale itself into compost and exhale a fresh quarter in the time it takes to cross it. Nothing you map stays mapped. The place is in a permanent storm of its own creation — a green hurricane that never moves because it is *everywhere at once*, and you are standing inside its eye, which is not calm, only differently violent.

## The sound

It is deafening, and the sound is the growing.

You do not expect plants to be loud. These are. The mass strains against itself with a continuous bone-deep groaning, the creak of a ship's hull multiplied by a forest, by a city. Stalks split open with reports like cannon. The fibrous tearing of separation, the wet seam of fusion. A million leaves unfurling all at once make a sound like a downpour that never lands. Somewhere always a tower is failing, and its fall is a long collapsing roar that lasts minutes and overlaps the next fall and the next, so that the floor of sound never drops, only changes its register.

And under all of it — felt more than heard, in the teeth, in the chest — a low organic drone, the sound of total metabolism, the engine-hum of three-on-the-scale life burning itself forward as fast as it can go. You shout and cannot hear yourself shout.

## The water

It is damp, not wet. The water here is internal — it lives in the plants, in the swelling stems, in the beads of condensation that fur every surface and drip from the underside of every arch in the warm air. Now and then a stalk ruptures and a season's worth of stored water spills out in one gush, a brief flash flood that the floor absorbs in seconds. There are no rivers. There is no rain from above, only the constant sweating of the green, the slick of moisture on bark, the moment of cool when a droplet finds the back of your neck before the heat swallows it again. Thirst is strange here: you are surrounded by water that is always *almost* available and never quite offered.

## The light, and what's wrong with it

The light is ordinary. This is the most unsettling thing of all.

It falls clean and white and even, the way light falls on a normal afternoon, except there is no sky — only the closing-and-opening canopy far above, the green ceiling that should make everything dim and underwater-dark. But the light comes anyway, full and shadowless, sourceless, as if the whole place were lit for a stage. It does not flicker when the canopy thickens. It does not warm or cool with any sun. It is simply *daylight*, persistent, indifferent, illuminating a world that has no business being illuminated, and the contradiction sits behind everything you see like a wrongness you can't point at.

## The rule of the world

There are no animals. Nothing scurries, nothing flies, nothing watches you from the green. This city the size of a valley, this riot of growth and collapse, is utterly without animal life — and so the motion is *wrong*, because everything moves like an animal and none of it is one. The vines that reach across your path reach with intent and have no creature behind them. The doorways that open seem to open *for* you, the rooms that form seem shaped to a body, and there is no maker, no mind, only the plant doing the thing a plant should not do: *arranging itself as if it expected company.*

That is where reality thins to nothing. The city is built and unbuilt by no one for no one, and yet it is unmistakably a *city* — it has the grammar of habitation, the rhythm of a place meant to be lived in — and it performs that grammar in an endless turbulent loop with no inhabitant ever arriving, no inhabitant ever having existed. You walk through the longing of vegetation to be civilization. You are the only animal. You may be the thing it has been growing toward, or you may be the thing it grew to replace.

The walls heal behind you. The streets close. The light stays bright. The noise does not stop.

It is becoming a city again. It will never finish. It has never not been finishing.
