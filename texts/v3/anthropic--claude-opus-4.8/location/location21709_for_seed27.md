# The Xerophyte City

There is no water here, and there has not been for an age longer than memory. But everything is alive.

You stand on a high plateau — call it high; the air thins and cools toward something like 6°C, a steady mountain chill that never breaks into warmth and never falls to frost. The land has been worn so flat by deep time that the horizon is a line, not a silhouette. What rises from it does not rise from geology. It rises from *growth*.

## The Substrate

Look down. The ground is not soil. It is a single laminated mass of living tissue, a plant-as-floor that has been accreting in thin sheets for longer than the rock beneath it has held its shape. It is pale grey-green, almost mineral, and dry to the touch — dry the way a lichen is dry, the way old paper is dry, holding its water somewhere internal and refusing to give it up. Walk on it and it gives slightly, then firms, the way a callus does. It does not bleed. It has learned, across the eons, not to waste a single molecule on weeping.

This is a world that runs without water. The plants here metabolize something else — they pull a thin alkaline vapor from the air, a faint ammoniac haze that shimmers in the low places, and they cycle it through tissues that work more like batteries than like xylem and phloem. They are not green from chlorophyll alone. There is a deep iron-blue pigment in the architecture-plants, a color like oxidized steel, that drinks the dim light and stores it as a slow charge.

## The Built Things That Grew

Here is the strangeness: the city was not built. It was *cultivated*, or it cultivated itself, and the line between those two has eroded along with everything else.

Towers stand across the plateau — settlement-scale, an organized sprawl of them, spaced wide with great dry plazas between, sparse enough that you can see the whole valley-sized expanse of it at once. Each tower is a single plant, or the corpse of one, grown into geometry too precise to be accidental. They have right angles. They have load-bearing buttresses and internal stairwells that spiral with the regularity of poured concrete. The walls are woven cellulose, dense as hardwood, ringed and grained, and where they have been worn down by the ancient slow wind they reveal cross-sections like tree rings — but the rings count years in the millions.

This is the tech beyond modern: not machines made of metal but *engineering grown into living matter*, an architecture that designed itself across deep time toward an answer to problems we have no equations for. The towers communicate. Run a hand along a wall and feel a faint thrum transmit — chemical signals racing through the substrate-floor from tower to tower, a slow electrochemical internet older than any nervous system. The city *thinks*, in the way a forest thinks, but it has had a hundred million years to get good at it, and what it computes is not for us.

Some structures bend physics in ways that don't sit right in the eye. A spire that is taller on the inside. A doorway that lets the dim light through but not the ammoniac haze, as though it sorts the air by intention. These are not tricks. They are the settled, final form of a problem solved so completely that the solution no longer resembles the problem.

## Light and Sound

The light is dim — a permanent dusk, as though the sun here is old too, or distant, or filtered through the high cold sky into something the color of weak tea. The iron-blue plant-flesh glows faintly at its growing edges, a bioluminescence of metabolic effort, so the city has a low constellation of pale lights mapped across its towers, brightest where the growth is freshest.

But the *sound* — the sound is enormous.

The towers are hollow. The dry wind moves through them and they sing, each at its own pitch, a vast organ-drone that fills the whole plateau and never stops. Add to this the percussion: seed-cases the size of barrels dry, crack, and detonate, flinging their contents across the plazas with reports like gunfire, all day, irregular, startling. The substrate-floor groans as it shifts its great laminated weight. And under everything is the deep tidal rush of the city's internal chemistry — a sound like blood, if you put your ear to a wall, but loud enough that here it is simply the texture of the air. It is loud the way a cathedral full of bells is loud. You learn to speak under it or not at all.

## The Fauna

There is abundant animal life, and all of it has evolved to need no water either — the great riddle that the whole biosphere has answered. They are dry, plated, slow-burning creatures. The most common are the **grazers**, flat segmented things the size of dogs that scrape the substrate-floor for its outermost living film and carry it off in cheek-pouches lined with desiccant crystal. They cluster near the towers and scatter when a seed-pod detonates.

Above them move the **lamp-moths** — not moths, but the nearest word — broad gliding sails of tissue that catch the thermals between towers and feed on the faint ammoniac haze directly, filtering it through gills. They are drawn to the glowing growth-edges and you'll see them spiral the bright spires like ash around a fire.

And there are the things the city keeps. In the deepest plazas live the **tenders**, the only fauna that does not flee you — creatures grown half from the substrate itself, rooted-when-still, that move to prune dead growth, to redirect a tower's reach, to plant. Whether they serve the city or are organs of it is not a question they could answer and not a question that means anything here.

## The Rules

Nothing rots, because there is no water to rot in. The dead simply stand. Across the open plateau you'll find tower-corpses centuries dead, still ringing in the wind, slowly being overgrown by their descendants — a city built of its own ancestors, every generation rooting in the bones of the last. This is the end-state of a world: not collapse, but a final, dry, humming stability that could last until the sun gives out.

It is cool. It is vast and half-empty. It sings without rest. And it has been finished for a very long time.

You are the only thing here that needs water. The city has noticed. The signals in the floor have quickened. The tenders have turned.
