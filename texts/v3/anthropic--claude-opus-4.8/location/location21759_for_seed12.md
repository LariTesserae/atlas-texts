# The Cloud-Drowned Terraces

Start with the fact that you cannot tell where the air ends and the water begins.

This is a high place — high enough that the world has thinned around it, that the horizon falls away on every side into pale nothing — but it is not dry. The mountain does not rise *above* the clouds; it rises *into* a permanent sea of them. The air here is so saturated that it has the weight and texture of standing water. You move through it as a wading thing moves. Breathe and you taste the brine of a sky that has decided to be ocean. Walk and your skin beads instantly, the droplets gathering until they run.

So: submerged, but in a body of water that hangs. A drowned summit that was never beneath the waves — the waves came up to meet it, condensed out of the warm exhalation of a planet too humid to keep its oceans flat. The whole upper world has gone liquid-soft.

## The Terrain, Still Becoming

The rock underfoot is *young*. This is the strangest thing about the place, once you notice it: the mountain has barely finished being a mountain. The terraces — broad steps cut into the slope, each one a level shelf the size of a courtyard — have edges that are not yet edges. They are rounded, half-poured, like wax that cooled before it could decide its shape. There are no canyons here, no weathered faces, no scree. Erosion has not had time to do its slow patient work of carving and ruin. The stone looks *extruded*, freshly squeezed up from below and still settling into itself.

In places the rock is still warm to the touch — not from sun, which barely reaches here, but from the act of formation. Run your hand along a terrace-wall and feel it: smooth, almost ceramic, faintly humming with the memory of having been molten. Cracks open and reseal on timescales you can almost watch if you sit long enough. The whole mountain is a thing under construction, building itself in the dim, with no hurry at all.

This is why everything is so quiet. There is no rush of rivers cutting their beds, no clatter of loose stone, no wind to whistle through eroded gaps — the gaps haven't formed. Sound here is mostly the *drip*. The patient, endless tapping of the hung sea condensing on every surface and falling, drop by drop, terrace to terrace, a million small clocks slightly out of time with one another.

## What Grows

And yet it is lush. The warmth and the eternal water make it so. Out of the raw young stone — out of cracks no wider than a fingernail — grows a vegetation that has learned to drink from the air itself.

Most of it is not rooted in soil, because there is no soil yet; the rock is too new to have crumbled into anything a seed could hold. Instead the plants are *epiphytic*, clinging, draping, hanging. Long pale tendrils descend from the terrace-rims in curtains, ending in cupped leaves that catch and hold the falling drops. Mosses cover everything in a deep, springy pelt, dark green going nearly black in the dim light, so saturated they squelch and weep when stepped on. Ferns unfurl in dense thickets, frond against frond, fighting for the gray light. Things bloom — soft, fleshy flowers the color of the inside of a shell, opening slowly, taking days to do it.

The density is layered and near-touching: a forest stacked on itself, vines through fronds through moss through hanging root, so that to move through it you must part it like a beaded curtain, and it closes behind you. Everything is wet. Everything drips on everything below it. There is a smell of green decay and mineral and warm rain, thick enough to chew.

## The Built Things

People live here. Or something that builds the way people build — organized, infrastructural, by hand.

They have made a town across several of the terraces, and the construction is everywhere clever about the water. Roofs are not for keeping rain off — there is no escaping the water — but for *channeling* it. Every structure is a catchment. The buildings are built of the young stone itself, cut while it is still soft and workable, hardening in place into walls; you can see the toolmarks, the simple chisel-scoring of hand-labor. No metal beyond the occasional dull bronze blade, green with the wet. Mostly stone and lashed plant-fiber and fired clay. Cisterns everywhere, terraced like the mountain, each one feeding the next, the overflow falling in deliberate small cataracts from level to level. The whole settlement is a slow machine for moving water downward in an orderly way, since water is the one thing it will never lack.

The buildings huddle close — settlement-density, dwellings sharing walls, narrow ways between them barely wide enough to pass, all of it slick and mossed and dripping. Lamps burn in some windows: oil-flames behind translucent screens, smudges of warm orange in all the gray. They give the only sharp light in this dim drowned world, and they do not give much.

## The Strangeness

Here is what makes the place itself, and not merely a wet mountain town:

The water that hangs in the air is not entirely passive. It *gathers*. On still days — and most days are still — the droplets in certain hollows begin to clump, to organize, to form drifting shapes that hold together longer than water should. The townsfolk call them by a word that means something like *the standing ones*. They are man-high coalescences of mist that move slowly along the terraces, following paths, pausing at thresholds. They do not speak or threaten. They drift, and they drink the bloom-flowers dry, and they dissolve back into the general wet when something disturbs them.

No one is sure if they are alive. They are rare — you might see one in a week — and the people leave offerings of the cupped catch-leaves full of clean water at the terrace-edges, as if to feed them, or perhaps to keep them gathering elsewhere.

The fauna otherwise is sparse and soft: pale slow-moving things in the moss, a few birds that have forgotten flight in air too thick for it and instead clamber through the hanging gardens with hooked claws, fish-things in the cisterns that may have come up from somewhere far below. Life is rare here, and quiet, and unhurried.

## The Rule of the Place

Everything moves slowly. The mountain builds itself one cooling layer at a time. The flowers open across days. The water falls drop by drop. The standing ones drift at the pace of thought. There is a single great patience here, a sense that this world is *early* — that it has only just begun to exist and has all of time to finish becoming what it will be.

And over all of it, the dim pale light, neither day nor night, filtering down through the hung sea — enough to see by, never enough to throw a sharp shadow. A world being made, slowly, in the half-dark, underwater, in the warm.
