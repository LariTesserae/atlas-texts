There is a city the size of a thumbnail, and it is built into the inside of a geode.

Begin with the rock. We are very high — at the top of something, though there is no way to see the bottom, no way to confirm there is a bottom. The geode is lodged in the throat of a mountain that has only recently finished being a mountain. The stone around it is still sharp; the fracture planes have not yet been rounded by any patient hand. Where the outer shell has split, you can see the grain has barely begun to weather — a few flakes, a dusting of pale grit settled in the seams. Young rock. It does not yet know how to be soft.

Inside, the geode is dry. This is the first thing the body notices, before the eyes adjust: there is no moisture here at all. The air is mineral and faintly metallic, the way a struck flint smells. No dew gathers on the crystal faces. No film of damp anywhere. The cold is the cold of high altitude and the cold of stone that water has abandoned — about the temperature of a cellar in early spring, cool enough to make you keep your hands moving.

The light is dim and it comes from the crystals themselves. The geode's lining is amethyst and smoke-quartz, and within the stone, deep in the lattice, something glows — not a fire, not a lamp, a slow phosphorescence the color of old bruises and weak honey. It does not flicker. It is the most stable thing in the whole place. Everything else moves.

Now the city.

They have built on the crystal terraces — the great hexagonal faces of quartz that step down the curved inner wall like the galleries of an opera house turned inward upon itself. The construction is dense, layered, packed shoulder to shoulder: foundries welded into the flat of one crystal, dwelling-cells stacked up its slope, walkways flung crystal-to-crystal across the gulf of the hollow center. Everything is small. The largest tower would not reach your knee. But scale is relative inside, and to the builders these are cathedrals.

Who builds? The fauna and the builders are the same. They are abundant — that is the word for it, abundant past comfort, a saturation of bodies. Picture something between an ant and a clockmaker: hard-shelled, many-legged, each one trailing a faint thread of the crystal-glow caught in the chitin so that the streets seem to be made of moving sparks. They pour. There is no other word. They pour up the terraces and down them, into the foundry-mouths and out, dragging filaments of metal teased from the rock, dragging little carts, dragging each other.

This is an industrial place. The geode rings with it — though "rings" overstates the sound, because the strangeness here is that all this manufacture is *mostly quiet.* You expect a foundry-city to roar. Instead it whispers. The engines run on something patient; the hammers fall on padded anvils; the work is done in a hush broken only by the dry tick of carapace on quartz, the small exhalations of bellows, a distant grinding like teeth in sleep. The loudest thing is the occasional crack of the young rock settling somewhere in the shell above, and after each crack the whole city pauses — a held breath of ten thousand small bodies — and then resumes.

Because the place is turbulent. This is the truth underneath the quiet. The terraces are not stable. The crystals are still *growing* — the geode has not finished forming, and what the builders have made is a settlement on the slope of a living thing. A new face of quartz will shoulder up through a foundry floor in the span of an afternoon and the whole district must be torn down and re-laid higher. A walkway snaps as its anchor-crystal lengthens and twists. Whole quarters are abandoned, swallowed, rebuilt, three times in a season. The builders do not seem distressed by this. They have made an architecture of catastrophe — joints that give, foundations that float, towers designed to be dismantled in the time it takes to dismantle them. The chaos is the climate. They build the way a thing breathes in a storm: continuously, without ever finishing, the construction itself a kind of weather answering the weather of the stone.

There are no plants. There has never been a plant. But there is something that takes their place — a slow fungal bloom, or something simpler than fungus, a felt of pale filament that grows on the spent slag and the dead crystal-faces where the glow has faded. The builders farm it. They carry it in their mandibles, they tend its grey lawns in the dim under-galleries, they eat it, or feed it to the engines, or both. It is the only soft thing in the geode and they treat it with what looks like tenderness.

What are they making? This is the part I cannot resolve, and I think it is the heart of the place. They mine the glow from the crystal — the honey-and-bruise light, harvested in threads — and they spin it, in the foundries, into something they carry up. Up the terraces, up the unstable towers, up toward the split in the shell where the young rock shows its sharp new edges and the cold pours in. They are taking the light somewhere. They are paying it out into the crack like a thread, hand over hand over many-handed, against all the collapsing and the rebuilding, against the growing stone that ruins their work as fast as they make it.

Maybe they are trying to seal the geode. Maybe they are trying to escape it. Maybe the light is simply what they owe, and the crack is where it is owed, and the city exists only to keep the payment moving.

The geode does not say. The geode only glows, and grows, and cracks, and somewhere far above, a flake of fresh stone falls, and ten thousand small bodies hold still, listening, in the cool dry dark.
