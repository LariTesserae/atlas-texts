There is a place I have been trying to describe for a long time, and I keep arriving at it from the wrong direction. Let me approach it now from the side that smells of resin.

It begins as a garden that has never known rain. Everything green here grows out of dryness — out of clay that someone fired, out of wax, out of woven fiber and pressed pith. There is no water and there has never been water, and yet the place is lush. This is the first thing you must hold without trying to resolve it: the leaves are thick and waxy and crowded, vines lap over vines, broad fronds lean into the warm air — and not one drop moves anywhere. The plants drink something else. Light, perhaps. Or the slow exhalation of the makers. The sap, when a stem breaks, is dry — it comes out as a fine warm dust that smells of cinnamon and clay and hangs in the air a while before settling.

Hold that, and now look at what it's all made of.

Nothing here grew on its own. Every plant is a built plant. Walk close to any leaf and you see the seams — it has been folded from thin baked sheets, or carved, or wound from cord and lacquered until it gleams like something living. The "trunks" are turned on lathes, fluted, joined with wooden pegs. The undersides of the fronds show tool-marks, the small confident chatter of a hand-adze. There is no metal to speak of — a few bronze pins, a knife somewhere, used long ago. Everything else is the patient work of fingers: ceramic, fiber, wax, fired earth, bone. Someone made the garden, leaf by leaf, and the making is primitive in the way the oldest things are primitive, which is to say total and unhurried and without doubt.

And it is small. Not vast, not a world — a room, really, or a grove the size of a large room, with edges you could walk to in a hundred slow paces. You feel the boundary before you see it: the made-vines climb up to a certain height and then there is a wall, also made, also crowded with constructed growth, so that the boundary is itself a thicket. There is a ceiling of sorts, far up, hung with fired-clay foliage, and light comes down through it the way light comes through a real canopy — broken, warm, ordinary. Normal light. You could read by it. That ordinariness is almost the strangest thing in here.

The density is the next thing the body notices. Everything is near-touching. The made-plants are layered four and five deep, leaf over leaf, vine through vine, so that to move you must part them, and they part softly, dryly, with a faint ceramic click where glazed edges kiss. There is barely a volume of empty air in the place. It is saturated. You are never not touching something growing that was grown by hand.

Now the fauna, because there is a great deal of it.

The animals are made too — and they are not still. This is where the place stops being a museum. Things move in the constructed underbrush: small jointed creatures of wood and cord and fired clay, beetles the size of a thumb whose shells are real lacquer, birds folded from stiffened cloth that shift their weight from foot to foot on a branch and turn a painted eye toward you. They are abundant, these things — the garden teems with them, a slow teeming. Nobody winds them. Nobody pulls a string. They move because in this place the made and the alive are not two categories. A thing that has been made carefully enough, here, simply *is* alive, and the makers seem to have known this and made accordingly, and the place is therefore full of small dry breathing.

It is, despite all this life, mostly quiet. This is important. The animals move almost without sound. A clay beetle crossing a wax leaf makes less noise than your own pulse. The folded birds do not sing — or they sing so far below hearing that you feel it in your teeth rather than your ears. Now and then a single sound: a leaf settling, a soft tock of two ceramic things meeting, a long sigh from somewhere deep in the layered green that might be the place breathing or might be a frond cooling. The quiet is not emptiness. It is attention. It is the quiet of a great many things being very careful.

And it is warm. Pleasantly, then insistently warm — the warmth of a kiln two rooms away, of a body, of midafternoon under cloth. The warmth comes up out of the floor, which is also made, tiled in dark fired plates that hold the heat, and it rises through the layered green and pools under the canopy. The warmth is part of why everything seems slow. Time moves here at the pace of warmed honey. The dynamic of the place is patient: things change, but across long arcs you can almost see — a vine adds a leaf over the course of an afternoon, a clay bird relocates one branch over while you weren't watching, the whole garden very slowly turning toward a light source that itself moves too slowly to catch. Nothing is urgent. Everything is in motion. These are not contradictions here.

The place feels young. Freshly made. The seams are still sharp, the tool-marks crisp, the glazes bright and uncracked — nothing has had time to wear. There is no erosion to speak of because there is no water to do the eroding and not enough age to have done it any other way. The garden was finished recently, by some reckoning, and it knows it; there is that brightness to it, that not-yet-settled quality, like a thing whose maker has only just stepped back from it.

But here is where I have to tell you the real shape of it, the thing the parameters point at and circle and won't name.

The making does not stop at the edge of the objects.

I said everything is made — the leaves, the beetles, the floor. But spend long enough in the warm quiet and you understand that the *space* is made too, and the *time*, and the rules. There is no natural substrate anywhere — not under the tiles, not behind the walls, not before the present moment. The garden is built all the way down. And a thing built all the way down has no obligation to behave.

So: the distances do not stay put. The boundary you measured at a hundred paces is at eighty now, or at three hundred, and the room is still small — smallness here is a quality the place keeps regardless of its size, the way a key keeps its signature regardless of the tune. You can walk toward a wall and arrive at the center. You can part a curtain of made-vines and find the same curtain of made-vines parting around your own hands from the other side.

The animals know you in advance. A clay bird turns its painted eye toward where you will be standing a moment before you go there, and is not surprised, and this is the only way it ever shows surprise — by having none. The beetles arrange themselves, very slowly, into the shape of the path you have not yet decided to take, so that your decisions arrive already illustrated underfoot.

Cause and effect are still here, but loosely pegged, like the joints of the wooden creatures — they hold, mostly, with a little play in them. Break a leaf and the dry cinnamon sap comes out *before* the break, settling onto your fingers in warning. Reach for a fruit — there are fruits, fired-clay fruits, warm and impossibly heavy — and find your hand has already been holding it for some time, that you began holding it earlier than you arrived, that the holding is older than you are.

And the makers. You never see them. But the whole place is so saturated with their care — every leaf turned by a hand, every beetle given its small slow life by a hand — that their absence is the densest thing in the garden, denser than the layered green. You begin to suspect that they did not leave. You begin to suspect that *being made* is what it feels like, from the inside, to be in their attention, and that the warmth is the warmth of being held, and that the quiet is the quiet of someone working who does not want to wake you, and that you, walking here, parting the dry vines, are also one of the made things — newly finished, sharp-seamed, not yet worn — being moved one branch over while you weren't watching, turning slowly toward a light.

That is the place. It is a small warm crowded handmade garden with no water in it, full of slow careful constructed life, mostly silent, recently completed, where everything including the rules has been folded by hand from baked sheets of something, and where the folding has not stopped, and where you should not be too sure that you walked in on your own.

It smells of resin and cinnamon and warm clay. I keep arriving at it from the wrong direction. I think that is because there is no right one — only the one the makers had in mind, and they have not told me, and the beetles, who know, are still very slowly spelling it out across the floor.
