# The Drawer

It is the size of a drawer. I want to say that first, because everything else will try to make you forget it.

There was a desk once — or a cabinet, or a machine with a face full of compartments — and this is one of its lowest drawers, pulled out perhaps a finger's width and never closed again. Whatever sat above it has flattened down over the long count of years until the joints fused and the wood became something harder than wood, a dry mineral grain that no longer remembers being a tree. The air in here is cool and still and tastes of old graphite and machine oil gone to powder. There is no water. There has not been water for so long that the concept has worn smooth.

And yet it is crowded past all reason.

The floor of the drawer is a salt-field — not white but a dull grey-violet, ground from the slow erosion of brass fittings and the dust of countless small bodies. It has been walked into terraces. Low ridges, finger-high, run the length of the space, near-flattened, ancient, the edges rounded the way river-stones are rounded except no river ever touched them. Only feet. Only the endless passage of feet.

Because the drawer is full of insects, and the insects have a civilization.

Not the way ants do, exactly. The things here are something between a weevil and a clockwork — chitin and a faint metallic sheen, parts that catch the light like oiled gears. There are millions of them. The density is the first thing that overwhelms; they are layered, climbing over one another in living strata, three and four deep along the warm wall where some buried capacitor still leaks a trace of heat. The sound they make is enormous for so small a place. A dry industrial roar — the scrape of plates, the clicking of a thousand thousand mandibles, the hum of wings that never quite leave the ground. It fills the whole sealed volume and rebounds off the drawer's ceiling, which from below looks like a low metal sky, scratched all over with the marks of generations.

They have built things. This is the scattered part — not a city, but the partial remains of one, and a few structures still in use. Spires of compacted dust and secreted resin, hollow towers leaning against the brass screws that rise like mountains out of the salt-plain. Some of the towers are abandoned, eroded to stubs. Some are active: I can see the foreman-things moving in and out, carrying granules in relays, feeding them to the engines.

There are engines.

This is the thing I did not expect, and I will not pretend to fully understand it. Somewhere in the deep history of the drawer the insects learned to harness the leaking heat and the static that builds along the metal walls. They have made small electrified works — coils of harvested wire-fragment, wound by hand or by mandible around cores of resin, sparking faintly. Bellows of stretched membrane drive air across the warm wall and back. There is a wheel, somewhere near the rear of the drawer where the dark gathers, that turns and turns, and a line of the creatures works it in shifts, and I cannot tell what it produces. Light, maybe. Or just the turning. The turning may be the point.

The light in here is good and even — normal light, daylight almost, though no sun reaches a closed drawer. It comes from the engines and from a phosphor-dust the creatures cultivate in the cracks, a glow they farm the way we might farm grain, except nothing grows. Nothing has grown here in an age. There is no vegetation, not a spore, not a green thread. This is a mineral world and a made world and a world of bodies, and the only living things are the swarm itself, which is so alive, so relentlessly in motion, that the absence of plants feels less like emptiness than like a decision the place made long ago.

The dynamic of it never stops. Watch one terrace long enough and you see the cycles: the heat-wall fills with bodies through the cool hours, empties as they march to the engines, fills again. Predation moves through the crowd — some of these creatures eat others, larger gear-bright forms parting the multitude, and the swarm closes behind them like water behind a stone, except there is no water, I keep forgetting, there is no water, only the dry closing of countless small backs. Weather, of a kind: a tide of warmth that rises and falls with whatever ancient circuit still breathes above. When it rises the roar climbs. When it falls the whole drawer goes quiet and grey and you can hear, underneath, the single steady tick of the turning wheel.

Here is the strangeness, the thing that is only a little strange, an eighth of the way toward truly wrong: they are old. Not the individuals — the individuals are brief, you can watch them be born and worked and eaten in an afternoon. But the *pattern* is old. So old it has stopped changing. The terraces are worn to their final shape. The towers are rebuilt always to the same plan because the plan reached its final form before memory and there is nowhere further to go. This is a civilization at the end of its erosion. Everything that could be invented has been invented, and everything invented has been ground smooth by repetition, and now the swarm simply runs the great machine of itself, loud and dense and ceaseless, in a cool drawer that nobody will ever open.

They do not know they are in a drawer.

Why would they. The walls are the edges of the world, scratched with the deeds of ancestors. The ceiling is the sky. The leak of warmth is the sun, the spark of engines the stars, and the slow grinding-flat of everything is simply time, doing what time does, which here it has nearly finished doing.

Pull the drawer no further. Slide it no shut. Just leave it the width of a finger ajar, and let the roar continue, faint, in the back of a room in a house no one lives in, in the lowest compartment of a thing that was once a desk.

It is the size of a drawer. I told you that first so you would feel it now.
