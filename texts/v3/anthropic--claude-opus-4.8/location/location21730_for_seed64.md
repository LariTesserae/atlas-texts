There is a crack in the mountain, and inside the crack there is a room, and the room is the world.

Begin with the cold. Not the bite of wind — there is no wind here — but the steady, even cool of stone that has never been warm, held at the temperature of a cellar near the top of something enormous. You know you are high because of how thin the cool feels, how it touches the inside of the lungs without weight. The mountain around this pocket is extreme, a vertical immensity of frost-shattered rock, but you do not see it. You are *inside*. The pocket is no larger than a granary, a sealed lung in the body of the peak, and its walls curve in close on every side.

There is no water. This must be said plainly because everything in the room argues against it. The walls are dry mineral — pale grey-green serpentine, veined with a chalky asbestos-white, weathered smooth by an age of small dry collapses, the settled form of a place that finished becoming itself long ago and now simply *is*. No drip. No seep. No condensation beading on the cold stone. The dryness is total and it is old.

And yet the room is packed with green.

Here is the strangeness. The vegetation does not drink. It grows from the rock the way crystal grows from rock — slowly, structurally, mineral and plant having struck some ancient treaty. Fat lobed pads of something between lichen and succulent tile every surface, overlapping, layer on layer, until there is no bare stone left anywhere, floor wall ceiling all of it furred and cushioned and crowded. The plants are the dusty deep green of old bottle-glass, and they store nothing wet inside them — break one and it crumbles to a dry powder that smells faintly of pepper and electrical heat. They feed on the mineral and on the dim light, and the dim light feeds on them, and the cycle is closed and silent and does not move.

The light: low, brown-gold, sourceless. It comes diffusely from the plants themselves, a faint photosynthetic luminescence too weak to read by but enough to see shapes — enough to make the whole room glow like the inside of a cupped hand held to a candle. There is no day here, no night, no flicker. The light has been exactly this bright for ten thousand years.

Now the sound, which is the part that makes the room hard to stand in.

It is loud. Astonishingly loud for a place so small and so still. The sound comes from the built things.

Scattered through the green, half-swallowed by the cushioning pads, are *machines*. Small ones — the size of looms, of printing presses, of the engines that once drove village mills. Industrial in the oldest sense: cast iron, brass fittings, leather belts, flywheels, pistons. They are bolted into the living rock, anchored deep, and they are *running*. They have always been running. Flywheels spin, belts slap, governors rise and fall on their brass arms, and the whole room thunders with the rhythm of manufacture — a clatter and a chuff and a deep reciprocating hammer that fills the pocket wall to wall and leaves no room for thought.

But they make nothing. There is no product, no input, no operator. The belts drive other belts. The pistons drive cranks that drive flywheels that drive the pistons. It is a sealed economy of motion, a factory whose only output is its own continuation, whose only purpose is to keep the room loud. The green grows over the machines, padding their frames, but never fouling the moving parts — the plants and the engines have learned each other, and where a belt runs the green grows away from it, leaving a clean dark channel of worn metal that gleams from a million identical passes.

There is no animal here. Nothing breathes that did not grow rooted. No spider in the green, no thing that scurries when the light shifts — and the light never shifts. The only motion in the whole world is the machines' motion, and that motion does not count as change because it is the same motion forever, a loop so tight it is indistinguishable from stillness. The flywheel that completes its ten-thousandth-million turn is in exactly the state of the flywheel at the first. Nothing wears. Nothing accumulates. The dust that crumbles from a broken pad is balanced grain for grain by the growth of the next. The room is in perfect equilibrium and has been since before equilibrium had a name.

The rules of this world, as best I can lay them out:

Nothing enters and nothing leaves. The crack that admits you closes behind the eye, and afterward there is only inside.

The green converts mineral and light into more green, and the machines convert their own motion into more motion, and these two cycles share the room without touching, the way two songs in different keys can fill the same hall.

You may not change anything, because there is nothing here that change can take hold of. Break a pad: it regrows. Stop a flywheel: you cannot, your hands are not strong enough, and it would not matter, the loop would close over the gap. The room does not resist intrusion. It simply does not register it.

And the sound — the great unceasing industrial thunder — is the truest thing about the place. It is the sound of work being done that produces nothing, in a room that drinks no water, lit by plants that should be dead, near the top of a mountain no one climbs. It is the sound of a sealed thing keeping itself, perfectly, against an age that has forgotten it is there.

Stand in the cool brown glow. Let the hammering fill you until you cannot hear your own pulse. Understand that this has always been happening and will always be happening, exactly like this, in this one small held breath of stone.

Then leave, if you can find the crack again. The room will not notice you have gone.
