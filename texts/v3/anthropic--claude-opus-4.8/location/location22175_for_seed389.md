# The Smelting Heights

Begin with altitude. You are higher than birds choose to fly, higher than rain wants to fall, on the raw shoulder of a mountain that has not finished deciding its own shape. The rock here is young — not weathered into character but freshly extruded, glassy in places where some heat below pushed it up and let it cool too fast. The ridges have sharp edges still. Nothing has had time to round them. This is geology in its first draft, the corners not yet sanded by ten thousand years of wind.

And yet — a city.

Not a city that grew, but a city that was installed, bolted onto the unfinished mountain like a brace onto a green bone. It climbs the slopes in terraces of riveted iron and cast pipe, foundry-stacks and condensation-towers, walkways strung between outcrops that the builders did not bother to level. They built around the rock's refusal. A staircase will rise forty steps and then simply end against a cliff that wasn't there in the plans, and a ladder is bolted on as afterthought, and the next street continues a hundred feet up, slightly out of true. The whole settlement leans into the incline at an angle that the body never quite forgets.

## The Heat

It is hot the way a kiln is hot — not the wet swelter of a jungle but a dry industrial heat that comes up through the iron decking and presses on the soles of the boots. The mountain is warm from within. Whatever made it is still making it, somewhere down in the unfinished core, and the city sits on that warmth like a hand on a stovetop kept just below the threshold of pain.

This is why the city is here at all. The heat is the reason. Down in the lower terraces the great smelting-houses run on the mountain's own thermal bleed, drawing it up through bored shafts, and the foundries process — what? Ore, surely. The rock is rich and raw and young, full of metals that haven't been locked into stable forms yet. The place harvests the mountain's own indecision.

## The Water

You would not expect water this high, this hot, on stone this barren. But the heat and the cold air conspire. Each night the temperature aloft drops hard, and the warm exhalations of the foundries meet it, and condensation forms — runs down every iron surface, collects in the long copper gutters that lace the city like veins, drips from the underside of every walkway. The whole place sweats and is drunk by its own thirst. Cisterns catch it. Pipes carry it. There is no river, but there is a slow internal weather: fog at the cold hours, beading at dawn, the patient tick of drops into catch-basins all through the quiet day.

Sometimes, rarely, an actual rain comes — a cloud snags on the peak and unloads, and for an hour the iron rings differently and the gutters race. Then it stops, and the dryness reasserts itself, and within a day the only moisture is again the city's own breath returned to it.

## Vegetation, or its absence

Nothing grows. There is no soil — the rock is too new to have become dirt. But in the wet seams, in the dripping joints where condensation pools, something that is not quite plant and not quite mineral has taken up residence: pale crusts, faintly metallic, that spread along the damp iron like frost that doesn't melt. Not lichen. Closer to a slow chemical bloom — a self-organizing tarnish that feeds on the dissolved metals in the condensation and grows by deposition, the way a stalactite grows, but patterned, branching, almost deliberate. The workers call it *bloomscale* and scrape it off the structural members because it weakens the iron, but it always returns, slow, patient, in the dark wet undersides where nobody looks. It is the closest thing this world has to life that isn't human or machine. Whether it is alive is a question nobody up here has the leisure to answer.

## Sound

Here is the strangeness that strikes you first, after the heat: it is quiet. A city this industrial should roar. There should be the clamor of the foundries, the hiss of pressure, the din of mechanized labor. Instead the sound is muffled, low, almost reverent. The engines run, but they run *slowly* — turning over at a tempo measured in the long breath rather than the heartbeat. A piston completes its stroke and you have time to anticipate the next. The smelters glow and seethe but their work is gradual, a melting that takes days. This is not a place of fast production. It is a place of patient extraction, and patience makes little noise.

What sound there is comes mostly from water: the drip, the trickle in the gutters, the occasional gulp of a cistern filling. And the wind, thinned by altitude, sliding over the sharp new ridges with a high clean note. And under it all a deep, slow, almost subsonic pulse from far down inside the mountain — the heat-engine of the world itself, beating once every long while, so low you feel it in the iron of the deck plates before you hear it.

## Density

For all the quiet, the place is crowded — not with people but with *things*. Pipes upon pipes, stacked and crossing, lagged in cloth gone stiff with mineral deposit. Towers leaning against towers. Walkways three and four levels deep, so that you walk in perpetual partial shade beneath the structure above. The light that reaches you is normal daylight filtered through grids of iron and skeins of fog — bright enough to see clearly, but always interrupted, always falling in slats and patches. Look up and you see not sky but layer after layer of construction, and only at the edges, where the city stops and the raw mountain resumes, does the open air break through. Then the scale hits you — the slopes running up beyond the last terrace into bare glassy rock, and other peaks across the gap, just as young, just as unfinished, hazed in their own thermal exhalations. A whole region of new mountains. Nobody has built on those yet. Or nobody anymore.

## Fauna

Almost nothing lives here. Once in a long while something moves at the edge of sight — a creature adapted to the heat and the height, leathery, slow, pale from a life without much sun, that comes up the cold gutters to drink the condensation and licks the bloomscale from the iron. The workers don't molest them. They are too rare to fear and too strange to name. They appear, drink, vanish back down the wet seams. Whether they nest in the mountain's warm interior or simply wander up from somewhere lower, nobody knows. They are part of the slow weather of the place, like the fog.

## The rule of this world

Everything here is *becoming* and nothing has finished. The mountain is still rising. The metals are still settling into forms. The bloomscale is still spreading. The city itself, you realize, is never complete — there is scaffolding everywhere, half-built terraces, pipes capped and waiting to be extended, and crews working at a pace so slow it looks like they aren't working at all, fitting one rivet, then resting, then fitting another, as if construction here must match the unhurried tempo of the rock it stands on. You cannot build a finished city on an unfinished mountain. So they don't try. They build *with* the becoming — a settlement that intends to be permanently provisional, growing as slowly as the stone, harvesting the heat of a world still in the act of making itself.

And over all of it the patient drip of the recaptured water, the slow pulse from below, the quiet that is not peace exactly but the held breath of a place that knows it is not done yet, and has all the time it needs.
