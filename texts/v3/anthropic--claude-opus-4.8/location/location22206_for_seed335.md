There is a sound here before there is anything else, and the sound is the first thing you must understand.

It comes up through the soles of your feet. A low, periodic working — a stroke, a pause, a stroke — too slow to be a heart and too steady to be weather. It is loud. Not loud like thunder, which announces itself and recedes, but loud the way a room near a mill is loud: a sound you stop hearing and then feel as a pressure behind the eyes, an ache in the molars. The whole place is built on it. Everything else arranges itself around that beat.

Now look. The place is small. You could walk across it in the time it takes to grow cold, and you will grow cold, because it is cold here — a wet cold, the kind that lives in the air as droplets and settles on the hairs of your arms before you notice the air has touched you. This is a pocket, a hollow, a single contained thing. Stone walls rise around it, not high, but enough to make a room of the world. They are young stone, freshly broken, edges still sharp — black and grey, glittering faintly where some mineral catches what light there is. No moss has had time to soften them. No rain has had time to round them. They look as though they were cracked open last week and the inside of the earth is still surprised to be air.

And the inside of the earth weeps. Every face of every wall is wet. Water gathers in the seams, beads, runs, hangs from the undersides of ledges in slow swelling drops that let go one at a time. The floor of the hollow is low and soft, marsh-edge ground, black peat-water standing in the prints your boots leave. Reeds grow from it — too many reeds for so empty a place, a thin lush fringe of green-grey, beaded with condensation, leaning. Between the reeds: long stretches of nothing. Water and silt and the occasional pale stone. This is the strangeness of the scale and the emptiness together — the place is intimate, but mostly hollow. Vast distances between one living thing and the next, all packed into a space you could shout across.

The light is dim and comes from no sun. There is no sky here, or the sky is too far up the broken walls to matter. The light is the color of an overcast afternoon held permanently at the moment before rain, and it seems to come from the water itself, from the wet stone, a faint cold gleam that does not brighten and does not fade.

Now — the stroke, the pause, the stroke. Find its source.

Toward the far wall, where the reeds thin to nothing and the black water lies still as poured iron, something moves in the rock. Not on the rock — *in* it. The young stone is not only stone. Set into it, grown through it, indistinguishable from it at the edges, is a piece of machinery. An immense slow piston, or something like one: a shaft of dark wet metal rising and sinking in a sleeve of fitted rock, hissing softly with each stroke, exhaling a thin cold steam that drifts and joins the condensing air. There are no rivets in it, no welds, no maker's mark. It has the same fresh sharp edges as the broken stone, as though it formed when the stone formed, as though the earth here crystallizes equally into mineral and into engine. Tubes of metal thread away from it into the walls and vanish. Somewhere out of sight, behind the stone, the rest of the machine works — gears the size of nothing turning gears the size of mountains, you cannot tell, you only feel them, the loud patient industry of a factory that no one built and that makes nothing you could name.

It is warm near the piston. The only warmth in the cold room. And so the fauna gather there.

They are abundant — that is the other thing the emptiness conceals. The hollow looks lifeless until you watch, and then the whole far end of it is alive in slow motion. Pale amphibious things cling to the warm metal, flat-bodied, eyeless, breathing through skins that catch the steam. Long-legged insects walk the surface of the black water on feet that dimple but do not break it. Something larger moves under the peat — you see only the bulge of its passage, the reeds nodding in a line as it goes beneath. They have all organized themselves around the engine's heat and the engine's rhythm. When the piston strokes down, the pale things flatten and hold. When it lifts, they feed — on the warmth, on the moisture, on the thin films of mineral the steam lays down. A whole ecology running on the exhalations of a machine that is also the rock that is also the place itself.

Predators come. You watch one: a low dark animal, sleek with the wet, that picks its way along the warm seam and takes one of the pale clingers in a snap, and the snap is the only sharp sound in all this rolling low thunder, and then it is gone back into a crack in the young stone, and the survivors flatten and hold and wait for the lift.

Nothing here is still. That is the last thing. The cold is not stillness — the cold is *weather*, a slow circulation: the steam rises warm, meets the wet cold air, condenses, falls as a fine drizzle that you only feel as a gradual soaking, runs down the weeping walls, gathers in the peat, is drawn back somewhere into the engine to rise again as steam. The water goes around. The light pulses faintly with the strokes if you watch long enough, or you imagine it does. The reeds drink and lean. The animals feed and freeze. The piston works and works.

It is not a ruin, because nothing has fallen. It is not a workshop, because no one is coming. It is a young place, an hour old in the life of the earth, that has emerged already breathing — stone and water and engine grown together in the dark and the cold, running its single slow process for no reason, going around, going around, loud in a hollow so small and so empty that the sound has nowhere to go but through you.

Stay long enough and your own pulse finds the stroke. Stay longer and you stop being sure which of you is keeping time for the other.
