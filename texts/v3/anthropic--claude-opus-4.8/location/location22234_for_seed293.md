# The Drowned Quarter at Slack Tide

Start with the water, because everything here starts with the water.

It is not a sea, exactly, though it tastes of one—warm and green and slightly sweet, the way water gets when too many living things have been breathing through it for too long. It lies a hand's depth over most of the ground, and where the ground rises it pools in the seams and runnels and the worn-out hollows of things that used to be sharper. The whole place is at the bottom of something. You feel that in your knees before you understand it: this is a low place, a settled place, the floor of a basin so old the walls have forgotten they were walls.

There's no horizon. The vegetation closes it off.

## The Substrate

Everything that isn't water is plant, and most of what looks like water is plant too, eventually, if you wait. The ground is a peat-flesh, a saturated mat of root and rhizome and the bodies of ten thousand generations of growth pressed into something between soil and meat. You can walk on it. It gives, then holds. It exhales when you step—a soft wet sound, and a smell of fermentation and flowers.

The structures here are grown, not built, but they were *built* once, in the sense that something organized them. This is the strange part, the part I keep circling. There are streets. There are the unmistakable bones of a town—a grid, gone soft, gone over. Towers of laminated cellulose rise in loose ranks, swollen and listing, their walls thick as ramparts and smooth as the inside of a gourd. Doorways gape, healed half-shut like old wounds. The avenues run straight for a while and then lose the thread, dissolving into reedbed and standing water and the broad flat pads of something lily-like that could hold a sleeping dog.

It was a city. It is now an organism that remembers being a city. The geometry persists in the way a scar persists—deep instruction, slowly relaxing.

## Sparse, Despite Everything

You'd expect such total vegetation to crowd. It doesn't. This is the thing that takes longest to feel right: the place is *open*. The plant-mass is total but the *presences* are scattered—a great tower here, then a long shining reach of empty water, then a single colossal flower-engine standing alone in the flooded plaza like a monument no one comes to anymore. Room-scale, grove-scale. You could cross the whole quarter in an afternoon if it would hold still.

It will not hold still.

## The Turbulence

The slowness is a lie told by the long view. Up close, everything churns.

The water is in constant low riot—not waves but *pulses*, a tidal breathing with no moon to drive it, the whole sheet of standing water heaving an inch up and an inch down on a rhythm of maybe forty seconds. With each pulse the plant-towers flex. Pods along their flanks swell and split and fire seed-matter across the avenues with a sound like wet applause. Bladders inflate and the towers groan and lean and right themselves. Mats of floating green knit together over open water in the space of an hour and tear apart again in the next. The lily-pads rotate slowly to follow the light. Whole reedbeds lie down in unison and rise.

It is loud. Not roaring—*busy*. A bazaar made of vegetable sound: the clatter of seedfall, the suck and gulp of the breathing water, the creak of laminated trunks, the high thin whistle of air forced through some thousand stomata-pipes when the towers exhale together. And under it, always, a deep slow organ note you feel in the sternum, the sound of the whole basin settling another hair's-width into its final shape.

## The Engineering Underneath

Now the part that doesn't belong to any natural marsh.

Cut into any tower, peel back the soft green wall, and the cellulose is *patterned* at a scale too fine to be growth. Channels thinner than nerve, branching with the impossible cleanness of something designed, carrying not sap but *signal*—water laced with something that moves faster than diffusion should allow, faster than chemistry, pulses of decision running the length of a trunk in the time it takes to blink. The plants are computing. They were *made* to compute, long ago, by hands that are gone, and they have kept doing it across however many ages it took for the city to drown and the walls to soften and the makers to become peat underfoot.

The turbulence is the computation. The pulse of the water, the timing of the seedfall, the rotation of the pads—these are the visible surface of an enormous slow thought that the whole quarter is thinking, has been thinking, will go on thinking until the basin flattens entirely and the water spreads thin enough to break the circuits.

It is not a hostile thought. It is barely aware of you. You are weather to it. A passing pressure.

## Fauna

Abundant, and all of it complicit. Things move in the water—soft-bodied, translucent, half-plant themselves, grazing the signal-channels and being grazed in turn, ferrying material from tower to tower. They are the maintenance crews of the great old machine, descended from pollinators and pests, long since folded into the work. Some are large—slow grazers the size of cattle, walking the avenues on rooted feet, leaving trails of churned light where they disturb the luminous algae. Some are tiny and constant and form the haze of midges that hangs over every pool, each one carrying a fragment of the calculation between its wings.

They are loud too. They are most of the sound.

## The Light, The Air

Normal light—daylight, ordinary daylight, which is the strangest thing of all in a place so strange. It comes down green-filtered and even, no sun visible, a steady diffuse glow as though the sky itself were one more soft wet ceiling. The air is thick. It condenses on everything; the towers weep, the pads bead, your own skin goes immediately damp and stays that way. Warm. Bathwater-warm. Breathing it is like drinking.

## What It Is

A town that was given a mind and then given to the water, and asked—by no one still present—to keep thinking. It has settled almost all the way down into being only a marsh. Almost. The grid still holds. The signal still runs. The towers still lean and right themselves on the breathing tide.

It is finishing something. Slowly, in turbulence, at the bottom of the world. And it does not need you to know what.

You should leave before slack tide ends. When the water starts to fall, the avenues close.
