There is no plant here, and yet the place is crowded.

Begin with the floor. It is not soil — soil requires the long collaboration of roots and rot, and nothing has ever rooted here. It is a pavement of mineral tubes, hollow stone reeds, each the width of a wrist, standing close-packed and vertical like the pipes of an organ that grew instead of being built. They are pale grey shot through with rust and the green of wet copper. They are young; you can tell because their edges are still sharp, their rims unsoftened, broken stubs showing clean fracture rather than weathered crumble. Water has not yet had the centuries it needs to round them down.

And there is water. It rises inside the tubes from somewhere below, and it weeps from the broken rims, and it stands in the gaps between them in long dark slivers. The whole field sweats. After what passes for rain here — a slow descending mist that never quite resolves into drops — the surface gleams as if lacquered, and the gaps brim and overflow, sheeting water from tube to tube in a thousand small spillways. The air is always near to condensing. Walk through it and you collect moisture on your skin without ever feeling a drop fall.

It is cool. Not cold — nothing cracks with frost — but cool the way a cellar is cool, a temperature that seems to belong to the stone rather than the sky. Your breath would show.

Now the sound. This is the thing you will not be ready for. The tubes are not silent. Water moving in hollow stone makes a voice, and there are uncountable hollow stones, and so the field roars. Not one roar but layered ones — the deep gargle of the wide tubes near the spring-points, the high singing whistle where wind crosses a narrow rim, the constant percussive plink and gulp and swallow of overflow finding its way down. It is the sound of a flooded cathedral, of a hundred fountains played by something that does not understand rest. The loudness is total and steady; you stop hearing it as event and start hearing it as the shape of the air itself. To speak you would have to lean close and shout, and even then the field would take half your words.

The light is dim, and the source is uncertain. The sky — if it is sky — is a low even ceiling of pale grey that gives no shadows and no sun. Light here is a quality of the mist, a softened greyness that fills the gaps between the tubes with depth without ever brightening into day. Distances dissolve. The field of pipes extends outward past the immediate, valley-wide, a basin of standing stone reeds receding into vapor until the far ones are only a texture, a grey nap on the world, and beyond that nothing resolves at all.

Living things are rare but they are here. In the standing water between tubes, something pale moves — not fish, not quite. Soft segmented things the length of a finger, eyeless, that climb the inner walls of the tubes and graze whatever mineral film grows there. They are the only animals, and they are slow, and they make no sound that could be heard over the water. Once in a long while one will be flushed up and out of a rim by a surge, and lie stranded and gleaming on the wet stone, and slowly work itself back into a gap. There are no birds. There is nothing that flies. The vertical air belongs entirely to mist and noise.

The strangeness builds the longer you stay. You begin to notice that the tubes are not randomly placed. There is a grain to them, a faint spiral organization across the whole basin, as though the field were the cross-section of something enormous and coiled, sliced flat and filled with water. And the water's voice, layered as it is, sometimes seems to phase — a hundred separate spillways briefly aligning into a single swell of tone, a chord that holds for three or four seconds and then disperses back into chaos. This happens slowly, on a cycle you can almost time but never quite. It is the field's only fast event in a place where everything else changes across ages.

And there are the marks of others. Scattered, never gathered: here a low wall of tubes that have been broken to a single even height and packed with smaller stones, deliberately, to make a bench or a barrier — you cannot tell which. There a single standing slab, dragged from somewhere with no slab in it, set upright in a cleared circle and grooved with channels that catch the weeping water and route it into a carved bowl. The bowl is always full. Whoever cut it understood that here you do not need to fetch water; you only need to give it somewhere to gather. No tools remain. No dwellings. Just these few patient interventions, very old, their sharp edges gone soft while the natural tubes around them stay crisp — so the made things are older than the stone that surrounds them, which should not be possible, and is, and is part of why the place feels watched.

Nothing hurries here. The tubes grow, but over lifetimes — a rim thickening by the width of a hair in a year, mineral patiently laid down by the rising water. The animals graze. The mist descends. The far edge of the basin never comes any nearer no matter how long you walk, because the walking is slow and the basin is vast and the grey eats your progress.

What stays with you, afterward, is not the sight. It is the sound, and the way the sound had a shape, and the way that once or twice, in the aligning of the chord, you were almost certain the field was about to say a word.
