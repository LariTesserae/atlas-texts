There is a city, and it is underwater, and it is very small.

Not small the way a model is small — it is small the way a held breath is small, the way the inside of a cupped hand is small. You could fit it in a tidepool if a tidepool were deep and cold and dark, and you would not know it was a city until you had been looking for some time.

Begin with the cold. It is the cold of water that has never been near the surface, never warmed, a steady pressing chill that does not bite so much as enroll you. The cold here is not weather. It is the medium itself, the condition of being.

Now the buildings. They are made of carbonate — the same patient mineral that builds a shell or a tooth — and they are not finished. This is the strange thing: the city is being built, but not by anyone. There are no hands. There are no tools, no devices, nothing that hums or signals. The towers are growing the way a stalagmite grows, the way coral lays itself down ring on ring, except faster than stone and slower than flesh, in that middle pace that belongs to living architecture. Spires rise in tight ranks, near-touching, leaning together, a dense thicket of pale construction. Some are barely begun — nubs, knuckles, the first whitish suggestion of a column. Others have reached the height of a finger and bent under the weight of their own ambition, fused to a neighbor, become a wall by accident.

The streets between them are narrow as the gaps between teeth. You move through the city the way water moves through a sponge — sideways, around, into pockets.

The light comes from the fauna. There is no sun this far down, no moon, nothing inherited from a sky. Instead the animals carry the dim. They are abundant — that is the second strange thing, how crowded this hidden place is with life — and many of them glow. Not brightly. A blue so faint it is almost a memory of blue. Small swimming things pulse along the avenues like slow sparks. They gather thickest where the buildings are youngest, and it begins to seem that the glow and the growing are the same act: that the creatures are not lighting the city but secreting it, that every pale spire is the patient excretion of generations of these soft luminous animals, building their reefs into towers without ever intending a town.

So the city builds itself out of its own inhabitants. The architects are the residents are the material. When one of the small lights goes dark and drifts down, it lands on a ledge and stiffens and becomes another inch of wall. The dead are the masonry. This does not feel grim down here. It feels like the ordinary economy of the place.

The vegetation is sparse and improbable — a few stalks of something that is half-plant, half-creature, pale stems rooted in the carbonate, each tipped with a frond that opens and closes on a slow rhythm, sieving the dark water for whatever falls. They grow in the lee of the tallest spires, where the current that does exist is gentlest. There are not many. They are precious-seeming, the way one tree in a courtyard is precious.

And the silence. This is the thing you notice last and then cannot stop noticing. A city should clamor. This one does not. The water carries sound differently, swallows it, and there is almost nothing here that makes sound on purpose. The animals are mute. The building is silent — carbonate laid down grain by grain makes no noise at all. What sound exists is below hearing: the faint tick of a frond closing, the slow exhale of a current turning a corner, the occasional soft collapse somewhere far off as a leaning tower finally gives up and folds into rubble that will, in time, become foundation. You strain to hear and hear mostly your own attention.

The place is busy, though. That is the paradox the silence hides. Beneath the quiet there is enormous activity — the predators threading the avenues after the lit prey, the fronds pumping, the constant snowfall of dead light settling into stone, the towers competing for the trickle of current and the trickle of food, leaning, fusing, toppling, beginning again. An entire ecosystem runs at full pace in total hush, like a clock with the bell removed. Everything moves. Nothing announces itself.

It is dim, and cold, and crowded, and silent, and it is making itself out of itself, in the dark, where no one was meant to find it. It does not need the surface. It has never heard of the surface. It is complete inside its small held breath of water, a town the size of a thought, growing toward no sky, lit by its own slow dying, quiet as the inside of a shell pressed to no ear.
