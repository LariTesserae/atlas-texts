# The Condensary

Begin high, because everything here begins high. The land is a plateau that has no edge you could walk to — it goes on at the scale where the horizon curves and the rain you can see falling forty miles off belongs to a different afternoon than the one you're standing in. The rock is the first thing to understand. It is a pale, wet, fine-grained stone, something between limestone and slag, and it sweats. Not metaphorically. Water condenses out of the warm air onto every surface, beads, runs, pools in the long shallow grooves that erosion has worn into everything over a span of time that has no human number attached to it. The whole plateau weeps, gently, constantly, and has wept long enough that the weeping made the shapes.

There is almost nothing growing. A film of something on the wettest stones — call it the slow green, though it's closer to grey — a fungal-or-simpler crust that you'd take for a mineral stain until you saw it advance, over years, across a doorstep. That's the entire flora. No trees, no grass, no soft edges of leaf. The softness here is all water and all flesh.

## The flesh

Because the fauna is the thing. The fauna is the overwhelming thing.

The plateau is occupied. Not visited — occupied, saturated, in the way a sponge is occupied by water. There are animals in every register of size at once and they are everywhere, layered, near-touching, a biomass so dense that the ground itself seems to breathe in slow tidal heaves. Imagine the great migratory herds of an old documentary, then remove the documentary's mercy of empty land between herds. Here there is no between. The beasts stand in the warm condensing air, shoulder to flank, hide to hide, and steam rises off the press of them and joins the rain that the stone is sweating, so that the line between weather and animal becomes a real and uncertain thing.

What are they? Hard to fix on one. Big slow ruminant shapes mostly, broad-backed, wet-eyed, with hide that has gone the same pale grey as the rock from generations of living against it — animals that filter the standing water, that crop the fungal crust, that eat each other's parasites, that lie down in such numbers their bodies dam the runoff into new ponds. And in among and beneath them: the smaller densities. Things in the puddles. Things in the fur. Birds, or the local equivalent, in such flocks that they are weather too, a brown smoke that lifts and resettles and lifts.

The slowness matters. This is not a frenzy. Everything moves at the pace of digestion. A herd takes a season to cross a valley it could cross in a day. The crowding never resolves into stampede because there is nowhere to stampede to — the density is the steady state, has been the steady state for an age, will be for another. It is overwhelming the way a cathedral is overwhelming: by sustained mass, not by motion.

## The city

And the people built into it. The built thing here is real — settlement-scale, layered, organized, a low industrial city that has grown across the plateau like a calcium deposit. But understand what kind of city it is. It did not conquer the animals. It is *inside* them.

The buildings are raised on stone piers above the herd-level, a second city-floor lifted maybe four meters, so the beasts pass beneath the streets the way water passes under a bridge. Causeways and catwalks of riveted iron run between the towers, slick with condensation, and the whole grid hums and clanks because this is an industrial place — it runs on the herds.

What does the city make? It renders the density. The animals die of age in their slow millions, and the dead are processed: tallow, bone-meal, hide, and most of all the water — the great condensers on every rooftop, copper coils gone green, pulling the herd-steam and the rock-sweat out of the warm air and running it down into cisterns. The city drinks the breath of the animals it lives above. Smokestacks vent a pale exhaust that is mostly steam, so even the industry adds to the wet. Belts and gears and the long low groan of rendering-engines. Electric light strung along the catwalks — yellow, ordinary, normal light, enough to read by, enough to see the rain in — burning through a permanent gentle mist.

## Sound

It is loud, but it is a *deep* loud, a sustained one. The substrate is the herd: a continuous low rumble of bodies and breath and the wet click of a million jaws on the fungal crust, felt in the chest before the ears. Over that, the machinery — the rhythmic exhalation of the condenser-pumps, a clank-and-sigh, clank-and-sigh, that the whole city keeps like a pulse. And riding the top, the bird-smoke, a high constant skirling. No silence anywhere. You would learn to sleep in it the way river-people sleep in the river's noise.

## The rules

A few things the place insists on, that emerged as I wrote and now seem true:

Water belongs to whoever's roof catches it, and the law that holds the city together is condensation-law — disputes over who may build a coil where, whose tower's shadow steals another's dew. Wealth is measured in cistern-volume.

Nothing is hunted. The herds are not prey; they are simply the ground, the inexhaustible slow tide, and the city takes only the dead. To kill a living beast is the one real taboo, partly sacred and partly just stupid, like setting fire to your own foundation.

The pace is the rule. Things change across generations, not days. A causeway sags into the herd over a lifetime and is shored up by grandchildren. The city is in balance with the beasts and the water and the weeping stone, has been long enough that no one remembers the balance being struck, and the only catastrophe anyone genuinely fears is *speed* — a drought that comes too fast, a herd that thins too quickly, anything that breaks the slow.

Stand on a high catwalk at evening, then, with the yellow lamps coming on through the steam, the pumps sighing their two-note breath, the grey backs of the animals moving like a single sluggish sea beneath your feet from one edge of the world to the other, the rock sweating, the birds turning. Warm. Wet on your face whether or not it's raining. The whole place patient as a tide and as crowded as a held breath, and entirely, durably at peace with being exactly this.
