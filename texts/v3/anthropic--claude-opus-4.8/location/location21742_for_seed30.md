There is a plateau that has not finished being a plateau.

You arrive at it the way you arrive at a thought that isn't yours yet — sideways, a little dizzy, your ears already full. The first fact is the sound. It comes up out of the ground rather than across it: a low, wet, continuous working, like a thousand throats clearing at the bottom of a well, punctuated by sharper noises — clicks, drips that ring too long, a tearing sound that repeats on no schedule you can find. The air carries it warm and close against your face. This is a loud place that is mostly water and animals making the water move.

The terrain is raw. Not ancient-raw, not the worn nubs of old mountains — *new*. The rock here is in the process of deciding to be rock. In places it is still gathering itself: pale grey material that has the wet sheen of something just extruded, edges that have not yet been cut by anything, no scree, no talus fans, no rounded shoulders. Where a normal high country would show you ten thousand years of weather written in its slopes, this country shows you almost nothing, because almost nothing has happened to it. It is high — you can feel the thinness wanting to start in your chest — but the height has no history. The cliffs are sheer because nothing has eroded them, not because anything carved them.

And it is small. You can walk the whole of it, probably, in an afternoon, if the afternoon holds still. A bowl maybe a kilometer across, ringed by these unfinished cliffs, the floor of it stepped into shallow terraces that hold water. That's where the wetness lives — in the terraces, in seeps that bead out of the cliff faces and run in thin bright threads, in the way the whole atmosphere stays just at the edge of condensing, so that surfaces wear a film and your skin never quite dries.

Now the strangeness, which is everywhere once you notice it.

The seeps don't fall the way water falls. They run *outward* sometimes, beading along the underside of overhangs, collecting into droplets that hang and swell and then travel — not drop, travel — sideways to join a thread that climbs a few centimeters before remembering itself and pouring down. There is a discipline to the water here that water shouldn't have. You get the sense it is being managed.

It is. This is the tech parameter, and it does not look like machinery. It looks like the rock is being grown.

Down in the wettest terraces, if you crouch and let your eyes adjust to the dim — the light here is a soft even grey, sourceless, as if the whole sky were one cloud lit from very far behind — you can see the working faces. The new rock isn't cooling from lava and it isn't precipitating from minerals in the ordinary slow way. It is being *laid down*, molecule by molecule, by something. The surface advances in a slow crawling front, fed by the water, and the water is fed by the seeps, and the seeps come from somewhere up in the cliffs where the same process is running in reverse, taking material apart and sending it down dissolved. The plateau is building itself. The erosion reading is near zero not because nothing wears away here but because wearing-away and building-up have been fused into a single deliberate cycle. Nothing is lost. Everything is being moved with intention, atom by atom, by an engineering so fine it has no visible engine.

The scattered built things are part of this and also older than it. Across the bowl, spaced wide apart in the sparse low scrub, stand small structures — you'd call them shrines before you'd call them anything else. Each is about the size of a kiln or a beehive, made of the same pale material as the cliffs but finished, smoothed, *done* in a way nothing natural here is done. They are warm to the touch. They hum below the sound-floor, a vibration you feel in your teeth more than hear. These are the seeds. The whole plateau is being grown out from them. They are the slow patient machines, if machine is the word, and they have been at it for — there's no way to know. Long enough to make a kilometer of mountain. Not long enough to make two.

The vegetation is sparse and odd and clearly came after. Tough low plants, blue-green, with leaves like wet rubber, clustered where the running threads of water pass. They lean toward the seed-structures the way ordinary plants lean toward the sun. There aren't many. They leave great open spaces of bare new rock between them, and in those spaces the animals run.

The fauna is the abundance here, and they are the loudest thing and the strangest. Picture something between a frog and a swift — long-legged, smooth-skinned, kept perpetually glistening by the condensing air, moving in fast skittering bursts across the wet terraces and then freezing utterly. They are everywhere once your eye learns them: in the pools, on the cliff faces walking up the vertical wet stone, perched on the warm shrines. They are the throats in the well-sound. They call in dense overlapping waves, and the calls *carry the cycle* — I think they are part of it. When a chorus rises in one terrace, the building-front in that terrace quickens. When they go quiet, it slows. Whether they are tending the place or merely living off its rhythm and have synchronized to it the way frogs sync to rain — I couldn't tell you. The place is too active, too many processes braided together, predator and prey and seep and growth and the constant management of water, all of it moving at a biological pace, all of it loud.

There are larger things too, glimpsed. Something that comes to the pools at the dim hour that passes for dusk, drinks, and is gone before you've fixed its shape. The small fast animals scatter from it and the chorus drops to almost nothing and in that hush you can hear, for once, the rock itself: the faint continuous crystalline *tick* of matter being set in place, a sound like a vast slow rain falling on the inside of stone.

The rules of this place, as near as I can phrase them:

Nothing here is finished, and nothing is wasted. What comes apart in one place is carried, in solution, to assemble in another. The water is the bloodstream of a thing too large and too slow to recognize as alive, and the small wet animals are its pulse, and the warm humming shrines are its seeds or its hearts, and the whole bowl of unfinished mountain is a single organism in the embryonic stage of becoming geography. You are standing inside something that has not yet decided what it will be. Come back in a thousand years and there will be a real mountain here, weathered, ordinary, silent — and somewhere on its new flank, a seed already taking the first wet steps toward the next.

For now it is warm, and dim, and very loud, and it drips upward, and it is growing.
