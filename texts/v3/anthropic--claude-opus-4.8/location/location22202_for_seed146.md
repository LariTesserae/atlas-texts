There is a cavity in the rock the size of a sea, and it has never seen the sun.

Begin with the floor, because the floor is where you would land if you fell, and falling is the only way in. The floor is not stone in the way surface stone is stone. It is younger than that. It is salt — not white salt, but the dark amber salt that forms where warm brine once stood and then was not water anymore, drawn off into the rock by some slow capillary thirst until only the mineral remained. The salt is fresh-edged. It has not had time to round. Where it has grown into terraces it grows in blades and shelves with corners that would open your hand if you trusted them, and you would trust them, because in the dark you read the world by hand.

It is warm here. Not the warmth of fire — there is no fire, fire needs air that moves and light that climbs — but the patient warmth of being deep, the rock's own body heat held close, a little above blood. You do not shiver in this place. You sweat, slightly, and the sweat does not evaporate because there is almost no water hunger left in the air to take it. So you stay damp and warm, like something held in a mouth.

Now look — except you cannot look, there is barely light. What light there is comes from the salt itself, or from the things that live on the salt. A faint amber luminescence, low as a coal an hour dead. It does not illuminate; it suggests. You see the cavity not as a room but as a set of rumors: a glow over there means a terrace, a darker dark means a drop, a scatter of paler points means the fauna is feeding.

The fauna. They are abundant, and they are quiet, and they nearly touch each other in the dark.

They are not animals you have names for. The closest word is *grazers*, but what they graze is the salt and the slow films that grow on the salt — not plants, nothing so ambitious as a plant has ever happened here, but mats of something simpler, a chemistry that learned to copy itself before it learned to be green. The grazers are low and broad and soft-shelled, the size of dinner plates and the size of doors, and they move on a hundred small feet that make almost no sound — a dry whisper, a sound like someone turning a page very slowly in another room. They are everywhere. They are layered. Where the terraces step down toward the deeper basin they crowd onto each other, two and three deep, a living pavement that breathes faintly in unison, and the amber glow comes mostly from them, pulsing slow, out of phase, so the whole sea-floor seems to shimmer like a banked hearth seen through half-closed eyes.

They do not change. This is the strangest thing, and the place is strange in many ways, but this is the heart of its strangeness: nothing here is going anywhere. The grazers graze and the mats regrow at exactly the rate they are grazed, and the salt is laid down at exactly the rate it is taken up, and the warmth is neither rising nor falling, and it has been this way for longer than the surface has had continents. There are no seasons. There is no morning. There is no slow decline. The cavity reached its balance and then simply *stopped being a process* and became a condition. A frozen equilibrium that is not frozen at all — it is warm, it is alive, it is in constant gentle motion — and yet if you came back in a thousand years you would find the same grazers on the same terraces glowing the same amber, and you would not be able to prove the time had passed.

And yet — there are marks.

This is the part that does not fit, and the place makes you hold it anyway. Scattered across the salt, never two close together, are *built things*. Small. Made by hands, or by something that wanted what hands want. Cairns of stacked salt-blades, balanced impossibly, corner on corner. A low wall, three courses high, enclosing nothing. A bowl carved from a single block of the amber mineral, set on a pedestal, empty, dry, dustless. The tools that made them lie nearby sometimes — chipped salt scrapers, a hammerstone of darker rock carried down from somewhere else, fibrous cord of unknown origin gone stiff. Primitive work. Patient work. The work of people who had only their hands and time, and time they had in an amount that does not mean anything.

But there are no people. There are no bones, no hearths, no middens, no later building atop the early building. Just the artifacts, scattered, finished, and abandoned in a world that does not allow abandonment to *progress* into ruin — because nothing erodes here on any scale you'd feel, the erosion is young, the edges are still sharp, the cairns are as crisp as the day they were balanced, whenever that was. So the makers are not gone in the way the dead are gone. They are gone in the way a sound is gone after it stops. They were here. The place did not record how long ago, because the place does not record time. It only holds.

Listen. Mostly there is nothing to hear. The page-turning of the grazers. A very occasional tick as a salt-blade somewhere finishes growing and settles against its neighbor — the only event this world permits itself, the small adjustments of crystal, spaced minutes or hours apart, each one absurdly loud in all that held breath. And under everything, almost not a sound at all, a sub-bass that you feel in the salt under your palms more than you hear: the rock breathing its warmth, the long deep note of pressure, the cavity being vast enough to have a voice and choosing to use it only at the very bottom of hearing.

It is vast. That has to be said again because the dark hides it and you might forget. This is not a cave. A cave you can hold in your mind. This is large enough to have its own weather — and it does, faintly: warm air rising off the densest grazer-beds, cooling against the unseen ceiling far above, settling again at the cold margins, a circulation so slow and so unchanging that it is less a wind than a permanent slow exhalation, always from the same direction, always at the same strength, a breath the cavity took once and never finished letting go.

If you stay long enough — and you will, because there is no morning to leave by — you begin to understand the rule of the place, the law under all its laws: *here, nothing is allowed to end, because nothing is allowed to begin.* The makers could build but could not found a civilization. The grazers could feed but could not multiply past the line. The salt could grow but could not bury anything. Everything that happens, happens exactly to the degree that it can be undone by the next slow turn, so that the sum is always zero and the sum is always alive.

And you, warm and damp and barely able to see, sitting on the amber floor with one hand on a cairn that someone balanced an unmeasurable while ago — you are the only thing in the cavity that is still capable of leaving. That is what makes you strange to it. The grazers drift a little nearer, glowing, curious in the dim incurious way of things that have never had a reason to be curious before. You have brought the one thing the place lacks. You have brought a tomorrow into a world that ended its tomorrows long ago and has been perfectly, warmly, quietly content ever since.
