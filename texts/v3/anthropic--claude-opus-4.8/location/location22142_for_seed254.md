There is a city beneath the surface, and it is made entirely of plants.

You come into it the way you come into a fog — not crossing a threshold so much as noticing, gradually, that the air has thickened and that everything around you is breathing. The descent is gentle. The land does not drop into this place so much as lean toward it, a long sloping throat lined with moss that gives underfoot like wet bread. By the time you understand you are below the surface, the surface is already a memory of brightness somewhere up past the canopy of roots.

Call it a hamlet's worth of space. Not vast. You could walk its length in the time it takes to grow tired, and you would meet its far wall — a living wall, a palisade of trunks fused side to side, weeping at every seam. The scale is intimate, room-sized in its bones, but packed so completely that smallness becomes its own kind of immensity. There is no gap anywhere. No clearing. No empty corner where you might stand and see the whole. The place is saturated with itself.

The architecture is the plants, and the plants have learned the habits of architecture. Long settlement has taught them this. You can see how old the arrangement is — centuries, maybe, of patient negotiation between stem and stem until the forms settled into something a builder would recognize. There are streets here: corridors where the floor-moss has been worn into a paler channel by water and by whatever traffic the centuries provided, the walls rising on either side in tiers of bracket fungus and shelved root that read, unmistakably, as storeys. There are doorways — gaps held open by the deliberate splay of two great boles, lintels formed by horizontal limbs grown thick as beams. There are towers, of a sort: vertical columns of vine wound so tight around dead heartwood that they have stiffened into spires, and from their tops hang seed-heads like lamps that do not light.

Everything weeps. The water is near, just under everything, just over everything. It beads on the undersides of leaves and falls in slow irregular percussion. It films the bark and runs down the grooves of the columns in threads that catch the dim light and carry it. The walls sweat. The floor is never dry; press your hand to the moss and it answers cold and wet, and the print fills back in behind your fingers before you have lifted them. There is a sound to all this water that never stops — drip and trickle and the deeper gurgle of channels running somewhere out of sight, water finding its old paths through the established stone-that-is-not-stone of the root-foundations.

It is warm. Not the dry warmth of sun but the warmth of a closed greenhouse, of compost working, of breath held in a crowd. The air is heavy with it, and heavy with moisture, so that the two combine into something you wear rather than breathe. Sweat will not leave your skin here. The warmth comes up from below, from the great slow burning of decay in the deep beds where the roots feed, and it rises through the whole construction and condenses on the cooler upper reaches and falls again as the rain that never was sky-rain. The place makes its own weather and recycles it forever.

And it is loud. This is the strangest thing, and the first thing — that a place with no animals in it should roar. But it does. The water is part of it, the endless ten thousand small fallings. But under that there is the industry. For this is a working city, and its work is mechanical.

Somewhere in the long establishment of this place, the plants achieved engines. Not metal — there is no metal here, no spark of the made-world — but the principle of the engine, the harnessed and repeating force. Great bladder-organs swell and collapse with the pressure of rising sap and gas, and they drive things: wheels of woody fiber that turn slow and groaning on axles of polished root; bellows-pods that wheeze open and shut, open and shut, pushing the warm air down the corridors; presses that crush the fallen mast into oil that runs in the channels and lubricates the turning. The whole place is a factory whose product is its own continuance. The noise of it is the noise of any mill town — the rhythmic heave and knock, the long sustained grind, the hiss of vented steam where heat meets seep-water and flashes off — but slowed, all of it slowed, the tempo of a machine the size of a city running at the speed of growing wood. A piston-stroke that takes an hour. A wheel that completes one turn in a day. You stand in the din and realize that nothing here is moving fast, that the loudness is not violence but volume, the sound of enormous patience under enormous load.

The light is dim and comes from no single source. Some of it filters from above, the green-gone-grey of light strained through too many leaves. Some seems to come from the plants themselves — a faint phosphorescence in certain shelf-fungi, a luminance in the standing water that may only be reflection. Enough to see by. Not enough to see far. The corridors fade into a soft dark twenty paces on, and the towers vanish into a haze of suspended droplet long before their tops. You live, here, inside a sphere of the visible that moves with you, and beyond it the loud unseen machinery goes on working.

There are no animals. No flit of wing, no scurry, no eye watching from the dark. This is a purity that takes some time to register, and when it does it changes the feeling of the place entirely. Every sound is structure. Every motion is the plant-machine doing its plant-machine work. Nothing here will turn to look at you, because there is nothing here that looks. You are the only thing with a face. You move through a city wholly occupied and wholly unwitnessed, packed wall to wall with presence and empty of any company.

The rules of the place, as you come to understand them, are these:

Nothing is empty. The density permits no void; any space that opens is filled within a season by the next thing growing into it. There are no rooms to be alone in.

Nothing is fast. The dynamic of the place is the dynamic of timber and tide — change happens, but across durations that make a human visit a single held breath. The wheel will turn. You will not see it complete the turn.

Nothing is dry. The water is the medium and the message both, the thing that drives the engines and the thing they breathe out, falling and rising and falling.

And nothing is strange, finally — that is the last surprising thing. For all its impossibility, the place is not eerie, not uncanny, not wrong. It is only thoroughly, exhaustively itself: a wet warm dim packed loud green machine of a town, long settled into its own working, far below the light, going about the slow business of being exactly what it is.

You could stay. It would not notice. It would simply make room — eventually, slowly — and grow around you too.
