# The Canopy at the Roof of the World

Begin with the height, because everything here begins with the height.

You are higher than weather usually goes, higher than mountains have any business letting things grow — and yet the green is total. This is the first thing that refuses to make sense, and the place will ask you to hold several such refusals at once. There are mountains here, but they are old mountains, ground down across a span of time that the mind cannot grip — not the jagged young peaks that knife into thunderclouds, but vast rounded shoulders of stone, worn nearly smooth, the way a riverbed cobble is smooth, the way a tooth is smooth after a lifetime of use. Ancient stone at impossible altitude. The summits do not stab; they swell. They are the backs of something enormous that lay down before the first thing crawled, and never got up, and was slowly buried in itself.

And over all of it: the forest.

Not a forest growing *on* the mountains. The forest *is* the surface. The plant-mass has become the ground you would walk on, layer upon layer of root and stem and rotting trunk and new shoot, a substrate of living and half-living matter so deep that the original stone is a rumor beneath your feet, felt only as a hardness somewhere far below, the way you feel your own bones. You do not walk on earth here. You walk on the accumulated body of ten thousand generations of plants, sprung and fallen and sprung again from each other's corpses. The whole high country is upholstered in life.

But — and here is the second refusal — it is nearly empty.

## The Hollowness

For all that green, the space *between* things is vast. The trees are titanic and far apart. Each one is a cathedral of a single organism: a trunk wide as a tower, rising out of sight, holding up a roof of leaf so high and so broad that beneath it lies a great dim hollow hall, columns of mist standing in the spaces, and then — a long emptiness — and then the next tree. You could walk for a day in the understory and pass only a handful of these giants. The density is almost nothing. The volume is colossal and mostly air, warm and damp and still, cathedral-empty, the way the inside of a bell is empty before it is struck.

The plants have eaten the world and then spread themselves thin across the top of it, the way a single sheet of gold leaf can be beaten across a whole roof. Total vegetation, near-total void. Both are true. The forest is everywhere and there is almost nothing in it.

## The Sound

Except — there is no *almost* about the sound.

The silence you would expect from emptiness does not come. Instead the air is a wall. The noise here is the loudest thing about the place, louder than the wind that should howl at this height and somehow doesn't, louder than anything you have stood inside before. It comes from the fauna, and the fauna is the third refusal, the deepest one.

Because where are they?

You hear them in the millions. A shrieking, sawing, droning, hammering, trilling, booming chorus that has no gap in it, no single voice you can pull free — a solid sound, a substance, pressure against the eardrum from every direction at every height of the canopy at once. Insects that must number past counting. Throat-singers in the high leaves. Things that clap, things that knock wood, things that ring like struck metal, things that make a sound like rain falling upward. A din so total that it stops being sound and becomes the medium you move through, the way water is the medium a fish forgets.

And yet you can walk an hour and *see* nothing larger than your hand.

The fauna is overwhelming and invisible. It lives in the green ceiling, in the deep substrate underfoot, in the hollow trunks, in the layers — never in the open halls where a body moves. The empty space is a space the animals have agreed to leave empty. They press against its edges, all of them, the whole roaring mass of them, packed into the living architecture, and the great central void is a held breath in the middle of an unending scream. You move through the one quiet that exists, and it is not quiet at all.

## Water

It is damp but not wet. The moisture comes sideways and from below — condensation beading on the underside of leaves the size of sails, dripping in slow irregular percussion you can't hear over the chorus but can feel on the back of your neck. Mist pools in the hollows between the giant trees and burns off and gathers again. Now and then a real rain comes, brief and warm, and the whole forest changes pitch — the roar deepens, the invisible millions adjusting — and then it passes and the dripping resumes its private clockwork. There are no rivers up here. The water never gathers enough to run. It hangs, it beads, it breathes in and out of the green. The place is moist the way a lung is moist.

## The Marks of Hands

Scattered through all of this — rarely, so rarely you might decide you imagined the last one before you find the next — are the signs that something with hands once lived here, or still does.

A platform lashed into a fork of one of the great trees, the bindings made of braided plant-fiber, grey with age, the wood beneath it gone soft. A row of notches cut in a trunk, deliberate, spaced for a foot, leading up into the canopy and stopping at nothing. A bowl of fired clay, cracked, half-swallowed by a creeping stem, sitting in a hollow as though set down a moment ago and forgotten for a thousand years. A net, or the memory of a net, its mesh half-rotted, still strung between two roots, still — and this is the unsettling part — still seeming to wait for something.

No metal, or almost none. What was made here was made by hand, from the forest, given back to the forest. The builders left no walls, no roads, nothing that fought the green. They worked *with* it, lightly, the way you might rest your hand on the flank of a sleeping animal. And they are gone, or nearly gone, scattered to the same emptiness as everything else. You will not meet them. You will only keep finding the soft grey evidence that someone, patient and few, climbed up to the roof of the world and lived in the spaces the animals left, and listened to that wall of sound every day of their lives, and made bowls.

## The Rule of the Place

Here is what governs this world, if anything does: *everything has had time to settle into what it most truly is.* The mountains have finished being mountains and become long swells of patience. The plants have finished competing and become the ground itself. The animals have finished spreading and filled every surface so completely that the open air is the only frontier left, and they have chosen — for reasons no human builder ever decoded — to leave it alone. The erosion here is not destruction. It is *arrival*. This is the final form. Nothing is on its way to becoming anything. It already became.

You stand in the warm dim hollow between two trees older than your species, ankle-deep in the soft body of the forest, mist standing in columns, water beading and dropping unheard, and the sound of ten million unseen living things pressing on you like deep water — and you understand that you have walked into a place that finished a long time ago and simply kept going, full and empty and screaming and still, at the very top of everything.
