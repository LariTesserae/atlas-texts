# The Worndown City

There is a city here, but you must learn to see it. The first lesson is patience; the second is forgetting what cities are supposed to look like.

You stand on a high shoulder of rock — call it a plateau, though *plateau* implies the violence of being lifted, and nothing here remembers violence. Everything has been smoothed. The mountains that must once have stood around this place are gone, reduced to long swells the color of old teeth, and the city sits in a saddle between them at an altitude where the air is thin enough to make your skull ring.

It is cold. Not the sharp cold of winter but a settled, patient cold, the cold of stone that has not been warm in a very long time. Your breath fogs and hangs, reluctant to disperse in the still air.

## The Stones That Were Walls

The buildings have been worn nearly back into the ground. This is the strangeness of the place: it was clearly *organized* — there are streets, you can feel the streets under your boots as faint troughs, the long memory of where feet went and went and went until the rock itself gave way. There are walls, but the walls are knee-high now, rounded over like loaves of bread, like things sucked smooth. Whatever was carved into them eroded away epochs ago. Run your hand along a wall-stub and you feel no edge, no joint, no seam — just the cool continuous flow of granite that has forgotten it was ever cut.

Doorways survive as gaps. Towers survive as nubs. There is a great rounded mound that must have been something enormous — a hall, a temple, a market under a vault — and now it is a low dome you could walk over without noticing, except that it rings hollow underfoot, and somewhere far below in that hollow, water moves.

The density is the thing that unsettles. A city should be full. This one is nearly empty — vast distances between one worn stub and the next, courtyards the size of fields, an emptiness that the surviving stone organizes without filling. You understand suddenly that the emptiness *is* the city. The buildings were always mostly air; time has simply taken the walls and left the rooms.

## Water

Moisture comes and goes. Most hours the air is merely damp, beading on the lichen, gathering in the worn cups of the stones where rainwater stands black and still and reflects the bright cold sky. Then a cloud crosses the saddle — they come fast at this height, ragged grey things — and rains for ten minutes, hard, drumming on stone, and stops. The water runs into the old streets because the streets are the lowest channels, and for a while the dead city has rivers, thin silver threads following routes that traffic wore a hundred thousand years ago. Then they sink into the hollow places and are gone, and you hear them muttering underground.

## What Grows

Despite the cold, despite the height, it is green here. Lush, even, in a low and clinging way. Cushion plants pack the cracks — dense pincushions of tiny leaves, hard as cartilage, studded sometimes with flowers so small you'd take them for grains of colored sand. Moss sheets the north faces of every stub in deep velvet. There are ferns in the wet hollows, unrolling slow fiddleheads, and a kind of trailing vine, dark and leathery, that has colonized the worn domes and drapes off them like hair off a skull.

The green softens nothing. It only emphasizes how thoroughly the made things have been unmade — the plants don't grow *on* ruins, they grow on *terrain*, having long ago stopped distinguishing.

## The Sound

And here is the contradiction the place insists on. Everything is ancient, emptied, smoothed, still — and yet it is *loud*.

The wind, first. At this height the wind moves through the saddle constantly, and the worn city is full of openings — the doorway-gaps, the hollow domes, the troughs of streets — and the wind plays all of them. The whole place is a ruined instrument. There is a deep continuous tone from the great hollow dome, felt in the chest more than heard. There are higher voices, reedy and shifting, from the smaller gaps. When the wind gusts, the city *sings*, a vast chord that swells and falls and never resolves, never repeats, an organ played by no one for no one across unmeasurable time.

The water adds to it: the drumming rains, the underground rivers gurgling beneath the hollow stones with a sound like distant applause, the drip and trickle from a thousand wet ledges. Stones, contracting in the cold, crack now and then with reports like gunshots that echo off the bald hills and roll away.

It is not quiet here. It has never been quiet here. The emptiness has a roaring voice.

## The Living

Life is rare and you feel it as event. A bird — large, dark, riding the wind on stiff wings — crosses the saddle perhaps once an hour, and never lands. Something small and grey lives in the cushion plants and you only ever see the grass-stems twitch where it passed. Twice a day, maybe, a herd of pale long-legged animals moves across the far swell of hill, too distant to name, leaving and gone.

The scarcity makes each creature enormous in the attention. When the dark bird crosses, the whole place seems to orient toward it.

## The Rule of This Place

I think the rule is this: *here, only time is allowed to build, and only slowly.* No hand has touched these stones in so long that the very idea of hands has eroded out of them. There are no tools, no devices, nothing made that is still being made. The city was finished — not completed, *finished*, the way a sentence finishes when the speaker dies. And the wind took over the work, and the water, and the moss, and they have been at it ever since, very patiently, with no intention to ruin and no intention to preserve, simply continuing.

You came up here expecting silence and a dead place. Instead you found something that won't stop singing — a city worn down to its breath, holding its single long unresolving chord in the thin cold air, full of empty rooms and the loud, ongoing, indifferent music of being slowly, gorgeously unmade.

Stay until dark and the chord changes pitch as the cold deepens. That is the only clock left.
