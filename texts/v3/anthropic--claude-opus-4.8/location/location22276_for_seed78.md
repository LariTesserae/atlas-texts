# The Hollow Canopy

Begin with the cold, because the cold is the first thing the body knows here. Not the bitter cold of ice — there is little ice — but the steady, marrow-deep chill of high places where the air thins and the sun, when it comes, comes weak and sideways. You are high. Higher than the cloud-deck most days. And it is dark.

The darkness is the second thing. Understand it: this is not night. There is no day to relieve it. The light that filters down — and it does filter, in long failing shafts — has crossed too much living matter to arrive as anything but a green-black dimness, a held-breath twilight that never resolves into morning. Your eyes adjust and adjust and find only more gradations of the same dark.

Now the third thing, the thing that makes this place itself: the vegetation. It is not *on* the landscape. It *is* the landscape. There is no soil you could dig to, no rock you could call bedrock — or if there is, it lies impossibly far below, beneath strata of root and stem and fiber laid down so thick and so long that the plant has become the ground, the wall, the ceiling, the air's container. You walk on plant. You breathe plant-exhalation. The mountains here are made of growing things.

---

Picture the structure, because it took me a while to picture it and I want you to see it clearly.

Imagine a forest, but vertical past all sense — trunks the width of valleys, rising kilometers, fusing overhead into a roof so dense and so high that it generates its own sky. Beneath that roof: emptiness. Vast hollow naves between the great stems, columns of cold dead air, spaces so large that mist gathers into slow internal weather, that a falling drop of condensation takes long seconds to reach whatever waits below. This is the paradox of the place. It is *total* vegetation and it is *almost entirely empty* — because the plant has built cathedrals, and cathedrals are mostly the space they enclose.

The density is near-void. You could walk for a day between two trunks and meet nothing — no animal, no second plant, no marker — only the floor of woven fiber underfoot, soft and faintly warm with slow decay, and far overhead the unseen ceiling, and on all sides the brown-green columns receding into dark until distance swallows them. The scale is such that these chambers have horizons. You lose the sense of being indoors or out. You are inside a living thing the size of a country, and the inside of that thing is mostly nothing.

---

The water comes as condensation. This high and this cold, the plant breathes moisture into the still air and the air cannot hold it; it beads on the underside of every great leaf-vault, gathers, swells, and falls. So there is rain inside the world — intermittent, local, unpredictable. A trunk a kilometer off may be sheeted in slow drip while you stand dry. The floor channels what falls into thin black runnels, riverlets that thread the fiber and pool in the low hollows and never quite gather into true rivers. Damp, not drowned. Pockets of wet in the great dryness of the upper air. You learn to read the drip-lines, to hear where water has decided to fall.

And you *can* hear it — this is the strangest sensory fact of the place. It is dark and nearly empty and yet it is **loud.**

---

The sound. I did not expect the sound.

The columns are resonant. The hollow chambers between them act as throats, as pipes, as the bodies of vast instruments, and the plant is never still. It creaks. The great trunks flex against their own weight and against the internal weather, and the flexing travels as deep groans, as percussive cracks like ice or gunfire, as long descending moans that roll between the columns and double back as echo. Wind that you cannot feel down here is screaming through the canopy a kilometer up, and what arrives below is the bass note of it, a continuous organ drone underneath everything. Water-falls drum. Things move in the dark and their movement carries.

You are never in silence. The emptiness is *acoustically full*. Sound is the medium by which the place asserts that it is alive and busy, even where your eyes report nothing at all. I think the living things here are made by this. I think they hunt and hide by ear, because eyes earn little in this dark.

---

The fauna, then — abundant, the parameter says, and so they are, though you will not often see them; you will hear them, and feel the air of their passing.

They are creatures of the vertical and the dark. The largest move along the trunks rather than the floor — broad, soft-footed, climbing things with limbs adapted to grip the fibrous bark, slow as the cold demands, patient, conserving. They make the long moaning calls that you mistake for the trunks themselves until one passes close. Smaller things glide between columns on membranes, navigating by sound, snapping it back off the great pillars and reading the echoes. There are pale crawling colonies in the wet runnels, eyeless, that strain the falling water for whatever drifts down from the canopy's distant ecology. And there are predators — the dynamic parameter insists on this, the ecosystem in motion — and you know them when the constant sound suddenly *stops*, a pocket of hush opening in the loud dark, all the smaller voices cutting out at once. That silence is the most frightening thing in the world here. It moves.

The whole place is in motion at a biological pace. The trunks themselves are not fixed; they grow, they buttress, they shed and rebuild. Sometimes a chamber closes over years as new growth fills it; sometimes a column dies and falls — an event of catastrophic scale, a slow thunder that reshapes the geography for kilometers and feeds the dark floor for a generation. Weather cycles inside. Mist gathers and breaks. The young-ness of the place is visible in this: it is still *forming*, sharp-featured, the fiber-floor unweathered, the trunks fresh and angular where older worlds would be rounded down. This landscape was made recently, in whatever scale of time the plant keeps, and it has not yet been smoothed.

---

And there are marks of hands. Scattered, faint, but real.

Someone lives here, or did. On the broad faces of certain trunks, where the bark is flattest, there are carvings — not decorative, I think, but functional: notches in patterns, depth-cut grooves that catch the falling water and route it, simple cisterns hollowed into burls. I find small structures lashed into the angles where buttress-roots leave the trunk: platforms of cut fiber, shelters woven from the same living material the world is made of, here and there a tool — a blade of hardened heartwood, a hook, a vessel of dried leaf-skin. Primitive in the precise sense: made by hand, without metal, by patient craft and long knowledge. Pre-metal because there is no metal here; there is no stone here; there is only the plant, and so the people of this place have learned to make everything from the plant, and they have made beautifully.

I imagine them as the abundance's most careful members: climbers, listeners, readers of the drip-lines and the silences. They are few — the built parameter is *scattered*, single shelters separated by the vast empty naves — and they navigate by sound across distances that would be unthinkable in the dark without ears trained from birth. They tap the trunks and listen to the answer come back changed, and in the change they read the shape of the world ahead, the open chamber, the closing growth, the predator's hush.

---

One last thing, the unusual thing, the slight wrongness that the place has and will not explain.

It is this: the plant seems to *know* where you are. Not menacingly. But the drip-lines shift toward you over hours. The fiber-floor is warmest precisely where you choose to rest. The shafts of weak green light, when they come, fall just often enough where you are walking that you begin to suspect the canopy parts to let them through. It is too slight to prove, too persistent to dismiss. The people leave offerings at the bases of certain trunks — small woven things, a portion of water carried up — and do not speak about why, and tap a particular soft rhythm against the bark before they sleep, an asking, or a thanks.

The world is one organism. You are inside it. It is cold, and dark, and loud, and almost entirely empty, and it is paying a slow attention you cannot read.

That is the Hollow Canopy. Walk softly. Listen for the silence that moves. And when the great columns groan, understand that this is the closest the place comes to speech.
