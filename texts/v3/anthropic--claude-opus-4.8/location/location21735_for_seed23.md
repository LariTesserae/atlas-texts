There is a town built on water that does not flow.

Begin with the cold. It is the first thing and the constant thing — a high, clean cold, the kind that lives above the treeline, where breath becomes visible and stays visible, hanging in the still air like punctuation. At this elevation the sky is enormous and the light is merciless. Not warm light. White light, flat and total, the sun's full weight pressed against a thin atmosphere. It comes from above and it comes from below, bouncing up off all that water, so there are no real shadows here, only the faint blue ghosts of them, and a person standing in the open is lit from every direction at once, like an object on a surgeon's table.

Now the water. It is everywhere and it is almost still. The town sits in a shallow basin scooped between worn peaks — old peaks, peaks that have given up their sharpness, smoothed to humped shoulders of grey stone by a thousand patient ages of frost and thaw. The basin holds water the way a saucer holds it: a thin sheet, ankle-deep, sometimes knee-deep, spread across the whole floor of the place. Beneath it, the rock. The rock weeps. Springs seep up through every seam, so the sheet is constantly, quietly replenished from below, and there is no clear line between what is pool and what is wet stone and what is merely the air gone heavy with condensation. The atmosphere itself drips. Mist beads on every surface and runs down in slow threads.

And yet — quiet. This is the strange part, the part that takes a while to settle into. All this water, and almost no sound. The springs do not gurgle; they only swell. The sheet of water does not lap, because nothing pushes it. When you walk, your footsteps make a soft kissing sound and then nothing. The silence is dense, packed, a presence in itself, as if the cold has frozen sound the way it would freeze a sound's source. You can hear your own pulse. You can hear, very far off, the click of a stone settling. That is most of what there is to hear.

The town. Here is where it becomes itself.

The builders worked in stone and in something else — a pale fibrous matter, woven and packed, that might be reed or might be the dried bodies of something. Hand-laid, every course of it. No metal to speak of, or only a little, soft and dull, used at the joints. The structures rise close together, almost touching, leaning their shoulders into one another like people crowded for warmth. Narrow lanes thread between them, half-flooded, so that to move through the town is to wade. The buildings are low and many-windowed, and the windows have no glass, only open squares through which the white light pours and the mist drifts. Rooftops at the level of your chest. Doorways you must stoop through. It is a town built at a smaller scale than you expect — a hamlet's footprint holding a city's density — and walking it feels like walking through a model of a town, or a town built for a slightly smaller, more numerous people.

Because it is numerous. This is the next thing.

The fauna. The water teems. In the ankle-deep sheet, in the seeping channels, in the flooded lanes — small swimming things in uncountable abundance. Pale, translucent, finned and unfinned, some no larger than a grain, some as long as your forearm. They do not school in any orderly way. They surge. The whole shallow surface of the town is in constant motion with them, a turbulence of bodies, a boiling that never makes a sound — that is the strangeness sharpened to a point: all this churning life, and silent. They mass in the doorways. They pour over the low rooftops where the water collects. They are not afraid of you; they part around your shins and close again behind. When the light strikes the water just so, the entire town glitters with the wet bright flickering of them, ten thousand small backs catching sun.

And the town responds to them. This is the rule of this world, the law you slowly understand: the place is not still. It only sounds still. Everything is changing all the time, fast, faster than a town should change. The fauna build. Or unbuild. They carry the pale fibre in their mouths and add it to the walls; they gnaw it away; they pack the seams and open new ones. A lane that ran north at dawn runs east by midmorning. A doorway closes over with woven matter and a new one opens beside it. The structures rise and slump and rise again on the labour of these silent swimming multitudes, and the only sign of all that frantic work is the faint kiss of water displaced and the slow patient shift of stone. Turbulence under a skin of quiet. The town is being made and remade continuously, and it has been doing this so long that it has reached a kind of balance — eroded, weathered, settled into its own restless equilibrium. It is old. It has always been changing and it has always looked, at any single instant, exactly like this.

The vegetation is sparse and stubborn: thin grey-green mats clinging where the springs run warmest, a few hardy stalks standing in the flooded lanes like forgotten posts, beaded with mist. Almost an afterthought. The life here is animal life, and water, and stone, and the unbearable clean light.

Stand in the middle of it. Cold in your lungs, the visible breath hanging. Water to your shins, never warming, the small bodies thronging past. The white light from everywhere, casting almost no shadow. The town leaning close around you, growing and ungrowing, silent. And under it all, the seep of the weeping rock, bringing up more water, always more, from somewhere far below — the source that keeps the whole strange engine turning, that has kept it turning longer than there has been anyone to wade through it and wonder.

You could stay a long time, and learn its silence, and never once hear it speak.
