Start with the cold, because the cold is the first fact and the last.

This is a place very high up — near the top of whatever world holds it — where the air has thinned to a blue scarcity and every breath feels borrowed. The light comes weak and slantwise, the dim grey-violet of an afternoon that never commits to noon. There is no sun-disc to find; just a brightness smeared behind cloud-rind, enough to throw faint shadows but never sharp ones. Everything is half-lit, half-revealed, as if the place has not yet decided to be fully visible.

The ground is young. That's the strangest thing about it, and it takes a while to understand. These are not old mountains worn into wisdom; these are mountains being made, raw and unfinished, their faces still sharp in a way real cliffs never are. The rock has barely been touched by weather. Edges that should be rounded are cleaved and bright. There are no scree slopes, no talus fans, almost no soil — erosion has not yet had time to do its patient grinding. The peaks stand in plates and shards, freshly broken, surfaces still showing the crystalline fracture of their birth. You get the sense that if you came back in an age, the whole geometry would have shifted, settled, softened — but right now it is all angle and intention, geology mid-sentence.

And it is wet. Counterintuitively, gorgeously wet. Up here the cold air can hold almost nothing, so the moisture is always condensing out — beading on the raw rock, weeping in thin films down the new faces, gathering in the cracks and freezing and thawing and freezing. The stone glistens. In the hollows where the broken plates meet, shallow tarns sit black and perfectly still, skinned with the first attempts at ice. Mist sits in the valleys below the ridgelines and moves slowly, a great pale animal grazing along the contours. When it lifts you see further than you expect — this is open country, a whole region of these embryonic summits, ridge beyond ridge fading into the haze, a valley's worth of raw world laid out and almost wholly empty.

Empty is the right word. The density here is near nothing. You can walk for an hour and pass nothing but rock and the occasional cushion of plant life — and the vegetation is sparse, too, hardy and low. Mosses the color of bruises in the seams where water collects. Lichens, slow and patient, the first colonists of new stone, painting it in faint rusts and yellow-greens. A few clenched alpine plants hunkered in the lee of boulders, leaves waxed against the cold, flowering when they can in tiny defiant colors. Nothing tall. Nothing crowded. Vast intervals of bare wet rock between one living thing and the next.

It is mostly quiet. The dominant sound is water — not roaring, just the constant fine articulation of it: drip, trickle, the small click of melt finding a lower place, the hush of mist. Wind comes and goes across the ridges with a thin keening, but mostly it is the patient percussion of moisture, the sound a place makes while it is still being built.

Now — the fauna. Here the place surprises you again. For all its emptiness it is alive, abundantly, almost extravagantly alive, just spread thin across the enormous space. The creatures of high cold places: birds first. Choughs and accentors and great soaring things riding the updrafts off the new ridges, their cries carrying impossible distances in the clear air. Things with thick fur picking across the rock — ungulates that should not be able to grip these surfaces but do, congregating at the tarns, their breath smoking. Pikas and marmots and small hot-blooded engines of survival tucked into the rockfields, gathering, watching. The whole ecosystem is in motion — predator and prey, the slow turn of who eats whom, weather cycles sweeping through, mist arriving and departing, water phase-changing all day long. It is dynamic, busy with process, busy with living, but the living is *distributed*, so that any single view shows you almost nothing and the whole shows you everything.

There are no built things. No walls, no roads, no marks of making. Untouched in the literal sense: nothing here was put here.

And yet.

There is one parameter that does not fit, and I've been circling it. *Industrial.* High up, near the top, in a wet cold empty raw new world with no buildings and abundant beasts — something mechanical, something engined, something of scaled production.

So let me find where it lives.

It is in the *air.* Listen past the water and you'll catch it: a low continuous hum, almost subsonic, that you feel in your sternum before you name it. It comes from no machine you can see. It comes from the *weather itself.* This is a place where the great work of the atmosphere has become something like an engine — the condensing, the freezing, the lifting of mist, the cycling of water through its phases happening at such scale and such relentless regularity that it has the cadence of a factory floor. The mist does not drift idly; it moves in shifts, in pulses, drawn up the valleys and exhaled back down on a schedule you could set a clock by. The freeze-thaw cracking of the new rock keeps a steady mechanical rhythm — tick, crack, tick — the sound of production, of a world manufacturing its own mountains. The fauna moves to it, fits its rhythms to the great hum, so that the migrations and the feedings have the quality of a vast distributed assembly line: every creature a worker, every process geared into the next.

There is no smoke, no fire, no metal. The industry is the *planet's* — a world caught in the act of building itself, high and cold and wet and almost empty, running its slow tremendous machinery of stone and water and breath under a dim violet light, while across an enormous sparse landscape a million small warm lives turn the great wheel without ever knowing they do.

That is the place. New mountains, weeping. Few plants, many beasts, almost no company. Quiet on the surface and engined underneath. A world still coming into being, working, in the cold, near the top, where the air is thin and the light won't quite arrive.
