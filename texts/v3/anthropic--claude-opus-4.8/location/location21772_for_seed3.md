# The Unfinished Heights

Begin with the air, because the air is the first thing that tells you where you are. It is thin and cool here, the kind of cool that doesn't bite but reminds — a coolness with altitude in it, the upper register of the world. You breathe a little harder. The light comes flat and grey through a permanent high overcast, a dim silver that never resolves into sun or storm. It is always the hour after dawn or the hour before dusk; the day does not commit.

And the mountains are not finished.

That is the thing you understand slowly, the way you understand a face in poor light. These are not weathered peaks worn smooth by ten million years of rain. They are *raw* — geology caught mid-thought. The stone has the wet-clay sheen of something just extruded, edges still sharp because nothing has had time to round them, faces cleaving in clean planes that no erosion has softened. Veins of unweathered crystal stand proud of the rock, not yet leached away. In places the mountainsides show the *grain* of their making, like a thing pulled from a mold while still warm. You get the sense that if you came back in a thousand years the ridgeline would have a different silhouette — not eroded down, but grown *up*, pushed higher by the slow patient engine underneath.

Because something is building these mountains. Still. Now.

## The City That Came Early

And here is the contradiction the place asks you to hold: someone built a city on land that isn't done being made.

It clings to the high shoulders of the range — a real city, layered and organized, with the density of generations of construction stacked on construction. Foundries and mills, chimney-stacks, the iron skeletons of cable-houses and ore-lifts. Pipe runs lashed to cliff faces. Electric arc-lamps strung between towers, burning a sour yellow against the dim, the only warm color in the whole grey palette. The architecture is industrial-vertical: structures that grip the slope with buttresses and pilings, walkways that bridge the gaps where the world hasn't connected itself yet.

It is loud. This is a working place. The constant percussion of stamping mills, the deep periodic *thud* of pile-drivers, the hiss of escaping steam, the grind of cable through pulley-housings, somewhere a foreman's bell. Sound carries strangely in the thin air — flattened, arriving slightly too late, echoes that don't match their sources because the canyon walls they should reflect from aren't built yet.

But — and this is where the *sparse* comes in — the city is not a solid mass. It is scattered across the heights in clusters with great gulfs of empty raw mountain between them. A foundry-district here, perched on a ledge; a residential terrace there, half a mile off across a chasm, reachable only by cable-car that sways across the void. Between the inhabited nodes: bare new stone, slopes of fresh scree, the silence that is not really silence because the machinery-sound seeps into everything. Much space between the presences. You can stand in the heart of the works and look out at vast emptiness.

## What Grows, What Lives

There is water, but not much, and it behaves oddly because the watershed isn't formed. No rivers have carved their proper channels yet — the streams wander, indecisive, finding low places that will be valleys someday. There's condensation: the cool air meets the warm exhaust of the foundries and weeps. Pipes sweat. Walls are damp. In the mornings a thin mist pools in the unfinished hollows and burns off slowly, slowly — everything here is slow, gradual, patient. The mist takes hours. The rain, when it comes, is fine and intermittent, more a dampening than a downpour.

In the damp pockets, life has taken hold with surprising vigor. The vegetation is *lush* where it manages to root — but it manages only in patches, so the green is intense against the grey. Ferns and bryophytes and broad soft-leaved things colonize the seep-lines and the warm leaking joints of the pipework. Moss runs riot on the north faces. Hanging gardens drip from the abandoned upper galleries. The plants are opportunists, drinking the machine-sweat, eating the strange new soil.

And the fauna — abundant, everywhere, more than the place should support. Things that have adapted to *both* worlds, the made and the unmade. Cliff-dwelling creatures that nest in the rafters of the cable-houses. Pale things in the damp hollows. Flocks of something bird-shaped that ride the thin air in spirals, calling in the flattened acoustics. The animals seem unbothered by the city; they thread through it, around it, treating the industrial structures as just another kind of geology — which, on this timescale, they are.

## The Breakdown

Now the weirdness, which has been waiting at the edges of everything I've said.

Because the mountains are still being *made*, reality up here has not fully set. The world is at 2.6 out of 3 on the scale of *coming apart*, and what that means is this: causality is soft at the edges. The unfinished places are unfinished in more than geology.

Walk far enough from a settled district and the rules thin out. There are slopes where *down* is a matter of opinion — where water from a seep runs upward along the grain of the raw stone, following the direction the mountain is still growing. There are mornings when the mist doesn't burn off but instead *remembers* — holds, in its grey body, faint impressions of shapes that walked through it days ago, replaying them slow and silent. The echoes that arrive too late sometimes arrive from sounds that haven't been made yet; the foundry-bell tolls and three workers look up because they heard it ring an hour ago.

The new stone, where it is freshest, is sometimes *not yet committed to being stone*. Miners report faces that are soft as wax for a season and then harden; veins of crystal that are, briefly, transparent all the way through the mountain, so you can see the dim grey light of the far side glowing in the rock like a held breath. Some districts have been built on ground that later decided to be elsewhere, and the cable-cars now run to addresses that float, slightly, disconnected, hanging in the thin air with their gardens dripping and their lamps lit, reachable but no longer *placed*.

The people who live here have made rules for it, the way people always do. You don't walk in the unset country after the mist comes down. You don't trust an echo until you've heard its cause. You build on the oldest, hardest stone you can find — the parts of the mountain that finished being made before the city came — and you understand that the heights you live on are provisional, a draft, that the engine underneath is still composing the world and may yet revise the chapter you are standing in.

---

## The Engine Below

What is building the mountains? No one has been down to see. The works tap the heat of it — the foundries run on the warmth that bleeds up from below, the patient enormous warmth of creation-in-progress. The deep periodic thud that everyone in the city has stopped hearing, the way you stop hearing your own heart, is not a pile-driver. It is older than the city and it does not keep the city's time. It is the world, pushing itself up another fraction of an inch into the cool dim air, in no hurry at all, building toward a shape that none of the living will be high enough to stand on.

The city is loud, but the mountain is louder, on a frequency too slow to hear.
