There is a room the size of a chapel, and it is full of green.

Not green the color so much as green the *condition* — a saturation so complete that the air itself seems chlorophyll-thick, and breathing it feels like drinking something just shy of water. Everything here is plant, or was, or is becoming. The walls are plant. The floor is a mat of fibrous root-flesh forty layers deep, springy underfoot, warm with the slow heat of its own decomposition. You stand inside the body of a single vast vegetable intention that has long since forgotten it was ever many things.

Let me find the edges of it, because there are edges. This is small. You could cross it in twenty paces if the floor let you walk straight, which it does not.

**The terrain.** The ground swells and sags in long muscular ridges, like the inside of a throat. Pools collect in the low places — shallow, tea-brown, never deeper than your shin — and these are everywhere, because the water table sits a hand's breadth below the surface and weeps up through the root-mat constantly. Step wrong and the floor exhales a little flood around your ankle. The whole place is wet the way a sponge wrung halfway is wet: never dry, never drowned, holding.

**The architecture.** Here is where it gets strange. Through the green substrate run the bones of something that was *built*. A doorframe, cast in iron, swallowed to its lintel in living tissue. A run of pipe — industrial pipe, the wide-bore kind meant to move volume — emerging from one wall and plunging into the floor, its flanges still bolted, its surface furred with moss but its purpose unmistakable. A pressure gauge, its glass intact, its needle resting at some long-dead reading. Someone made machinery here. A pumphouse, maybe, or a fermentation works, or a greenhouse boiler-room. The plant came after. The plant came *for* it.

And the strangest part: the machinery still works. Not all of it. But the pipe shudders, at intervals, with a deep peristaltic surge, and a slug of brown water moves through it and arrives nowhere in particular, gushing out a cracked elbow-joint into one of the pools. Something pumps. There is no engine you can find — only the plant, which has perhaps grown its own muscle into the dead metal and learned to use it, the way a hermit crab uses a shell. The factory has been digested but its rhythm kept, kept like a swallowed clock.

**The sound.** This is loud — loud in a way that takes a moment to parse, because none of it is voice. There is the wet machine-pulse, the THUM-and-gush of the pump-thing, every nine seconds or so, regular as a tired heart. Under that, a continuous percolating fizz: the root-mat respiring, releasing gas, ten thousand small bubbles surfacing in the pools at once. And over both, a high steady whine that you eventually locate in the iron — the metal resonating, the whole structure singing a single industrial note as fluid is forced through narrow places. It is the sound of a working plant in both senses. You cannot hear yourself think clearly. The place thinks instead of you.

**The light.** Normal daylight, somehow — there must be a way for it to come in, a roof-gap or a wall of green-stained glass too overgrown to see through, but the light arrives anyway, ordinary and grey-gold, lying on the leaves like it lies on any ordinary afternoon. This is the gentlest thing here. It refuses to be uncanny. It makes the strangeness worse by treating it as nothing remarkable.

**The ecology.** Almost no animals. This much plant, this much water, this much warmth, and yet — rare fauna, deliberately rare, as if the green tolerates few guests. You might find one thing: a single pale slow beetle the size of a thumb, working the underside of a leaf, and it will be the only moving creature you see in an hour. The plant does its own pollinating, its own circulating, its own everything. It has made itself complete. It does not need the bees it has outlived.

**The rules.** Change comes slowly here. The pump's nine-second pulse is the fastest thing in the room; everything else moves on the timescale of growth and rot, the patient give-and-take that has held this place in balance for a very long time. It is *established* — settled into its own contradiction, machine and marsh, dead works and living works, and it has been settled like this longer than anyone built to remember. Nothing is happening. Everything is, slowly. The needle on the gauge will not move this year, or next. But the moss on the gauge's rim is one cell deeper than it was a decade ago.

Stand still long enough and the floor begins, very gently, to grow up around your boots. Not fast. Not hungry. Just thorough. The way the whole place is thorough.

This is a small green organ in the body of a larger dead thing, keeping its borrowed heartbeat, breathing its own wet air, lit like an ordinary day, and waiting for nothing because it already has everything it needs.
