# The Cold Engine Garden

Begin near the ground, because the ground is where the strangeness first becomes legible.

You are very high. The air has the thinness that makes your pulse loud in your own ears — except there is almost nothing else to hear, so the loudness is not a relief but a measurement. This is one of the upper reaches of a range that has no proper name because no one has ever come here to name it. The peaks go up past where peaks usually stop, into a darkness that is not night but altitude: the sky here is a deep ink-violet even at what should be noon, and the stars are visible all day as faint cold pricks, because the atmosphere is too thin to scatter the light into a proper blue.

So it is dark. Not the dark of a closed room — the dark of a place where the sun is a small white coin too far away to do its work.

And yet — vegetation, lush, everywhere.

This is the first contradiction, and the place resolves it for you slowly.

---

The plants do not photosynthesize from the weak sun. They glean their light from below.

Down in the rock of these mountains, threaded through the established and weathered stone, run veins of a warm dark metal that was never smelted by any hand. The mountains, over an age beyond reckoning, have grown their own machinery the way a body grows bone — not built, not made, simply *settled into being.* These are the engines. They are geological. Vast slow turbines of mineral, condenser-channels of crystallized vein, flues of hollow basalt that breathe a thin warm exhaust into the cold. Nobody installed them. They are as natural here as a delta is natural elsewhere, the long balanced product of erosion and pressure and time, and they hum at the threshold of hearing — a sound so low and so constant that after an hour you stop perceiving it as sound and start perceiving it as a slight wrongness in your bones, a sense that the mountain is *thinking.*

The plants drink from the engines. Their roots wrap the warm veins. Their leaves are not green but a dark iridescent bronze-black, oily, faceted like the eyes of insects, and they glow — faintly, internally — with the warmth they pull from the deep machinery. A whole slope of them looks, from across a gorge, like a city seen at a great distance at night: scattered ember-points in the blackness, breathing brighter and dimmer in slow tides.

---

Water comes intermittently and never as rain in the ordinary sense.

It is too cold and too high for rain. Instead, the warm breath of the buried engines meets the freezing dark air and *condenses,* so that the whole landscape sweats. Moisture beads on every bronze leaf, gathers, runs down. In the dawnless mornings — there are no proper dawns, only a slight paling of the violet — the condensation is so thick that the slopes drip in a soft uncountable patter, the only generous sound this place makes, ten thousand tiny taps from leaf to leaf to stone. Then the warmth-tides shift, the engine-breath moves elsewhere in the mountain, and a slope goes dry and silent and faintly steaming while the moisture migrates to the next.

So water walks across the range. It is here, then there. The valleys are dry one week and weeping the next, following the slow respiration of the stone machines below. Pools collect in the rock hollows where the veins run densest, and these pools are warm, and steam rises off them straight up into the cold thin dark in tall unmoving columns, because there is so little wind.

There is so little wind. That is part of the silence. At this altitude the air is too thin to carry much sound and too still to make much. A rockfall on a far peak reaches you as a faint dry whisper, delayed, like a memory of a sound rather than the sound.

---

Now the fauna, which is abundant, and which is the strangest thing of all.

In a place this empty — and it is empty, vast hollow distances between any one presence and the next, a single creature might be the only living thing in a whole valley — abundance does not mean crowding. It means *variety,* and persistence, and the steady pulse of an ecosystem that has learned to live on warmth and condensation and the dark.

The animals here are warm-seekers. They are drawn to the engine-veins the way desert things are drawn to water. The largest are the slow grazers — call them the kettle-beasts — great shaggy low things the size of small hills, six-legged, moving so slowly across a slope that you might mistake one for a boulder until, over an hour of watching, it has changed its place. They press their long bellies against the warm rock and graze the bronze plants, and the warmth they hoard radiates off them, so that smaller creatures shelter in their long fur, and the whole beast is its own migrating ecosystem, a walking warm island in the freezing dark.

The smaller things glow, like the plants. Insects that have eaten the iridescence and carry it as cold light in their abdomens drift between the slopes in loose constellations, the only moving lights in the dark, slow because the air is thin and cold. There are pale long-limbed predators that hunt by the heat-glow of their prey, seeing in warmth the way we see in light, walking with terrible patience through the near-void. They are silent. Everything here is silent; sound is expensive in thin air, and the creatures have learned to do without it. They signal in heat and light: a slow brightening, a deliberate cooling, a flush of warmth across a flank that means *here I am* or *come no closer.*

---

And the dynamic of the place — this is the thing that surprises you most, given the emptiness and the silence and the dark. The place is intensely *active.* It is busy at a scale and tempo that the human eye is poorly built to catch.

The engines breathe in cycles of hours. The water walks. The warmth migrates. The grazers follow the warmth, and the predators follow the grazers, and the light-insects follow the blooming of the plants, which follows the deep tides of the buried machinery. The condensation falls and gathers and steams and falls again. Slopes go from weeping-fertile to dry-dormant and back across a week. Nothing here is still — but everything moves so slowly, and so quietly, and so far apart, that to stand in it is to feel you are in a profound stillness, when in fact you are inside an enormous, patient, breathing machine of an ecosystem, every part of it turning, none of it touching, the whole vast hollow cold engine-garden working through its long dark seasons without ever once raising its voice.

---

The rules of this world, then, as you would learn them if you stayed:

Warmth is the only currency. It is mined from the deep mountain, hoarded, traded, hunted by.

Light is something you make from the inside, or you go without.

Silence is the default and a sound is an event — a falling stone, a drip, the once-a-season groan of a vein shifting in the rock — and you will remember each one.

And the made and the grown are not opposites here. The machinery is grown. The garden is mechanical. Nobody built any of it and nobody ever will, and it does not need them, and it will go on breathing in the violet dark long after you have descended back into the thick warm noisy lower world that you mistakenly believe is the real one.
