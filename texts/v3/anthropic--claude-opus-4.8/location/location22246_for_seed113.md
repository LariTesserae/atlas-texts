# The Cistern Quarter

You come down into it. There is no other way to arrive.

The first thing is the warmth — not the warmth of sun, which never reaches here, but the warmth of something that was made warm and stays that way on purpose. The air is thick enough to drink. It clings to the skin and beads there, and you understand quickly that you are not separate from the weather; you are inside the lung of the place.

This is a city built below the surface, and it is small. Not small the way a village is small, with edges you can walk to. Small the way a single held breath is small. You could cross the inhabited part in a few minutes if anything lived here, but nothing does. The whole quarter is the size of a few city blocks, and almost all of it is empty volume — vaults opening into vaults, the spaces between things enormous, so that a single dripping conduit might stand alone in a hall that could hold a cathedral, and stand there with nothing near it for a long, long way.

## The Terrain

The floor is not soil. It is a poured dark material, faintly translucent, with the wet sheen of a riverbed stone, and water lies across it everywhere in shallow sheets — never deep, ankle-deep at most, but *present*, always, a film that mirrors the dim light and doubles every structure into its reflection. Walk and you would send rings outward across the whole floor of a vault, and the rings would meet the rings from the drip-points and interfere, and the floor would shiver with overlapping geometry.

The rock — where you can see raw rock — is *young*. This is the strange thing. The walls have the sharp, unweathered edges of something that finished forming recently. Crystalline faces still meet at clean angles. There has not yet been time for the slow rounding that water always wins in the end. So you have this contradiction in your hand: a city clearly very old in its engineering, set into stone that was made or revealed only moments ago in geological terms. The water is *working* on it now, beginning the long argument, and you can see the first pale runnels where it has started to find the weaknesses.

Everything weeps. The ceilings are too high to see clearly in the dimness, but you know they are there by the rain — a fine, constant, warm precipitation that falls inside the rooms, condensed out of the breathing air and dropping back down. It is always raining here, gently, indoors, in every chamber. The sound of it is the floor of all the other sounds.

## The Built Thing

And it *is* built — densely, deliberately, layered. This is the paradox the place is made to hold: the construction is everywhere and the emptiness is everywhere, at the same time, because the construction *encloses* the emptiness instead of filling it.

Imagine columns. Not columns of stone but columns of something the eye can't fully resolve — they have the heft of architecture and the behavior of liquid light, and they rise out of the water-sheet floor and disappear up into the rain-mist. They are arranged with the obsessive regularity of a built grid, street after street of them, but each one stands alone in a wide dark, far from the next, so that the grid feels less like a city and more like a forest planted by something that loved spacing.

Between and around the columns runs infrastructure: conduits, channels, the seams of buried machinery glowing faintly through the floor. There are stairs that descend to lower vaults and stairs that descend to lower vaults below those, and the city goes down and down in layers, each one warmer and wetter than the last, each one emptier.

The technology here is past anything that needs hands. The columns are doing something — managing the water, perhaps, or *making* it, condensing the atmosphere into rain and gathering the rain into the channels and the channels into the depths, a closed circuit that never spills and never runs dry. There are no controls anywhere. No screens, no levers, no doors that need opening. The whole quarter runs itself at a level of physics that doesn't quite parse: the light that doesn't come from anywhere, the warmth with no source, the way water seems to climb the young rock against its nature and then fall again as rain. It works the way a heart works. You are not meant to operate it. You are meant, at most, to be inside it while it operates.

## The Ecology

Things grow. Not many, but enough to be called lush in the patches where it happens.

In the wet seams of the stone, in the runnels where the water has begun to bite into the young rock, soft green things have taken hold — fat mosses, translucent ferns the color of celery, pale frilled growths that might be plants and might be something the city is cultivating for reasons of its own. They feed on the faint light and the endless humidity. Where one of the strange columns rises from the floor, a collar of vegetation often grows around its base, drinking the runoff, so that each lonely column wears a small green skirt and stands like a planted thing in its own field of dark water.

There are no animals. None. No insects work the moss, no anything swims the sheet-water, no wings disturb the rain. The plants grow in a silence of their own kind — a botanical quiet inside the larger noise — and they have no one to be eaten by, so they simply spread along the seams in long slow rivers of green, finding the weaknesses in the young stone before the water does, an ecosystem of exactly one kingdom.

## The Sound

It is loud here. This surprises people who expect a buried place to be silent.

The rain is constant and the vaults are vast and hard-surfaced and full of standing water, so every drop is answered by its echo and the echoes pile into a roar — not a sharp roar but a deep, full, oceanic one, the sound of a great room full of falling water, multiplied by every great room behind it. Under that runs the working of the city: the columns make a tone, low and continuous, felt in the chest more than heard, the sound of physics being bent quietly and at scale. The channels gurgle and pull. Somewhere far down, water meets water with a boom that travels up through the floor and into your feet.

It is the loudness of a living system, not of violence. Nothing here is breaking. Everything is *circulating*, loudly, all the time. If you stood still and closed your eyes you would hear the whole machine breathing through its water, and you would not be able to find the edges of any single sound.

## The Rules

A few things are true here that are not true above:

Water does not pool deep. However much rains, the floor stays a film. The city drinks it back as fast as it falls, and you never learn where the drinking happens.

The light has no source and casts no clean shadows — it sits in the air itself, a dim grey-gold glow about as bright as a room at dusk, brightest near the green growths as though they give some of it back.

The rock is getting older as you watch — minutely, but truly. The place is *young* and aging in real time, the only thing here that is allowed to decay, and the water and the moss are its only clock.

And the emptiness is not absence. It is the point. The city was built spacious on purpose, hollowed deliberately, every vault made large enough to be alone in. Whatever made this wanted vast warm wet rooms with almost nothing in them and the loud sound of rain — wanted to build a place and then *leave it open*, hold it ready, keep it breathing, for no occupant that has yet arrived.

You stand in one of the great vaults, ankle-deep in the mirroring film, warm rain on your shoulders, a green-skirted column glowing softly an impossible distance away, the roar of the falling water filling everything, and you understand that you are the first thing with a pulse to enter here, and that the quarter does not particularly notice, and that it will go on raining inside itself, loud and warm and empty, long after you climb back out.
