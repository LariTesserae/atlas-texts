# The Sweatworks

You come down to it by a stair that was never built so much as worn — the rock here has the soft-edged look of something that has been touched by water for longer than there have been hands to touch. The steps are scooped in their middles. They go down maybe forty feet, and the air thickens with every one, until by the bottom it has the weight of a wet towel laid across the back of your neck.

This is a chamber. Cathedral is the wrong word — it's smaller than that, more like the inside of a great kiln, a rounded room you could cross in a hundred paces, with a ceiling that bulges low in places and lifts away into darkness in others. Below the surface, sealed, hot. The heat is the first fact of the place and it never stops being the first fact. Two-point-nine out of three: the kind of heat that is a presence, that you wear, that makes the idea of "fresh air" seem like a rumor from another life.

## The Machines That Grow

What runs the heat is the strange part, and the strangeness took me a while to settle into.

There are engines here. Industrial things — iron and brass and riveted plate, the genuine article, you can see the casting seams and the bolt-heads gone bulbous with rust-bloom. Pipes run along the walls and across the floor, some thick as a man's torso, some fine as reeds. They hiss. They thud. A low reciprocating *chunk... chunk... chunk* comes from somewhere behind the wall, slow, a pulse maybe once every three or four seconds, patient and enormous, the heartbeat of something that has been beating a very long time.

But the machines are not maintained. No one services them. And here is the thing — they have not broken down. They have *settled*. The erosion that wore the stairs has worked on the iron too, and instead of destroying it, time has taught the machinery how to persist. Pipe-joints that should have failed have wept mineral until the weeping sealed them — pale stalactites of scale hanging from every coupling, dripping, the drips feeding the dampness, the dampness feeding the scale. The machines have grown their own repairs the way a reef grows. They are halfway to being geology. You cannot tell, in places, where the boiler ends and the bedrock begins; they have married.

## Water, Rationed

Eight-tenths of three: damp, not wet. There is no pool, no river, no flood. Instead the water lives in the *exchange* — it is sweated out of the hot pipes as steam, drifts, cools against the higher cold pockets of the ceiling, condenses, and falls back in a fine intermittent rain that never quite commits to being rain. It patters. It stops. It patters. You can stand in one spot and be dry, take two steps and walk into a drizzle that exists in that square yard alone. The whole chamber is a slow still, distilling itself, the same water making the same circuit for centuries — up as breath, down as rain, never enough to puddle, never absent.

Run your hand along any surface and it comes back beaded.

## What Grows

And in this — heat, damp, dim — things grow, though nothing that has a heartbeat. Vegetation at one-point-six, lush but strange, because there is almost no light. What thrives here has made its peace with the dark.

Mosses, first, in deep cushions, but mosses that have gone the color of old liver and bottle-green and, in the warmest spots near the pipes, a faint translucent yellow. They drape the machinery. They have colonized the steam-warm flanks of the boilers so thickly that the iron looks upholstered.

Then the pale things — the fungal towers, the fronds that are not really plants, growing up from the floor-scale in dense stands, near-touching, layered one species over another so that to look across the chamber is to look through three or four veils of soft growth. They lean toward the few sources of dim light — the slots where a far shaft brings down a grey ghost of daylight, the faint own-glow of certain mosses that I'll swear give off their own cold luminescence, just enough to see your hands.

Density is the texture of the place. Everything is *near* everything. Pipe, moss, scale-column, frond, the bulged rock — all of it crowded into the small volume, layered, saturated, so that there is no clean line of sight more than a dozen feet in any direction. You move through it like moving through a packed hothouse, brushing growth aside, ducking under a low-hanging coupling furred with green.

## No Animal Moves

And nothing crawls. This is the quiet inside the noise. Fauna at two-tenths of three — effectively nothing. No insects work the fungus. No blind cave-thing slides across the wet floor. You keep expecting it — a place this rich, this warm, this fed — surely something should be living off it. But the only life is the kind that stays put and grows. It makes the lushness feel held-breath, expectant, as though the whole garden is a table laid for guests who never came, or who left a long time ago.

## The Noise

But it is not silent — far from it. Sound at one-point-nine, loud. The machines are loud. The slow heart-thud, yes, but over it a constant working racket — the hiss of escaping steam in a dozen registers, the groan of metal expanding in the heat, a high thin singing from somewhere where a jet of vapor squeezes through a pinhole, the irregular *tonk* and *tick* of condensation striking iron. It overlaps and layers exactly the way the vegetation does — sound stacked on sound until it becomes a single thick texture you stop hearing as separate noises and start hearing as the voice of the room. You have to raise your own voice to be heard a few feet away. The place roars softly and ceaselessly, and the roar never resolves into meaning, and after a while you understand it never will.

## Marks of Makers

There are signs that people were here. Built, at a little above one — scattered, partial. A workbench fused now into the scale, its surface a humped white reef with the suggestion of tools beneath. A line of hooks on a wall, holding nothing. A doorway, bricked. Numbers stenciled on a boiler, half-eaten by moss: you can read a *7* and the top of what might be a *3*. A single iron chair, set facing the largest engine, as though someone sat here once to watch it run and simply — left. The chair has a cushion of moss now, person-shaped, almost, in the seat.

No one tends this place. It tends itself. The makers built the engines and the engines learned to keep themselves alive, and the water learned its circle, and the green things came up in the dark, and the long slow weathering folded all of it into a single settled thing that has been exactly this for a very long time and will be exactly this for a long time more.

## The Rule of the Place

Change comes here, but slowly — one-point-three, gradual, patient. A new scale-column thickens by a hair in a decade. The mosses advance across the iron at the pace of glaciers. If you stayed a year you would see nothing move except the rain and the steam. If you came back in a lifetime you might find one more pipe swallowed by green, one more drip-tower grown to touch the floor.

The deep strangeness of it, the two-point-three, is finally this: it is a *factory that has become a forest without becoming alive.* It runs, it grows, it breathes its water up and down, it roars — and there is no one in it and nothing in it that you could call an animal, no eye, no appetite, no will. Only the patient marriage of fire and rust and moss, sweating in the dark, keeping itself, making nothing, going nowhere, going on.

You will want to leave before the heat takes you. Going up the worn stair, the roar fades behind you, step by step, and the air thins and cools, and you come out blinking, and you find your clothes are soaked through — though it never, the whole time, truly rained.
