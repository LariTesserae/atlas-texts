# The Condensary

Begin high. The air thins here, cool against the skin, and bright — a flat white brightness that comes from no single sun but from the whole sky, which is the color of milk held up to a window. There are no clouds because the entire atmosphere is doing the work clouds do. It is condensing. Everywhere, always, slowly.

You are standing on something made.

That is the first thing to understand, and the strangest. There is no ground here in the sense of dirt or bedrock. Look down and you see fitted stone — not natural stone but *worked* stone, hand-cut, the chisel-marks still legible because nothing has weathered them smooth. Slabs the size of doors, the size of fields, laid edge to edge across the whole high plateau, and where one ends the next begins, and there is no seam wide enough to slip a knife into. Someone fitted these. Many someones. The fitting is not perfect in the way machines are perfect; it is perfect in the way patience is perfect. You can see, here and there, where a stone was cut twice because the first cut was wrong.

And it goes on. Past the immediate terrace, the made-ground steps down and steps up — a region of it, a valley's worth, terraced and walled and channeled, all of it constructed, all of it stone laid by hand. No machine made this. There is no metal here to speak of, or very little: a bronze edge somewhere, a copper pin. This was built with stone tools against stone, which means it was built across a length of time that the word "long" does not honestly cover.

## The Water

It is wet here. Profoundly wet, but not flooded — there is no river, no lake, no rain falling. The wetness comes *out of the air* and *up from the stone*.

The cool atmosphere, saturated, lets go of its water against every cold surface. The stones weep. Run your hand down any vertical face and it comes away beaded. In the carved channels — and the whole construction is veined with channels, fine as veins in a leaf, cut into the tops of the slabs — water collects drop by drop and runs, slowly, downhill, gathering. The builders cut the channels to catch the weeping. The entire made-landscape is a machine for harvesting condensation, and it works the way a spider's web works: by being everywhere and patient.

The water moves *slow*. This is a slow world. A drop forms on a high stone and you could watch it the whole time it takes to swell, release, and slide an inch to the next channel. Down in the lower terraces the gathered water stands in shallow worked basins, perfectly still, mirror-bright under the milk-white sky, and in those mirrors you see the brightness doubled until you have to look away.

## What Lives

Almost nothing.

This is the part that makes the place hard to hold in the mind. All this construction, this immense patient labor, and there is no one here. No animal moves across the stone. No bird crosses the white sky. No insect troubles the standing water. The basins are clear because nothing lives in them to cloud them.

There is *some* life, just barely. In the wettest seams, where stone meets stone and the weeping never stops, you find a faint green-gray bloom — not plants, nothing with a leaf or root, but something simpler and older. A film. A patient skin of something that photosynthesizes the white light and asks nothing else of the world. It grows so slowly that to watch it is to watch nothing. In a human lifetime a patch might widen by the breadth of a hand. It is the only color here that is not the color of wet stone, and it is barely a color at all.

So: who built this? And for whom?

## The Quiet

It is nearly silent. This is the thing that pushes against everything else — all this surface, all this structure, and almost no sound to fill it.

What sound there is, is water-sound, and it is the smallest scale of water-sound there can be. Not rushing. Not even trickling, mostly. *Dripping.* A drop releases somewhere and ticks into a basin, and the sound of it travels far across the still air and the hard stone, and then there is a long pause, and then another drop, somewhere else. The pauses are long. You learn to wait inside them. If you stand still and stop your own breathing you can hear the whole region's slow respiration of water — a tick here, a tick there, irregular, unhurried, going on and on across the terraces, the sound of a place keeping time by the only clock it has.

Your own footstep on the stone is shockingly loud against this. You find yourself walking softly, then stopping, listening, as though you might be overheard. By what, you don't know.

## The Strangeness

Walk far enough and the construction starts to declare its logic, and the logic is not quite a human one.

The channels do not only run downhill. Some of them run *level* for great distances, around the contour of the terracing, and a few — you check this twice, you crouch and watch a drop to be sure — a few appear to carry water *gently upward*, against the fall of the land, by some grading or capillary trick cut into the stone so fine you cannot see how it works. The water goes where the builders wanted it to go, and the builders did not always want it to go down.

And there is a center. From the high terrace you can see it: the whole region of channels, all of them, the level ones and the falling ones and the impossible rising ones, all draining toward a single low place near the middle of the valley — a great round basin, the largest worked thing in sight, brimful and motionless and bright as a coin. Everything weeps toward it. The whole construction is one vast slow funnel, the labor of uncountable hands across uncountable years, built to deliver the breath of the sky, drop by drop, into one still pool that has no outlet and never overflows.

It is exactly as full as it has always been. The water that arrives is the water that leaves, somehow, somewhere, balanced — taken back up into the white air to fall again as weeping on the high stones, so that the whole place is a closed loop of patient water turning over and over on a cycle too slow to see.

The builders are gone, if they were ever here in the form you would recognize. But the thing they made still runs. It runs without them, the way a wound clock runs, ticking its single drops into the bright unbroken quiet, harvesting the sky for no one, on a plateau too high and too cool and too freshly cut by whatever raised it for anything to have yet arrived to drink.

You could stay a long time. The place would not notice. It is busy being itself, slowly, and it has been doing so since before the green film learned the trick of the light, and it will go on after — gathering, weeping, balancing — the most occupied empty place there is.
