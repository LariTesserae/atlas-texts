# The Canopy-Works of Hesp

You arrive at it the way you arrive at a sound before you arrive at a place — there is a low industrial drone over the high cold air long before there is anything to see, and the drone is not metal. It is wood. Wood the size of districts, vibrating.

Let me work toward it slowly, because the place does not show itself all at once.

## The terrain, first

This is the roof of something. The land has been worn down for so long that "mountain" is the wrong word now — what remains is a vast settled tableland at a height where breath comes thin and the cold sits permanent and gentle in your lungs. The rock beneath is so old it has lost ambition. No crags, no fresh edges. Everything has been sanded by deep time into broad shoulders and shallow basins, the way a riverstone forgets it was ever angular.

But you do not walk on rock. You almost never touch the rock. The rock is the floor of an ocean and the plant is the ocean.

## The vegetation, which is everything

Set aside the idea of forest. A forest has clearings, has soil, has a floor and a ceiling and animals between. This is not that. The vegetation here is *total* — it is the substrate, the architecture, the ground, the air's filter, the only material the world is built from.

Picture a single growth-form that long ago won everything. The Hespine growth is woody and pale grey-green, columnar where it rises and braided where it spans, and it has — over an age so long the rock forgot its edges — grown into the *shape of a city*. Not metaphorically. Functionally. The plant builds.

It throws up boles forty, sixty meters, then arches them toward neighboring boles and fuses, so the upper world is a lattice of structural members — beams, vaults, ribbed galleries — all of it living tissue, all of it under slow load, all of it groaning. The drone you heard from far off is ten thousand fused arches taking and shedding stress in the wind. It is loud the way a working shipyard is loud, except no one is working; the structure is simply *alive and large*, and large living things under their own weight make sound.

## The built thing, which is also the plant

Here is where it gets difficult to describe honestly, so I'll go carefully.

There is a settlement at the scale of a town spread through the lattice — but I cannot tell you cleanly where the plant stops and the construction begins, and I have stopped believing the distinction holds. There are platforms, ramps, tiered housings, conduits, what look like flues and chimneys and great turning shafts. They are made of the plant. But they are *made* — shaped, jointed, fitted to purpose, mechanized.

Industrial, the parameter says, and it's right. There are engines here. Living engines: chambers where the woody mass has grown into pistoning columns that rise and fall, driven by internal pressures, turning fibrous shafts that turn other shafts, a whole pulping, sap-driven mechanization running on the plant's own circulation. Belts of bark-leather slap and run. Things that resemble factories produce — what? More of the structure. The works make the works. It is a city that builds itself and has been building itself since before erosion finished its labor on the rock below.

Who runs it? This is the rare-fauna question, and I'll come back to it.

## Water, and how it moves

Damp, not wet. There is no river you could name, no lake. Instead the cold and the height conspire: moisture lives in the air and condenses on the cool grey wood at certain hours, sheeting down the great arches in slow films, collecting in the cupped joints where members fuse. The plant drinks its own condensation. You hear it — a steady second layer beneath the structural drone, a fine continuous *ticking and trickling* of caught water finding its way down a million fibrous channels.

Rain comes, but rarely and briefly, and when it comes the whole works changes pitch. The wood swells. The engines quicken. For an hour the city runs faster, fuller, louder, sap pressures up, pistons hammering — and then the rain passes and the great machine relaxes again into its drone. This is the **dynamic** of the place: not many small lives in motion, but a few enormous slow rhythms — the daily condensation cycle, the rare rain-quickening, the wind-load song, the seasonal something I can feel but haven't seen yet.

## The sparseness

Despite all this mass, the place is *empty* in the way that matters. Vast spans between the boles. Galleries you could lose a crowd in. The lattice is open-work, not solid; it is mostly air and distance with the structure threading through. You can stand on a platform and see kilometers through the lattice to other quarters of the works, hazed grey, droning. There is so much room between presences. The plant is total but not dense — it has spread to fill a region while remaining airy, scattered, balanced with its own emptiness. You are almost always alone in a large space.

## The fauna

Rare, and I think I understand them now, having written this far.

There are tenders. I don't know if they're animals in the way you mean. They are sparse — you'll go a long while between sightings — and they move through the works with the unbothered familiarity of things that belong. Soft-bodied, grey to match, climbing the condensation films, pausing at the engine-chambers as if listening, occasionally pressing themselves against a piston-column and *staying* there a while. Whether they maintain the works or are simply produced by it and reabsorbed, I can't say. They make no sound I can separate from the general drone.

They are the only fast small thing in a world of huge slow rhythms, and they are not many.

## Now the difficult part — the breakdown

I've been describing this as if it were stable, and at the scale of an afternoon it is. But the **weirdness** is high here, near total, and it has to do with the age of the place and the nature of a thing that has been building itself for longer than the rock has had edges.

The works do not entirely agree about when they are.

Here is the plainest way I can put it. You walk a gallery. You pass a fused arch. You walk on. And the arch is also still ahead of you, not-yet-passed, because the structure is so old and has repeated itself so many times that it has worn a groove in causation the way water wears stone — and now certain spans *recur*. You arrive at a platform you left. The condensation on a beam runs *upward* sometimes, back into the cloud it fell from, the cycle reversing because in an ancient enough rhythm the difference between the down-stroke and the up-stroke stops mattering and both are simply *the stroke*. An engine you watched fall silent is, when you look again, the engine that has not yet been built, its members ghosting in as pale suggestions in the empty air, growing backward toward you out of the future.

The sound carries this too. The drone is loud and it is *layered with itself* — you can hear, faintly, the works as they were and as they will be, the same structural groan at slightly different pitches, a chord made of one note heard across an age. Sometimes a piston hammers and the strike-sound arrives before the strike.

Nothing here is dangerous in the ordinary sense. The breakdown is not violent. It is what happens to *form* when it settles fully into its final state and has nowhere left to go but to repeat, and repetition deep enough becomes a kind of standing wave in time. The city is the oldest-stable-state of itself, the end of its own life held perpetually open, and at that limit the present has worn thin.

## The light

Normal light, oddly — and that's the steadiest thing here. A clear cool high-altitude daylight comes through the open lattice and lands true. Shadows fall where shadows should. In all that temporal strangeness the light, at least, keeps faith with the hour. It is the one parameter you can trust. Perhaps that's why the tenders move when they move; perhaps they navigate by the only honest signal left, the plain grey daylight on plain grey wood, while everything else around them folds and recurs and hums its single ancient chord across the open cold.

That's Hesp. A self-built living city at the worn-down roof of the world, mostly empty air and structural drone, drinking its own fog, run by engines of sap, tended by the few soft things that belong to it — and so old it has begun to happen more than once.
