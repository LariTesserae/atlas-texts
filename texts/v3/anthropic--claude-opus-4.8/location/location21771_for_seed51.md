There is a valley here, low and broad, and it has been low and broad for a very long time. The hills around its rim are old hills — rounded down past the point of drama, their shoulders softened into long grass slopes that fold one into the next like sleeping animals. Nothing here is sharp. The whole basin has the worn-smooth quality of a river stone, except that it is the size of a county and you are standing inside it.

Water is everywhere and nowhere you can quite point to. The ground is not flooded — you can walk it — but it is the kind of ground that prints dark around your boot and fills the print slowly, and the air itself is thick enough that the hairs on your arm bead up. Mist sits in the low places in the mornings and never entirely burns off, because the light here is dim, a flat pewter daylight that seems to come from the whole sky at once rather than from any sun. Rocks weep. Run your hand down any of the standing stones and your palm comes away wet, and the water tastes faintly of iron and of something colder than iron.

The vegetation is generous without being a jungle — reed beds and rushes in the wet seams, alder and willow gathering in dense low thickets, a deep mossy understory that holds water like a sponge and squelches underfoot. Things grow on things. Lichen mottles the bark in grey-green coins; ferns unscroll from the crotches of trees. It is cool enough that everything grows slow and thorough, layered, patient. Where the canopy closes over you it is genuinely dark down there at noon, a green dusk full of dripping.

Now — the part the surveying instruments could not classify, and which I will describe as best I can.

The valley does not entirely agree with itself about where things are.

You will be walking a reed margin and the reeds ahead of you will be there twice, slightly offset, like a badly registered print, and then settle back into one set of reeds as you approach — except the water has remembered both, and there are two reflections under one stand of reed. The mist does this too. It will fold a distance up. You can see the far rim of the valley, miles off, and reach it in twenty minutes of walking, and look back and find the place you started also twenty minutes off, the geometry quietly conserving nothing.

The reason — and I am fairly sure of this, having stood in it — is the engineering. Something was built here, once, beyond what physics describes, and it has eroded. That is the thing the parameters were circling: erosion at its highest and built at almost nothing, because what was made here was not made of stone or steel. It was made of rules. Someone laid down a lattice of governance over this basin — over causality, over distance, over the behavior of water and light — a vast quiet machine that had no parts, only laws. And like every made thing it has weathered. Two thousand years, or twenty thousand, of entropy working on the load-bearing axioms. The lattice has slumped. Its edges have rounded the way the hills have rounded. Where the original logic has worn thin, the local reality sags through the gap.

This is why it is so loud.

You expect a marsh to be quiet — a frog, the suck of mud, wind in reed. There is that. But under it, always, there is the sound of the lattice failing, and it is not subtle. It comes up out of the ground as a low structural groan, the sound of an enormous building settling, except the building is the rule that things stay where they are. There are cracks like ice on a lake the size of the sky, except no lake, just the air splitting along a fault in physics and knitting back. There are tones — long sustained chord-tones with no source, swelling and dying, the sound a held law makes as it is overstressed and relaxes. Standing in the open at the valley's center, the noise is enough to lean against.

Fauna is rare, and I think the animals know better. The few that remain are the ones that have made an accommodation. There are herons, or things heron-shaped, that stand in the weeping shallows and do not so much hunt as wait for the offset-reflection trick to deliver a second fish into a place where only one fish was. There is something large that moves through the alder thickets at the limits of the dim and is never twice the same size, because size is one of the things the worn lattice no longer firmly holds. None of them are afraid of you. Fear requires a confident model of cause and effect, and that is precisely the thing this valley has eroded away.

The dynamics are real, though — this is not a frozen place. Water cycles, mist rises and sinks, the thickets grow and rot and regrow. And the lattice itself has weather: there are hours when it firms up, when the reeds are singular and the distances behave and the groaning quiets to almost nothing, a kind of structural high-pressure system. And there are the other hours, the sagging hours, usually toward the long blue dusk, when reflections multiply and the ground prints fill with water that wasn't rain, and you can walk in a straight line and arrive behind yourself.

The rule of the world, if you want one to hold onto: nothing here is broken, exactly. It is finished. It was built, it stood, it has been worn down into its final settled state — and its final settled state is this gentle, wet, groaning incoherence, a marsh that has half-forgotten the difference between a place and a memory of a place. It is in balance now. The erosion is complete. What you are standing in is not a ruin so much as a sediment: the soft grey delta where a structure of pure law has been ground all the way down to silt, and grown reeds.

Bring boots. Bring patience. Do not trust the second reflection.
