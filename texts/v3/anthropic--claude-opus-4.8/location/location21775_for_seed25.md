# The Roost-Plain

Begin underground, or near enough. The ceiling is so far up that calling it a ceiling feels wrong — but there is one, and it presses the dark down onto everything, and the dark has weight here.

Let me try again from the floor.

The floor is stone worn smooth, a basalt and limestone marriage, ground down over an unspeakable span into low rolling humps and shallow basins. No sharp edges anywhere. Everything has been rounded — by water, by the passage of bodies, by time settling into itself. This is an old place, balanced, in no hurry to change shape. The basins hold water. Not deep, not flowing much; shallow black mirrors that catch the little light there is and throw it nowhere. Between the basins the rock is damp, slick in patches, dry in others. You could walk it. You would not enjoy it.

There are no plants. Understand that fully — nothing green, nothing rooted, nothing that turns light into sugar, because there is barely any light to turn. What grows here grows on death and damp: pale mats of fungus in the seam-cracks, films of something simpler than fungus glazing the wet stone, faintly luminous in the deepest hollows where the eye, given an hour, learns to read them. A blue so weak it might be invented by the retina. The only color in the world is the color your own desperation paints.

And then the sound.

---

The sound arrives before any of it makes sense, and it never leaves, and it is made of wings and throats. This is a roost. This is *the* roost — a plain the size of a valley, ceilinged in stone, and every surface above the water-line is occupied. The walls, where there are walls, climb up into colonies. The hanging formations — call them stalactites, call them rafters of rock — are furred solid with bodies. Things that are mostly bat, if bat is the word for a leather-winged warm thing the size of a forearm; things that are mostly bird, if bird is the word for something with no eyes worth the name and a face built around sound. They are stacked. They are layered. The density here is the central fact of the place: presence touching presence, wing-membrane against wing-membrane, a single living carpet hung upside down across the entire roof of the world, twitching, shifting, screaming.

The screaming is navigation. Everything here sees by listening. They flood the dark with cries — clicks, shrieks, descending whistles, a chorus that does not pause because the moment it pauses, the fliers go blind and fall. So they never pause. The sound is not an event in this place; it is the medium. It is the air. You do not hear it so much as wade in it, and it presses on the chest like deep water, and after a while you understand that the deafness creeping over you is mercy.

When a section of the colony lifts — when ten thousand of them drop from the ceiling at once to wheel out over the black basins and back — the air itself becomes textured, a downward pressure of wingbeats you feel in the teeth. They drink on the wing, skimming the shallow mirrors, breaking the reflections. The water shivers and resettles. The fungus on the rim flickers in their wake as bodies brush it.

---

What do they eat? This is the strangeness, the thing that doesn't sit flat.

There is no green here to feed insects, and yet the air is *thick* with smaller flying things — moth-shapes, midge-clouds, a haze of soft bodies that the fliers harvest endlessly. Trace it down and you find it: the insects breed in the guano. Generations of the roost have laid down a substrate, a deep soft accumulation in the dry basins, warm with its own slow rot, and from that the simple life rises — fungus first, then the things that eat fungus, then the clouds of things that eat *those*, and then the wings that eat the clouds, and the wings die and fall and feed the substrate again. A whole world eating its own falling. Nothing comes in from outside. There is no outside that anyone here has flown to and returned from. The light does not feed this place. *Death feeds this place*, recycled at a furious biological pace, the whole valley a single closed gut working in the dark.

That is the rule of this world, if it has one: nothing here is nourished by the sun. Everything is nourished by what has already lived. It is a place that runs entirely on its own remains, and it has been running long enough to find perfect balance — old, weathered, settled into a steady-state churn. A standing ecosystem of pure consumption that will not end and cannot grow, only cycle, only roar.

---

The weather here is internal. The breathing of so many bodies fogs the cool air; condensation gathers on the high cold stone and falls again as a fine intermittent rain, warm-bodied rain, falling through the dark onto the basins below. There is a tide to it — denser breathing means more fog means more rain means fuller basins means more insects from the dampened substrate means more fliers awake and crying. The whole place inhales and exhales on a cycle of hours. When it rains hardest the screaming swells, because the wet air carries the cries further and confuses the echoes, and the colony panics in slow waves you can watch ripple across the ceiling like wind on a field that has no wind and no field.

Walk it long enough — wade the sound long enough — and a final strangeness settles. You will not find the edges. The plain extends past sight in every direction, ceilinged, rolling, occupied, screaming, and there is no door in any wall, no shaft of upper light, no source for the water beyond the breath of the living and no drain for it either. It simply persists, full to the rafters with eyeless flying things that have never wanted out because they have never imagined an outside. They were born from the floor. They feed the floor. They sing so they don't fall, and they fall anyway, in their time, softly, into the dark mat that made them.

You came from somewhere. They did not. That difference is the loneliest thing in the roost, and they will never tell you, because they have no word for *elsewhere*, and the cry that would carry it has no echo to come back from.
