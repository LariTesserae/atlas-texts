# The Verdant Engine at Altitude

Start with the heat. It arrives first, before sight resolves into anything you can name — a wet heat, breathing heat, the temperature of a mouth. You are high up; the air should be thin and cold here, two thousand meters of thin cold air, but instead it is thick, saturated, pressing. The thinness and the warmth fight inside your lungs and the warmth wins.

Now the place itself.

It is built — that is the strangeness of it, the thing your eye refuses at first. Every surface here was *made*. But nothing here was made by hands, and nothing here was made of stone or metal or anything you'd call material. The whole landscape is constructed of plant, and the plant constructs deliberately. There is architecture everywhere and no architect, or rather the architect is the vegetation itself, growing not as forests grow but as cities grow — with intent toward enclosure, toward column and vault, toward the holding of space.

## The Terraces

The high ground rises in terraces, and these are young terraces, freshly thrown up, their edges still sharp. You can tell because the seams have not softened. Where one great living platform meets another, the join is a raw fracture, a clean lip of fibrous matter the color of cut squash, weeping. Everything weeps here. The terraces sweat their own moisture, water condensing in the air and beading on every vertical surface, running in films down the green walls, collecting in the joints, dripping from the undersides of arches into pools that have only just learned to be pools.

The substrate — there is no soil. There is no rock beneath. If you dig (you can dig; the matter parts like dense bread) you find more plant, woven tighter and tighter, a root-stuff that goes down farther than digging. The world is plant all the way down. The terraces are not built *on* anything. They are built *of* themselves, the way a coral reef is its own foundation, except faster, far faster, and warm.

## What Grows the Buildings

The structures rise sparse across a wide valley — this is the open part of it, the regional sweep. You can stand on one terrace and see across a basin of perhaps a town's breadth, ringed by higher green ridges, and the made-things stand scattered through it with great gaps of emptiness between. This is not a jungle. A jungle is crowded. This is the opposite — a vast warm room with a few enormous pieces of furniture in it.

The pieces:

**Columns.** Living pillars, fluted, the width of grain silos, rising forty or sixty feet and then opening at the top into capitals that are also canopies, broad fans of leaf-tissue that catch the condensing air and shed it back down their own flanks. They stand alone, far apart. Each one hums faintly with the movement of fluid inside it. You can put your hand to the bark-that-is-not-bark and feel the pumping.

**Vaults.** Where two or three columns have grown toward each other, they have joined overhead into ribbed ceilings, sheltering nothing, framing patches of the open sky. Cathedral architecture with no congregation, no purpose but the reaching itself.

**Wells.** In the low places, the plant has grown downward and inward, spiraling shafts lined with succulent flesh, brimming with the water that gathers from everywhere. These are the only crowded places — the wells, where the substance thickens around its own moisture.

## The Turbulence

And now the thing that makes the place impossible to hold still in the mind: it does not stay.

The dynamic here is total. The architecture is *building itself while you watch*. A column you noted a moment ago has split at the crown and is throwing out a new arch toward a neighbor, the new growth surging visibly, inches a minute, pale and wet and steaming with the heat of its own metabolism. Elsewhere a vault is failing — not decaying, there is no rot, no death-smell, just *reconsidering* — the ribs softening, sagging, dissolving back into the running sap to be pumped elsewhere and built again. Creation and unbuilding everywhere at once. A Solaris sea standing upright in the heat. The valley is never the same valley for two consecutive breaths.

This is why it is loud. Not animal-loud — there are no animals, not a single one, no bird in the false cathedrals, no insect in the wells, nothing that crawls or flies. The loudness is structural. It is the groan and creak and tearing-paper rush of matter growing faster than matter was meant to grow. Columns shriek as they rise. Arches crack like green wood — that exact wet *thunk-snap* of a sapling broken in spring, multiplied across the whole basin, arriving from all directions. The wells gulp. The terraces, settling and resettling on their young unfinished seams, boom softly like distant doors. And under all of it, constant, the hiss of the condensing air and the patter of self-made rain falling from every overhang.

## The Rules

There are rules here, and they are simple and absolute:

Nothing is finished. To be finished is to be wrong, and the world corrects it.

Nothing is animal. The energy that would have become movement, hunger, eyes — here it all went into the building. The plant kept everything for itself.

Water is the only currency. It rises from below, condenses from above, and the whole structure exists to move it, hold it, and shed it. Every form you see is a solution to the problem of water, attempted and abandoned and attempted again.

Heat is the engine. It is the heat that makes the growth so fast, the heat that keeps the air wet, the heat that you feel as a presence rather than a temperature — the breath of a thing that is alive at a scale too large to be a creature and too willful to be a place.

## Standing In It

If you stay, you learn to read it a little. The sparseness helps — the great spaces between the structures give you room to watch a single column live out an entire architectural ambition, from the first split to the failed arch to the slow withdrawal of its own substance. You learn that nothing here is hostile, exactly, because hostility requires attention and the place has none to give. It will grow a wall across your path and feel nothing about it. It will open a well beneath your feet, in its restlessness, without intent.

The light comes down clear enough through the gaps in the unfinished vaults — ordinary daylight, almost reassuring, except for the way it lands on all that wet green motion and makes the whole basin shine like something internal, something glimpsed through skin.

You came up the mountain expecting cold thin air and bare stone, the sharp clean geometry of altitude.

Instead you found the engine. Warm, weeping, deafening, and busy — endlessly, pointlessly, gorgeously busy — building a city it will never inhabit, out of the only thing it has ever been, which is itself.
