There is a place where the water came up through the floor of a thing that men had built, and stayed.

You find it by following the low ground. The land here doesn't rise to meet you anywhere — it lies flat and slightly sunken, a basin maybe the size of a few streets, ringed by old slag banks that have gone soft and rounded under a hundred winters of rain. The banks weep. That's the first thing you notice: the rocks are not dry. Every surface holds a film of moisture, and where the slag has cracked, dark water sweats out and beads and lets go, and the falling of those drops is most of the sound there is.

This was a works of some kind. You can tell from what remains — and not much remains, which is the strange part, because what's here is mostly intact and what's gone is gone completely. A single brick gable stands with its windows empty. A flywheel lies half-sunk in the marsh-water, taller than a man, its rim furred green, and you cannot find the engine it belonged to. There are pipes. The pipes are everywhere, and this is where the place stops being only a ruin.

Because the water that came up through the floor did not simply flood the works. It learned the works. It moved into the pipe-runs and the culverts and the casting-pits, and now it lives there with a kind of restlessness that has nothing settled about it, even though everything else has settled completely. The basin is in balance — the slag is weathered, the brick is at its long ease, the moss has had its centuries — and yet the water inside the works will not hold still. It rises in one channel and you hear it gurgle and pull, and then it drops, and a cold throat-sound comes up out of an iron mouth somewhere, and then a different channel fills. It pours through the standing pipes in slow surges. It overspills a sluice and the sluice runs for a minute and then runs dry and then runs again, with no rain to feed it, with no engine to pump it. The whole dead industrial body of the place is being breathed through by water that behaves as though it were being made and unmade somewhere just out of sight.

The vegetation is thin and chosen. Almost nothing grows in the open basin — the ground is too sour, too metal-bitter — but along the warm sides of the surviving brick there are sedges, and a few birches, pale and small, leaning the way the prevailing weather pushed them when they were saplings. The leaves are out but sparse, more sky than leaf. They drip. Everything drips. The temperature is the cool of a cellar in early spring, the kind that gets into the seams of your coat, and the air is so close to its dewpoint that your breath barely shows and the far gable goes faintly soft, as if the basin keeps trying to become fog and not quite managing.

There is hardly any animal life. Once, a heron — grey, motionless, standing in the flooded casting-pit on legs like the pipes around it, so much a part of the place that you don't see it until it moves. A few water-skaters where the surges leave a momentary calm. Nothing sings. The silence is real and large and the dripping moves around inside it without filling it.

Now — the rule of the world here, the thing you must understand if you stay.

The water is doing something. Not flooding, not draining, not the patient cycle of a marsh. It is *running the works.* Every minute the basin reconfigures: a pipe that was full empties and a culvert that was silent begins to talk, and there is a logic to the sequence, almost, a sense that the dead machine is being driven through its motions by the only thing left that can drive anything. The flywheel does not turn — but the water moving through the pit beside it surges in a rhythm, and if you stand long enough you start to feel that the rhythm is the rhythm the wheel once turned at, that the place is remembering its own labor and the water is the form that memory takes. It builds and it tears down. A standing column of water rises in a vertical pipe — held, somehow, longer than it should be — and then collapses with a slam that you feel in the soft ground, and the whole basin shivers, and the dripping pauses for one held second across the entire ruin, and then resumes.

That is the turbulence of the place. Not storm. Not violence you can point at. A dead body kept in restless industrial motion by water that has gone slightly wrong, slightly *willing,* in a basin so quiet you can hear the iron think. Stillness in everything built and grown; frenzy in the one element that should be calmest. The works are at rest. The water is at work, and will not stop, and has no product, and makes nothing but the long surging breath of a place that has outlived its purpose and found a stranger one.

You leave by climbing the weeping slag. From the rim you look back and the basin is already softening into its almost-fog, and you can hear, faint and even, the sluice running and stopping and running, keeping a time that nothing measures.
