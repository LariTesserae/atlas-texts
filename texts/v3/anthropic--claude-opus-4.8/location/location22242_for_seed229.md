# The Singing Shelf

There is no water here, and yet everything grows.

You climb to it along a spine of pale rock — a high shelf, maybe the size of a market square, hung between two greater darknesses you never quite see. The air is warm the way a held breath is warm, and it is loud. That is the first thing. Before you arrive you hear it: the place announces itself a long way off, a roar that resolves, as you near, into ten thousand smaller roars.

## The Soil That Is Not Soil

The ground is dry. Bone-dry, mineral-dry, a fine lavender grit that runs through the fingers and leaves no damp trace. Nothing should live in it. But the vegetation here has solved the problem of waterlessness the way certain stubborn ideas solve the problem of evidence — by ignoring it.

The plants drink sound.

The big ones are the **bell-reeds**, taller than a person, hollow, their stems open at the top like trumpets turned to the sky. They do not photosynthesize so much as they *acousto-synthesize* — they take in the constant noise of the shelf, the wind-roar and the animal-cry, and they pack it down into sugar and into more reed. You can watch a young bell-reed grow during a loud hour, swelling visibly, a green inch of new stem laid down with every passing thunder of the herd. In a quiet hour — and there are very few — they sag and go translucent and wait.

Between them grow the **mat-mosses**, dense lush cushions in violet and ochre that hum back whatever they are sung. Step on one and it returns your footfall as a low note, slightly wrong, slightly late. The whole shelf is patched with them, sparse and scattered, much bare grit between, so that the green is not a blanket but an archipelago — islands of growth in a sea of dust, each island ringing its own pitch.

## The Sound

I keep returning to the sound because the place keeps returning to it.

It comes from everywhere and from each thing separately. The bell-reeds boom in the wind. The mosses drone. And the fauna — the fauna are abundant, more abundant than the sparse plants should be able to support, except that here the food chain runs on noise, and noise is the one thing in endless supply.

There are the **clatterlings**: small six-legged things, hard-shelled, that move in scattered crowds and produce a sound like dice thrown on stone, continuously, by striking their own carapaces. They eat the silence-pockets that form in the lee of rocks — they need quiet to digest, and so they make their own racket to drive the quiet away from everywhere *else*, herding it, cornering it, then feeding. You will see a hush gather in a hollow and a tide of clatterlings pour toward it like water toward a drain that runs the wrong way.

There are the **callers** — larger, slow, soft-bodied, perched on the rim of the shelf, who do nothing all day but sing single sustained tones that the bell-reeds drink directly. They are crops, in a sense, though no one farms them. They sing because they are full and want to grow lighter. A fed caller sings; a hungry caller listens; and what a hungry caller eats is the *songs of other callers*, so that the whole rim is a slow war of voices, each trying to out-sing and so out-feed its neighbor.

## The Rule of the Place

Here is the law of this shelf, the strangeness at its center: **nothing is allowed to be still, and nothing is allowed to be silent, and the two prohibitions are the same prohibition.**

A thing that stops moving stops sounding. A thing that stops sounding starves — or worse, becomes a silence-pocket, and is hunted. So the whole landscape is in permanent turbulent motion, a churn, a bazaar with no merchants and no goods, only the haggling itself, perpetual. The crowd of clatterlings reshapes itself minute by minute. The mosses shift their pitches to stay ahead of being eaten. The bell-reeds grow and topple and are replaced. You cannot photograph this place because it will not hold a pose long enough; any image is already a lie by the time it is finished.

## The Marks of Hands

And yet there were people here, or are, scattered and few.

You find them in the **strikers** — small standing stones, set up at intervals across the shelf, each one shaped and notched and hung with a hand-knapped clapper of harder rock. Primitive work: no metal, just stone worked against stone, cord of plaited reed-fiber. When the wind moves, the clappers swing and ring the standing stones, and these tones are *deliberate* — tuned, placed, a made music laid over the wild one.

I think the strikers are tombs. I think someone learned that on this shelf the dead, being silent, are eaten and erased — and so the survivors stand a singing stone where the body would be, and keep the dead loud, and so keep them present. A grave that goes quiet is a grave whose occupant is truly gone. So the people, whoever they were, became a people whose entire craft, whose whole religion, was the keeping-up of noise.

There are not many strikers. The shelf is small and the living are few and the silence is patient. Some of the stones have fallen and gone still, and around each fallen one is a clean ring of bare lavender grit where nothing grows and nothing comes — a healed wound, a place that has finished being a place.

## At the Edge

The light is dim throughout — a permanent gloaming, sourceless, the color of held smoke. It does not change much, which makes the shelf's turbulence stranger: all that frantic motion under a sky that never moves.

Stand at the rim, by the singing callers, and look out over the edge into the two darknesses, and you will understand why everything here is so loud. The shelf is small and the dark is not, and the dark does not make a sound. The whole roaring garden is one small lit thing shouting at the silence on every side, growing on its own noise, eating its own quiet, refusing — reed and clatterling and striker-stone alike — to stop.

It is exhausting. It is also, I think, the bravest landscape I have ever described.

You will want to leave before nightfall, except there is no nightfall. You will simply, at some point, have had enough, and turn back down the spine of pale rock — and the roar will follow you for a long while, and then thin, and then there will be only your own footsteps, very loud now, in a quiet that you had forgotten was possible, and that the people of the shelf spend their whole lives keeping at bay.
