# The Worn Tablelands

Start with the height, because everything here is decided by it.

You are high — high enough that the air thins to a clean, warm transparency, high enough that the horizon curves away in every direction into a haze of distance. But there is no peak. There is no summit, no jagged crown of rock. The mountains here finished being mountains a very long time ago. What remains is a vast plateau, a tableland worn down past sharpness, past drama, into something older than ambition. The stone has been rounded the way a riverbed pebble is rounded, but over a scale of time that makes rivers seem hasty. Whole ranges have been sanded flat. You stand at the roof of a world that has forgotten it was ever steep.

The ground is a pale, warm-toned stone, ochre and bone and the soft grey of old teeth, swept into long low swells like the backs of sleeping animals. Underfoot it is smooth, almost polished, glassy in places where some mineral has wept to the surface and set. Cross it and you feel how the whole surface is one continuous skin — not slabs and joints, but a single ancient pour, healed of all its cracks. The erosion here is total and finished. Nothing is being broken down anymore because nothing sharp is left to break. This is the end-state. The landscape has settled into its final form and intends to keep it.

## Water

It comes and goes, and mostly comes from the air rather than the ground. In the warm hours the plateau breathes a faint haze; toward the cool of evening that breath condenses, and the polished stone darkens with a film of moisture that beads in the shallow basins. There are no rivers up here — they finished too, long ago, leaving sinuous dry channels you can trace by their slightly smoother floors. But in the low places, the rounded hollows between the stone swells, water gathers: shallow pools the color of weak tea, ringed with mineral salts, warm as a held breath. They never run. They evaporate and refill on a slow tidal rhythm of the days. Step near one and you may startle the surface — though it is not the surface moving.

## The living saturation

Here is what overwhelms.

The plateau looks, at first, almost barren — sparse low plants, cushion-things clinging in the cracks, grey-green and leathery, hoarding the night's dew. You think: a quiet place, a dry high emptiness.

Then you understand that the ground is *occupied*. Not richly vegetated — densely *inhabited*. The animal life here is layered into every available pocket, near-touching, packed into the warmth and the moisture-traps with a saturation that has no business existing in such a worn-out place.

The cushion-plants are not plants. Or — some are plants, and around and through and beneath them live colonies of soft-bodied creatures the size of a thumb, translucent, that filter the dew and the haze through fringed mouths. They cluster so thick that the cushions pulse faintly with their breathing. Each hollow in the stone is a stacked city: filter-things at the bottom near the water, ribbon-creatures looped through the cracks above them, and over everything a slow drift of pale gliding animals that ride the warm thermals coming off the rock, wings like wet paper, never landing, eating the things that the dew brings up.

The density is vertical and total. Where there is any shelter at all — the lee of a swell, the shadowed wall of a dry channel — life is stacked from the stone to the height of your knee, and you cannot put a hand down without touching something that flinches.

## The strangeness of it being quiet

This is what unsettles, and it takes a while to name. With this much life packed this close, you brace for noise — a chorus, a din, the riot of a crowded ecosystem. It does not come.

It is almost silent.

The overwhelming fauna is a *silent* fauna. The filter-things breathe without sound. The gliders ride the air without a single beat. The ribbon-creatures move by slow peristaltic flexing, mouthless or nearly so. The only sounds are the faint hiss of warm air over stone, the occasional tick of a pool releasing a bubble, and a low subsonic pressure you feel more than hear — the collective slow respiration of millions of small bodies, a pulse just beneath the threshold of the ear.

The animals communicate by other means. Watch long enough and you see the colonies flush color in waves — a basin will run pale blue and then deepen to violet and back, a signal passing through the packed bodies like wind through grain. This is their speech. They are talking constantly, urgently, in light. The silence is not emptiness; it is a whole language held below sound.

## The marks of someone

Scattered across the tablelands, far apart, you come upon the built things.

Low cairns of the polished stone, stacked by hands — they must be hands — into squat rounded towers that match the worn aesthetic of everything around them, as though the builders learned their forms from the erosion itself. Some have a hollow at the top that holds dew, deliberately, a made pool. Around these the silent fauna gathers thickest. The structures are old but not ancient; they have the patina of long use, not abandonment. Whoever made them maintained the dew-cisterns and then, recently or not, stopped. There are no tools anywhere. No metal, no worked edges beyond what stone-on-stone can shape. Whatever tended these places did so with bodies and patience and nothing else.

You do not see them. But sometimes, at the violet hour when the condensation comes, a larger flush of color crosses a whole valley of hollows at once, coordinated, deliberate, far too organized for the small creatures — and you wonder whether the builders are not gone at all, only very slow, only speaking in a register of light and time you are too quick to perceive.

## The pace of the world

Nothing hurries. The day turns warm and the haze rises; the evening cools and the dew falls; the pools breathe in and out across the slow cycle. The gliders circle for hours without descending. The color-speech of the colonies moves at the speed of contemplation. Change happens, but across durations that make a single human visit a held breath in a conversation lasting ages.

This is a finished place — eroded to its last calm form, settled, patient, near-eternal — and into that stillness an enormous quantity of quiet life has poured itself and learned to be still too. It is not dying. It is the opposite: it is so old and so full that it has stopped needing to rush, stopped needing to be loud, stopped needing anything but the dew, the warmth, and the long unhurried language of light passing body to body across the worn roof of the world.

Stand still. Let the air settle on your skin in beads. Wait for the violet hour. Watch the valley answer.
