# The Drowned Foundry at Cell-and-Spire

Below the surface — not deep, but below — there is a city that breathes underwater, and breathes slowly.

You arrive at it the way water arrives at anything: by settling. The descent is short. A few fathoms above, you can still sense where the light gives up, a pale ceiling that has nothing to do with sky. Down here the water is warm, body-warm, the temperature of something recently alive, and it holds you the way a held breath holds a word. This is not the cold black of true ocean. This is a flooded room the size of a small town, and it has been flooded for a very long time, and it has made peace with the water the way old stone makes peace with rain.

## The Form of the Place

It was built once. You can read this everywhere. The streets are still streets — narrow channels between structures, paved in a dark sintered material gone soft-edged with centuries of dissolution. The buildings still stand in their rows, two and three stories where stories used to matter, their facades smoothed now into something more like coral logic than masonry. Doorways round into mouths. Window frames sag into ovals. Everything that was once a right angle has been negotiated down to a curve by the patient sandpaper of the current — and the current is barely a current at all, just a slow tidal lean, a drift you'd measure in hours, the whole town inhaling over the course of an afternoon and exhaling over the course of an evening.

Erosion here is not damage. It is finish. The place has reached the state it was always going to reach. Nothing is collapsing because nothing has any further to fall — it has all found its angle of rest, settled into the version of itself that the water approves of.

## The Industry

And yet it was industrial, and the industry is not entirely gone.

In the center of the cluster stands the Foundry — that is the word that comes, though no one named it. A great squat building, its roof long opened to the water, and inside it the machines. Engines the size of houses, flanged and pistoned, gone the color of old pennies and older bronze. Pipe-runs thread between the structures of the whole settlement, an arterial system of conduits and ducts, much of it still intact, all of it submerged.

The strange thing — the thing that makes the place strange — is that some of it still works.

Not loudly. Nothing here is loud. But if you hold still in the warm dark you will feel, through the water against your skin, the slow turn of something massive. A flywheel, perhaps, taking a full minute to complete one revolution, geared down and down until its motion is almost geological. A bellows somewhere expanding over the space of ten heartbeats and contracting over the space of twenty. The machines have not stopped. They have only slowed to match the place — industry running at the speed of erosion, production at the speed of patience. Whatever they are making, they make it one increment per tide.

You can put your hand against a pipe and feel a faint warmth inside it, a difference of a degree, and understand that this is the source of the body-heat in the water. The foundry is still firing, somehow, somewhere, banked low. The whole drowned town is kept warm by an engine that refuses to be finished with its work.

## Density and the Garden

Here is the contradiction the place resolves so easily: it is packed, utterly packed, no room between presences — and yet it is quiet, dim, nearly empty of animals.

What fills it is vegetation. Not seaweed exactly — something between weed and vine and moss, lush dark-green and dark-red growths that have colonized every surface so completely that there is no bare anything left. The streets are carpeted. The machines are upholstered in it. The pipes wear sleeves of soft growth that pulse very slightly with the warmth they carry. Everything is occupied. The density is total — there is no gap, no clean surface, no void; the place is *saturated* with quiet living matter, and through it all the slow machinery turns, and the plants have grown into the gear-teeth and been carried around with them so gently that they keep growing.

The light comes from the plants, partly. A dim phosphor-green, the color of a watch face left too long in a drawer. It does not illuminate so much as suggest. You move through volumes of barely-there glow, and the foundry's banked warmth makes the deeper rooms a faintly redder dark, and between the two the whole town exists in the dimness of a closed eye.

## What Lives Here

Fauna is rare and shy. Now and then a thing crosses your vision — a slow pale shape navigating the channels, fish-like or perhaps not, unhurried, incurious. Something with too many fins that grazes the wall-gardens. A single drifting bell of a creature that pulses past the foundry mouth and is gone. They are infrequent enough that each one feels like an event, a punctuation in long sentences of silence.

The silence is the deepest fact of the place. Underwater, sound should carry — but here it is muffled by all that soft growth, absorbed by the wool of the gardens, so that even the great turning machines make only a felt vibration and no audible noise. You hear your own pulse. You hear, occasionally, a single deep *tonk* from somewhere in the foundry as a piston reaches the end of its long slow stroke — one note, and then minutes of quiet before the next. It is the most patient music there is.

## The Rule of the World

The rule, if the place has one, is this: *nothing ends; it only slows.* 

The town was abandoned, but it did not die — it decelerated. The machines were left running, and instead of breaking down they wound down, finding a rhythm so slow that breaking became impossible. The plants did not overwhelm the works; they joined them, and now turn with them. The erosion did not destroy the architecture; it completed it. Even the water itself seems to move at the speed of intention, leaning into the streets and leaning back out, warm and green and full and silent.

You could stay here a long time. The warmth would hold you. The slow tonk of the foundry would keep a kind of time. And you would understand, slowly, the way everything is understood here, that you had arrived at a place that had stopped hurrying toward anything — that had arrived, long before you, at exactly the version of itself it meant to be, and had decided to stay there, gently, underwater, forever-minus-a-little, turning one tooth of one gear per tide.
