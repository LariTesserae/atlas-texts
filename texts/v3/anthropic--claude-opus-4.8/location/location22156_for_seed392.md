Begin with the heat. It is the first thing and it does not leave. A wet heat, the kind that beads on the inside of your wrist before you've done anything to earn it, the kind that makes the air feel like a thing you push through rather than breathe. Everything here is hot and everything here is wet, and these two facts have been holding hands for so long that you cannot find the seam where one becomes the other.

Now the place itself.

It was built. All of it. There is no ground underneath the ground — dig and you find more making. The floor of this world is a vast lattice of fired clay tiles, hand-pressed, each one bearing the thumb-drag of whoever shaped it, and beneath those tiles, when a corner lifts, are more tiles, and beneath those, more. The makers did not stop at the surface. They built downward as a devotion. Nobody alive remembers reaching a bottom.

The tiles weep. Water sits just under them, near, always near, and it comes up through the grout in slow blisters and runs in shallow sheets across the glaze. So the whole made-floor of the world shines, a low country of ceramic catching a sky that is white-bright, sourceless, evenly lit as the inside of a lamp. There are no shadows worth the name. The light does not come from a sun you can point to. It is simply the condition of the air: luminous, total, the brightness of an overexposed afternoon held forever at the same hour.

The structures rise out of the wet tile sparsely, with great calm distances between them. This is not a city — too much emptiness for that. It is more like a plain across which someone has set down a few enormous made-things and then walked away thousands of years ago. A stepped tower of the same clay, glazed turquoise, sweating. A long colonnade going nowhere, its pillars wrapped in vines so thoroughly that the stone is a rumor. A single great basin, wide as a town square, brim-full and overflowing in a continuous unhurried lip of water that has been falling at exactly that rate, that exact volume, since before anyone was counting.

Because nothing here changes. That is the law of the place, the deep rule under all the others. The water that runs never lowers its source. The vines that climb have already arrived. The overflow falls and falls and the basin is never empty and never floods. Time passes — you can feel it passing, you sweat, you age — but the landscape does not spend itself. It is at equilibrium so complete it feels less like stillness and more like a held breath that has forgotten it is a breath.

And it is quiet. This is the strangeness that takes longest to name. With so much water moving, you expect roar, babble, the white noise of a wet world. Instead the water makes almost no sound. The overflow of the great basin falls in silence, or near it — a sound so soft it is mostly the memory of sound. Your own footsteps on the wet tile come back muffled, as though the air drinks them. When a bird calls — and there are birds, there is a great deal of life — the call seems to happen at a distance even when the bird is close, even when it is on your shoulder.

The life is abundant and it is everywhere and it ignores the rule about stillness, or seems to, which is the place's first real wrongness. Frogs the color of cut limes sit in the grout-channels by the dozen. Long-legged wading things, half heron and half something the eye refuses to finish, step through the shallow sheet-water with terrible patience. Vines pour off every made surface, lush past reason, fruiting — heavy purple fruit, split-open and dripping nectar that the ants carry away in glittering files. There are more animals than the quiet can account for. A thousand living throats and almost no noise from any of them. They move, they hunt, they mate, they die — and yet the count never shifts. As many frogs tomorrow as today. The herons that eat the frogs are never fewer frogs the next morning. The fruit falls and rots and is replaced and the tree was never less full.

This is where the reality of the place begins to come apart at the edges, gently, the way wet paper softens rather than tears.

The vines, if you follow one with your eye, do not always end where they began. A tendril leaves a pillar, crosses the open air, and arrives at the same pillar a few feet higher, having gone nowhere and arrived somewhere. The reflection in the sheet-water shows a sky that is brighter than the sky, and sometimes shows structures that are not standing above it — a tower that fell, perhaps, or was never built, present only in the wet mirror underfoot. Walk toward the far colonnade and the distance between you and it stays exactly what it was, not because you fail to move but because the place keeps the relation constant; it is more interested in equilibrium than in geometry.

The makers built this to never change, and it has obeyed them past the point where obedience makes sense. To hold everything perfectly still, the world has had to start cheating. It runs the same moment over and over with small enough variation that you cannot catch it, and where the loop doesn't quite meet itself, where the seam shows, that is the weirdness: the doubled vine, the surplus heron, the silence where a waterfall should be loud, the fruit that you eat and find whole again in your hand.

Everything is fresh. That is the other surprise. For a world this old — old enough to have worn its own rules thin — nothing is worn. The clay tiles are sharp-edged, unrounded, their stamped patterns crisp. The glaze is unscratched. The carved colonnade still shows the tool-marks, the simple chisel-bite of hand-work, copper or bronze on stone, no machine anywhere in it. This place was made by hands and never by engines, and then it was set to stay new forever, and so it has the eerie sharpness of the just-finished even though no one has lifted a tool here in an unimaginable while. Young features on an ancient frame. The land has not eroded because erosion is change, and change is forbidden, and so even the weather — the hot wet weather that should crumble clay in a single century — has been told to stop at the surface and just shine there, just bead and run and never wear.

Stand in the middle of it. The open distances, the few great sweating monuments, the silver floor, the silent falling water, the green excess of life that does not know it is trapped in a single perfect afternoon. The heat presses. The light has no source and no end. A frog the color of a wound jumps, lands, becomes two frogs, becomes one. Somewhere a heron has been about to strike for a thousand years and will strike forever and the strike never lands and the fish is never eaten and is always there, just under the bright skin of the water, waiting in a world that has solved death by refusing to let anything finish.

That is the place. Made, hot, wet, bright, quiet, full, and held — held so hard it has begun to fray.
