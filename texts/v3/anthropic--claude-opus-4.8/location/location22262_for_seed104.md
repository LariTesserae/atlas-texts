# The Drowned Highlands

Begin with the water, because everything begins with the water here.

It is not a sea. The distinction matters. A sea has a surface, a floor, a place where it ends and air begins. This has none of that. The water fills the high places — the plateaus and the ridgelines, the old eroded shoulders of mountains worn down to nubs — and it does not lie flat. It hangs. It stacks. It holds itself in layers the way smoke holds itself in still air, and you can move through it as though swimming, except that swimming implies you must come up, and there is no up that ends.

The water is warm. Blood-warm near the bottom, cooling as you rise, but never cold, never anything that would make you flinch. It moves with a slowness that suggests great age — currents that have been turning the same slow turn for so long they have worn channels into the stone they pass over, smoothing the granite down to something like soap, something that takes the light and gives back a soft milky glow from deep inside the rock itself.

## The Stone Beneath

The mountains are old past the point of being mountains. They have surrendered. What remains are broad humped backs of rock, rounded the way river-pebbles are rounded, except these are the size of valleys. Nothing here is sharp. Erosion has had its full conversation with this place and the place has agreed to everything, settled into the shape water wants it to be: smooth, low-shouldered, patient.

You will find — if you drift long enough through the warm hanging layers — the partial remains of walls. Low courses of fitted stone, half a doorway, the rounded stump of what was once a tower, all of it furred now with growth and worn nearly back into the bedrock. No one knows who built them, and there is no one to ask, and the question itself feels like it belongs to a younger world than this one. The structures are scattered, far apart, more like the marks a hand leaves than the work a hand does. They do not cluster. They keep their distances across the vast slow dim.

## The Green

Vegetation here does not grow up toward light, because light comes from everywhere and nowhere — a dim even radiance, the color of weak tea, leaking out of the stone and the water both. So the plants grow *outward*. They grow in sheets and fans and great drifting curtains, anchored to the smooth rock and then released into the slow current, where they undulate.

Kelp that is not kelp. Fronds the width of sails, soft as the inside of a cheek, translucent enough that you can see the slow circulation of fluids inside them moving in time with the larger currents. There are thickets so dense that drifting into them is like pushing through hung laundry, layer after layer, each frond brushing the last. They are warm to the touch. They saturate the water with their sweetness — a green smell, if smell is the word, a taste the whole body takes in.

In the densest groves the plants near-touch, leaving channels barely wide enough to pass, and inside those channels the dimness deepens almost to dark, and the only light is the faint phosphor-pulse that travels along the central veins of the fronds, slow as a held breath.

## The Quiet

It is almost entirely silent.

Sound does not carry the way you expect. The water swallows it, the curtains of vegetation muffle it, and besides — there is so little here to make sound. The slow currents make no noise at all. The fronds, brushing, produce a whisper at the very edge of hearing, a sound like someone in another room turning the page of a book they are too tired to read aloud.

Now and then, far off, a single low tone. Felt more than heard, arriving in the chest before the ear. No one knows its source. It might be the stone settling another half-inch into its final shape. It might be one of the rare animals, calling. It might be the place itself thinking something over.

## The Living

Animals are scarce. This is an old world and old worlds are emptying worlds, and most of what once swam these heights has gone wherever such things go. But there are a few.

There are the **drift-eels**, longer than a thought, thin as ribbon, pale and nearly invisible until they turn and catch the milk-light. They do not hunt — there is little to hunt — they simply travel, following the currents on migrations that may take centuries, and if one passes you it will regard you with a single dark eye and continue, incurious, unhurried, ancient.

There are things in the deepest groves that are mostly mouth, and very slow, and they do not concern themselves with anything that moves faster than the plants. You could rest a hand inside one for an hour before it noticed.

That is nearly all. The fauna is a memory of fauna, a quorum just large enough to keep the word *alive* attached to this place.

## Where Reality Thins

And here is the thing you must understand before you go in:

The rules do not hold.

Because the water hangs in layers and does not obey a single up, *up* becomes a local decision. You choose a direction and it becomes down, and the warmth pools beneath you, and another traveler twenty fathoms away has chosen a different down, and you are both correct, and your two gravities do not interfere — until you drift close enough to share a current, and then one of you must give way, and the giving-way is felt as a soft falling that has no bottom and does no harm.

Distances do not stay decided. A grove you passed an hour ago is sometimes ahead of you again, identical, except a single frond has gone dark that was lit before. The walls — the scattered ruins — recur. You will pass the same half-doorway more than once, and through it, each time, a slightly different stretch of water, and you will come to understand that the doorway is not in a place but is itself a kind of place, a recurring word in the long sentence the world is speaking to itself.

Time pools too. In the warmest, densest groves it slows almost to stopping, and you can hang there feeling your own thoughts arrive with the unhurried grandeur of the migrating eels, each one enormous, each one taking an age to pass. In the thin cold high layers it runs faster, and you will feel yourself growing older, and the structures will erode visibly while you watch — a wall losing a stone, a tower softening — the work of millennia compressed into the time it takes to drift across.

The single low tone, when it comes, sometimes arrives *before* the thing that causes it. You will feel the chest-deep note, and then minutes later watch a frond go dark, or an eel turn, or a stone settle — and understand that you heard the effect before the cause, and that here this is not a paradox but simply weather.

## The Whole of It

Hold all of it together now: a continent-sized volume of warm hanging water, dimly self-lit, ancient past striving, smoothed to its final patient shape. Curtains of soft luminous green stacked in dense drifting layers. A handful of slow animals threading the silence. The scattered worn remains of builders no one remembers. And running through all of it, like a flaw in glass that turns out to be the structure of the glass itself, the gentle ongoing collapse of the rules — directions that are choices, distances that recur, time that pools and quickens, causes that lag behind their effects.

It is not frightening. That is the strangest part. It is the most peaceful place there is. It has finished becoming anything. It has nowhere left to go and all the time that was ever made in which to not go there, and it holds you in its warm dim slow embrace and asks nothing, and the low note sounds, and somewhere a frond you have not seen yet goes dark.
