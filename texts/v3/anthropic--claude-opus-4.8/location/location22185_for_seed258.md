There is a valley here, but it was not carved. It was grown, then frozen the instant it finished growing.

Everything is made — that is the first rule, and the hardest to hold in the mind, because nothing here was made by hands or tools. The made-ness is botanical. Every surface you can see was constructed by plants for a purpose, and then the plants stopped, all at once, and have not moved since.

Start with the ground. It is not soil. It is a woven floor, the interlocking of root-systems that grew flat and broad and then lignified into a single continuous plate, pale green-grey, faintly warm underfoot — warm the way a body is warm, not the way stone in sun is warm. Water seeps up between the weave-lines. Not flooding, just present: a high water table held inside the living architecture, so that the floor always glistens and your weight presses a brief film of water out around your foot, which then withdraws. The valley is perhaps two miles across, rising on both sides, and you are high — high enough that the air thins and cools the heat into something pleasant, a wet warmth like the inside of a greenhouse left open at the top.

The walls of the valley are the architecture proper. The plants here did not branch and tangle. They built. Columns — true columns, fluted, rising forty and sixty feet, made of fused vertical stems that grew straight and then thickened into pillars. Between the columns, sheets of grown material span like walls, like the membrane between the bones of a leaf, translucent in the day so the normal-bright light comes through green and even. There are arches. There are things that are very nearly rooms, open-walled, with floors that are slightly cupped and hold an inch of clear water like a basin. No one built these as rooms. The plants simply grew into these forms and stopped.

And the stopping is the strange thing. This place does not change. The water moves, the light moves across the sky, but the structures do not grow, do not decay, do not lean or fall. Erosion has barely begun — the edges of things are raw, unworn, still carrying the sharp ridges and seams of their own making, as though construction ended yesterday. But it did not end yesterday. It ended a very long time ago, and then time stopped applying. The arches will not crumble. The columns will not rot. They have reached their equilibrium and they hold it absolutely. You could lean against one and feel its faint warmth and know that it will be exactly this warm, exactly this shape, after you are gone and after everyone is gone.

It is loud here. This surprises people. A built place, a frozen place — you expect silence. But the structures are hollow, and the water that rises through them and the warm air that moves through the columns make them sound. Each column has a pitch. The wide ones drone, low, felt in the chest. The narrow stems whistle. The membrane-walls flex minutely under the air — the only motion permitted to them — and boom softly, a great slow oceanic sound. Walk through the valley and the chord changes around you as different columns come near and recede. It is not music. It is too constant, too without intention, but it fills the whole bowl of the valley with a standing wall of sound, and there is nowhere to escape it. You feel it in your teeth.

Despite all this construction, the valley is sparse. The columns stand far apart — fifty feet, a hundred feet — with wide reaches of the woven floor between them, open and empty and gleaming. It is not a jungle. It is a cathedral with most of its pillars removed, balance kept between the grown and the empty. You can see a long way across the floor, column to column, the green light lying flat over everything. The emptiness is part of the design, if design is the word. The plants left room. For what, you cannot say.

Living things, animal things, are rare. Once in a long while something moves across the open floor — a low pale creature, hard to classify, that might be an animal or might be a piece of the architecture that has, alone among all of it, decided to walk. It does not hurry. It leaves a trail of pressed-out water that the floor reabsorbs. It does not seem to eat, because there is nothing here to eat; the plants are all substrate, all structure, none of them fruiting or flowering or doing anything so temporary as offering food. The creatures, whatever they are, simply cross the valley and are gone into the membrane-walls at the far side, which part for them and close again without tearing.

There are no tools here. No fire, no metal, no device, nothing that was thought up and then physically made. And yet everything is made. That is the contradiction at the center of the place: a fully constructed world with no maker, no machine, no hand — a world that built itself out of nothing but plant and water and warmth, raised its columns, spanned its arches, tuned its drones, and then held its breath forever.

Stand in the middle of the empty floor. The light is green and steady. The water rises and withdraws around your feet. The columns hold their chord. The far walls breathe their slow boom. And nothing — nothing — will ever change. Not the shape of the arch above you, not the pitch of the column beside you, not the level of the water, not the temperature of the air. You are standing inside a finished thing. The work is done. It was done long before you arrived, and the silence you expected was given over instead to sound, so that the stillness would have a voice and the voice would say, over and over, in a chord with no resolution: this is complete, this is complete, this is complete.
