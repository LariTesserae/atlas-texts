There is no horizon here because there is no light to make one. You learn the place the way the things that live here learn it — by everything except the eyes.

Start with the heat. It arrives first, before you've moved a step: a warmth that comes up from below as much as down from anywhere, the warmth of a thing recently made and still settling into itself. The ground is young. You can feel it. There's none of the worn softness of old land — no rounded stones, no deep loam built up over ten thousand seasons of leaves. Instead the floor is sharp. Ridges of stone, fresh-split, edges that haven't been touched enough to dull. Mud that has not yet decided whether to be rock. In places the ground is still faintly tacky, as though it was poured not long ago and only the skin of it has set.

And over all of that — growth. This is the second thing you understand. The vegetation is not on the land; it *is* the land, or nearly. It has taken the young sharp stone and wrapped it, threaded it, stacked itself in layers so deep that the actual floor is a rumor somewhere below your knees, below your waist, below further than you want to reach. Vines as thick as your arm cross vines as thick as your leg. Broad wet leaves the size of doors lie flat and overlapping like roof tiles, and beneath them another tier, and beneath that another, so that the whole world is roofed and floored and walled in living matter — pockets and chambers and corridors made entirely of plant. You move through it the way you'd move through the inside of a lung.

The water is here but it is *kept*. Not pooled, not flowing in any honest river — the heat and the density don't allow standing water to stay standing. Instead it lives in the leaves. Every broad surface holds its cup, every cupped surface drips into the one below, and so the whole tangled architecture is a slow machine for passing moisture downward, drop by drop, level by level. Condensation beads on the undersides of things and lets go. When it rains — and it rains in bursts, without warning, a sudden roar somewhere up in the unseen canopy — the water doesn't fall to you so much as it is handed down through the layers, arriving by the time it reaches you already warm, already tasting of everything it passed.

Now the sound. I have been saving it because there is no way to be honest about this place without admitting that the sound is the place. It is deafening. Not loud in the way of a single great noise — loud in the way of ten thousand small noises that never stop and never agree. The insects alone would be enough: a sawing, ratcheting, shrilling drone that fills every register at once and seems to come from inside your own skull. Layered over that, things calling — not birds, exactly, though some of it is wing-borne; deep throat-pulses you feel in your chest, high needling cries, clicks that come in volleys, a low collective hum that might be a million wings or might be the ground itself still cooling. And under everything, the wet percussion of the dripping, the constant ticking handoff of water from leaf to leaf, which would be soothing if it were quieter and is instead simply another voice in the wall of voices.

The fauna is overwhelming and I mean that as a precise description, not an exaggeration. There is no volume of this place that is not occupied. Brush a leaf and a dozen things move off it. Step and the ground reorganizes around your foot — things flee, things investigate, things you displaced settle again. They are *layered* the same way the plants are: the deep-burrowers in the warm mud, the crawlers on the trunks, the gliders in the middle tiers, and above them, in the dark canopy you'll never see, the great ones — you know they're great because of the way the whole structure shudders when they move, a slow tide that runs through the vines and tells you something enormous has shifted its weight a hundred feet up. None of them mind you much. You are simply more terrain, another warm surface, a passing event.

The dark is total but the place doesn't behave like a dark place. There's a faint, restless, untrustworthy light that comes from the life itself — a greenish glow on certain fungal shelves, a cold blue along the bodies of things that crawl, brief sparks where something startles and flares. It never adds up to *seeing*. It only marks where things are, like reading a sky of low stars that move. The weirdness lives here, in this glow that breathes — pulses that travel through whole colonies of luminous things in waves, so that you'll watch a far wall of the tangle light up in a slow rolling sweep, brighten, dim, as if the forest were passing a thought from one side of itself to the other.

This is a turbulent place. Nothing holds. A bank of growth that blocked your way collapses under its own wet weight and opens a corridor; an hour later that corridor has closed because something grew across it or something built across it. There is making and unmaking constantly, the young land still convulsing gently, the growth racing to cover every new surface, the animals tearing it back down to eat and nest and pass through. It is an argument with no resolution and no participants who want one. Stability would be death here. The place stays alive by never being finished.

And built things: almost none. But not quite none. Here and there, if you feel along the trunks, you'll find a worked edge — a stone someone chipped, a stick set deliberately, a knot of vine tied by a hand and not by accident. Something here makes tools. Something patient enough to shape, simple enough to leave no walls, smart enough to live inside this churning dark without owning it. You won't meet them. You'll just keep finding the small, deliberate marks — a sharpened point, a notch, a line of placed stones — and understand that you are not the first thing to move through here trying to read the place by everything except the eyes.

You are, by all evidence, simply not very good at it yet.
