# The Slow Canopy

Begin high, because everything here is high — a plateau so old it has forgotten it was ever a mountain. The peaks wore themselves down across ages no creature here would have a word for, and what remains is a broad green back of the world, two-thirds of the way to whatever sky there is, breathing wet air.

There is no ground. I want to be careful about this, because it is the first true thing about the place. If you dig down through the warm and dripping layers you do not find soil, you find older plant. The whole plateau is vegetable all the way through — a substrate of compacted life, fibrous and dark and faintly warm, the way a compost heap is warm, the slow metabolism of an entire continent turning itself over so gradually that a single fold of it might take a century to complete. You walk on the top of a thing that is, in its lowest reaches, the same age as the erosion that flattened the mountains. It is the oldest living tissue, and it is patient past the point of resembling life.

Out of this substrate everything grows, and into it everything returns, and the distinction between the two is unclear.

## The Layers

Look up and the air is full of structure. The canopy is not a roof but a depth — tiers of broad, soft, water-holding leaves stacked perhaps a hundred meters into the dimness, each tier catching what the one above lets fall. Light arrives already old by the time it reaches you, filtered green through layer upon layer, never bright, a perpetual underwater dusk that does not change much between what should be noon and what should be evening. The sun is a rumor up there, a brighter smear behind a thousand translucent membranes.

Because the leaves hold water, the whole structure weeps. Not rain exactly — rain happens above, in the true canopy, where the plateau makes its own weather and clouds form against the leaf-towers. Down here in the layered middle it simply drips, endlessly, from tier to tier, a constant soft percussion of fat droplets falling onto leaf, onto fiber, onto the slow black substrate. The sound is mostly this: water moving downward without hurry. Drip and trickle and the occasional sigh of a heavy frond unloading its accumulated pool all at once with a hush. Beneath the dripping there is a deeper register you feel more than hear — the substrate itself, settling, the long peristaltic murmur of the living floor.

The air is warm and so thick with moisture it is almost a substance. To breathe it is to drink a little. Everything is beaded. Your skin would be wet within a minute and stay wet, and you would stop minding.

## The Strangeness

Here is where it tilts away from any forest you know.

The plants do not keep to their kingdom. The substrate beneath you respires in slow tidal swells — you can watch a hillside of it rise and settle over the course of an hour, as if the plateau were a vast lung breathing once per season. The leaf-towers lean toward warmth rather than light, since light is scarce and even, and so they bend toward the bodies of large animals and hold the lean for days, a forest slowly crowding around the heat of a sleeping thing, and slowly releasing it when the heat moves on.

Some of the growths have given up on photosynthesis entirely, down in the dim, and live by other means — long pale ropes that find the warm-blooded and lie alongside them, not parasitic, not quite, more like the plant and the animal have agreed to be warm together for a while. There are flowers that open only to sound and close in silence, so that a passing call ripples a slow wave of blossoms opening down a corridor of trunks, petals the color of bruised plum, and then a slow wave of closing behind it, like the forest blinking.

And the water remembers shapes. This is the part I trust least and tell you anyway. Where droplets gather and hang on the underside of the broadest leaves, they sometimes do not fall. They hold the form of whatever last passed beneath — a hanging chandelier of water shaped vaguely like a thing that walked through, persisting for hours, then losing its shape and falling all at once. No one made these. They are simply something water does, here, given enough age and enough stillness.

## The Living Things

Fauna is abundant but unhurried, like everything. Nothing here moves fast because nothing needs to — there is food in every direction, the food is patient, and a fast metabolism would be a kind of vulgarity.

There are the climbers, broad-handed and slow, that live their whole lives in a single tier of canopy and never touch the substrate, grazing the soft leaves, moving through the wet green dimness with a deliberation that looks like grief. There are the floor-things, low and wide and warm, that the plants lean toward — herds of them moving across the plateau at a pace of perhaps a kilometer a day, the canopy bending after them and straightening behind them so that they travel inside a slow green wake. There are small bright animals in the middle air, the only quick things, that ride the falling droplets and feed on the sound-flowers, and their movement is the closest the place comes to a flicker.

You will hear them rarely. A long low call from somewhere in the towers, answered minutes later from far off, the sound-flowers opening between the two voices. Then quiet again, and the dripping, and the breathing floor.

## The Marks

Built things are few and faint and very old, in keeping with everything. Here and there in the substrate you find shapes that were once made — a ring of denser fiber, too regular to be accident, grown over now and almost reabsorbed, the kind of thing that might once have been a wall or a pen or a marker, by hands long gone, in a time before the mountains finished wearing down. There are no tools, no devices, nothing that hums or holds a charge. Whatever made these worked in the only material there is — they shaped the living substrate, bent it, encouraged it, and then they stopped, and the substrate kept growing without them, and now their work is just a slightly more deliberate region of an endless deliberate green.

No one tends the marks. No one needs them. They are simply the last sentences of a thought the plateau had a long time ago and has mostly finished thinking.

## The Rule of the Place

If the world here has a law, it is this: *nothing is wasted and nothing is fast.* Death is just the slow descent of a thing through the tiers into the warm dark floor, where it becomes substrate, where it becomes the next tier's leaves, where it rises again as a frond leaning toward something warm. The whole vast plateau is a single organism so old it has stopped being able to die, only to circulate. It has no center and no edge you could reach in a life. Weather is born and dies inside it. Animals are born and die inside it. You could walk for a generation and never leave the green dusk, never hear more than the dripping and the occasional far call, never feel the air go dry.

It is not lonely, exactly. It is too full to be lonely. It is only very, very patient, and it would not notice you, and it would take you in just the same.
