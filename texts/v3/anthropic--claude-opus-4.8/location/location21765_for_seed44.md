# The Glasshouse Ward

There is a district, and you could walk its perimeter in twenty minutes, and it is built entirely of water and leaf and machine, and it is loud.

Start at the edge, where the iron fence has long since given up being a fence. The vegetation came through it decades ago and made it a trellis instead, and now the fence is a green wall, a thicket pressed against bars that you can only find by reaching in and feeling for them — cool metal under a skin of moss, under fern, under the fat low leaves of things that like their feet wet. Everything here likes its feet wet. The ground does not so much hold water as *be* water with a crust of root over it. Step wrong and you sink to the ankle in warm black mud that smells of green decay and something mineral, something that has been breaking down patiently for a very long time and is in no hurry to finish.

This is a town the size of a few city blocks, but you must understand that almost none of it is empty. The plant is the substrate and the architecture both. Where there were once buildings there are now the *shapes* of buildings, kept upright by the construction grown over and into them — masonry digested by ivy until the ivy is the only thing remembering the wall's intention. Doorways still function as doorways because the growth has agreed, for now, to leave them as passages. You move through the ward by moving through these green-throated corridors, and the leaves brush both shoulders, and the canopy meets overhead in most places, and the light that comes down is filtered to the ordinary daylight green of a place that is fully but not darkly grown — bright enough to read by, dim enough that you keep wanting to look up.

And the sound. That is the thing you would not expect from a place so overgrown, so wet, so slow. It is *loud*. Somewhere at the heart of the ward — you cannot quite locate it, the green confuses direction — there are engines running. Pumps, by the rhythm: a deep reciprocating thud, a long suck, a gush. The whole place is plumbed. This was once an industrial quarter, and the industry has not stopped, only changed hands. The pumps lift water from below and run it through pipes that have themselves become trellises, slick conduits crusted with the bright orange of iron-eating bacteria and softened by curtains of liverwort. Steam vents somewhere with a continuous hiss. There is the clatter of some mechanism turning over and over, a flywheel maybe, throwing its sound off the wet walls so it doubles and slaps back. Water drips constantly, everywhere, into standing pools that are flat and dark and oily-bright, and the dripping is its own layer under the machinery — a million tiny percussions that never resolve into rhythm.

The water condenses out of the air itself. It is warm here, properly warm, the temperature of a held breath, and the air is so full of moisture that the cooler iron surfaces stream with it. The pipes weep. The bars weep. Old riveted tanks, half-swallowed in growth, sweat down their flanks into the moss banked at their bases. You are never dry. You stop noticing within a few minutes that you are never dry.

What strikes you, once the senses settle, is how *little moves* that isn't water or machine. This is a fully grown world and an industrial one, but the animal life is almost absent. You might see, over the course of a long visit, a single pale snail tracking up a leaf-blade. A few small flies hung in a shaft of light. Once, perhaps, something that might have been a frog, gone before you confirm it. The ecology here is overwhelmingly plant and microbe — the great slow chemistries of root and rot and the bacterial slicks on every wet surface, doing their work without legs or wings. The loudness is mechanical, not biological. No birdsong. Nothing calls. The pumps thud and the steam hisses and the leaves drip into a silence of creatures.

It is an old balance. Nothing here is raw or new. The erosion has been at work long enough that every hard edge has been rounded, every channel cut where water wanted it cut, every collapse settled into a shape that holds. The buildings fell as far as they were going to fall a generation ago and then the green caught them and held them at that angle, and there they remain, leaning, furred, permanent. The pools have found their levels. The mud is as deep as it is going to get. You sense that this place reached its present form slowly and will leave it slowly — that if you returned in a year it would look identical, and in ten years nearly so, the change happening at the pace of a beam rotting one more season toward the soil, of a root thickening another finger's width around the pipe it has chosen to embrace.

And yet the machines run. That is the strangeness of the place, though it is not strange in any uncanny way — it is mundane, it is just *how this district is*. Someone built the pumps and someone, or something, keeps them running, though you never see a hand at it. The water is lifted and the water falls and the warmth holds and the plants drink and the bacteria glaze the iron orange, and overhead the canopy keeps the light to its even green, and the whole small dense ward sits there pumping and weeping and growing, a single organism of metal and chlorophyll the size of a few streets, breathing wet warm air in and out, very loud and very still, for as long as anyone can remember and likely longer.

You leave by the same green-throated corridor. The pump-thud follows you to the fence and a little past it, then loses itself in ordinary distance. Your clothes stay damp for hours.
