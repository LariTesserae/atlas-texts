# The Boiling Plateau

There is no water here, and there never was, and yet everything moves as though remembering it.

The land is a high tableland of fired clay and salt-glass, lifted some two kilometers above a horizon you cannot find — because there is no lower country to fall toward. The plateau simply ends in haze, all around, as if the world were a single raised platform and the rest had been deferred. Underfoot, the ground is the color of old terracotta gone almost white at the cracks. It is hot enough that the air above it stands in visible columns, slow and braided, rising without ever quite leaving.

Nothing grows. Not because life failed here, but because life took a different route and never bothered with the green branch of the family.

## The mechanism in the rock

What you take, at first, for geology is industry. The plateau is veined with engines.

They are not built. No one built them. They are the way the stone organizes itself at this temperature — long mineral assemblies of crank and flange and slow piston, grown the way crystals grow, lattice by patient lattice, into machines that turn. Pistons the length of a body rise and fall once an hour. Flywheels of banded agate, wider than a house, complete a single rotation across the better part of a day. They are doing work. You can feel that they are doing work. You cannot find what the work is for.

This is the source of the sound. The plateau is *loud* — a vast, low, mechanical breathing, the groan of stone bearing against stone, the periodic concussive sigh of some pressure releasing through a vent and the long inhale as it builds again. It never stops. It has the unhurried confidence of a process that has run for an age and intends to run for several more. The noise is so constant that within an hour you stop hearing it as sound and begin feeling it in the bones of the jaw, the sternum, the wrists.

## The fauna

And here is where the world stops being merely strange and becomes unwell.

The plateau is *crowded* with animals, and there is almost nothing here for an animal to be. They pour across the empty ground in numbers that the empty ground cannot explain — there is no water, no plant, no prey-and-predator chain you could draw — and yet they come, in their thousands, overwhelming, a fauna without an ecology.

They are made, partly, of the machine-stone and partly of something hotter and less settled. Things like beetles with gears for thoraxes, clicking across the clay in migrations that go nowhere and arrive nowhere. Things like long pale herd-animals, more numerous than the herds of any savanna, grazing on heat itself — lowering their heads to the radiant ground and drawing warmth up through mouthparts of fused glass. Flocks overhead that are not quite birds, that flicker because they are partly *probability*, present at the edge of vision and absent when looked at directly, so that the sky is loud with wingbeats whose owners you cannot quite catch existing.

They breed, somehow, out of the abundance of heat, the way condensation would breed in a wetter world — except here the surplus is thermal, and so the surplus becomes *creatures*, spontaneous and uncountable, surging up out of the warm places and dying back into them.

## Where the world thins

This is the part that is hard to hold steady in the mind.

The reality of the plateau is not reliable. It runs at high heat and high strangeness and at those settings the rules go soft.

Cause and effect have come slightly unstuck. A flywheel turns *because* it will release pressure later — the work flows backward as readily as forward. The migrating gear-beetles arrive at a place and only afterward decide to have departed from somewhere. You will see a herd-animal fall and crumble to warm dust, and then, hours on, see it lower its head to graze, fully whole, the dust having been a thing that happens *near* it rather than *to* it.

Distance, too, is negotiable. The horizon-haze is not far away; it is exactly as far away as your attention, and it retreats when you walk and presses close when you forget it. The plateau is therefore both regional and infinite — a valley-sized place that contains more interior than its edges should permit. You can walk a straight line for a day and arrive where you began, not because the world is round but because *straight* has stopped meaning what it meant.

And the heat hallucinates. At this temperature matter has opinions. The salt-glass dreams aloud; you will pass a sheet of it and hear, faintly, the memory of an ocean it never held, the slosh and break of a tide that this world skipped entirely. The non-existent water haunts the place precisely because it is so absent — a phantom limb of a planet, an ache where a sea should be.

## The pace of it

For all its loudness and crowding, nothing here is in a hurry. This is its mercy.

The engines turn at the speed of geology. The migrations cross the plateau over generations. The reality-thinning is not a violent flicker but a slow seep, a thing you notice only by staying — like watching a shadow move, you cannot catch the moment of change, only the fact that change has occurred. The whole place is in balance, weathered into its own contradictions, long settled into being impossible. It has been this hot, this strange, this full and this empty, for longer than measurement.

You could stand here a long time. The heat would take you eventually — there is no water, remember — but slowly, patiently, the way everything happens here. And as you went, you might find yourself uncertain whether you were a visitor dying on the plateau, or simply one more creature the surplus heat had dreamed up for an afternoon, grazing on warmth with the rest of the impossible herd, about to crumble and be grazing again.

The flywheels turn. The vents breathe out. The animals pour across the dry red ground toward nothing, and have always been arriving, and will not stop.
