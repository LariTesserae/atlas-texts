# The Cistern Continent

Begin underwater, because everything here does.

This is not an ocean. The water has edges — not a shoreline, but walls. You understand this within the first minute of looking: the blue stretches in every direction beyond seeing, but it is *contained*. Somewhere far overhead the light comes down clean and ordinary, the daylight you'd know from any cool morning, slanting through a great mass of water that has been poured into a thing built to hold it.

The thing built to hold it is the floor.

It is concrete. Not natural rock — concrete, poured and set, in panels the size of provinces. You can see the seams. They run dead straight to the horizon and past it, expansion joints filled with dark sealant, drainage channels cut in geometric grids, the whole bottom of the world a single engineered slab. In places the concrete is still pale and unweathered, the formwork lines from its casting still legible — board-marks, the grain of timber pressed into stone, fresh as if the molds came off last season. This place is young. Whatever made it made it recently, and at a scale that has no business existing.

How recently, and how big: the seams have only just begun to silt. The corners are still sharp. Calcium has barely started its slow white bloom along the joints. A continent's worth of floor, and it reads as *new construction*, the way a freshly drained reservoir reads — clean, raw, waiting.

## The Vastness and the Emptiness

Here is the strange part, and it is not strange in the way of dreams — it is strange in the way of true sizes.

The cistern is enormous enough to have weather. Currents the width of rivers move through it, driven by temperature differences across distances too great for the water to stay still. Cool water — everything here is cool, the steady chill of deep cellars, of stone that never sees the sun directly — sinks and slides along the floor in slow rivers within the larger sea. There are fronts where two currents meet and the water shimmers with the boundary, a visible seam in the liquid to match the seams in the floor below.

And yet it is *empty*. This is the contradiction you have to hold: vast and nearly vacant. You can swim — or drift, or be carried — for what feels like a day's travel and pass nothing. No structure. No fish. No plant. Just the lit blue and the concrete plain receding. The emptiness is not menacing. It is simply the dominant fact, the way silence is the dominant fact in an unfilled hall. There is so much room and so little in it that the few things present become enormous in the attention, the way a single cough fills an empty cathedral.

## What Grows

Vegetation is rare and clings to structure. On the seams — only on the seams, where the sealant gives purchase and the joint traps sediment — there are growths. Long pale weeds, kelp-like but the color of weak tea, anchored in the drainage grooves and streaming in the current's direction like banners. They mark the geometry. From above you could read the whole continental grid by its weeds, the architecture diagrammed in living lines.

Between the seams the concrete is bare. Now and then a single growth has found a crack — a hairline fracture in a young panel — and a frond stands alone there, miles from any other, leaning in the flow. These solitary plants are landmarks. You'd give them names if you lived here.

## The Engines

This is built, and not abandoned. It is *run*.

At intervals — vast intervals, a horizon or two apart — rise the works. Intake towers. Pump-houses. Sluice gates the height of office blocks set into longer walls that vanish into the murk. They are made of the same concrete as the floor, but armored over with iron: riveted plate, gone the green-brown of submerged steel, banded with conduit. They hum. They more than hum — this is a loud world, and the sound is its second great fact after the emptiness.

The pumps move the sea. You feel them before you reach them, a pressure-throb in the chest, a low industrial pulse that travels through water far better than through air. Close to, the noise becomes a grinding roar of machinery built to shift water by the cubic mile — the deep churn of impellers, the clang and shudder of gates working, the bass complaint of structures under load. Bubbles boil up from the intakes in silver columns and climb toward the distant light. Outfalls vent in great soft explosions of current you can feel as a shove from a hundred meters off.

It is electrified, somehow — there are cables strung in catenary arcs between the towers, and lamps burn on the works, redundant against the daylight, marking the structures in the murk like the lights of refineries seen across a night plain. The technology is heavy and frank. Engines and pipes and valves. Nothing miraculous. Just industry, scaled past comprehension, keeping the great cool water moving in its bowl.

Nothing here is weird. That's worth saying plainly. Everything obeys the rules you'd expect — water is heavy, sound carries, iron rusts, machines wear. The only impossible thing is the *size*, and size is not magic, only excess.

## The Living

And there is life — abundant, where it occurs, which makes the emptiness around it sharper.

The fauna gathers at the works, because the works are where the food is. The pump intakes draw a constant rain of small drift, and where there is drift there are feeders. Around every engine-tower hangs a teeming cloud: schools of pale fish wheeling in the pulse of the machinery, packed dense as smoke, thousands of bodies turning at once where for a hundred miles around there were none. Larger things hunt them — sleek dark predators built like seals or like nothing you know, moving through the schools in slashing runs, hanging in the warm wake of the outfalls.

There are colonies on the iron. Things that filter, things that cling, banks of soft bodies cementing themselves to the warm pipes where the current is rich. The works have grown reefs of their own life, fouling the machinery the way mussels foul a hull. Crews would scrape them, if there are crews. The structures suggest crews — there are ladders, hatches, walkways gone to rust — but you never see one. The engines run themselves, or are run from somewhere out of sight.

So the ecology is concentrated into islands of abundance in a sea of nothing. Each tower is an oasis, loud and crowded and alive, and the spaces between them are the great quiet blue, almost void, where you might drift for hours with the schools far behind and the next light not yet risen over the curve of the floor, alone in a cool lit infinity with concrete below and water above and the distant throb of pumps you cannot see but can always, faintly, feel — the heartbeat of the place that holds the sea in its hands and will not let it rest.
