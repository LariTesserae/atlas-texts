# The Drowned Lattice

Begin underwater. Stay underwater — there is no surfacing here, no membrane of air to break toward. The world is liquid all the way up and all the way down, and the cold of it presses evenly on everything, a temperature just above the point where water would rather be ice. Cold enough that movement slows. Cold enough that thought, if there were any here, would come patiently.

Now: it is all made.

That is the strange thing your hands learn first, before your eyes adjust to the near-total dark. There is no seabed of silt, no mother-rock beneath. Everywhere you reach, you touch worked surface — fitted stone, hewn beam, the squared edge of something a hand shaped long ago. The floor is a floor. The walls are walls. You are not in an ocean that happens to cover a city. You are inside a construction that goes down past any bottom and out past any horizon, and the water is simply the medium that fills it, the way air fills a house.

## The Builders' Lattice

It was assembled by hand. You can tell by the tooling — every block bears the soft scallop of an adze, the bite-marks of chisels, the slightly-off geometry of work done by eye rather than by machine. No two joints are identical. Whoever made this had only stone tools and rope and patience, and they had an unaccountable amount of time, because the structure is vast — vast enough to hold its own slow currents, its own internal weather of cold downwelling and colder rising. You could travel for what would be days, if days meant anything down here, and pass through hall after hall, colonnade after colonnade, never finding a wall that was not placed by intention.

And it is *old*. Past old. The edges that the chisels left have been rounded by the water's endless gentle work until the construction has half-returned toward the smoothness of natural things. Erosion has done to this lattice what it does to mountains — worn it toward its final, settled shape, the shape a made thing takes when it has been a made thing for so long that the difference between artifact and geology has begun to dissolve. The stairways have become ramps. The carved faces on the pillars have become suggestions of faces, then suggestions of weather. The whole place has aged into something nearly flat in its spirit, low and broad and finished, no longer rising toward anything.

## What Grows

It is not empty. Cold and dark and made, but lush — improbably, densely lush.

In the absence of animals, the plants have inherited everything. They do not need light here; there is almost none — only the faint blue-grey ghost of illumination that seeps from no clear source, the color of the inside of a closed eye. These are not sun-plants. They are filter-feeders and chemical-drinkers, growing in layered tiers across every worked surface: pale kelps the color of bone, soft mosses in matted carpets, branching weeds that fan out from the joints of the masonry like slow green fire. Fronds hang from every lintel. Threads of pale vine bridge the gaps between columns, swaying in the patient currents.

They grow in saturation — many living things per handspan, near-touching, overlapping, one species rooted in the body of another. The density is the density of a flooded greenhouse left for ten thousand years. And yet it moves so slowly that you would have to wait a long time to see any change at all. A frond bends. An hour later it has bent a little further. The whole forest breathes on a cycle so long that a human watcher would call it stillness.

## Sound, or Nearly None

Quiet. Water-quiet, which is its own kind of quiet — not silence but the muffling of all sharp edges. Your own pulse becomes loud. Sometimes a deep structural groan travels through the stone from somewhere immense and far, a single low note that takes a long time to arrive and a long time to fade, the sound of the lattice settling another hair's breadth into its final form. Sometimes a frond brushes another. That is most of it. There is nothing here that hunts or calls or flees. The plants make no sound, or none a person could hear.

## The Rules of This Place

Light does not travel far, so the world is only ever as large as your reach plus a few arm-lengths of dim blue. The vastness is real but unseeable — known only by how long you can travel without ending. You learn the place by hand, by the feel of tooled stone and soft weed, the way a blind person learns a house they have lived in forever.

Nothing decays quickly. Nothing grows quickly. Nothing is dangerous quickly. The whole world runs on the patience of cold water and old stone, and its only rule is the rule of slow things: that everything here is being worn, gently and forever, toward the same smooth ending — the moment, still unthinkably far off, when the lattice and the forest and the cold will have become one indistinguishable settled thing, and there will be nothing left to tell the made from the grown.

Until then it holds: a hand-built cathedral with no roof and no floor and no light, drowned and green and quiet, going on past any edge a swimmer could find.
