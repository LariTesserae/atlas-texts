There is a room the size of a thing that has not decided whether it is a room. Call it a cell, a pocket, a held breath in the rock. You enter it without remembering an entrance, and once inside you understand that the world has elected to be very small here, and very intense about it.

The floor is new stone. I mean new — basalt that cooled within memory, its surface still sharp at the edges where it pulled apart, the cracks not yet softened by any patient hand. You could cut yourself on the geometry of this place. Nothing has had time to round itself off. The fissures run in clean dark lines, and from every one of them, water comes.

Not a flood. The water here is not theatrical. It is constant. It weeps from the seams in the rock, beads on the ceiling that hangs low overhead, gathers and falls in slow fat drops that strike the floor and are gone into the next crack before they can pool. The whole chamber sweats. The air is thick enough to drink, hot and close, a steam that does not rise to escape because there is nowhere to escape to — it just hangs, recirculating, condensing on the cool of your skin and the warm of the stone alike. You are wet within a minute. You will stay wet.

The heat is the kind that has weight. It comes up through the soles of the feet from somewhere below the new floor, where the rock is presumably still arguing with itself about being solid. Where the water meets that under-heat, it gives off a faint sulfur sigh, and small ferns — the only living green in the whole pocket — have taken the gamble of growing in the seams. Sparse. A clump here, a single frond there, pale and overlarge, trembling in the moving air. They are spaced like punctuation in a sentence mostly made of silence. Between them: bare wet stone, and a great deal of nothing, and the dark.

There are no animals. Not a beetle, not a fly drawn to the warmth, not the small skittering things you would expect in a warm wet place. Whatever this is, it is too young or too strange for them. The ferns hold the entire census of the living, and they don't move except when the air pushes them.

But it is loud.

This is the thing that doesn't fit, the thing that makes the chamber feel haunted by an industry it cannot quite contain. Somewhere — behind the wall, beneath the floor, embedded in the very rock that makes this room — there is a machine, and it is enormous, and it is running. You cannot see it. You will never see it. But you feel it in your sternum: a low, grinding, rhythmic labor, the sound of pistons the size of buildings, of belts and gears turning under a strain that never lets up. A boiler-throb. A factory's heartbeat conducted through stone, magnified by the close wet walls until it fills the pocket completely and there is no room left for your own thoughts. It rings off the basalt. It makes the falling drops shiver before they fall.

And here is the strangeness of it, the thing that takes a while to land: the machine is *slow*. The sound is huge but the rhythm is geological — a great cycle that takes long minutes to complete, each stroke arriving like a tide. So you sit in the loud, hot, weeping dark and you wait for the next beat, and it comes, patient and titanic, and the ferns nod, and the water falls, and you realize that whatever is being manufactured here is being manufactured on a timescale that has nothing to do with you. Something is being made. The heat is its furnace. The water is its coolant or its byproduct. The new rock is, perhaps, its product — the floor you stand on freshly extruded, the whole chamber an organ in some patient subterranean works that builds the ground itself, one slow stroke at a time.

Built things confirm this only partly. There are marks. On one wall, a row of iron staples driven into the stone, rusting in the wet, holding nothing — and a fragment of pipe, jointed, capped, going nowhere visible, warm to the touch. A single gauge, its glass fogged from inside, its needle resting somewhere in the lower register, twitching once with each beat of the unseen engine. Scattered evidence that someone, or something, tends this. Not crowding it. Just the occasional artifact, left where the rock could hold it, half-swallowed already by the seam-water and the rust.

The light is ordinary, which is its own quiet shock. Not the red glow of furnace-country, not the dark of a true cave — just a plain, even, daylight-colored light with no obvious source, enough to read the room by, falling on the wet stone and giving the steam a soft glow where it hangs. As if a normal afternoon were sealed inside the rock with all this machinery and heat.

You could stay a long time. Things change here, but slowly — the gauge climbs a hair over an hour, a new bead of water finds a new path down a new crack, a fern unfurls so gradually you'd have to leave and come back to know it had. The pocket keeps its own long patience. The engine beats. The walls weep. The heat presses on, gentle and total.

And nothing tells you what is being made.
