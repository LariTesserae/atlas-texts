# The Sump Works

You come upon it low, because there is no other way to come upon it. The land does not rise. It only deepens, fold after fold, until you are walking in a basin you did not notice yourself entering — and by then the air has already changed. It has weight here. It condenses on the back of your hand as you walk, beads on your eyelashes, collects in the hollow of your collarbone. The whole place breathes water out of itself constantly, and the water never quite falls and never quite lifts. It hangs.

This is a small place. You could cross it in an afternoon if the ground let you, which it does not. Call it a quarter-mile across at most — a bowl in the world, bounded on every side by black shale walls that weep. Not metaphorically. The walls run with thin films of water, sheet after sheet, slow as syrup over the dark rock, so that the whole rim of the basin glistens like something just born or something just skinned. Where the films pool at the base they go still and oily and reflect the dim sky in long smears.

There are almost no plants. I want to be clear about this, because the place is so dense with *life* that you will reach for the wrong word. What grows here is not green. It is fungal, or near-fungal — pale tiered shelves climbing the wet shale, the color of old wax and bruised pearl, layered so thickly that the rock beneath disappears under brackets and frills and soft hanging tongues. They overlap, near-touching, every available surface taken. In the lowest, wettest crevices something simpler still — mats of something that might be slime, might be colony, might be a single organism the size of a room, breathing slow gas-bubbles that rise and break the surface of the standing water with a sound like a patient man clearing his throat.

The temperature sits just below comfortable. Cool enough that the standing water never steams, cool enough that the fog stays heavy and low and does not burn off. You can see your breath if you look for it, though the place's own exhalations make it hard to tell which fog is yours.

## What was built here

Someone worked this basin. Long ago by the look of it — long enough that the work has settled into the landscape the way an old scar settles into skin, no longer a wound, just a feature.

Scattered through the bowl stand the bones of an industry. Pumping engines, mostly — cast-iron beam pumps the height of a house, set on stone footings sunk into the wet ground, gone the deep brown-black of iron that has been wet for a hundred years and oxidized into a kind of stability. They were built to lift water. That much is obvious from the geometry: the great rocking beams, the cylinders, the channels cut to carry the lifted water somewhere up and out of the basin. Someone, once, was trying to drain this place. Trying to keep it from becoming exactly what it has become.

They failed, or they finished, or they left. Hard to say which. Because the strange thing — and this is where the place stops being a ruin and becomes something else —

**some of the engines are still running.**

Not all. Most stand frozen, beams locked at half-stroke, fungal shelves growing along the iron the way they grow along the shale. But here and there, deep in the fog, you hear one working. A slow groan of metal. A long suck and a longer release. The patient *chunk... chunk...* of a beam rising and falling, lifting water nobody tends, on no schedule, drawing from a source no one fed. The sound carries strangely in the wet air — it seems close, then far, then close again, as if the engine were moving, which it is not.

There is no one here. No furnace tended, no coal stacked, no operator. And yet the working engines are *warm*. You can feel it if you come close — a band of warmth around the cylinder, a faint dryness in the air immediately beside it, where the fog thins and the fungal growth pulls back into a clean ring of bare black iron. Something feeds them. The place feeds them. The water it lifts pours up the channels and over the rim and is gone, and the basin refills from below as fast as the engines empty it, and this has been going on, by the patient evidence of the rust and the wax-pale brackets, for longer than the machines should have been able to last.

## What lives here

The fauna found the warmth first.

Around the working engines, in the dry rings, things gather. Pale soft-bodied creatures, eyeless or nearly so, that look assembled from the same material as the fungus — translucent, slow, drinking the warmth through their skins. They cluster on the cylinders in numbers, layered, near-touching, like the fungal shelves themselves, and when you approach they peel off and pour back into the wet dark without hurry. There are many of them. The place is *abundant* with them, and with the things that eat them: longer, faster shapes that move through the standing water with a sound like a sheet being drawn off a bed, that hunt by the pulse of warmth and the soft give of soft flesh.

And there are flyers, of a sort — things in the fog that you mostly do not see, only hear, a dry papery beating somewhere above, circling the warm engines like the warmth were a lamp and they the moths of it. Now and then one drops through the fog to skim the water's skin and rises again with something pale and wriggling, and the bubbling mats go quiet for a while afterward, as if something in the colony had felt the loss.

## The rules of the place

Three things, slowly learned, govern this basin:

First, the water rises as fast as it is taken, and so the level never changes, and so the engines never finish, and so they never stop. The drainage is eternal because it is futile. This is the heart of the place. It is a machine built to defeat a flood, perfectly preserved in the exact moment of not defeating it, kept running by the thing it cannot drain.

Second, warmth is the only currency. There is light, but it is thin — a dim grey ceiling of fog that brightens and dulls with a day you cannot see the source of. Nothing lives by that light. Everything lives by the engines' warmth, and so the whole ecology of the basin is arranged in rings, concentric and overlapping, around the dozen or so machines that still breathe. The dead engines are dead zones, cold iron under wax, avoided.

Third — and you will only understand this if you stay until the light dulls toward whatever passes for evening — the engines are not all running on the same rhythm, and yet over a long enough listening, their strokes *drift toward each other.* The far one slows; the near one quickens; and for a few minutes the whole basin pulses as one slow heart, *chunk... chunk... chunk...*, water lifted and lost across the entire bowl in unison, the fog shivering with it, the soft pale creatures going still on the warm iron, the hunters holding in the black water. Then the synchrony breaks, the engines scatter back into their separate times, and the place returns to its layered, patient, weeping work.

No one built this rhythm. No one is here to hear it. It simply happens, the way water finds its level, because the place has had so very long to settle into being exactly itself.

You should go before the next synchrony. People who stay for the second one tend to stay for the third.
