# The Cistern Warren

You come down into it by a stair that someone cut a very long time ago — the treads worn into shallow basins, each one holding a thumb's depth of water that you step around out of an instinct you can't name. The air thickens as you descend. Warm. Not the dry warmth of a sunned stone but the close, breathing warmth of a kept place, a warmth that has hands.

Then the stair opens out and you are inside.

## What it is

It is built — all of it, every surface, no exception. There is no rock here that someone did not lay, no floor that did not begin as a decision. But it has been built for so long, and re-built, and patched, and let go, and patched again, that it has crossed back over into something like nature. The masonry has the authority of a cliff. Brick the color of old blood, set in courses that drift and sag where the ground beneath has shifted across centuries, so that the walls breathe outward in slow bellies and the arches above you have settled into curves no mason intended but that hold, somehow, still hold, in a balance argued out over generations between weight and want.

This is the *erosion* of the place — not the wearing-away of stone by river, but the wearing-in of a made thing until it sits in its own true shape. Mortar has dissolved and re-set as mineral crust. Salts have bloomed white across the lower walls in deltas and feathers. The corners are gone, rounded off by ten thousand shoulders and ten thousand drips. Everything here was sharp once and is now soft, and the softness is what makes it permanent.

## The scale of it

It is not vast. You could cross the main chamber in forty paces if it were empty, but it is never empty. Think of it as the size of a flooded chapel, a sunken hamlet's worth of rooms folded into each other — a few large chambers and a dense honeycomb of smaller cells, alcoves, niches, sumps, channels, and crawl-throughs packed around them. The boundaries are real and close. You can feel the edges of the world here, the dead-end walls, the places where the warren simply stops. It is a contained thing, and the containment is part of its character: a place that knows exactly how big it is and has filled every cubic foot of itself.

## Density — the saturation

This is what hits you hardest, after the warmth. *Everything is occupied.* The volume is layered, packed, near-touching, floor to vault. Tiers of brick shelving line every wall, and on the shelves: clay vessels, thousands of them, fat-bellied and narrow-necked, glazed in browns and greens, stacked three deep and nested where they can be. Hung from the ceilings on cords of twisted fiber: bundles, baskets, dried things, ropes of something, woven mats rolled tight. Ledges jut at every height. Ladders — wooden, smooth-rubbed, lashed not nailed — connect the levels in a tangle, leaning, crossing. Pipes of fired clay run everywhere, slung along the walls, dripping at their joints, carrying water from somewhere to somewhere. There is no clean line of sight longer than a few paces. The eye keeps catching on the next thing and the next.

## The water

It is damp, not drowned. Water is the warren's pulse but it comes in pockets and intervals. From the surface, far above, rain finds its way down through fissures and runnels and the old clay pipes, arriving in fits — a sudden cold patter from a particular crack that runs for a while and then stops; a steady weep from a green-furred wall that never stops; condensation that beads on the cool vessels and runs and gathers and drips, drips, drips, so the whole place ticks with falling drops in a hundred different pitches. There are shallow channels cut into the floors to lead it, gutters worn glass-smooth, and a few deep sumps — black mirrors — where it pools and is dipped out by hand. Most of the warren is merely moist. The stone sweats. Your skin stays slightly wet the whole time you are down here, and the warmth keeps it from ever being cold, so the dampness is almost comfortable, almost amniotic.

## What grows

Sparse, but present, clinging to the wet seams. Pale mosses, mineral-fed, the green of something kept from the sun — not lush, just enough, in cushions where the weeping walls feed them. Threadlike roots have come down from above through the cracks, white and blind, dangling, having traveled a long way to find this moisture. In the wettest sumps, soft growths float — not quite plant, possibly cultivated, possibly just arrived. Fungus runs along the timber of the ladders in shelves and bracket-forms, and in one chamber someone has clearly been growing it on purpose, on racks of damp packed substrate, pale caps in ordered rows. The vegetation is sparse because there is little light and little soil — but where it can hold, it holds tenaciously, and it perfumes the warm air with the smell of growing dark.

## The fauna — and the sound

This is the loud part. This is why you can barely hear yourself think.

The warren is *full of animals,* and they are doing all of their living right next to you. Birds — small, dark, swift — nest in the high niches by the hundreds, and they come and go through the surface fissures in flickering streams, and they *scream,* a chittering wheeling racket that echoes off the curved brick and never settles. Things move in the pipes. Things scurry on the shelves and knock the vessels so they ring — a constant arrhythmic clay-bell music underneath everything. Bats, surely, in the deeper cells, the dry pockets — you hear the leather-flutter and the thin high cries at the edge of sense. There are creatures in the sumps that surface and slip and plop. Insects in clouds where the moss is, a low electric drone. And all of it ricochets, doubles, layers, because the place is hard and curved and closed: every sound is many sounds, the original plus its ghosts. The dripping water is the metronome; the animals are the orchestra; the brick is the instrument that makes it all unbearably present.

It is loud the way a forest at dawn is loud, the way a market is loud — not noise but *life, too much of it, all at once.*

## The making, the hands

Whoever lives here, or lived here, works by hand and simple craft. There is no metal to speak of, or only the simplest — a copper hook gone green, a bronze blade worn to a sliver. Everything is fired clay, twisted fiber, lashed wood, cut stone, woven mat, packed earth. The vessels were thrown on a wheel turned by foot. The pipes were coiled and fired. The ladders were shaped with stone tools and bone awls and patient time. You can read the hands in everything — the fingerprints set into the clay, the wear-patterns of grip on every rung, the places where a repair was made by someone clever with little. This is a place of long, manual, intimate work. It was made slowly, by people who touched every surface of it, and it shows.

## The light

Normal — which is the strangest thing of all, this far below. The light comes down the fissures in shafts and spreads, caught and bounced by the wet brick and the pale salt-bloom and the glazed vessels until the whole warren glows a low warm amber, enough to see by clearly, enough to read the courses of brick and the faces of the birds. In the deep cells it darkens to true black, and there someone keeps small lamps — clay dishes of burning oil that add their soft flicker and their thread of smoke to the warm wet air. The light moves, because the shafts shift as the sun moves far above, so the warren's brightness wanders across its surfaces through the long day like a slow tide.

## The one unusual thing

Here is what is slightly wrong, slightly more than ordinary:

The vessels remember sound. The big fat clay jars, the thousands of them on the shelves — when the place goes briefly quiet, as it does in rare lulls, you can hear them giving back the noise of an hour ago, faint, the bird-scream and the drip-music played back low through their narrow necks, delayed. No one made them to do this. They simply have, over the centuries — soaked in so much sound that the warm wet clay holds it the way it holds the damp, and lets it seep slowly out again. You cannot quite be sure whether the racket you are hearing is happening now or is the warren breathing back its own past. In a place this loud, you would never notice, except in those rare hushes, when the warren remembers aloud what it was a moment ago, and you stand in the amber wet warmth among the dripping and the dangling roots and feel, briefly, that you are inside something that is alive and keeps its own quiet diary in clay.

## The rule of the world

The rule is simple: *nothing here is finished, and nothing here is empty.* Every drop is going somewhere. Every shelf is full. Every wall is settling toward its truer shape. The animals fill the air and the people fill the walls and the water fills the channels and the past fills the vessels. It is a place at capacity, in balance, very old, still working — a made thing that has lived long enough to become a habitat, warm and wet and crowded and loud, going about its endless slow business in the dark beneath whatever surface world forgot it was ever down here at all.
