There is no light here, and yet the place is not invisible. You learn this slowly, standing at its edge, waiting for your eyes to adjust to a darkness that never thins. Sight is simply not the sense this country answers to. You come to know it the way you know a room by the warmth of its walls, by the pressure of bodies moving past you, by the way the air goes thick before it touches your skin.

Begin with the ground, because the ground is wet. Everywhere, wet. Not flooded — there is no sheet of standing water to wade, no marsh to cross — but the land sweats. Press a palm to the rock and it weeps back into your hand. The stone is young here, freshly made, its edges still sharp, fractured into clean planes that haven't yet been rounded by the long patience of rain. You can feel where it cracked. The breaks are recent. The whole basin has the texture of something that finished forming an hour ago and is still cooling, still beading moisture out of its pores, exhaling a warm steam that hangs at knee-height and never quite rises and never quite settles.

The land is low and broad. It opens. Walk in any direction and the floor of it keeps going, gently, a valley wide enough to hold a town, dished and shallow, ringed somewhere far off by ridges you sense more than meet. The scale of it is the first thing that frightens you — not because it is vast, but because it is *open*, and you cannot see, and so the openness is a thing you fall into rather than look across. Your footsteps tell you. The echo comes back slow and undecided. There is room out there. A great deal of room, sparsely furnished.

Vegetation grows, but thinly, and it grows wrong. Reeds, or things shaped like reeds, push up out of the weeping rock in scattered stands — you find them by touch, cool and rubbery, segmented, faintly warm at the joints as though there were blood in them. Between the stands, long stretches of bare stone. The plants do not crowd; they keep their distances like people who have agreed not to speak. When you brush one it bends and stays bent, taking a long, long time to right itself, as if the act of returning to vertical is a decision it has to think through over the course of a night.

Now the difficulty. The land is empty of plants but it is *crowded*. This is the thing your body keeps insisting on before your mind will hold it. The air is dense with presence. Things are near you, layered, stacked, near-touching, breathing — a whole population packed into a volume that your other senses report as nearly bare. The fauna is abundant and the fauna is everywhere and the fauna is, for the most part, silent. You feel them the way you feel a held breath in a dark house: the warmth of a flank passing inches from your arm, the displacement of air as something large and slow turns over, the dry click of a limb set down with care. They move at the speed of the reeds. Everything here is in no hurry. The animals graze on the steam, perhaps. Or on each other, over the span of years. They pass you and keep passing, an endless slow herd that does not stampede because nothing here is ever startled, because there is nothing to see and so nothing to flee from, only the warm crowded dark and the patience of it.

And then the second difficulty, the one that doesn't resolve: the place will not stay one place. This is where it breaks. You set your hand on a sharp wet edge of stone and when you take your hand away and set it down again the edge is somewhere it was not, or is two edges, or is the same edge but you are standing now on the far side of it without having moved. Distances do not commit. The slow herd that passed you on the left passes you again on the right without turning. You count your own footsteps and the count comes out wrong, not because you miscounted but because the steps did not all happen in the order you took them. The young rock is so freshly made that the rules of it haven't set. Cause has not yet fully separated from effect. The world is wet in more than water — wet in its logic, things bleeding into adjacent things, the membrane between *here* and *also-here* still permeable, still weeping like the stone.

Listen, though. For all of this, it is quiet. Mostly quiet. The breakdown does not roar. The crowd does not clamor. There is the soft, near-constant drip of stone giving up its water, a sound so even it becomes silence. There is the long sigh of the steam-herd breathing. And there is, underneath everything, far off and low, a sound that does not belong — a mechanical sound. An engine. Engines. The deep periodic thud of something industrial turning over somewhere out in the open dark, a rhythm too regular to be alive, the breath of pistons and the grind of scaled production. You never find it. You walk toward it and the wet logic of the place folds the distance and you arrive nowhere, or arrive back at your own footprints filling slowly with water. But the engines keep working. Something out there is *making*, at factory scale, in total darkness, tirelessly, to no visible end.

You come across its leavings. Built things, scattered, partial. The corner of a wall, freestanding, its mortar still soft. A length of pipe half-grown into the rock as though the rock had risen around it. A single iron stanchor bolted to nothing, weeping rust into the weeping stone, warm to the touch. These are not ruins exactly — ruins are old, and nothing here is old. These are *interruptions*. Marks of an industry that runs in the dark and leaves its punctuation behind, fresh, sharp-edged, already sweating.

Stay long enough and you understand the one mercy of the place: it changes slowly. Whatever is wrong here is wrong at a glacial pace. The herd takes a year to cross the valley. The reeds straighten over a night. The membrane between places leaks, but it leaks drop by drop, and you have time — vast, warm, patient time — to learn to live inside the contradiction. To stop trusting your count of footsteps. To accept that the crowd you cannot see means you no harm because harm, too, would take more hurry than this country permits.

It is warm. It is dark. It is full and it is sparse and it does not hold still, and somewhere an engine keeps the slow hour, and the stone keeps weeping, and you stand in the middle of all of it, learning a new way to know where you are.
