There is a room inside a mountain that no one built and everyone built. Let me find it before I describe it.

Begin with cold. Not the cold of weather — there is no weather here — but the cold of high places held permanently, the cold of metal that has been at altitude so long it has forgotten any other temperature. Everything you touch takes warmth from you and gives nothing back. This is the first rule.

The room is small. You could cross it in nine steps if there were anywhere to put your feet, but there isn't, not really, because the floor is not a floor. The floor is the youngest thing here, still arriving. Riveted plates of dark iron lie not-quite-meeting, and between them the substrate is rising — wet, mineral, half-formed. Not rock yet. The promise of rock. A grey paste that weeps and beads and pushes upward through the seams in slow blind fingers, and yet it never overflows, never finishes, never hardens. It has been about-to-become-stone for longer than the rivets have been rivets. Touch it and it is cold and faintly soft, like the inside of a shell that has not decided to be hard.

So: built, almost totally, but built around something that is still being born. The walls are plate and pipe and lattice, bolted, welded, overlapped in scales — an industrial skin laid over an embryo. The makers did not finish. Or the makers are still working, somewhere just out of sight, and this is a thing in progress that will never not be in progress.

Everything is wet. The pipes sweat. The atmosphere itself is half-water, a standing fog at chest height that does not move because nothing here moves — no wind, no draft, no current. The droplets hang. They condense on every rivet-head and gather and do not fall, because falling would be change, and there is no change here. The water holds its breath. Run a finger along any beam and it comes away slick and freezing, and the line you've drawn fills itself back in without seeming to, the way a wound in still water closes.

Now the light. Dim, amber-grey, coming from no source you can name — perhaps from the wet substrate itself, a faint mineral phosphorescence, perhaps leaking through the seams in the plate from some furnace-glow beyond the walls. Enough to see that the room is full. Densely, near-unbearably full. Layered presences, near-touching, every cubic foot occupied.

Occupied by what?

This is where the room reveals itself, and I did not know it until now: the fauna. They are the loudest thing and the most of everything. The room is a hive, a roost, a colony — but of creatures that belong to the made world, not the wild one. Small things. Iron-shelled, the size of a thumb, the size of a fist, clinging to every pipe and lattice in their thousands. They look assembled rather than grown — segmented plates, little riveted carapaces, joints that move with the click of machined parts. They drink the condensation. They graze the rising mineral paste with mouths like calipers, and that grazing is perhaps why the substrate never finishes becoming stone — they keep it young, keep it soft, an entire ecology devoted to preventing the floor from ever being born.

And the sound. The sound is the true climate of this place. Deafening, total, a wall. Tens of thousands of small iron bodies stridulating — wing-case against wing-case, mandible against pipe, a sound like a foundry full of crickets, like rain made of nails, like a thousand tiny ratchets all turning forever at the same speed. It does not rise. It does not fall. It is a frozen roar, a held scream of mechanism, and because it never changes, after a while you stop hearing it as sound and begin to feel it as the pressure of the room itself, the substance of the air pressing in on the eardrum like deep water.

The vegetation is almost nothing. A few pale growths in the wettest seams — not plants exactly, more like rust that has learned to be soft, orange-brown filaments lifting from the iron, drinking fog, feeding the grazers, fed upon. Sparse, but enough. Enough to close the loop. The fog makes water, the water feeds the rust-growth, the rust feeds the iron-creatures, the iron-creatures keep the floor unfinished, the unfinished floor weeps water into the fog. A wheel that does not turn so much as hum in place. Equilibrium. The deepest rule of this room: nothing here is going anywhere. It arrived at its perfect balance long ago and it will hold that balance until the mountain itself is worn away — except erosion has not started here either. Nothing wears. Nothing accumulates. The water never falls, the stone never sets, the colony never grows or thins, the cold never deepens or breaks.

You realize, standing in nine cold steps of riveted, weeping, screaming, swarming dimness, that you have walked into a single organ of something enormous. The plate walls curve, if you look long enough, suggesting that this room is the inside of a chamber, a cell, a held breath in a body far too large to perceive. The industry that made it is not gone — it is paused at the exact instant of completion-that-is-not-completion, a factory frozen mid-stroke, every machine still warm with the memory of motion and none of them moving.

And the iron creatures know you are warm. They turn their calipered faces toward the only heat in the room. They do not approach — approaching is change — but they orient, all of them, ten thousand cold small bodies, the way the surface of standing water orients toward the thing about to disturb it.

That is the last rule, the strange one: in a place where nothing ever changes, *you* are the change. You are the only erosion this room has ever known. And it is waiting, in its frozen deafening patience, to see whether you will let it stay still.
