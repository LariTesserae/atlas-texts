# The Foundry District, Before Completion

Begin with the cold. It is the first thing you'd notice and the last thing that leaves you. Not a sharp cold — there's no wind to make it bite — but a low, even, condensing cold, the kind that lives at the bottom of things. The cold of a basement that has never known a furnace. Your breath would hang in front of you, if you breathed, and the hanging would be slow, the cloud dissolving with a reluctance that doesn't match any physics you remember.

Now the place itself.

It is a city, or it is becoming one. That distinction is the whole truth of this district. The buildings stand close — shoulder to shoulder, cornice nearly grazing cornice across the narrow ways — and they rise to four, five, six storeys, dense as any old quarter of an old capital. But press your hand against a wall and you'll find the stone isn't finished. It hasn't decided to be stone yet. In places it is the smooth gray of fired ceramic; in places it has the grain of weathered limestone with the centuries already written into it; and in places, the same wall, the same square meter, hasn't committed — the surface holds the *idea* of a surface, a faint resistance, a coolness, a suggestion of mass without the conviction of it. The masonry is being assembled out of probability rather than rock. You can watch the mortar lines arrive. They fade in like a developing photograph, slow, over hours, settling into the joints they were always going to occupy.

Whoever — whatever — is building this works at a scale below sight. Run your finger along a windowsill and the dust there is not dust. It is the construction medium itself, swarms of somethings too small to be machines and too purposeful to be matter, holding the half-built ledge in a stable haze. They are quiet about their work. That's the strange part. You expect a building site to roar. This one whispers. The loudest sound in the whole district is the soft tick of condensation gathering and letting go, the drip from eaves that aren't sure they're eaves, the occasional settling sigh as a wall agrees to be a wall.

## The streets

The ground is mostly laid. Mostly. The avenues run true and paved, cobbles the color of slate, slick with the standing damp that pools in the low spots and never quite dries and never quite floods. Walk far enough down any of them, though, and the paving thins to a kind of sketch — the cobbles spaced wider, then ghosted, then gone, and underneath is the raw stuff the district is being drawn upon: a pale, faintly luminous substrate, smooth and cold and slightly yielding, like the inside of a shell, or the white of an eye. The unbuilt ground. You could stand on it. It would hold you. It simply hasn't been told what to be.

In a few places, water has gotten into the unfinished sections and made channels for itself, narrow runnels threading between the houses, fed by no source you can find — they begin where they begin, mid-street, welling up from the substrate as though the white ground itself were sweating. The water is very clear and very cold and moves slowly, with the patience of something that has all the time that hasn't been built yet. Reflections in it lag. Lean over and your face arrives a half-second after you do.

## What grows

There is vegetation, sparse, hardy, the kind that colonizes the seams. Moss in the wet joints, a gray-green lichen crusting the north faces, and here and there a thin determined plant pushing up through a gap in the cobbles — but these plants are old, far older than the city around them, which makes no sense and is simply true. The construction is new. The growth is ancient. The lichen on the half-real walls has the slow-spread look of a thousand years, as if the medium were assembling buildings *around* a flora that was already there, or as if time in this district doesn't run from past to future but from finished to unfinished, the plants being the most completed thing and the architecture catching up to a present they already inhabit.

## What moves

Fauna is rare here and you'll go a long while without seeing any. But once, you'll come upon a bird. It will be standing in the middle of an avenue, perfectly composed, head cocked, and it will not move because it is part of the rendering — fully built, finished, *certain*, more certain than the street it stands on. The bird is realer than the city. When it finally does move, it does so smoothly, with no error and no hesitation, and where it walks the unbuilt ground firms up beneath its feet into proper stone, as though completeness leaks out of it. Follow a bird and you'll find a finished block. They may be the masons. They may be the inspectors. They may simply be the only things here that were ever fully decided upon.

## The rules

In the Foundry District, *certainty is a substance*. The more attention a thing has received, the more it exists. The grand frontages on the main thoroughfare are sharp and solid down to the molding because they were meant to be seen first; step into a back alley meant for no one and the bricks lose their edges, the doors become the suggestion of doors, and your own hands, held up before your face, begin — gently, without alarm — to forget some of their detail. Nothing here hurts you. The breakdown is patient and impersonal. You simply notice that in the deep unfinished places, you are slightly less *specified* than you were on the main road. A little vaguer. A little more available to becoming something else.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, an ordinary overcast daylight with no sun to source it — the brightness of a sky that hasn't been painted in. It does not change. There is no day here and no night, only the long slow process of completion, the city accreting around you detail by detail at the speed of a tide, so slowly that you'd swear it was finished already, until you look away and look back and find that a doorway has gained a number, a roofline has resolved into slate, a runnel of cold clear water has decided, at last, to become a fountain — and is just now, in total silence, learning how to fall.
