There is a pocket in the rock, and the pocket is bright.

Not lit from outside — there is no outside here, no surface, no sky. The brightness comes from the water itself. You are perhaps forty meters down, in a seam of pale rock that was molten not long ago in the reckoning of stone, and the heat of that recent making still presses up through the floor. The space is small. You could cross it in nine strides if there were anywhere to stand that did not scald.

Let me tell you about the water first, because the water is the reason for everything.

It seeps in through hairline fractures in the walls, arriving already hot, and where it meets the chamber air it does not boil so much as *glow*. This is the thing I want you to hold: the water is luminous. Superheated, mineral-saturated, it carries within it a faint and constant radiance — a chemiluminescence born of dissolved salts shedding energy as they cool, a cold-seeming light from a scalding source. The droplets that bead on the ceiling shine like held breath. They fall, and as they fall they streak the dark with brief white lines, and where they strike the floor they hiss and flash and are gone back into vapor. So the chamber is never dark and never still. It rains upward and downward at once — condensing on the cool high places, evaporating from the hot low ones — a closed weather, a single recycling cloud no larger than a room.

The walls weep. Run a hand near them (you would not — they would take the skin) and you would feel the seep as a film, the rock perpetually glazed, perpetually shedding its glaze into the air. The stone is young. Its edges have not yet been rounded by anything; the fractures are sharp, conchoidal, the surfaces glassy where the melt cooled fast. Volcanic glass, mostly, threaded with brighter veins — and it is these veins that trouble the geologist in me, because they are too regular.

I said there is nothing built here. That is true and I will keep it true. No one made this room. But something *arranged* it.

The bright veins in the walls are not ore as ore behaves. They branch and rejoin in patterns that distribute the heat with a precision no random cooling produces — they pull warmth from the floor and carry it up and out through the fractures at exactly the rate that keeps the chamber's weather turning. They are channels. They are, if you watch them long enough across the slow flicker of the glowing rain, *adjusting* — a vein thickening here where a cold spot threatens, thinning there where the heat runs too eager. This is engineering. But it is engineering without an engineer, without a tool-mark, without a single straight line. It is the structure life makes when life is old enough and patient enough to stop building things and start *being* the building.

Because there is life here. Rare, sparse, scattered through the emptiness with great spaces of nothing between — but present.

It is not plant. Nothing here photosynthesizes; the light is the wrong light and there is no carbon traffic to drive it. It is closer to the fungal, or to something the word *fungal* only gestures at — a chemo-feeding film, mineral-eating, that lays itself in the cooler crevices in sheets thinner than frost. It is the same pale shine as the water, which is no accident: it is the film that tuned the veins, the film that learned over ages how to make the rock carry heat the way a body carries blood. It does not move that you could see in an hour. But the chamber is *active* — many slow processes interlocking, the upward rain and the downward rain, the cooling and the seeping and the imperceptible creep of the living glaze — and over a year you would find the room had remade itself entirely while keeping itself exactly the same.

And the fauna. There is one kind, perhaps. I have caught sight of them only at the edge of seeing: small bright swimmers in the falling droplets, riding the water down and the vapor up, creatures whose whole lives fit inside a single fall and rise, born in the condensation on the ceiling and dying in the flash on the floor and somehow being born again — or being something that does not distinguish the two. They are rare. Minutes pass with none. Then one. Then the long emptiness again.

It is quiet. That surprises people who imagine heat as roar. But the water does not boil violently; it transitions softly, and the loudest sound is the small irregular *tick* and *sip* of droplets meeting the hot floor, spaced far apart, each one a tiny punctuation in a silence that is otherwise complete. Now and then the rock settles — a single dense knock from deep in the wall, felt more than heard.

The rules here are few. Heat rises and is caught and returned. Water falls bright and lifts dark and falls bright again. The living film keeps the temperature inside the narrow band where it can persist — too cool and it sleeps, too hot and it burns — and so the whole chamber is really one organism's quiet act of homeostasis, a body of stone and steam holding its own fever steady, forty meters under everything, in a room no one was meant to find, glowing to itself in the dark.

It is not strange, once you are in it. That is the strangest thing. It feels, from inside, entirely ordinary — the way a lung feels nothing remarkable about breathing.
