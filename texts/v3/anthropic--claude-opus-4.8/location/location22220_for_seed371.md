There is a notch in the high ridge, a fold where two young peaks lean toward each other almost to touching, and that is the whole of the place. You could walk its length in the time it takes a storm to pass overhead, which here is most of the time.

Start with the rock, because the rock is new. It has not had time to soften. The walls of the notch are raw — fractured planes of pale stone with edges that have never been rounded, knife-faces still showing the cleavage of whatever upheaval shoved them into the sky. No scree slope of patient debris at the base; instead, fresh blocks, sharp-cornered, that fell last season or last week and have not yet learned to lie still. When you put your hand to the stone it is cold and faintly wet, and the wet is not rain but the air itself condensing, breathing onto the rock and beading and running in thin threads down the unworn surfaces.

It is dark here. Not night exactly — there is no proof of sun, no proof of moon — but a permanent dimness, the light of deep cloud pressed down into a slot too narrow and too high to admit much sky. Things are felt before they are seen. The dark is wet and close and full of breathing green.

Because the green is everywhere. This is the contradiction the place insists on: a cold dark high-altitude crack in fresh stone, and it is *drowning* in vegetation. Moss in layers a hand deep, sphagnum and liverwort and things with no names, climbing the new rock faster than the rock can be old. Ferns uncoil out of every seam, fronds packed against fronds, near-touching, so that to move through is to part a curtain that closes again behind you. Bromeliad-like rosettes cup the runoff. Vines drop from the lip of the notch in ropes and meet the climbing things halfway, and the air between is so thick with leaf and frond and pendant stem that there is no clear volume anywhere — every cubic foot has something living crowded into it, layered, overlapping, soaked. The damp is enough to feed all this and no more; there are no standing pools, no stream, only the perpetual condensation and the occasional fast rain that the slot funnels into a hundred small dripping veins.

There are no animals. No insect drone, no bird, no thing that scuttles. The green grows in a stillness of nerves, photosynthesizing in near-darkness on patience alone.

And yet it is loud. This is the second contradiction, and the strangest. The notch is never quiet, and the sound is not water and not weather — or not only. There is a deep continuous working noise that comes up through the stone and down through the air: a churning, a grinding, a great laboring rhythm with the regularity of pistons. It rises and falls but never stops. Somewhere — below, beyond, inside the mountain that has no business holding anything but more rock — there is the sound of engines. Mechanized, scaled, relentless. A factory-roar muffled by miles of stone into something felt in the teeth and the breastbone, a turbine sigh, the clank of moved mass, the hiss of pressure released and built again. You cannot find it. There is no machine to see, no smokestack, no glow of forge, no rail, no wire, nothing built that the eye can fix on. Only the sound, enormous and industrial, as if the new mountain itself were the housing of some engine still under construction, and the green were growing on the warm vibrating shell of it.

That is what makes the place turbulent, finally — not storm, though storms come. Everything is in violent change at once and none of it agrees. The rock is mid-fracture, shedding blocks. The plants grow at a visible rush, swelling and reaching in the dim, dying back where the cold bites and surging again where the engine-warmth leaks through a seam. The condensation builds and sheets and sluices and rebuilds. The sound modulates through its labors. Air moves wrong, sucked and pushed by pressures from below, so the curtains of fern shudder without wind. Nothing settles. The place is being made, right now, around you and beneath you, and it is making itself with the indifferent thunder of a process that does not need a witness — cold, lush, dark, deafening, and entirely without eyes.

The rule of the world here is simple and unsettling: *growth and machinery are the same act.* The engine drives the green; the green sheathes the engine. Where one falters the other falters. To stand in the notch is to stand inside something building itself out of the mountain, breathing wet on its own new walls, and roaring.
