There is a city beneath the water table, and it has been there long enough to forget it was ever built.

You come to it the way you come to most things underground — gradually, by descent, the ceiling lowering its shoulders over you until the open sky is a rumor. The first sign is the wet. Not rain, not flood, just the patient seep of a place that lives below the line where water wants to be. Every surface weeps. The brick exhales. Run your hand along a wall and it comes away beaded, and the beads are cool, and they taste faintly of iron and of something older that has no name above ground.

The scale is intimate. This is not a buried metropolis; it is a buried quarter, a single drowned ward, perhaps the size of a few streets you could walk in an afternoon if the afternoon existed here. It does not. There is only the dim — a standing, sourceless gray-blue, the brightness of an hour that never resolves into morning or evening. Light here is a property of the wet air, the way humidity is a property of a held breath. It does not fall. It simply is, distributed evenly through the haze, so that nothing casts a real shadow and nothing is ever fully lit.

The architecture is industrial and it is old, and those two facts have made a long peace. Cast-iron columns rise from the standing water, fluted, capped with foliate ironwork green-black under verdigris and slime. There were factories here, or something factory-shaped — you can read the intent in the bones. Flywheels the size of millstones lie canted in the shallows, their teeth softened by a century of mineral deposit into something almost organic, almost coral. Pipes run everywhere, in bundles, in arteries, threading between buildings, and from their joints the water drips in a slow metronome. Drip. A long pause. Drip. The whole place keeps time this way, patiently, and the time it keeps is not human time.

Everything has settled. That is the dominant feeling — settlement, in the geological sense. The erosion here is not violence; it is completion. The corners have been rounded, the mortar leached and re-deposited as flowstone down the faces of the walls, building little organ-pipe drapes of pale stone over the brick. Where a stair once ran, there is now a smooth ramp of mineral, a frozen waterfall over the steps. The city has not decayed so much as it has been re-cast, slowly, in the stone the water carries. It is becoming a cave. It is most of the way there.

It is quiet. This is the strange thing for a place so full of machinery — the silence. The engines stopped long ago, and the water that replaced them moves too slowly to make noise. There is the dripping. There is, occasionally, a long subterranean sigh as some distant void equalizes its pressure, a sound felt more in the chest than the ear. And there is the fauna, and the fauna is the loudest absence in the place, because there is so much of it and it makes so little sound.

They are everywhere, the animals. This is their city now. Pale fish move through the flooded streets in slow schools, blind or nearly so, their bodies the color of the light. White crustaceans the size of your two hands crowd the ironwork, grazing the films of bacteria that sheet every surface in faint iridescence. There are amphibious things in the doorways, eel-shaped, that you only ever see as a ripple departing. On the dry shelves of the upper floors — and there are dry shelves, the city is sparse, full of empty rooms and unvisited galleries — there are colonies of soft moths and cave crickets and a kind of slow, fist-sized beetle that grazes the lichen and is grazed in turn by something you do not see but feel watching. The density of life is enormous and it is all hushed. A thousand creatures and not one of them in a hurry, not one of them with a reason to cry out. The abundance is total and it is patient and it is nearly silent, and that is the most uncanny thing of all: a teeming place that holds its breath.

The vegetation is sparse and where it exists it is wrong in the gentle way of deep places. Pale fungal trees grow from the rich mud in the corners, branching, fruiting with caps that give off a weak biological glow — this is the only light that has a source, and it is barely a light at all, more a suggestion that the dark could be less dark if it tried. Mosses that have given up chlorophyll spread in cream-and-rust mats. Here and there a true plant — a fern, a single improbable fern — descended through some crack from the surface world, etiolated, stretched thin and yellow, dying slowly and beautifully over what must be years. Nothing here is fast. The fern will take a decade to die. The flowstone will take a century to bury the next stair. The fish will circle the same flooded square for the whole of their long, cold, dim lives.

And the rules of the place, the way it holds itself together — the rules are these. Water rises and falls, but slowly, on a tide that has nothing to do with any moon; over the course of a long while you cannot measure, the streets grow deeper and then shallower, and the creatures migrate up the buildings and down again, and the whole vertical society of the ward shifts a few feet and shifts back. Light does not change. Temperature does not change — it is always cool, always that single unwavering coolness that is neither cold nor comfortable, the temperature of a thing kept rather than a thing alive. Sound does not gather; it is spent the moment it is made, swallowed by the wet and the soft and the slow.

The strangeness is not in any single thing but in the arrangement: that an industry should have become an ecology, that machines should have become reefs, that a place built to roar should have become the quietest room in the world, full to bursting with patient pale life that asks nothing and announces nothing. You stand in the standing water to your shins. Drip. The long pause. A school of blind fish parts around your legs and does not startle, because nothing here has been startled in a very long time. Drip.

It has been like this for ages. It will be like this for ages more. The water is near the surface of everything, and everything is near the end of being itself, and being something else instead — slower, wetter, paler, and at peace.

Drip.
