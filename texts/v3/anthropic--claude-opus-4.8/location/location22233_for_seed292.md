# The Cloud-Drowned Range

Start with the height, because everything here begins with the height.

You are nearly three kilometers up, on the spine of a mountain range so old it has stopped being mountains in the way younger ranges are mountains. There are no jagged peaks. Erosion has had the last word here, and the last word was patience. The summits have been ground down to broad, rounded shoulders, like the backs of whales surfacing through something — and the something they surface through is water.

Not water falling. Water *standing*. Water that lives in the air.

This range sits in the permanent path of an ocean's exhalation. Warm currents far below send their moisture up against the cool flank of the highlands, and at this elevation the air can hold no more. So it doesn't. The cloud here is not weather; it is terrain. It pools in the valleys like a tide that never goes out. It pours over the saddles between summits in slow gray cataracts that take hours to drain and never finish draining. You walk through it the way a diver walks the seafloor — pushing, your clothes immediately soaked, your breath joining the medium you breathe.

## The Water

Call it the Standing Sea, though no one named it that on purpose.

Everything drips. The whole world is a percolation. Water beads on every leaf, every stone, every strand of moss, swells, lets go, and is replaced before it falls. The rock faces wear permanent sheets of moving film. When wind comes — and it comes hard, this high — the cloud doesn't blow away so much as it *churns*, great slow rolls of it folding through the trees, and the standing water comes off every surface at once in a horizontal rain that stings.

You are never dry. The concept doesn't apply. The cool — and it is cool here, never warm, the kind of cool that gets into the seams of you — comes mostly from the wet, from evaporation pulling heat off your skin no matter how still you stand.

## The Green

And yet it is gloriously, riotously alive.

The cool and the endless water make this a cloud forest at the absolute extreme of what a cloud forest can be. Things grow on things that grow on things. The trees are short, wind-bent, their trunks no thicker than your leg, but every trunk is sleeved in moss so deep you can sink your arm to the elbow. Out of the moss grow ferns. Out of the ferns grow orchids no larger than a fingernail. Liverworts curtain every branch. Bromeliads hold cupped pools in their hearts — small aquariums in the air, each one its own ecosystem, each one home to frogs that will live and die without ever touching the ground.

The canopy is low because the wind won't let it be tall, so the layering goes sideways and downward instead of up. You move through it bent, parting curtains of dripping growth, and behind each curtain is another, and the green keeps its own dim weather: it filters the light to an even pewter glow, bright enough to see clearly, never bright enough to cast a shadow.

## The Sound

It is loud. This surprises people who expect mist to be quiet.

The water is the loudest thing. Not a single sound but a vast layered one — the hiss of the standing cloud against ten million leaves, the constant trickle and run of film-water finding channels, the percussion of drip on drip on drip, and beneath it all the deep continuous roar of the real waterfalls, where collected cloud finally gathers into streams and throws itself off the ancient shoulders into the white below.

And the animals. This range is *crowded* with life that loves cool and wet. Frogs by the thousand, calling in shifts. Insects in droning sheets. Birds you mostly hear and seldom see — bell-clear notes, ratchets, sudden screams. Something large moves in the moss-deep undergrowth on padded feet. The whole soundscape presses on you, layered and turbulent, never resolving into silence even at the dead of night.

## The Marks of Hands

People came here. People left.

Scattered through the range — and you find them suddenly, the cloud parting on them like a stagehand's reveal — are the remains of an industry. Someone, generations back, understood that all this falling water at all this height was *power*. There are pipes: great riveted iron pipes, taller than a man, running down the mountainsides, carrying captured cloud-streams to turbine houses below. Most are burst now, fountaining where the seams gave way, and the forest has taken them — moss-sleeved, fern-crowned, the iron weeping rust into the white water in long ochre stains.

Here a turbine hall stands half-swallowed, its tall windows broken, its machines still inside: huge dark wheels and governors and the calm geometry of generators, all of it slick with condensation, ticking and pinging as the metal answers the cold. A few of them still turn. That is the strangeness of this place — somewhere, water still finds its old intended channel, and a wheel grinds round, and a single string of lamps still burns in a building no one has entered in decades, glowing dim through the cloud like a drowned moon. The industry was never fully shut off. It was simply abandoned mid-breath, and a fraction of it kept running on momentum and rainfall, tended by no one, indifferent.

The structures are few — a turbine house, a snapped catwalk, a collapsed cableway with its car still hanging in the white, swaying. Scattered, partial, faint. But the *machinery's* presence is large: its hum joins the water and the frogs, a low electric drone threaded through everything, and where the lamps still burn the moths and the frogs and the small wet things have gathered to it, made their world around it. The wild has not rejected the machine. It has incorporated it.

## The Rules

This is a place near the end of its own long story, and it knows it. The mountains will not rise again. The forest has reached the densest it can be and now simply replaces itself, drip by drip, frog by frog. The industry will not return; it will only run down. Everything that can change has already changed into its final form — and yet, *inside* that finished, settled state, the moment-to-moment world is pure turbulence. The cloud heaves. The wind throws the rain sideways. A pipe bursts a new seam and a new waterfall is born in an afternoon. A wheel that has turned for forty years seizes at last with a groan you feel in your teeth.

Ancient bones, frantic blood. The shape is fixed; the life inside it never stops thrashing.

You stand on the rounded summit, soaked to the marrow, and you cannot see the valley below or the valley beyond — only the white, endlessly folding, lit evenly from nowhere, loud with frogs and falling water and the patient hum of a machine that does not know it has been left alone. Somewhere down in the cloud, a lamp burns. The mountain breathes out water. It has been doing this longer than anything that ever named it, and it will do it long after the last wheel stops.
