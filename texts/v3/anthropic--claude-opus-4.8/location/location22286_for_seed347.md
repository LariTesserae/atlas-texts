There is a city beneath the water table, and it is cold, and it is full, and almost no one speaks.

Start with the going-down. The land at the surface is marsh — that much you can tell from above, a flat country of standing pools and sedge, the kind of place where every footfall finds water rising into the print behind you. But the marsh is only a lid. Where the ground sags, where the peat thins over older stone, there are mouths: sinkholes lined with fern, throats of pale limestone that the water has been chewing open for not very long. You can tell it hasn't been long because the edges are still sharp. The rock has not learned to be smooth. It breaks in fresh planes, in clean shelves and unweathered angles, and the streams that pour down them have not yet found their patient curves. Everything underground here is recently made. The cave is young and it shows.

Descend, and the light comes with you further than you'd expect. This is no true dark. The limestone is shot through with something — not phosphorescence, nothing so strange — but the rock itself is pale and the water everywhere reflects, and the shafts that open to the marsh above let down a wide, even, grey-green daylight that pools and travels. By the time you are well below the surface the light has become something like the inside of an overcast afternoon: enough to read by, enough to see the far walls, sourceless and constant. It is normal light in an abnormal place, and that is its own kind of unsettling.

Now the city.

It was not built. That is the first thing to understand. No hands laid these courses. But it is unmistakably a settlement — dense, organized, layered, structured at the scale of a town that has filled its valley and begun to climb its own walls. The builders are the water and the things that live in it. Calcite does the masonry. Where the ceiling weeps — and it weeps everywhere, the whole roof of this world is a slow continuous rain, fat cold drops falling and falling — the minerals come down with the water and stand up where they land. Columns. Whole streets of them. Stalagmites grown thick as towers, grown into terraces, grown into the rounded swelling shapes that look, in the grey light, like houses pressed shoulder to shoulder with no road between. Flowstone runs down the slopes in frozen cascades that read exactly like tiled roofs, like stepped foundations, like the cluttered roofscape of an old town seen from a hill. Rimstone dams hold back tier after tier of still pools, and the pools are stacked like the flooded plazas of a place that gave up on staying dry.

And it is packed. This is the thing the place insists on most. There is no empty room anywhere. Every surface bears growth — mineral or living, and often both at once. The formations crowd until they fuse. Stand in one of the lower galleries and you cannot find a clear sightline; everything is occupied, every niche has its column, every column has its skin of moss, every patch of moss has its small wet residents. Saturation is the law here. The world is full to its edges and pressing outward.

The cold keeps it slow. It is a cold that does not snap or bite but simply persists, a few degrees above freezing, the cold of deep water and patient stone. Your breath fogs. The drip-rain is colder than ice has any right to be without being ice. And because of the cold and the wet together the air is always thick with vapor — every drop that falls throws up a little ghost of mist, and the galleries hang full of it, so the constant light comes always slightly diffused, always softened, the far columns going vague and blue with distance.

The vegetation should not be possible and yet it is everywhere, because the light reaches and the water never stops. Not the green of the sun-world — this is the green of shade and damp, deep and slow. Mosses in beds a hand-span thick, swallowing the lower formations until the calcite towers wear hoods and shawls of it. Ferns crowd the lit shafts and lean out over the pools. Liverworts plate the wet rock in overlapping scales. Pale fungi stand in the truly dim corners like little cities of their own within the city. Algae greens every still surface of every pool until the water looks like jade. The plant life climbs the architecture and the architecture is, in part, made of the plant life's leavings — root and lime grown into each other past telling apart.

And it lives — abundantly, but quietly. This is the strangest gift of the place: the silence. With all this water you'd expect a roar, and there is none. The drips fall into deep moss or into still pools and make almost no sound; the moss drinks every echo before it can form. The streams move slow and full and silent through their fresh-cut channels. So the fauna moves through a hush. Blind white fish hang in the jade pools and the milky water, unhurried. Pale shrimp work the gravels. Salamanders the color of the rock — eyeless, ghostly, frilled with red gills — drape across the wet ledges and barely stir. Snails by the thousand graze the moss; you can hear them only if you press your ear close, a tiny collective rasp. Things with too many legs run the ceilings. Soft moths that have forgotten flight crawl the fern-stems. Bats, surely — but even they roost in silence here, and when they move they move like cloth.

The loudest sound in this whole drowned town is the occasional fall: a column too saturated, a flowstone roof gone heavy, a sudden collapse and slump of stone and a single deep splash, and then the silence sealing over it again. The city is being built and unbuilt constantly, fast for stone and slow for everything else — water cutting new throats, calcite raising new towers, moss advancing, a pool breaching its rim and finding a lower room to flood. An ecosystem fully in motion and almost wholly without voice.

There are no people. No tools, no fire, no mark of any maker but the water. And yet you keep reading the place as a city, because it has every shape of one — the crowding, the layering, the streets and roofs and stacked flooded squares, the sense that everything here is *occupied*, that there is no vacancy and never has been. A town that built itself out of cold and patience, that filled every room before anyone arrived, and that goes on living its slow green silent life under the marsh, lit by a grey daylight that should not be able to reach so far down, and somehow does.
