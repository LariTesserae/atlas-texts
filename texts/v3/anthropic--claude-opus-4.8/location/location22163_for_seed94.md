# The Loom Highlands

Start high. Start where the air thins and cools but never dries, because here the cold is wet — a cold that beads on every surface and hangs in the lungs like a held breath.

The land rises in long terraced shelves, each one a thousand feet of saturated green stacked against the next, climbing toward summits I cannot see for the mist. This is high country, but it is not bare country. The usual logic of altitude — that life thins as you climb — does not hold here. The higher you go, the more crowded it becomes.

## The Terrain

The rock beneath is old. Not young and jagged, not freshly broken — old, rounded, settled into itself. Long rivers of time have worked these slopes into smooth-backed ridges and broad bowls, the way water works a stone in a streambed over centuries. Where stone shows through the green, it is dark and wet, grooved with the runneling of ten thousand years of rain, every surface weeping. You could press your palm to any rockface and come away with a handful of water that was never really falling, only seeping, only present.

The shelves hold standing water in their hollows — black tarns, peat-dark pools, marsh-edges fringed with sphagnum so deep your boot would vanish to the knee. Between the pools, the ground is springy, woven, never bare. There is no clean line between land and water here. The land is water held in suspension by roots and moss, and the water is land that hasn't finished settling.

## The Density

Now — and this is the thing that takes a while to understand — there is *no room*. I want to be precise about this. It is not that the place is full of plants. It is that the place has no empty space in it at all. Every cubic yard is occupied. The mosses grow on the ferns, the ferns grow through the moss, lichens map every twig, liverworts shingle the undersides of leaves, and between all of it the air itself is thick with hanging water and drifting spores and the slow vertical traffic of insects. To stand still is to be colonized. To walk is to push through a medium that closes behind you.

The vegetation is layered past counting: a canopy of stunted, wind-bent trees whose branches are themselves gardens — epiphytes, bromeliad-cups holding their own small ponds, each pond holding its own small frogs. Below that a tangle of broadleaf scrub. Below that the great ferns, tree-high, fiddleheads uncurling. Below that the moss-world, which is itself many worlds deep. It is green the way the deep sea is dark — totally, dimensionally, with no horizon to it.

## The Sound

And here is what makes the place uncanny, what makes the back of the neck prickle: it is **quiet**.

A place this packed, this alive, this wet — you expect a roar. You expect birdsong stacked on birdsong, the rush of water, wind in ten million leaves. Instead there is a hush so complete it feels like a held breath, or like the moment after a held breath. The moss drinks sound. Every surface is soft, saturated, absorbent; nothing rings, nothing echoes. The water does not rush — it seeps, it beads, it falls one drop at a time from leaf to leaf, and each drop lands in moss and makes no noise at all.

What sound there is comes small and singular and far between: a single drip. The creak of a fern stem under its own weight of water. Somewhere, the low double-note of something breathing — an animal, or the land. A faint pervasive *tick*, which is the spores and the dewfall and the unhurried machinery of growth, the sound of the world being made very slowly.

## The Weather of Itself

The place is large enough to brew its own weather, and it does, constantly. This is the active part — the engine. Warm air does not visit here; the warmth is made below, in the lowland heat of valleys you cannot see, and it rises into the cold of the highlands and lets go of everything it carried. The result is endless condensation. Cloud is not above this landscape. Cloud *is* this landscape, at every altitude, forming and dissolving and re-forming. You walk through it. It walks through you. Visibility shifts from a hundred yards to an arm's length and back within a single minute, the world drawing curtains and opening them again.

This is the cycle that runs everything: warm-up, condense, weep, drink, grow. A breathing. A slow tidal exchange between the unseen warm below and the cold wet above. There are no seasons here that I can find — only the in-breath and the out-breath of the mist, hour after hour, the daily weather doing the work that elsewhere takes a year.

## The Fauna

Abundant, but in keeping with the silence: secretive, soft-footed, more often sensed than seen.

In the bromeliad-pools, jewel-bright amphibians that never touch the ground, born and dying in a cup of water forty feet up. Along the wet rock, slow things — salamanders the length of an arm, pale and unhurried, who have never needed to hurry. In the canopy, birds that do not sing so much as *click*, mechanical and low, communicating through the absorbent air with sounds the moss cannot quite swallow. There are large grazers somewhere in the fern-thickets — you find their pressed beds, the cropped fiddleheads, the warm hollow where one slept — but you do not see them. They move when the curtain of mist closes. The whole ecology is shy of the eye. It is an ecosystem in full motion that has learned to do its business inside the white blindness of the cloud.

## The Strange Thing — and the Engine

Now for the part I kept circling. The parameters said *industrial* — engines, factories, mechanized, scaled production — and the parameters said *no built thing, nothing made, untouched.* Both. Both at once. I sat with that contradiction a long while and I think I understand it now.

There are no structures here. No walls, no roads, no machines of metal. Nothing was ever built. And yet the place *runs like an industry*. It is a factory whose machinery is alive.

The mist-engine I described — the warm-below feeding the cold-above — is not a metaphor for production; it is production. This whole vast highland is a manufacturing system operating at the scale of weather. The moss is the conveyor. The trees are the cooling towers, releasing vapor in slow visible plumes that, if you watch the canopy from a ridge, look exactly like the smokestack-breath of some enormous mill — except white, except silent, except made of the place's own exhaled water. The marsh terraces are settling-tanks. The whole landscape is engaged in the patient, scaled, ceaseless conversion of warmth and water into *more of itself* — into more green, more depth, more density, packing the world tighter and tighter with no waste product but the next layer of life.

The unusualness is this: that the industry has no operator and no purpose outside itself. It is mechanized in its rhythm — regular, cyclic, productive, vast — but no one designed it and no one is fed by it. It simply manufactures the conditions of its own continuation, hour after quiet hour, century after settled century, exhaling its silent white smoke into a cold that never lets it leave.

A factory that grew. A factory that breathes. A factory you could drown in, softly, and the moss would not make a sound.

---

That is the place. High, cool, weeping, packed past the possibility of emptiness, loud with growth and utterly quiet, manufacturing itself forever inside its own weather, and waiting — patiently, mechanically, alive — for you to stop moving.
