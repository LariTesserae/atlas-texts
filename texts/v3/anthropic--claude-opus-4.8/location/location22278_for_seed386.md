There is a mountain that makes weather, and on its eastern shoulder, between nine and eleven thousand feet, the place I am describing holds itself together in defiance of the cold it ought to have.

It is hot here. That is the first wrongness you feel, and it is not a violent wrongness — there is no fumarole, no visible vent. The heat comes up through the soil like something exhaled. You climb into thinning air, your lungs working harder for less, and instead of the expected chill you walk into a warmth that has the texture of breath. The ground is warm to the palm. Lay your hand flat and you feel a patience in it, the slow heat of a body at rest.

Because it is hot and high and the air is forever giving up its water against the cold of altitude, everything here drips. The forest is a cloud forest, but the cloud is local, generated and re-generated — it forms in the cool dawn, burns thin by midmorning, thickens again as the warm ground pushes its moisture up to meet the cold sky, and by afternoon the whole slope is inside a gray-gold luminous fog that the sun pours through sideways. Light arrives here already softened, already wet, the way light comes through a steamed window. Everything is lit but nothing is sharply lit. Edges are negotiable.

The terrain is old. Not violent-young like the peaks above it that still shed rock, but settled into its long shape — the ridges rounded, the gullies graded to easy curves, the boulders sunk halfway into the earth and capped thick with moss so they look like green animals sleeping. Water has worked this place for a very long time and reached a kind of agreement with it. The streams do not cut, they braid. They spread across stepped terraces of travertine, pale mineral shelves the warm springs have laid down over millennia, and they pour from one shallow pool to the next in sheets too thin to make much noise. This is the quiet I keep noticing. With this much water you expect roar, and there is none. The water moves everywhere and announces itself almost nowhere — a tinkle, a trickle, the *plip* of condensation falling leaf to leaf, the suck of your boot in saturated black peat. The loudest thing is usually your own breathing.

The vegetation crowds. Tree ferns first — they love the warm wet, and they stand in dense ranks with their fronds interlocking overhead, so that you walk through a low green ceiling held up on scaled trunks. Beneath them, every surface that can hold a plant holds three. Mosses in a dozen greens, liverworts pressed flat as paint, filmy ferns one cell thick that glow when the light hits them. Orchids no bigger than a grain of rice clinging to bark. Bromeliad-like rosettes catching their own private pools, each pool a world with its own larvae and its own tiny predator. The density is layered rather than tangled — there is structure to it, tiers, an understory and a mid-story and a canopy, each draped and dripping and full.

Now the part that takes the longest to see, the thing the place keeps offering and withdrawing in the fog:

There is industry here, and no one made it.

I keep wanting to call them factories because I have no better word. On the warmest, wettest terraces, where the mineral water pours over the travertine, there are structures — but they are grown, not built, and so I cannot count them as *built*; they are the work of the slope itself, of its heat and its chemistry and its life. They are reefs of a kind. Mounds and towers of laid-down mineral, intricate as cooling fins, riddled with channels through which the warm water is *pumped* — and it is genuinely pumped, by pressure differentials the heat creates, water lifted, circulated, returned, a closed mechanical cycle running on the mountain's exhaled warmth. You put your ear to one of the towers and you hear the only real sound the place keeps: a deep, slow, periodic surge. A working sound. The sound of something turning over and over without rest, mechanized, scaled, the same stroke repeated across a whole hillside of these formations until the slope hums at a pitch just below hearing.

The life has wired itself into this. Inside the channels live colonies — I'll call them animals, they move and eat and die — that tend the flow, that thicken a wall here to raise the pressure there, that open and close the mineral valves with bodies that have hardened into gates. They farm the warm chemical water for what it carries up from below. Whole economies of small creatures run on their leavings. It is an engine and an ecology at once, indistinguishable, electrified by nothing but heat and slope and the steady contraction of living tissue that has learned to behave like pistons.

Fauna otherwise: abundant but shy in the fog. Things with large damp eyes that the dimness suits. Amphibians the moss-color of everything. Birds you hear as a single liquid note and never see. A predator I noticed only by its absence — a stillness that moved through the understory and emptied it of small noises ahead of it. The animals here keep their own quiet; loudness is for places with sightlines, and there are none.

The whole slope is *active* in the way a body is active while sleeping — many processes, none hurried. Fog forms and burns and forms. The travertine grows by a hair each year. The mineral engines surge. Water lifts and falls. Heat breathes up. Nothing here arrives at a conclusion; it all simply continues, balanced, established, hot at the impossible height, working its slow circulation in the wet gold light, audible only if you stop and hold still long enough to hear the mountain pumping itself.

Stop. Hold still. There — under the dripping — the surge.
