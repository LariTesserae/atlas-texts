There is a place where the ground forgot how to be soft.

It begins as a flatland of fused sediment, low and wide, stretching past where the eye gives up. Not desert exactly — the air carries moisture, a faint everywhere-dampness that beads on cool surfaces and never quite falls. The mornings, if they can be called that, arrive with condensation gathering in the shallow troughs of the rock, little mirrors of standing water that the warmth does not dry and the cold does not freeze. The temperature holds at a steady warmth, the warmth of a machine that has been running so long no one remembers starting it.

Nothing grows. There is no green, no grey-green, no lichen even — only in the wettest seams a thin organization of something that might be fungal or might be the first rough draft of biology, a brownish film that does not spread, does not retreat, does not feed on anything visible. It simply persists at the edges of the water pockets like a stain that has reached an agreement with the stone.

The terrain has the look of long settlement. Everything is weathered into balance: the rock smoothed into low domes and gentle channels, the edges rounded, the slopes graded by some erosion that finished its work an age ago and walked away. There are no sharp things here. The whole region has the worn dignity of an old riverbed where the river is a memory and the bed is the only fact left.

And yet — and this is the strangeness — it is loud.

The sound comes from everywhere and from nothing you can point to. A deep mechanical respiration, the rhythm of pistons or bellows, rising through the bedrock itself. There are no factories. There are no engines. There is no smokestack, no wall, no wheel, nothing built by any hand. But the ground works. It pounds and hisses and turns with the unmistakable cadence of industry — a vast machinery running underneath the smoothed stone, audible through the soles of the feet, felt in the teeth. Steam vents in places, threading up through hairline cracks, and that is where the moisture comes from: the land is sweating the exhaust of some labor that produces nothing you can see and consumes nothing you can find.

The light is ordinary. That is part of what unsettles. A plain, even daylight lies over all of it, the diffuse light of an overcast noon, neither golden nor harsh, simply *enough* to see by. No sun is visible, no source, but the shadows are soft and the colors honest — the wet ochre of the rock, the dark of the water pockets, the pale exhalation of the vents. Under such reasonable light the industrial roar feels like a lie the world is telling, calmly.

Life is rare and incidental. Now and then a creature passes — low-bodied, unhurried, built like something that has learned to live beside enormous noise and stopped fearing it. They drink at the condensation pools. They do not call to one another; they could not be heard if they did. They leave no tracks on stone that takes no impression.

Across the open distance the features repeat with great spaces between them: a vent here, a wide shallow basin there, a low rise of fused rock a mile off humming at a slightly different pitch. Sparse, scattered, balanced with emptiness — a regional thing, a valley-sized fact, with room to walk for a long time between one presence and the next.

And nothing changes.

This is the deepest rule of the place. The water never quite gathers into rain and never quite dries. The warmth never climbs and never falls. The machinery beneath the stone never speeds up, never slows, never breaks down, never reveals itself. The erosion is complete and finished. The brown film at the pool edges neither grows nor dies. The animals come and go but their coming and going is itself a kind of stillness, a loop too long to perceive as motion. There are no seasons. There is no weather. There is no decay because there is nothing left soft enough to rot, and no growth because nothing here has agreed to begin.

It is a landscape at the bottom of some long settling — a world that ran its processes to exhaustion and arrived at a perfect, deafening equilibrium, and now simply continues. The roar is not the sound of work being done. It is the sound of work that finished long ago and forgot to stop, an idling that has become eternal.

Stand in it long enough and you understand that the noise is the silence of this place. There is nothing to wait for. Nothing is coming. The steam will rise, the water will bead, the unseen engines will keep their patient thunder, and the even light will lie over all of it, unchanged, for as long as there is a world to hold it.
