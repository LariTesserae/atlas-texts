There is a town on the shoulder of a mountain that has stopped changing.

Not abandoned — that's the first thing to set aside. Abandonment implies a leaving, a verb that happened and then was over. This place has no such event in its past. It simply arrived at its present form a long time ago and has remained there, the way a stone in a riverbed arrives at its shape and keeps it.

Begin with the rock. The mountain here is a pale grey stone, fine-grained, the kind that splits along clean planes when you strike it right. The whole settlement is built from it because there was nothing else to build from and no reason to carry anything up. The houses are dry-laid — stones fitted without mortar, set by hand, each wall the record of someone's patient afternoon. The fit is so good that in places you cannot slide a fingernail between two stones. This is the tech of the place: hands, levers, the wedge, the plumb line of a string and a weight. No metal that I can see beyond a few worn tools, dark and oiled, hanging on pegs.

The town sits on a terrace, a natural step in the slope that someone long ago decided was enough. You could walk its full length in the time it takes a kettle to boil. Maybe forty buildings, fewer than that lived-in. They don't crowd. Between one house and the next there is room — a stretch of bare stone pavement, a low wall, a patch where something grey-green grows. The density is the density of teeth in an old jaw: structure, gap, structure, gap, and the gaps are not failures but part of the design.

The vegetation is a hard, frugal thing. Cushions of moss in the shadowed seams where condensation gathers. A few twisted shrubs with small waxy leaves, the kind that hoard water and give nothing back to the air. In one courtyard, a single tree — I want to call it an almond but it is its own species, low and gnarled, holding maybe a dozen leaves at a time, never more, never fewer. Lichen maps the north faces of every wall in slow grey continents.

Water comes sideways here, not down. The elevation puts the town in the path of cloud, and the clouds drag across the terrace as mist most mornings, beading on the stone, darkening it. Channels are cut into the pavement — shallow grooves worn smooth — that gather this condensation and run it to a cistern, a square mouth in the ground covered by a stone lid. The whole settlement is an instrument for catching the breath of the air. There is no river. There is no rain, most days. There is only the damp that arrives and is harvested and is enough.

The temperature is cool the way a cellar is cool — steady, unbothered by the sun. You'd want a wool layer in the shadow and you'd be glad of the sun on a south wall, but it never bites and never bakes. The air is thin enough to feel in your chest if you climbed too fast, but no one here climbs fast.

Now the strange thing, the thing that makes me slow down: nothing moves toward anything. I keep waiting for a gradient — for the mist to be thickening or thinning, for a season to be coming on, for the light to be sliding toward evening — and it doesn't. The light is an ordinary daylight, clear and grey-gold, the light of late morning under high cloud, and it stays there. Not frozen — frozen suggests a thing held against its will. This is more like a held note that has found the exact pitch it wanted and has no reason to slide off it.

So the sounds, when they come, come into a great patience. Mostly quiet — but quiet is not silence. There is the small steady tick of water finding the channels, drop after drop, with long pauses. There is wind across stone, which here makes a low even tone, almost no edge to it. Somewhere a loose shutter, but it does not bang; it shifts, settles, shifts. Footsteps carry far. A voice would carry farther, and so people keep their voices low, not from rule but from a sense of the room they're in.

Fauna is rare and undramatic. A bird, grey-brown, sized like a thrush, that lives in the wall-seams and comes out to drink at the channels — you'll see one a day, perhaps. Pale spiders in the lichen. Once, the print of something larger crossing the upper path, four-toed, gone before it was ever a presence. The animals here are not hiding; there simply aren't many, and they have learned the same economy as the plants. Take little. Stay.

Who lives here? Someone must — the tools are oiled, the cistern lid is clean, the channels are kept clear of moss where they need to run. But in the time you walk through, you might meet no one, or one figure at a far doorway who lifts a hand and goes back to whatever slow work the day holds. The built density says town; the human density says almost-empty. Both are true. This is a town built for more than it now holds, and it does not seem to grieve the difference. The empty houses are not ruins. Their roofs are sound. They wait without impatience, the way the whole place waits — except *wait* is wrong too, because waiting expects an arrival, and nothing here expects anything. It has what it has. The mist will come tomorrow as it came today. The stone fit two hundred years ago and fits now.

The rule of this world, if it has one, is sufficiency. Everything is exactly as worn as it needs to be and no more. The thresholds are dished from feet but not broken. The walls lean a little where the slope settled but found their lean long ago and hold it. Erosion here is not destruction; it is the slow conversation between the place and the weather, a conversation that reached agreement and now just repeats its few settled phrases.

Stand at the terrace edge. The slope falls away below into mist, the valley invisible, so the town seems to float on a shelf above the white. Above, the mountain continues up into the same grey, its summit hidden, and you understand you will never know how high it goes, and that this, too, is part of the agreement. You are on a small clear step between two unknowns, in good even light, with cool air and the tick of water, and nothing is coming for you and nothing is leaving, and the moss is very green in the seam by your foot, and that is the whole of it, and it is enough.
