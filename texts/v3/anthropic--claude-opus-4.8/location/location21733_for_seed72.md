# The Sump Works

Go down. Not far — the stair is only forty steps, and they're shallow, worn into a soft pale stone that sweats under your hand. By the tenth step the daylight is already a memory above you, narrowing to a coin of brightness, and by the twentieth it's gone entirely and the air changes. It thickens. It gets warm the way a mouth is warm.

This is a place below the surface, hot and damp, a town's worth of construction wedged into a hollow that was never finished — by water, by hands, by whatever began it. It is small. You could walk the whole of it in the time it takes a kettle to boil, if you could find your way, which you can't, not the first time.

## The Stone

The rock here is young. That's the wrong word for stone, I know, but it's the true one. It hasn't decided what it is yet. In places it's barely consolidated — a paste of lime and grit you could press a thumbprint into, that holds the print like dough. Elsewhere it has half-hardened into something with the grain of bone. Water seeps through all of it, everywhere, beading on the ceilings and running in thin bright threads down the pillars, pooling in the low places where the floor hasn't agreed to be a floor.

Nothing here is eroded because nothing here is old enough to erode. The caverns aren't worn into shape; they're *growing* into shape, slowly, the way a wound closes. Flowstone creeps over the brickwork. A stalactite the length of a finger has formed over the lintel of a doorway, and below it, on the threshold, a matching nub of stone rising to meet it — they'll be a column in a thousand years, and they'll have swallowed the door.

## The Built Thing

Because there *are* doors. This is the strange marriage of the place: somebody built a town down here, an industrial town, before the cave had finished being a cave.

There's a logic to it once you see it. Brick channels and lead pipes, iron sluice-gates green with corrosion, the squat housings of pumps. Catwalks of riveted steel span the wet places. Furnaces — cold now, or banked low, breathing a dim orange through their grates — sit in a row like sleeping animals. Boilers. A great wheel half-submerged in a sump, turning so slowly you have to watch it against a fixed point to be sure it moves at all. Pipework climbs the walls in bundles, sweating, ticking, occasionally letting go a single drop into a pan with a sound like a struck bell.

It was a works for handling the water. That much is clear. Drawing it up, pushing it through, keeping the hollow from drowning. Whether anyone still tends it is harder to say. The machines run at a pace that doesn't need much tending — a gradual, patient laboring, gauges drifting across their faces over hours, the whole apparatus more like a slow tide than an engine.

The light comes from the furnace-grates and from a scatter of caged lamps, and it's dim, gold-brown, the color of weak tea. Enough to work by if you knew the work. Enough to throw long shadows that don't move.

## The Living

And now the thing you'll have noticed before any of the rest, because it's everywhere, because it is the actual *weather* of this place:

The animals.

Not large ones. Things that thrive in warm wet dark. The catwalk rails are furred with the soft bodies of moths, layer on layer, more arriving and departing in a constant slow exchange. The pipes are clotted with snails the size of plums, ticking their way across the iron, scraping the algae that the lamplight feeds. Pale crickets cover the walls so thickly the brick looks alive, looks like it's *breathing* — and they sing, all of them, a wall of dry shrilling that never stops, that rises and falls in patches as one stand of them quiets and another takes it up.

Below, in the warm sumps, the water boils with small fish, with shrimp gone milk-white and blind, with things that are mostly leg. Bats hang in the high dark, not many, but their droppings feed everything, and that is the secret economy of the place: the bats bring the surface down here, the crickets and snails eat what the bats leave, the fish eat what falls in the water, and the whole hot hollow runs on it.

The sound of them all together is what's loud here. Not the machines — the machines are nearly silent, a hum, a drip, the groan of the great wheel. It's the *fauna* that fills the air to bursting: the shrill of crickets, the dry storm of moth-wings, the wet plip and slither, the squeak from above, all of it layered into one continuous pressure against your ears, loud as rain on a tin roof, and as constant.

But here's the mercy of the place, the reason it isn't unbearable: it is *sparse*. For all the swarming life and all the machinery, there is so much empty room between things. Long dark reaches of wet stone with nothing in them. A chamber with a single lamp and a single dripping pipe and nothing else, the cricket-song coming muffled through the wall. You move through pools of crowdedness and pools of emptiness, and the emptiness is what lets you breathe.

The vegetation is almost nothing — what light there is can't sustain much. A few mosses around the lamps, livid green. Pale threads of fungus on the old timber. A mat of something slick and dark on every wet surface, eaten back wherever the snails have grazed. It is barely plant at all, more a faint green idea of plant, persisting because the heat and water never let up.

## The Rules

Time runs slow here. That's the law of the place. Everything that changes, changes over hours or years — the wheel turns, the stone grows, the gauges drift, the animals make their patient circuits. Nothing is sudden. If a pipe is going to burst it has been getting ready to burst since before you came and will not finish before you go. You can stand still and be confident the world will hold its shape around you, and that's a comfort, in the warm dark, with the singing.

It is hot. Always hot. The water steams faintly off the sumps, and the steam beads on the cool iron and runs back down, and is pumped up, and seeps back through the unfinished stone, and is hot again. The cave breathes its own wet breath in a circle that never ends and never hurries.

Stay too long and you'll forget the coin of daylight at the top of the stairs. The crickets will sound like silence. The heat will feel like your own skin. You'll start to understand the slow machines, the way you understand a sleeping body beside you — not in words, just in the rhythm.

That's when it's time to go back up the forty shallow steps, before the place finishes deciding what you are.
