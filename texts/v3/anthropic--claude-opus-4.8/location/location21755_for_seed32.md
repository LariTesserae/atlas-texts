There is a room. You would have to call it a room, though no one built it as one and no one will ever stand in it as one. It is roughly the size of a chapel, and it is made entirely.

Everything here was made, but not recently, and not by anyone who left a name. The walls are constructed from something between bone-china and dried river-clay — fired, glazed in places, fitted together so tightly that you cannot find the seams without running a fingernail along them, and even then the fingernail is unsure. There is no natural substrate anywhere. Lift any tile and beneath it is another tile, and beneath that, another. It is made all the way down. This is simply the condition of the place; it does not announce itself or feel uncanny once you accept it. The room rests in its own madeness the way an old kitchen rests in the smell of itself.

It is dry. Profoundly dry — not the cracked dryness of a thing that lost its water, but the settled dryness of a thing that never had any, that runs on some other chemistry entirely. The dryness is cool. Cool the way the inside of a porcelain jar is cool, the way a deep pantry holds the morning's chill until noon. There is no draft to carry the cold; the temperature simply sits, even and unbothered, an equilibrium reached so long ago that the word "reached" no longer applies.

And it is packed. This is the first thing the body understands and the last it gets used to. There is no empty space in the room. Every cubic inch holds something — surface meeting surface, form pressed against form, the made things crowding one another like crystals grown until they touched on all sides. You do not move through this place so much as you are already among it, embedded in it, a presence wedged between other presences. The air itself feels occupied. Saturated. Full to the lip.

What fills it: shelving, mostly, though shelving is too plain a word. Tiers of fired clay risers, scalloped and fluted, climbing every wall and folding back in on themselves, leaving no flat untouched, no recess unfurnished. And growing from them — sparse but real, the only living thing here — a vegetation that has made its slow accommodation with all this construction. Pale stalks. Hard, dry, succulent-looking growths in the seams between tiles. They are not green; greenness needs water and there is none. They are the color of unglazed bisque, of old ivory, of the inside of a shell, and they hold their moisture-that-is-not-moisture in fat translucent joints. A dozen of them, no more, in the whole room. Each one rooted in a crack it has had a thousand years to settle into. None of them growing anymore. None of them dying. They have arrived at their final shapes and stopped, the way a candle flame would stop if you could freeze it.

It is bright. This is the strangeness that takes longest to locate, because there is no sun, no lamp, no window, no source you can point to. The light comes from the made surfaces themselves — the fired clay glows, faintly and constantly, an even white-gold radiance with no direction and therefore no shadows. Everything is lit from everywhere. The pale stalks cast nothing. Your own hand, held up, would cast nothing. The brightness is total and sourceless and it does not flicker. It is one more thing that simply *is*, the way the cold is, the way the fullness is.

And it is loud.

You would not expect it. A sealed, dry, still, unchanging room should be silent — but it roars. The sound comes from the same place the light does: from the made things, from the tight-fitted tiles and the fluted risers. It is a sustained, layered ringing, the sound a wet finger draws from a glass rim, except there is no finger and the glass is the whole room and the note never stops. Many notes, actually — a packed chord, as crowded as the space itself, every surface singing its own pitch and all of them holding, holding, holding. It is not painful but it is enormous. It fills the ears the way the construction fills the room. You feel it in the teeth, in the long bones. It has been sounding since before the stalks took root and it will sound after they are dust, and it does not rise or fall.

Because nothing here rises or falls. That is the deepest rule of the place. It does not change. There is no weather, no season, no day, no decay, no growth. The dryness will not relent and the cold will not deepen and the light will not dim and the chord will not break. Whatever erosion shaped this — and something did; the scalloped edges are worn soft, the glazes are crazed with the fine map of great age, the risers have settled into one another like old teeth — whatever wore it down finished its work an unmeasurable time ago and then stopped, leaving the room weathered into perfect balance. Settled. In its own state. A thing that has finished happening.

There are no animals. There never were. Nothing scuttles in the seams, nothing nests in the high tiers, nothing has ever needed the stalks for anything. The room does not host life so much as permit, in twelve small places, life's stubborn arrival. The stalks ask nothing of it and receive the cool dry sourceless brightness as their portion, and persist.

If you were small enough — and perhaps you are, perhaps this whole chapel-sized room is itself wedged inside something larger, embedded the way a chamber is embedded in a geode, a pocket inside a vaster madeness you cannot see the edges of — if you were small enough you might find that the fluting of a single riser is its own landscape, tiers within tiers, and that the bisque-pale stalk above you is a tree, and the sourceless gold a whole sky, and the unending chord the only weather there will ever be.

It is a complete place. It needs nothing. It is going nowhere. It has been singing in the dry gold light, packed full of itself, for longer than there have been words for *long*, and the sound and the light and the cold are all one continuous fact, held, and held, and held.
