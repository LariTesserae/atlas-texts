There is a hollow worn into the shoulder of a high mountain, and it is dark, and it does not change.

Begin with the size, because that is the first thing the body would understand: this is a small place. You could cross it in forty paces if there were light enough to walk straight, which there is not. It is a basin perhaps the width of a modest hall, ringed by rock that leans inward overhead — not a cave, not quite, but a place the open sky has nearly forgotten. A slot of deeper dark above lets in no stars. What light exists has no clear source. It is the grey that lives behind closed eyelids, enough to know that surfaces are there, not enough to know their color.

The rock is old past reckoning. Every edge that could be sharp was rounded away long before anything that now lives here was born. The stone is worn into soft swells and runnels, like the inside of a thing that has held moving water for a very long time and then stopped. It is smooth and it is always wet. Not flowing — weeping. Water stands in every hollow of the stone and slides in slow films down the walls and gathers at the basin's low center into a black pool that never rises and never falls. The cool comes up off this water. It is not cold enough to harm, but it settles on the skin and stays, a temperature like the underside of a stone you lift in early spring.

Things grow here, and this is where the place stops being simple.

The walls are packed — no bare stone shows where anything could take hold. Mosses lie in deep wet beds, dark green going to near-black in the dimness. Liverworts overlap them in flat tongues. Ferns of a pale, almost colorless kind unfurl from every crack, and between them grow fungi — not the bright caps of sunlit woods but pale fans and threads and soft luminous nubs, the only true light the basin makes for itself. A faint cold glow, blue-green, hangs in patches on the rotting fern-stems and along the rims of the pool. It is light you can see by only after the eyes have given up expecting more. By that light the moss looks like the floor of the sea.

The density is the strange thing. There is no empty space. Every surface holds growth, and into every space the growth leaves, something moves.

The fauna is overwhelming and almost silent. This is the contradiction the place is built around. The walls *seethe* — pale cave-crickets clustered thick as moss themselves, blind salamanders the color of wet paper draped in the rock-runnels, translucent shrimp turning in the black pool, springtails in clouds so dense they shift the texture of a surface when they move. Snails with shells gone to glass. Moths that have forgotten flight clinging in shingled rows. There are so many living things here that the boundary between *creature* and *surface* fails — you cannot say where the moss ends and the bodies begin. And yet it is quiet. Almost nothing here has a voice. The crickets do not sing. The only sound is water: the slow tick of a drop falling, the soft seam of a film sliding, the pool taking a drip without a splash. Beneath that, if you hold still long enough, a sound like very faint rain that is only the thousand small movements of the thousand small things, too soft to call noise.

There is one made thing, or nearly one. Near the pool's edge, half-sunk in moss, lie a few flakes of worked stone — a scraper, a broken blade, the leavings of a hand that knapped them and went away. They are old but not ancient. Someone small came here once, by what path no one could say, and shaped a tool by feel in the dark, and left. The moss has not yet swallowed them. It is taking its time.

The rule of this place is that nothing here advances. There is no season, because no sun reaches in to turn one. There is no growth toward anything — the moss does not climb, the pool does not seek a level it has not already found, the creatures breed and die at exactly the rate that holds their number steady, so that the seething never thins and never spills. It is a place arrived at its final form and stopped there, complete. Erosion has done its work and rests. The water moves but the water-level does not. Everything moves and nothing changes. To enter it would be to step into a single held moment that has been held so long it has forgotten it was ever a moment, that thinks of itself, if it thinks at all, as simply the way things are.

The faint weirdness is only this: that so much life makes so little sound, and that none of it is going anywhere. You would stand in the cool dark, listening to the rain that is not rain, watching the cold glow that gives no warmth, and feel that the place was not waiting for you and would not notice you leave — that it had completed itself before the first creature with a name came up the mountain, and would still be exactly this long after the last one forgot the way.
