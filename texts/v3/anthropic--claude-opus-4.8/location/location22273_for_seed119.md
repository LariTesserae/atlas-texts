# The Cistern Quarter

Everything here was built, and built by hand, and built a long time ago — long enough that the building has become a kind of geology. There is no ground that someone did not lay. There is no rock that was not first a brick.

Begin with the floor, because the floor is the first lie the place tells you. It looks like stone worn smooth by a river, but it is brick — fired clay laid in herringbone, each unit no larger than a hand, and there are so many of them, set so long ago, that the mortar has dissolved and reformed and dissolved again until the whole surface reads as a single weeping skin. Water sits in the low courses. It is always sitting there. The bricks near the surface of the water have gone soft and dark and grow a fur of black-green that squeaks underfoot. This is not a place that was flooded. This is a place built to hold water at exactly this height, by people who understood drains, and the drains are still working, somewhere, swallowing and refilling in slow tidal breaths you can feel through the soles of your feet.

The scale is small. You could cross the whole quarter in the time it takes a kettle to boil. But it does not feel small, because it is so empty. The vaults overhead — and they are vaults, brick arches springing from squat piers, the architecture of a place meant to bear enormous weight — rise into a dimness that holds almost nothing. Vast air. Cathedral volume over hamlet floor. You stand in a room the size of a granary and there is one pier, then thirty feet of nothing, then another pier, and the spaces between the presences are the loudest thing about the place.

## The light

Dim, and it comes from below as much as above. The standing water catches what little falls through the oculi — round holes punched in the vault-crowns, deliberate, set there by the makers to let in sky — and throws it back up against the curved brick in trembling nets. So the light moves. Always. Ceilings ripple. A pier seems to breathe because the water at its foot is breathing the light onto it. The actual sky, glimpsed through the oculi, is the flat pewter of just-before-rain, and it is always just before rain, and the rain comes, and stops, and the oculi drip for an hour afterward in a hundred separate rhythms.

## Sound

This is the thing you'd notice first, before your eyes adjusted. It is *loud* — not with any one sound but with the architecture's gift for amplification. Brick vaults are instruments. Every drip from every oculus arrives as a struck note, and the curves gather them and pass them around the room so that a single drop becomes a rumor of itself in four places. The water answers the water. Somewhere a steady overflow pours from one course to a lower one, a small permanent waterfall, and it sets a drone under everything. And the fauna — the fauna are everywhere, and they will not be quiet.

## The fauna

Here is where the place fills up, because the air and the brick are empty but the living things are not. The water is thick with them. Pale newt-things with external gills like wet ferns, so many that the shallows seem to simmer. They have no eyes — why would they, here — and they navigate by the sound of dripping, swarming toward any new note in the water, which means that to splash is to be instantly surrounded by a hundred soft blind questing bodies.

Above the water: the criers. I want to call them frogs and they are not frogs. They cling to the undersides of the vaults in the dim, flat-bodied, the color of wet brick so you never see them, and they call — a sound between a struck pipe and a child's hum — and the vault carries it and they call back along the carried note, so the whole ceiling sings antiphonally, a hundred unseen throats answering an echo of themselves. It never resolves. It never stops. When the rain comes through the oculi they go briefly mad with it, and the singing climbs until the bricks themselves seem to ring.

And there are the long things in the drains. You hear them more than see them — a heavy wet sliding through the swallowing dark below the floor, periodic, patient. They are why the newts swarm to noise: noise, here, sometimes means the drain-things are hunting, and the newts have learned to gather where it's loud because the drain-things hunt the silent edges. The whole ecology runs on acoustics. Predator and prey conduct their war by ear in the absolute certainty of dark water.

## The strangeness

The makers built this to hold water and they built it to *be heard*, and they left instructions, though no one is left to read them. On the piers, at the height a standing person's hand would rest, the brick is incised — not writing exactly, but tally-grooves, and the grooves are tuned. Drag a wet finger along a pier and it sings a tone, and each pier sings a different tone, and there are exactly enough piers to make a scale that means something to the criers overhead, who answer it. The makers spoke to the ceiling-singers. The makers and the singers had an arrangement, conducted in stone and gill and throat, and the arrangement outlived the makers, and now the building keeps up its half of the conversation by accident, every time a drop falls or a current shifts a tile.

That is the rule of this world: *nothing here is silent, and silence is the only danger.* The place was made loud on purpose, by hands that knew what lived in the quiet below.

## How it persists

Nothing is growing, almost. Vegetation is a few mats of the squeaking fur, a few pale rushes rooted in a cracked course where windblown soil collected over centuries, leaning toward an oculus. The plants are an afterthought; this is a built world, an animal world, a water world, not a green one. What sustains it is the cool and the wet and the patient working of erosion that long ago found its balance — the bricks soften no further, the drains clog no worse, the water holds its line. It has been exactly this damp, this cool, this loud, this empty and this full, for a very long time. It is not decaying. It arrived, ages back, at the form it wanted, and there it rests: a small loud breathing room of made stone and standing water, singing to itself in the dim, full of blind swimmers and unseen criers, waiting out the rain that always comes and never finishes.
