There is a city here, but it would be more honest to say there is a forest that learned to manufacture.

Start with the substrate, because everything else grows out of it. The ground is not soil in the ordinary sense — it is a deep mat of living and half-living plant matter, layered down over so long that the lower strata have compressed into something like wood, like peat, like the dark grain of a violin. You can walk on it. It gives slightly, the way a forest floor gives, but it does not rot, because nothing here truly dies all at once. The whole place is one slow green organism that has been settling into its own shape for a very long time, and the settling is finished. This is what "established" means here: not ruined, not old, but resolved. A landscape that has stopped arguing with itself.

The scale is small — you could walk across the inhabited part in an afternoon. Think of a single deep grove, a hollow no wider than a town, bounded by walls of growth on every side. And within that boundary, the plants have done the work that brick and steel do elsewhere.

The buildings are grown. Not metaphorically — literally cultivated, trained, coppiced into towers. The structures stand at the density of a real town, two and three storeys, with what would be streets between them, but they are sparse against the volume of the place; there is much air, much vertical emptiness, long sightlines between one green tower and the next. The walls are interwoven trunks, the floors are pressed fiber gone hard as oak, the windows are gaps where the growth was persuaded not to close. Sap runs in channels inside the walls. The whole town is a single coordinated stand of vegetation that has been shaped, over generations, into the form of a town.

Now the strange part, the part that makes this a place and not merely a garden: it is industrial. There are engines. The vegetation does work. Somewhere in the lower town the great trunks turn — slow wooden flywheels, geared root-systems, bladders of sap that fill and empty under pressure and drive shafts of polished heartwood. The plants have been bred into machinery and the machinery is alive. You hear it before you understand it: a low, organic creaking, the sigh of bellows that are also lungs, the patient *tock* of a wooden escapement somewhere counting out a rhythm that is half clockwork and half heartbeat. This is a factory, but it runs on growth and turgor and the long muscular movements of things reaching toward light. There is no fire here. Nothing burns. The temperature is cool, persistently cool, and the air smells of green pith and cold water and crushed stem.

Water is scarce and precious and everywhere managed. It does not rain often. Instead the place lives on condensation — the cool air against the cool fiber makes beads, runnels, slow drips collected in grown gutters and stored in the swollen reservoir-trunks that loom at the town's edges like cisterns. You hear water more than you see it: a faint ticking of droplets, a distant trickle through interior channels, the occasional swallowing gulp of a pressure-bladder taking on its fill. When the rare rain does come it is an event — the whole grove drinks, the engines speed up, the creaking rises to something almost like song, and then it fades back into the quiet.

And it is quiet. That is the dominant fact of being here. For all the machinery, this is a hushed place. The engines are slow and soft. There are few animals — a rare beetle the size of a fist, lacquered and slow, that the people seem to tolerate; a thin pale bird, seen maybe once a day, that does not sing. Fauna is almost an afterthought. The motion of the place is botanical, not animal: tendrils reaching across the span of an hour, valves opening, the imperceptible turning of the great wheels. If you stand still you will feel that everything is moving, but slower than you, on a different clock — the clock of the escapement in the lower town, the *tock* that organizes the whole organism's day.

The light is ordinary, even, filtered green-gold through the canopy that roofs the grove. Not bright, not dim. Daylight with the edge taken off it. It moves through the day the way real daylight does, and the town responds — the engines have a rhythm tied to the angle of the sun, faster at midday when the plant-machines have power to spend, drowsy at dawn and dusk. This is the most active thing about the place: not noise, not crowds, but cycles. Light-cycles, water-cycles, the slow tidal breathing of growth and rest. A dozen overlapping processes, all of them patient, all of them turning.

Who lives here? Hard to say, and I think the place prefers it that way. There are dwellings, lit from within by something cool and phosphor-pale — not flame, perhaps a cultivated luminescence in the wall-fibers. There are tools left at the engines. There are paths worn smooth. But the streets are nearly empty when you walk them, and the work seems to go on without supervision, the way a body's organs work without being watched.

The whole impression is of a single quiet creature that has dreamed itself into the shape of a working town and then half-fallen asleep inside the dream, still breathing, still pumping, still drinking the cold dew — settled, finished, in balance, ticking softly toward nothing in particular, for as long as the water keeps condensing on its cool green walls.
