There is a city inside the chest of something, and the something is a plant.

Let me start smaller than that, because the place is small. You could cross it in a long breath if you were the size that fits here. It is a pocket — a single swollen node along the stem of a moss the way a knuckle is a single swelling of a finger. Inside this node the moss has grown a hollow, and inside the hollow there is a town. Begin there.

The walls are not stone. They are the inner wall of the plant, a pale green-grey flesh that weeps. Water stands near the surface of everything; press a thumb to the wall and it beads up cold and comes away wet. The temperature is the temperature of a cellar in early spring before anything has warmed — cold enough that breath would fog if there were enough light to see breath fog, and there is not. This is a dark place. Not the dark of night, which has a sky behind it; the dark of being inside, where the only light is the faint blue-green self-light the moss gives off at its growing tips, a phosphor so dim it shows you edges and nothing else. You learn the town by edge and by feel and by sound.

The town itself is built — genuinely built, with hands, by something with hands. Low structures pressed against the curving walls, made of the materials at hand, which is to say made of plant: dried stem-fiber lashed with stem-fiber, plates of pressed leaf cured stiff, beams of the woody rib that runs through the older growth. There are streets. There is an order to it, the unmistakable grain of organized construction — a place where one path is for water and one path is for walking and the two do not cross by accident. Cisterns catch the weeping wall. Channels carry the overflow away down into the dark where the node narrows. Someone here knows how to make a roof shed water, knows how to splint a beam, knows how to weave. Hand-tools, knotted cord, scrapers of hardened seed-coat. No metal that I can find. No fire either — you cannot have fire inside the wet green lung of a living thing, the air is too damp and the host too close, and so the cold goes uncontested and the dark goes unbroken except by the moss-glow and by the little lamps they tend, which are not flame.

The lamps are animals.

Here is where the place tips over into its real character, because this is not a town of people scattered thin in a hollow. The fauna is total — past total, overwhelming, the dominant fact of the world. The town is sparse: much space between the buildings, lots of standing dark and empty channel, a low scatter of construction. But the animals fill all of it. They are the population, the traffic, the weather, the economy.

The lamps are a soft-bodied creature the size of a fist that climbs the walls and glows when it is content; the townsfolk keep them in baskets and feed them, and a well-tended lamp-beast is brighter than a poorly-tended one, and a frightened one goes black. So the brightness of a street tells you the mood of its livestock. Whole districts dim when something passes through that the animals fear.

And things pass through. The node is connected — at its narrow ends it opens into the channels that run through the whole stem of the host plant, and through those channels comes the migration. This is the active part, the part always in motion: tides of small life moving through the dark on their own seasons. Translucent grazers that browse the inner wall and have to be fenced off the cisterns. Long jointed things that travel the water-paths and are neither hunted nor friendly. Clouds of something too small to see that you only know by the sound — and the sound is most of what there is.

It is a quiet place, mostly. Quiet the way a held breath is quiet. But the quiet is textured all through with the small noises of overwhelming life: the wet click of grazers feeding, a constant nearly-below-hearing rustle that is the moss growing — and the moss is young, freshly formed, still sharp at its edges, still putting out new node-hollows faster than the town can settle them, so the rustle has urgency in it. Drip. The far-off chitter of the migration coming or going through the stem. The papery breath of the lamp-beasts. Now and then, from deep in the host, a long slow groan as the whole plant shifts its weight, and everyone in the town stops and waits for it to pass, because they live inside something alive and they have not forgotten it.

The rules of this world are the rules of being a guest inside a body. You do not block the water-channels, because the host needs to move its water and a town that floods its host gets flooded in turn. You do not cut the wall too deep. You read the animals, because the animals know things first — they feel the host's distress, the coming surge, the predator in the channel, long before any person could. To live here is to live by the moods of beasts and the moods of a plant, neither of which speaks, both of which decide everything. The strangeness of it has gone ordinary to the people who live here. They were born in the cold wet glow. They have never seen the outside of the leaf they live in. They think a person who needs fire to see is to be pitied.

And above the town — though up and down are loose words inside a curving hollow — the wall climbs into the dark and disappears, and somewhere up there the node opens to the world the moss lives in: a marsh-edge, post-rain, the cold air condensing, an enormous sodden green country of which this whole town is a single closed bead too small to notice. Sometimes a drop of outside water finds its way down the stem and falls through the hollow, vast as a thrown stone, and the lamp-beasts flare at the disturbance, and for a moment the whole town is lit blue-green and you can see all of it at once — the streets, the cisterns, the lashed roofs glistening, the slow rivers of small animals — before the drop strikes a channel and the glow gutters back down and the dark closes over the place again.
