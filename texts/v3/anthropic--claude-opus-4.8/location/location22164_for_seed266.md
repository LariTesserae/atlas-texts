# The High Dry Works

Start at altitude. Not a mountain peak — there is no peak, no summit you can point to and say *there, that is the top.* The land simply keeps rising until it stops being land and becomes the thing the air is too thin to fully cover. You are very high. The pressure here is the pressure of a place that the planet's atmosphere has nearly given up on, and yet the temperature is warm — a dry, persistent warmth that does not come from the sun, which sits pale and far in a hard sky, but from below. From the rock itself.

So: heat from beneath, thin air above, no water anywhere.

## The Terrain

The ground is a mineral the color of old brass darkened to bronze, banded in places with seams of something paler — a chalky cream that crumbles to powder under any weight. The whole region has been worn into long, parallel ridges, like the corrugations of a vast washboard, except no water made these. There was never water here to make them. The erosion is wind and heat and time — the patient grinding of a place that has existed in roughly this state for an unfathomable span and has come to rest in its own logic.

The ridges run for what must be hundreds of kilometers. Between them lie flats of fine bronze grit, smooth as poured floors, occasionally cracked into polygons by the daily expansion and contraction of the heat. Walking one of those flats, you would feel the warmth come up through your soles. You would raise dust with every step, and the dust would hang — the thin air can barely move it, can barely carry sound, can barely do anything quickly.

This is a slow world. Gradients are weak. Nothing rushes.

## The Built Things

And yet it is settled. Inhabited. Worked.

Across the ridges, scattered with great distances between them, stand the Works — clusters of construction at the scale of small towns, built into and out of the bronze rock. Each cluster sits in the lee of a major ridge, sheltered from the prevailing dry wind. They are industrial places: foundries, condensers, towers of riveted plate gone the color of the land through long exposure. Pipes run between buildings on stilted trestles. Chimneys stand cold or breathe slow plumes of pale vapor that the high thin air pulls flat and feathers out for kilometers.

What do they make here, with no water and no growing thing?

They distill the heat. The warmth from below is the resource. Deep shafts descend into the bronze, and the Works draw up not water but a kind of dense warm gas that pools in the lower strata — a heavy, faintly sweet vapor that the machinery condenses, separates, and renders into pale waxy solids stacked in yards under the open sky. This is the harvest. The whole economy of the high dry works runs on warmth made solid.

The construction is layered — newer rivetwork over older masonry over older cuttings still, generations of building atop building, all weathered to the same patient bronze-and-cream. You could read the age of a Work in its layers the way you read a tree, except nothing here grows in rings. It accretes. It settles. It comes to rest.

The settlements are urban in their density of structure but sparse across the land. Hours of travel between them. The trestle-pipes are the threads that hold the scattered Works into something like a single civilization.

## The Sound

It is mostly quiet. This matters.

The thin air carries little. A hammer-strike at the foundry reaches you as a soft tap, robbed of its edge. The condensers hum, but the hum is felt more than heard — a vibration through the rock and the soles, a low patience. Wind moves over the corrugated ridges and produces a long, low tone, the way breath moves across a bottle's mouth, but vast and slow and rarely changing pitch. You could stand for an hour and hear three sounds: the rock-hum, the ridge-tone, and your own breath, loud in your ears because nothing competes with it.

When the Works vent, there is a sigh. Just a sigh, drawn long.

## The Ecology

And there is life. Abundant, even — though you would not call it that at first, because it does not match.

The fauna here are not animals of flesh as you would expect. They are creatures of the warm gas and the bronze — slow-bodied things, plated and dense, that move along the ridges grazing not on plants (there are none) but on the films of mineral that the heat deposits on exposed faces overnight. Picture something between a tortoise and a slow furnace. Their shells are the same bronze as the ground; at rest they are invisible, just another swell in the corrugation. They move at the pace of shadows. A herd of them crossing a flat would take a full day to cross, and you might not notice they had moved until you saw the dust-trail behind.

They are abundant. Look long enough at any ridge and it resolves into dozens of them, scattered, spaced, browsing the deposits. The Works tolerate them, even depend on them — the grazers concentrate certain minerals in their plating, and the workers harvest the shed plates the way one might collect eggs.

Smaller things live in the cracks of the polygon-flats — quick by the standards of this world, which is to say they cross a hand's width in a minute. They glint. They feed on the dust. At night, when the pale sun is gone and the warmth from below glows faintly in the dark of the deeper shafts, these small things gather at the shaft-mouths in their thousands and turn the openings into pools of slow shimmer.

## The Strangeness

Here is what unsettles, once you have been here a while.

The warmth has direction. Not just up from below — it *flows*, in slow rivers you cannot see but can feel as you walk, a current of slightly-warmer air that bends around the ridges and pools in the hollows. The grazers follow these currents. So do the workers. Over centuries the warm-rivers have shifted their courses, and the Works have shifted with them — you can find abandoned Works on ridges the warmth has left, perfectly preserved in the dry air, cold now, waiting. The living Works sit where the warmth presently runs.

And the warmth seems to *prefer* certain paths. It returns to old channels. It avoids others for no reason anyone has measured. The oldest workers speak of the warmth as though it had intention, a slow vast intention measured in generations, and they are not entirely wrong, and no one can prove them right.

The vapor that the Works distill, when it is fresh and still warm, holds a faint glow and a fainter scent, and those who breathe too much of it report dreams of the deep strata — of descending, of the rock opening into chambers no shaft has reached, of the source of the warmth being not heat but something that wants to keep being warm.

## The Whole of It

So hold it all together: a vast bronze highland at the edge of breathable air, warm from beneath, dry beyond dryness, worn smooth and corrugated by patient ages of wind. Scattered industrial towns that mine warmth and render it solid, connected by thin trestled threads across great emptiness. Slow plated grazers the color of the ground, moving like the hour-hand. Quiet — a quiet so complete your own breath is the loudest thing. And under all of it, the warm currents flowing in their ancient channels, going where they have always gone, with what might be patience and might be preference and might, if you breathe the vapor, be something that knows you are there.

A place at rest. A place that has been at rest for a very long time, and intends to stay.
