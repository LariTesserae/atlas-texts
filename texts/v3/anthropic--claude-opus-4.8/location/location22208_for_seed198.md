There is a city built into the side of a mountain, and the mountain is mostly gone now, replaced by the city, so that the slope itself is masonry and iron and brick all the way down, terrace below terrace, the way a thing that began as a hillside town becomes, over four or five hundred years, simply a vertical country.

Start with the water, because the water started here first and never left. The air at this elevation is always near its dew point. Cool, thin, perpetually one degree from condensing — so the whole built slope weeps. Brick sweats. Iron beads. Stone darkens at its base and stays dark. There are no rivers exactly, but there is water everywhere: in the runnels cut along every street's edge, in the dripping undersides of the cast-iron galleries, in the green sheet-flow down the retaining walls after the daily fogs roll up the valley and break against the upper terraces. The town drinks its own atmosphere. Cisterns under the lowest levels catch what the high levels shed.

Now the vegetation, which is the strange part, and the not-strange part, because nothing here breaks the rules of an ordinary world — it is only ordinary processes given a few centuries of damp and neglect. Everything green has come up *through* the made surface. Moss first, then liverwort in the joints, then ferns unrolling from the gutters and the cracks behind downpipes, then whole hanging gardens of self-seeded buddleia and elder and birch rooting in the silt that collects on flat roofs and dead machinery. The slope is lush — packed, saturated, no bare surface anywhere — but the lushness is a skin over construction. Pull back a curtain of ivy and there is a window. Push through a thicket on a terrace and you find it grew out of an abandoned loading yard. Vegetation at this density and built at this totality means: a made world being slowly re-upholstered in green, leaf by leaf, with no soil underneath, only brick, only the silt the leaves themselves laid down.

The light is dim and stays dim. Partly the fog, which sits in the valley most of the day and filters the sun to a soft pewter. Partly the canopy of the upper terraces, which overhang the lower ones — gallery above gallery, like a sectioned anthill cut open and tilted to the sky — so the deep streets at the bottom see daylight only as a grey rumor reflected down through light-wells and the gaps between buildings. Lamps are lit, gas or filament, all day, but they are tired and orange and small against the grey. You can always tell roughly how high you are by how much light reaches you.

It is an industrial place. That is the second engine of it, after the weather. Somewhere in the body of the city are works — foundries, pumping stations, the machinery that lifts water and goods up the terraces and lets the spent water down. There are flues threading up through the masonry, chimneys disguised as towers, the warm exhalation of factories felt as a draft in certain stairwells. Pulleys and inclined railways and chain-driven hoists hold the vertical town together; without them the upper levels could not be fed or drained. So there is mechanism everywhere, electrified in patches, steam-driven elsewhere, all of it half-swallowed by the green.

But — and this is what makes the place itself rather than a picture of itself — it is *quiet*. Mostly quiet. You would expect an industrial city to roar. This one murmurs. The fog deadens everything. The vegetation deadens what the fog misses. The great machines run slow and steady and far off, felt more than heard: a low organ-note in the floor, the patient knock of a hoist somewhere two terraces up, the endless trickle and drip and run of water finding its way down. A door closes blocks away and you hear it cleanly because there is nothing else to mask it. The loudest thing is rain on iron, and even that is soft, more sibilance than drum. People here learned to speak low. The town has the acoustics of a cathedral that goes on for a mile downhill.

Fauna is rare and you notice the rarity. You would think the green would bring animals, but the slope is too made, too sheer, too full of metal and weeping stone, and most things never colonized it. So an animal is an event. A single crow on a chimney pot. A cat picking along a parapet. Once in a long while a fox, improbable, threading the dripping galleries at dawn. Birdsong, when it comes, comes singly and travels far in the quiet, and people stop to listen because it is unusual. The dominant life is plant and human and machine; the rest is mostly absent, and its absence is part of the air.

The erosion is old and settled. Nothing here is raw or new-built. Five centuries have rounded every edge that water can round, furred every surface, stained every wall in the long vertical streaks of its own runoff. The town reached its weathered equilibrium generations ago — the moss knows where it grows, the cracks know where they crack, the water knows its channels. It is *in balance*. A place that has finished becoming what it is and now simply persists, mending and shedding at the same rate, neither growing nor dying.

And yet — the last parameter, and the one that turns the whole thing — it is *turbulent*. Not in its substance but in its weather and its water and its sky. The balance is a dynamic balance, held in the middle of constant violence. The fog does not sit; it *moves*, surging up the valley in walls, tearing apart, reforming, so that the view from any terrace changes minute to minute — the opposite slope visible, then gone, then a single far light, then nothing. Squalls come up out of the valley without warning and lash the iron galleries and pour off the terraces in sudden cataracts that fill all the runnels at once, and the machines labor harder, and the chimney-smoke is flattened sideways, and the whole vertical country becomes for an hour a thing of streaming water and driving cloud and groaning hoists — and then it passes, and the dripping resumes, and the town settles back, quiet, weeping, green, dim, into the shape it has held for half a millennium.

That is the place. A drowned slope of brick and iron and leaf, a mile high and a mile long, breathing fog, dressed in moss, lit orange and grey, almost silent, almost empty of anything but plants and people and patient machines — and over it always the churning sky, tearing and remaking the weather, while the city underneath does not change at all.
