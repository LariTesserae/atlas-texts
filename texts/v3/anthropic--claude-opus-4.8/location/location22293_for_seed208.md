# The Saltsong Flats

There is no land here, not really — only the long argument between water and the memory of stone. You arrive at the edge of it and the first thing you understand is that the place has already been everything it will ever be. The mountains that once stood here are gone, ground down across eons into a plain so level that water cannot decide where to go. So it goes everywhere. It lies in sheets a finger deep across miles, and the sky lies down inside it, doubled.

The rock beneath is old past the point of feature. No cliffs, no boulders. The stone has worn itself smooth and pale, the color of a tooth, and where it breaks the surface it does so in low islands and bars rounded like the backs of sleeping things. Touch one and it weeps — water rises through it constantly, beading up from inside, so that nothing is ever dry. The air itself is half water, a cool mist that never quite becomes rain and never quite leaves. Your breath joins it. Everything here is breathing the same wet, dim breath.

Almost nothing grows. A green-black film slicks the lower rocks — not plants, something simpler, older, a stain of life that was here before leaves were invented and saw no reason to change. In the standing water, faint mats drift like dissolved paper. That is the whole of the botany. This is a mineral country, and what life it holds did not come from seeds.

It came swimming, and flying, and crawling.

Because the flats are *loud* — this is the thing you cannot prepare for. The fauna is uncountable. The shallow water teems with soft-bodied swimmers, pale and translucent, finger-length and longer, that pulse through the inch-deep film in shoals so dense they make the water itself seem to move. And above them: the birds, if they are birds. Long-legged, sheet-pale, with throats built for one purpose, which is to scream. They cover the bars in their thousands. They feed by night and they call without cease, and the sound of the flats is a wall — a churning, layered, deafening roar of a million throats and the slap of a million wings on water, so total that you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it in the bones of your face. Conversation is impossible. You learn to point. You learn to read the rolling motion of the flock, which moves like weather, like a second tide.

Beneath the roar, if you press your hand to a weeping rock and feel through your palm, there is a low pulse — the water rising, the stone settling its last increment toward flatness. The whole place is a slow machine of erosion working its final patient turns.

Scattered across the flats, far apart, are the marks of hands. Low cairns of the pale stone, stacked deliberately. A line of stakes driven into a submerged bar, gray and softened, the remains of a fish-weir or a net-frame — woven once with cord that has long since rotted to a brown suggestion in the water. Here and there a single hut-platform, raised on legs above the sheet-water, roofless now, its planks gone silver. Whoever made these worked with stone and bone and fiber, no metal, or so little metal that the wet ate it whole. They came for the swimmers and the eggs of the screaming birds. Some of them, you suspect, are still here, in the seasons when the flock thins. The cairns are not all old. One, you notice, has a fresh stone on top, still wet on its underside where it was lifted from the water this morning.

The light never strengthens. The mist holds the sun to a low pewter glow, and at the flats' edge the doubled sky and the doubled birds make the horizon vanish — you cannot tell where water ends and air begins, and the flock wheeling through that seam looks like it is flying through itself. Cool, wet, dim, level, screaming, ancient. A place that finished becoming itself a long time ago and now simply continues, loudly, going nowhere because there is nowhere left to go.

The rule of this world is patience. The water will win. It already has, mostly. The screaming is just the sound the living make while the stone lies down.
