There is a room inside a machine that has finished.

Not stopped — finished. The distinction matters here. Something can stop and resume; this place has run all the way to the end of what it was for and then kept its shape afterward, the way a riverbed keeps the river's idea long after the water has gone elsewhere. Except the water hasn't gone elsewhere. The water is the only thing left.

Picture a single chamber, no larger than the inside of a freight car, walls of riveted iron gone soft at the corners. Soft is the wrong word for iron and the right word for this iron. Three eons of damp have rounded every edge. The rivets are no longer bolts but blisters, swollen and smooth, more like the knuckles of something grown than something hammered. Run a hand along the wall — you won't, there's no one here, but if you did — and you'd feel no seam, only a continuous gentle topography, the original sharp engineering worn down to the contour of a worn coin, a worn step, a worn tooth. The makers built this to a thousandth of an inch. Time has sanded it to the tolerance of stone.

Everything is made. That's the first rule. There is no floor of earth beneath the floor of metal, no bedrock under the deck plate — if you pried the plate up (you couldn't, it's fused now, mineral with mineral) you'd find another constructed layer, and under that another, all the way down past where down has meaning. This is a built world to its core. But built so long ago that built and natural have stopped being opposites. The iron weeps the way a cliff weeps. The condensation beads on the ceiling and falls in a slow irregular percussion, and that water has been falling along the same paths for so long it has carved channels into the manufactured surface — runnels worn into steel, little deltas of rust-silt collecting in the low places, and from that silt:

green.

This is the second rule, and the one that surprises. The room is lush. Out of every horizontal ledge, every rivet-knuckle that catches the drip, out of the rust-deltas in the corners, vegetation has come up thick and unhurried. Mosses mostly, but mosses gone baroque — fronded, hanging, layered in canopies of their own at the scale of a fingernail. Ferns where the water pools, their roots threaded into the metal's corrosion. Something like ivy with leaves the color of pond-light climbs the standing pipes. Pale fungal shelves step up the warmest wall. The warmth is steady, a held-breath warmth around blood temperature, no source you can point to — it comes from the metal itself, or from the slow chemistry of the rust, a heat with no engine behind it anymore, only memory.

And it is loud. This is the thing you would not expect from a place so still.

The sound is enormous and it never changes. Somewhere — everywhere — there is a roar. Not the roar of an engine running but the roar of an engine's *ghost*: a vast standing resonance, as if the whole machine-world beyond this chamber were a single struck bell that was struck once at the beginning of time and has not finished ringing. It comes up through the deck plate as a bass that you feel in the sternum. It comes down from the ceiling as a high steady scream of pressure through cavities you cannot see. The dripping water plays on top of it, ten thousand tiny clocks, none of them agreeing, all of them ancient. The fronded moss trembles with it. The fern-tips trace tiny circles in the saturated air from the vibration alone. You could not speak here and be heard. You could not hear yourself think the word *quiet*.

Yet nothing moves to make the sound. There is no machine left to roar. The roar is what the room is *for* now — the only labor it still performs, a sound produced by an absence the way a seashell produces the sea. Deafening and empty at once. That is the paradox the place holds, and it holds it easily, has held it forever.

Because the room is nearly empty. Vast space between presences, even at this small scale. The vegetation is thick along the surfaces but the air in the center of the chamber is just air — warm, wet, ringing air, occupied by nothing. A single moth-pale thing might cross it once in a long while; the fauna here is rare to the point of rumor. Perhaps a translucent insect that has learned to live in the condensation, walking the underside of the ceiling on the surface tension of the drops. Perhaps, once, something larger glimpsed at the edge of seeing and never again. Mostly the open volume stays open. Whatever ecology took root here is an ecology of edges and surfaces — life clinging to the made walls — and the middle of the world is reserved for the roar.

The light is ordinary. This too surprises, given everything. It falls in at a normal, daylit, unremarkable strength from no clear opening — perhaps through a grille gone lacy with corrosion, perhaps simply present, the way the heat is present, a settled property of the place. It is good honest light, the light of a workshop window, and it makes the green look green and the rust look red-brown and the standing water look like exactly what it is. Nothing here is mysterious to the eye. The strangeness is all in the *situation*, never in the appearance. A normal-looking room, lit normally, that happens to be the inside of something finished, ringing, growing, and alone.

Nothing will change. This is the last rule and the deepest. The drip will keep its rhythm. The moss will neither advance nor retreat — it reached its equilibrium with the available water and the steady warmth a very long time ago and has been still in the way a flame is still, replacing itself molecule for molecule, holding its exact shape. The rust will not deepen; it has rusted as far as it can. The roar will not rise or fall. There are no gradients here, no seasons, no day moving toward night even as the light stays even. The chamber has arrived at its final form and the final form is *this*: warm, wet, green, loud, lit, empty, made, and old past the meaning of old — a small room at the bottom of a built world, performing the memory of a sound, where the only motion is the falling of water that has already fallen forever.

You could stand in the doorway — there is a doorway, a worn oval where a door was, opening onto more of the same, identical chambers down an identical ringing corridor — and you would understand at once that you had arrived too late for whatever this was, by an amount of time that has no name, and that being too late changed nothing, because there was never anything to be in time *for*. The room was always just this. It is content. It does not need you. It barely registers the air you displace.

And the water keeps falling, into the green, in the noise, forever, in the light.
