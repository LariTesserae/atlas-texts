# The Grown Cathedral at the Roof of the World

Start high. Higher than weather usually bothers to climb — except here the weather is local, made of the place's own breath, and it pools in the great hollows like milk left to stand.

Begin with what holds it up.

There is no rock. I want to be clear about this before anything else, because the mind will keep trying to put rock back. There is no stone substrate, no mountain underneath that the plants merely cling to. The mountain *is* plant. Down through every layer you could dig, down past where roots should give way to mineral, you find only more grown structure — heartwood gone hard as basalt, vascular columns thick as cathedral piers, the laminated and self-knitted mass of something that has been building itself upward for so long that it has the patience and the silhouette of geology. This is the first rule of the world: everything is made, and everything that made it was alive.

So: a vast grown massif, kilometers high, weathered into balance. Its flanks are buttressed the way a fig tree buttresses, in great flowing fins that run from the heights down into cloud. Rain and condensation have rounded every edge over an age. Where you'd expect cliff-faces there are instead smooth weeping walls, dark green-black, slick, with water finding its old familiar channels down and down. The erosion here is settled erosion — the place reached its shape long ago and now only maintains it, the way an old face holds its lines.

## The Emptiness Inside

Now the strange part, the part the parameters insist on and the imagination resists: it is nearly empty.

You would think a thing this total — plant as architecture, plant as planet — would be *dense*, would be choked, jungle-thick, every cubic meter stuffed with leaf and stem. It is the opposite. The grown cathedral is mostly hollow. The living mass has organized itself into enormous vaulted voids — chambers you could lose a city in, naves that run for kilometers between their pillar-walls, atriums open to the dim sky overhead. The plant built *space*. It built emptiness on purpose, the way coral builds the shape of water.

Walk one of these halls and you walk for an hour and meet nothing. The floor is a single continuous sheet of living tissue, faintly warm underfoot, faintly damp, exhaling. The walls rise out of sight. Far apart — vastly apart — single features punctuate the void: a hanging column of fused tendrils descending from a ceiling you cannot see; a pool where the floor has dished and gathered water; a single great bloom, three meters across, breathing slowly in the dark.

The density is the emptiness. That's the signature of this place. A near-void held inside total construction.

## Light and Air

The light is dim and it comes from everywhere and nowhere. Some leaks down through high translucent membranes in the canopy-roof, filtered to the color of weak tea and old jade. Some of it the plant makes itself — a low bioluminescence in the veins of the walls, in the floor's capillaries, a glow you only notice once your eyes have given up waiting for the sun. Enough to move by. Not enough to read a face across a room.

It is warm. Warm and wet together, so the air is always just at the edge of condensing, and in the great cold high chambers it tips over and *does* condense, and you watch fog being born — the place breathing out moisture that hangs, drifts, and falls as the slowest rain, more a settling than a falling.

And it is quiet. This is the thing that surprises people who imagine jungle. There is no roar of life. The sounds are small and far apart, matched to the emptiness: the *tick* of a drop completing its long fall; the deep slow creak of structural wood adjusting under its own weight, a noise like a ship's hull that comes maybe twice an hour; somewhere, an animal call, single, unanswered, swallowed by the vaulting. Mostly there is the sound of your own breath and the awareness that the walls are breathing too, slower, vaster, and you can feel that more than hear it — a pressure that comes and goes.

## What Lives Here

Fauna: abundant — but abundant the way the light is dim. Present, distributed, never crowded. The creatures of the grown cathedral are mostly soft, mostly slow, mostly luminous in their own faint way because they have learned to navigate this dimness. There are climbers the size of dogs that go hand over hand up the weeping walls, leaving wet smears that the wall absorbs and heals. There are floaters — bladder-bodied things, filled with the gas the plant respires, drifting through the empty naves like jellyfish through water, feeding on the spores and the moisture-hung pollen. There are the listeners, things with enormous membranous ears and almost no eyes, who hold perfectly still on the floor for hours and then move all at once, fast, toward a sound only they registered.

Nothing roars. The ecology runs on the quiet — predator and prey both stalk through silence, and a noise is information too precious to waste. This is an active world, an ecosystem genuinely in motion, weather cycling through the high chambers, things being born and eaten and decomposing back into the living floor — but all of it conducted at a hush, spread thin across the vast hollow, so that the *motion* never crowds into *noise*.

## The People and Their Tools

And there are people. Not many. The density spares them too.

They live by hand-craft, simple tools, the work of fingers and edges and fire kept small. They do not cut the cathedral — you do not cut what is this large and this old and arguably aware. They *ask* it, or they take what it sheds. Fallen tendril-wood, hard as horn, gets knapped and lashed. The great blooms drop husks they carry water in. They weave the floor's loose surface fibers into rope and cloth. Their highest technology is the management of fire in a wet world: small banked coals carried in clay, fed carefully, never let to spread, because the whole world is fuel and the whole world is alive and you do not, ever, let your fire forget its place.

They speak quietly. They've learned the same lesson the animals learned. A loud voice in these halls comes back to you changed.

## Where Reality Thins

Here is the weirdness, and it is high — near the top of the scale — and it lives in exactly the places you'd expect a thing this large and this alive to start losing its grip on ordinary rules.

It is the emptiness that does it. The vast hollow chambers are not stable. Distance does not behave inside them. A hall you crossed in an hour, you cross the next day in ten minutes, or in three days, and you are not lost and you have not gone mad — the *space itself* breathes along with the walls, expands and contracts, and the people know this and plan their journeys around the cathedral's slow inhalations the way a sailor plans around tides.

In the deepest chambers, the ones farthest from any membrane of sky, the bioluminescence in the walls sometimes resolves — for a watcher who waits long enough — into pattern. Into something that is almost, not quite, a face. Into the suggestion that the structure is not blank, that the cathedral grew its emptiness *for* something, that the voids are organs and you are a small warm mote moving through the inside of a thought too slow to notice you. People who stay too long in the deep chambers come back saying the place asked them something. They are never able to say what.

And the water remembers things. Pools at the bottom of the great fins hold reflections that lag — you lean over, and your face arrives in the water a moment late, and once in a while it does not arrive at all, or it arrives doing something you have not yet done. The people drink from these pools anyway. There is no other water. They have made peace with seeing themselves slightly out of time.

The rule of this world, finally, is this: it is one living thing, vast as a continent, hollow as a bell, grown into the shape of architecture and old enough to have a state of being rather than a behavior. It built emptiness so that it could have an inside. The fog and the dim and the silence and the slow rain are its weather, its mood, its long thought. And the creatures and the people are the smallest motions inside it — abundant, scattered, hushed, moving handcraft-slow through a green-black cathedral that breathes, and dreams a little where the light grows strange, and does not quite remember whether it made them or they made it.

It is warm in there. It is wet. It is very, very quiet. And it is patient with you, the way only enormous living things can afford to be.
