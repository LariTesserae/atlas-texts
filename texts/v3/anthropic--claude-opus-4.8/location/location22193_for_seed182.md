Below the city there is another city, and this is the one I mean.

You come down to it not by stairs but by a slow descent of ramps cut into rock that is still, in places, warm to the touch — the stone here is young, freshly made, the kind that has not yet learned to be old. The walls show the marks of recent forming: sharp edges that haven't rounded, mineral veins that gleam like they only stopped flowing yesterday. Run your hand along them and you feel ridges still crisp, faces that catch light at angles a weathered stone never would. This is a place that was poured, somehow, not long ago, and is still setting.

It is hot down here. Not the dry heat of a furnace but the close, wet heat of a body — the heat of being inside something living. The air sits heavy on your skin and condenses against the cooler iron of the structures, so that everything drips. Not rain, exactly. The walls sweat. Water beads on the undersides of pipes and lets go, one drop at a time, and the floors are slick with the slow accumulation of that letting-go. There are channels cut to carry it away, runnels that thread between the buildings, and these are the closest thing this place has to rivers — thin warm trickles that move at the pace of patience, going somewhere, not in any hurry to arrive.

The buildings are packed. This is the thing you understand first and keep understanding: there is no space here that is not occupied. The structures crowd one another shoulder to shoulder, leaning, sharing walls, growing into and around each other the way roots do in a too-small pot. They are built — genuinely built, by hands or by something with intent — out of riveted plate and dark brick and pipe and the kind of machinery that has a function nobody now remembers. Stacks rise and bend and rejoin. Ducts run everywhere, looping between the close-set walls, and through them comes the only real motion in the place: a slow exhalation, warm, that you can see when the dim light catches it.

Because it is dim. The light comes from low sources — banked coals behind grates, the faint orange of element-wire strung in loops, a few high apertures where some grey luminance filters down from the surface and pools on the wet floors. Everything is the color of low fire and old iron. Your eyes adjust and adjust and never quite finish.

It is quiet. This surprises you, given the machinery — given the engines and the ductwork and all the apparatus of an industrial place. But the engines turn over slowly, almost reluctantly, with long pauses between their strokes, and the sound they make is more felt than heard: a low pulse through the floor, a soft mechanical breathing, the occasional far-off clank of something settling. Mostly there is the dripping. Mostly there is your own breath. The whole quarter seems to be conducting some enormous, gradual process at a tempo too slow for an animal mind to track — a single great cycle that will complete itself in a span of years, and that you have walked into the middle of.

The vegetation is what makes it strange.

Because it is lush down here, in the dim and the wet heat, and it should not be. Pale green things grow thick over the ironwork — not weeds breaking in, but vegetation that seems to belong, that the place was perhaps grown to support. Fronds and broad soft leaves and ropes of vine that drink the condensation off the pipes and feed on the warmth of the stone. They do not need much light; they have made their peace with the dimness, gone darker and more patient than surface plants, growing at the same unhurried rate as everything else. Some of them have rooted directly into the machinery, into the seams of the riveted plate, and you cannot tell anymore whether the plants are colonizing the engines or whether the engines were built to be a soil. The boundary has dissolved. A duct breathes warm air and a curtain of leaves stirs at its mouth and breathes back.

The animals are few. You go a long while seeing nothing move that isn't water or steam or the slow turn of a wheel. Then: something pale at the edge of a runnel, drinking — eyeless, probably, in this gloom, a small soft thing the color of the stone. It does not flee. It has no reason to. Whatever predator-prey arithmetic governs the surface does not run down here; there is no urgency in any of it. You watch it drink and it watches nothing and after a while it folds back into a crevice and is gone.

The whole quarter is small. That is the last thing, and the strangest thing, and the thing you only understand once you've walked its length: it is not vast. You could cross it in an hour. It has edges — the young stone closes it off on all sides, a held space, a single room of a place, though that room is crammed past saturation with structure and growth and slow machinery and warm water. It is a complete world the size of a few city blocks, sealed in fresh rock, breathing slowly, growing slowly, dripping, dim, hot, and almost entirely silent.

A made thing. Recently made. Still warm from its making. Densely furnished with life and engine both, indistinguishable now, and going about its long single purpose at the pace of something that expects to be here for a very long time.

You stand in it and you feel watched, though there is nothing to watch you. You feel that the slow pulse in the floor is keeping time, and that you have arrived somewhere in the middle of a count that began before you and will not end for years, and that the place is patient with you, the way warm stone is patient, the way water is patient, the way the leaves turning toward the warm duct are patient.

Then a drop falls from a pipe and strikes the channel, and the small sound of it travels a surprising distance in the quiet, and is the loudest thing you have heard in some time.
