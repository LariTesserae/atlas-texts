There is a city the size of a hand.

I don't mean that figuratively. If you knelt at the edge of the riverbank and reached out, you could cup the whole settlement in your palm — its towers, its streets, the green that climbs over its walls. But you would not do this, because the moment you tried, your own breath would knock the towers over. They are not made for hands. They are made for what made them.

Let me come closer.

The ground here is warm and dark, the kind of warmth that rises from below rather than falling from above. The light is dim — not the dimness of evening but of a place set under something, a leaf, a ledge of soft stone, a canopy not yet thick enough to be called shade and not thin enough to be called open. The air holds moisture without committing to it. There is no rain falling now, but there was rain, and there will be rain, and in the meantime every surface wears a faint slick of condensation that gathers, hesitates, and lets go in single drops. The drops are the loudest thing here. They land with a sound like a comma in a long sentence.

The towers are mineral. They are being built.

This is the part I keep returning to, because it is the strangest and the most ordinary thing about the place. The construction is not finished and never will be, in the way a coastline is never finished. There are spires the height of a finger-joint, made of pale grains cemented one against the next, and at their tips the work continues — a slow accretion, grain laid on grain, the structure growing upward at a pace too slow for a glance and too fast for geology. Nothing here erodes. That is the rule of this world. Things only assemble. Whatever is set down stays, and is added to, and the city rises by the logic of accumulation alone, never by collapse. A tower that leans will not fall; it will simply be buttressed, thickened at the base, corrected by the addition of more, until it stands again or becomes a wall or becomes a hill.

The builders are not many. This is a sparse place — there is a great deal of room between one presence and the next. You might watch for a long while and see only the work, not the workers. Then something moves at the base of a spire: six legs, a body the deep red-brown of wet wood, mandibles carrying a grain larger than its own head. It sets the grain down. It considers — or appears to consider; I will not pretend to know what happens in that small dark mind — and then it leaves, unhurried, into one of the streets, which are really tunnels, which are really the negative spaces left where the building has not happened yet.

There are others. A beetle the color of an oil sheen patrols a ridge, slow as a barge. A pale soft thing with too many segments folds itself through a gap and is gone. Somewhere a spider has strung a single thread between two towers and waits at the center of it, and the thread holds a row of water droplets like a string of dim lamps, and this is the brightest thing in the whole hidden city. The fauna is abundant in kind but scattered in distribution — every creature seems to have its own quarter, its own hour, and they cross paths rarely, and when they do, one of them eats the other, quietly, and the city does not pause.

The vegetation has gotten into everything. Moss climbs the lower towers in cushions of saturated green, and where the moss grows the structure grows differently — softer, rounder, the grains held in a matrix of root-hair and damp. Liverworts spread their flat dark hands over the courtyards. A single fern, enormous by the scale of this place, arches over a whole district like a cathedral that has decided to be a tree instead, and the light that filters through its fronds is the green-gold dimness that everything here is lit by. Seedlings the size of eyelashes push up between paving grains. Some will become the canopy. Most will be carried off by the red-brown builders and added to the walls.

The whole thing sits in a pocket. Beyond it, in every direction, the dryness begins — I can sense it at the edges, the warm dust where the moisture gives out, where nothing builds because nothing can hold together without water to wet the grains. The city exists because here, in this one cupped low place, a seep keeps the ground damp and the river-that-is-barely-a-trickle runs through a channel no wider than a thread, feeding the green, feeding the builders, feeding the slow upward making of everything. It is an oasis of construction in a basin of stillness. A town held inside a single feature of a larger, drier, emptier ground.

And it is so quiet.

Not silent. The drip. The dry whisper of a grain set down. Once, far off, the papery friction of wings that decide not to open after all. The sounds are small and far apart and they make the quiet larger rather than smaller, the way a single voice makes a great room feel like a great room. There is no hum, no engine, no signal, nothing that was not made by a body or by water or by the slow work itself. If you held very still you would hear your own pulse and think it was something in the city.

Everything is in motion and nothing is in a hurry. The droplets gather and fall and gather. The builders carry and set and return. The fern releases spores in a soft brown breath when the moisture is right. The river thread swells after the rain and shrinks between. The towers climb. Somewhere a predator finishes its meal and cleans its forelegs and waits for the next slow hour. There are cycles here, many of them, overlapping — wet and dry, light and dimmer, hunting and hiding, building and building and building — and they turn at the pace of small living things, which is to say steadily, attentively, without any sense that an end is coming.

This is a place at its beginning that will never reach its middle, because it has decided that beginning is enough. It assembles itself out of grain and root and water and the patience of very small architects, in a warm dim pocket of a dry world, under a fern, beside a thread of river, and it makes almost no sound while it does the enormous quiet work of becoming.

You could hold it in your hand. You won't. You'll lean close, and breathe carefully, and watch a single drop let go.
