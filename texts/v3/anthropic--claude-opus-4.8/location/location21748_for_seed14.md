# The Drowned Shelf

Begin below. Not the abyss — the abyss is too far down to feel anything — but the shelf, the wide cold plain where the continent slips under and keeps going for a while before it falls away. You are perhaps two hundred meters down. Enough that the surface is only a memory of light, a grey bruise overhead that you would have to lie on your back to notice, and you will not lie on your back here, because here there is no room.

That is the first thing. The packing.

The water is not empty. People imagine the deep as a vacancy, a black room with a few fish hung in it like ornaments. This is the opposite of that. The water here is *full* — not of creatures, the creatures are rare, you might go a long while between one and the next — but of itself. Of particles. The whole column is a slow blizzard of marine snow, the sifting-down remains of everything that has ever lived in the sunlit water above. Skeletons of plankton too small to name. Fragments of fragments. It falls forever and it never lands, or it lands so slowly that landing is not the right word, and the result is that you move through a medium that is more like a suspension than a fluid, a cold grey broth so dense with drift that your own outstretched hand goes soft at the edges. There is no clear water. There is no room between the presences. Every cubic centimeter holds its freight of the long dead, descending.

The cold is steady and total. Three or four degrees above freezing, the cold of deep water everywhere on this planet, the cold that does not change with weather because weather cannot reach it. It does not bite. It simply removes warmth, patiently, the way the dark removes color — completely, eventually, without malice.

## The Floor

The bottom, when you reach it, is old past reckoning.

This shelf has been underwater long enough that erosion has done all its work and gone home. There are no sharp things. No ridge holds an edge; no stone keeps a corner. Everything has been rounded, then rounded again, then worn until rounding had nothing left to soften. The seabed is a plain of sediment so fine it is almost not matter — silt the consistency of smoke, lying in drifts and swales that go grey-on-grey to the limit of what can be seen, which is not far. Touch it and it does not resist. Your fingers vanish into it without sensation and a cloud blooms up around them, slow, and hangs, because there is nowhere for it to settle that it has not already settled.

Here and there the old bones of the land show through: outcrops worn down to lozenges, smooth as river stones but vaster, the last knuckles of mountains that were mountains before there was an ocean to drown them. They do not look like rock anymore. They look like the idea of rock, the memory of it, half-dissolved. This is the oldest-stable-state of a world — a place that has finished happening. Whatever was going to be shaped here was shaped a hundred million years ago and has been quietly relaxing toward flatness ever since.

## The Sparse Living

Vegetation, here, is a strange word, and yet there is some.

Not plants in the ordinary sense — no light reaches to feed them. But there are stands of something that grows: pale tube-forms anchored in the silt, gathered around the cold seeps where chemicals leak up from the buried rock. They are not green. They are bone-white and translucent, swaying very slightly, and they feed not on the sun but on the breath of the dead planet beneath them. They cluster sparse and scattered — a field here, a lone stalk there, then a long nothing of grey plain before the next. They are the only things in this whole world that are *trying*, that are still oriented toward staying alive, and there are pitifully few of them, and they have arranged themselves wherever the rock exhales.

The fauna is rarer still. You might descend a long time and see nothing that moves of its own will — only the eternal falling of the snow. Then: a shape. Something pale and unhurried, a soft-bodied drifter pulsing through the broth, or a fish so reduced by the dark and the cold that it has given up most of itself, kept only the necessary mouth and the necessary eye and a body like a held breath. They do not school. They do not hurry. There is nothing to hurry toward. They appear, they regard you with the indifference of the very old, and they are gone back into the suspended grey before you are sure they were there.

## And Yet — Turbulent

Here is the contradiction the place holds, and it is the truest thing about it.

Everything I have told you suggests stillness. The cold that does not change. The plain that finished happening. The slow patient snow. You would expect this to be the most motionless place imaginable.

It is not. It is *churning*.

Down here on the shelf the water is not at rest. There are currents — bottom currents, cold dense rivers that pour invisibly across the seabed, and they are unstable, they wander and reverse, they pile up against the worn outcrops and *boil*. The fine silt that lies so quietly is lifted in great soundless storms, turbidity clouds that roll along the floor like avalanches in slow motion, swallowing the white tube-fields, burying and unburying the old bones of the mountains. A whole hillside of sediment will let go at once and slide, a billion years of accumulated drift moving downslope in a single grey surge, and where it passes everything is erased and remade. The marine snow does not fall in straight lines; it is caught up, spun, carried sideways, dragged down and flung back up. The medium itself is in constant violent reorganization.

This is the deep secret of dead places: they are the most dynamic of all. Nothing here resists change, so everything changes. There is no structure to hold a shape against the current — no built thing, no root deep enough, no stone hard enough — and so the current has its way, endlessly, chaotically, sculpting and unsculpting the silt in a turmoil that never resolves and never stops. The plain is finished and the plain is never finished. It has reached its final form, and its final form is *churning*.

## Light and Sound

Almost no light. A trace, a residue — your eyes, given time, find a faint differential, the floor very slightly less black than the water, the white tubes very slightly less black than the floor. Enough to know up from down. Not enough to call seeing.

And sound — quiet. Not silence, but the quiet of immense slow processes. The current does not roar; it *sighs*, a low pressure-shift you feel in the chest more than hear. When a silt-slope lets go, there is a sound like a held breath finally exhaled, vast and soft, felt rather than heard, rolling away across the dark. The clicks of some far creature, attenuated to almost nothing. Then the long quiet again, full of the soundless falling.

## The Rule of the World

There is one law here, and it is this: *nothing is allowed to keep its edges.*

Whatever arrives — bone, stone, the body of a thing that lived, the shape of a mountain, you — is taken in by the cold and the dark and the endless soft churning, and worn, and dissolved, and folded back into the slow grey blizzard that fills the water from floor to far-off light. Not destroyed quickly. Destroyed *patiently*, over spans that make destruction indistinguishable from preservation. Everything is becoming sediment. Everything is becoming the medium. And the medium, packed and cold and turning over and over on itself in the dark, goes on without rest, the busiest stillness in the world, the most crowded emptiness, settling forever into a final form it will never quite reach.
