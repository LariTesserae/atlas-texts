Go down far enough and the rock stops being rock and starts being architecture. Not a clean transition — there's no doorway, no threshold where the natural ends and the made begins. The limestone just gradually develops intention. Veins become conduits. A seam of quartz turns, over a few dozen meters, into something that runs too straight to be a seam.

This is a city in the warm dark, and almost no one is in it.

---

Start with the heat. It comes up from below, not from any fire you could find. The walls are blood-warm to the touch, and the deeper galleries hold a thick, close warmth like the inside of a mouth. You sweat down here. The sweat doesn't dry — the air is damp, three-quarters saturated, never quite raining and never quite still. Water beads on every overhang and falls in slow erratic drips that you hear long before you feel. Somewhere there's an actual river; you can tell by the sound, a low constant pressure of moving water folded into everything else. But you may walk for an hour and never see it. The wet here is mostly condensation — the city breathes and what it exhales collects on the stone and runs down in thin patient threads, carving channels that took ten thousand years to cut a finger's depth.

The erosion is the strange part, given the rest. This place has been worn smooth. Every edge that could have been sharp has been rounded by water and time into something that fits the hand. The construction is fantastically advanced — and it is also old, so old that the advanced parts have weathered like everything else. You find a panel of material that no geology accounts for, dark and faintly iridescent, engineered at a scale below sight — and its corners are rounded, its surface pitted, dust collected in its hollows. Whoever built this built it to last past their own comprehension of lasting, and then time caught up anyway, gently, and smoothed it all down into a kind of settled peace. Nothing here is new. Nothing here is failing, either. It has simply arrived at what it is.

---

The scale fools you. From inside any one chamber it feels like a cathedral — vaulted, the ceiling lost in dark, your footsteps thrown back at you. But the city is not large. Walk it through and you find its boundaries: the galleries loop, the great halls connect to perhaps a dozen others and no more, and then the worked stone reverts to raw rock and the rock closes in. It is a hamlet's worth of space, dressed in the grandeur of a metropolis. A small place that insisted on being built enormous. You could learn all its rooms in a week.

And it is empty. This is the thing that presses on you most. The construction implies crowds — these are streets, plazas, the broad shallow steps of places meant for gathering. The density of the building says *thousands lived here.* The density of the living says *no one.* You walk vast intervals of nothing between one presence and the next. A chamber that could hold a festival holds only you, and the drip, and the dark. The emptiness has weight. It is not abandonment exactly — abandonment implies a leaving, a before-and-after. This feels more like a place built for an occupancy that never came, or came so rarely that the rooms forgot the shape of it.

---

There is light, but barely — call it a rumor of light. The engineered material holds a faint glow in its depths, blue going green going nothing, just enough that after a long while in the dark your eyes find edges. You never see the source. The glow seems to come from inside the walls, released slowly, the way the heat is. You learn to read the city by these dim gradients, brightness pooling in the hollows where the dust has gathered, the high vaults staying perfectly black.

---

And it is *loud.*

This is the contradiction that takes longest to settle into. An empty place should be silent, and this place roars. The sound is not yours and not from any creature — it comes through the stone. Vast slow resonances, the whole city ringing at frequencies you feel in your teeth before you hear them. The moving water amplified through the rock into something like an organ note held forever. And over that, irregular: cracks, settling groans, a deep periodic boom from somewhere below that comes and goes like tide, like the place has a pulse. The drips are sharp and constant against the roar. You cannot get away from the sound. There is no quiet room. The emptiness is full of noise, and the noise has no maker you can find, and that combination — to be alone in a loud place — is the central unease of being here. You keep turning to look at what's coming, and nothing is coming.

The sound is also why the city is alive. Something is happening here, multiple somethings, on cycles. The warmth rises and falls over hours. The condensation thickens to near-rain and then thins. Pockets of the glow brighten and dim across the day, if there is a day. The booming below keeps its rhythm. Whatever engine runs this place is still running, untended, beyond anyone's hand, doing its work in the dark with no one to do it for. The dynamism is all process and no participant.

---

Life is rare and strange, suited to the wet warm dark. Pale things. There's a vegetation of sorts clinging to the wettest walls — sparse fans of something between fungus and fern, faintly luminous themselves, fed maybe on the heat and the mineral water and the unaccountable energy bleeding from the walls. They grow in the channels the water cut, following the wet. In the still pools you sometimes find blind soft creatures, slow, translucent, more rumor than animal. A thing crosses a far plaza on too many legs and is gone before you resolve it. The fauna here is real but it stays at the edge of you. It has the whole vast emptiness to keep its distance, and it does.

---

The rules of the place, as far as you can find them:

Water never collects deep — it always finds a channel and runs, so there are no still floods, only the endless threading drainage and the unseen river beneath. The warmth is constant and sourceless. The light is constant and sourceless. The sound never stops. And the engineering does not respond to you — there are no doors that open at a touch, no systems that wake to a presence. The city runs its cycles regardless. It was not built to be inhabited so much as to *continue.* You are a visitor inside a machine that has forgotten it was ever for anyone, kept warm and wet and singing in the deep dark, smoothed by its own slow weather into something almost gentle, almost at rest — a small enormous hollow place, working perfectly, alone.
