There is a city on the high plateau, and it has been dark for longer than dark has had a name.

Start with the ground, because the ground here is the oldest thing. The plateau is a mineral table lifted to the height where air thins to a held breath, and it has been worn so long that worn is the wrong word — it has finished. There is no more eroding to be done. The stone has reached the shape it was always going toward: low swells of a hard pale rock, dolomite or something older, polished not by water but by the slow patient hands of dryness itself, by temperature swinging from warm to less-warm across ages, by the abrasion of a wind so fine it carries no grit, only the memory of grit. Run your palm across it and feel nothing — no tooth, no dust. It has given up everything it had to give.

No water here. Not the absence of a lake but the absence of the *idea* of a lake. The chemistry of this place worked itself out without it. There are salts in the low places, but they are not evaporite — nothing evaporated. They simply settled out of the rock as it aged, pale efflorescences blooming at the joints of the stone like frost that will never melt because it was never cold and was never water. Bitter to imagine, sweet to the eye, if there were an eye and if there were light.

But there is almost no light. This is the strangeness that organizes everything else.

It is warm — comfortably, persistently warm, the warmth of a room that has been heated and then sealed and then forgotten — and it is dark. Those two facts do not usually keep company. Warmth on the high places comes from a sun, and a sun makes light. Here the warmth comes from below, or from within, or from the long slow exhalation of a rock that remembers being molten and has not entirely finished cooling even now. You stand on a high plateau in a deep night that is not night, because night implies a day on the other side of it, and there is no day on the other side of this. There is only the dark, even, total, with the warmth coming up through your feet.

And in the dark: the city.

You find it first by sound, and the sound is almost nothing. A low mechanical hum, far off, the kind of hum a thing makes when it has been running so long that the running has become its silence. Industrial. There are engines somewhere in the dark, turning. Belts, perhaps. The faint reciprocal sigh of something that pumps — but pumps what? There is no water and no air to speak of moving. It pumps anyway. The hum is so steady it stops being sound and becomes the texture of the dark, and only when you have stood in it for a long while do you notice the smaller sounds nested inside: a tick, very regular, off to the left. A settling, once, far away, the only thing that has changed in a thousand years and it will not change again for a thousand more.

The buildings, when you come among them, are real. Hands made these, or things with the patience of hands. They are settlement-scale — streets, blocks, the layered logic of a place built up over generations by people who knew where the last people had built. Towers of the same pale finished stone, fitted dry, no mortar, because mortar needs water and there has never been water. The joints are so close that across the whole height of a wall you could not slip a blade between two stones. Industrial chimneys rise among the towers, and these are dark too, and cold at the top though warm at the base — they do not smoke. They have not smoked in a very long time. The factories still run, but whatever they make, they make it without fire, or the fire is interior and gives no light.

This is the thing you keep returning to: the machines run and produce no light. The light should be here. A city this size, industrial, mechanized — there should be the glow of furnaces, sparks at the rail-joints, the windows lit from within. There is none of it. The energy goes somewhere else. You put your hand to a humming wall and it is warm — *that* is where the energy goes. The city converts all its work into a faint, even heat and the very lowest possible sound, and it keeps the rest. It hoards its light. Or it never had any to begin with, and the warmth and the hum are simply what is left when light has been subtracted from industry.

The streets are wide and mostly empty. Sparse — that is the word for how this place is inhabited. You can walk a long way between presences. And there are presences: rarely, in the dark, something moves. Fauna, the parameter said, rare, and rare is exactly right. Once in a long while a low shape crosses the street ahead of you, unhurried, the size of a dog or a goat, and it makes no sound on the polished stone, and you cannot see it, you can only feel the small disturbance of the warm dark as it passes. What does it eat, in a city with no plants and no water? It eats the warmth, perhaps. It drinks the hum. It has reached the same finished equilibrium as the stone — it neither grows nor starves, it simply persists, one of a scattered few, each keeping its distance from the others across the open dark.

There is no vegetation. There never was, or it ended so long ago that the difference has stopped mattering. This is a mineral world that built a mineral city and filled it with mineral machines, all of it warm, all of it dark, all of it quiet, all of it done. Done in the sense that nothing further is going to happen. The plateau will not rise or fall. The salts will not bloom further. The engines will not stop and will not speed up. The rare animals will cross the rare streets forever, and the dark will hold its even warmth, and the hum will go on being the silence of the place.

The strangeness is not in any one thing. Each thing is plain. The strangeness is in the *combination* — that something so plainly built, so plainly working, so warm and so populated by patient machinery, should sit in total darkness producing nothing you can name, going nowhere, having already arrived. A city at the end of its own purpose, still running, still warm, still humming, in the dark, on the roof of the world, where the rock has finished being rock and is simply waiting, with infinite patience and no expectation at all, for the one thing that is never going to come.

And the high air over it — thin, warm, dark — holds no stars. There is nothing above to make them. Only the plateau, and the city on it, and you, standing in the warmth with your hand against a wall that hums, in a place that has solved itself completely and has nothing left to do but be exactly what it is, forever.
