# The Terraced High Country

Start with the air. It is warm and full of water, the kind of warmth that lives in your lungs before you notice it on your skin. At this height — and you can feel the height, in the thinness behind the warmth, in the way breath comes a fraction deeper than it should — the moisture has nowhere to fall. It hangs. It beads on the underside of leaves. It collects in the hollows of stone and sits there, unmoving, holding the sky in small grey mirrors.

This is high country, but not the high country of bare ridges and wind. The mountains here are young. You can read it in their edges — the rock breaks in clean angles, fresh fractures, faces that have not yet been rounded by ten thousand years of weather. Where water seeps from a seam in the cliff it does so along a sharp lip, and the seep has not yet carved a channel. It simply weeps, straight down a flat new face, and pools at the base in a basin the rock itself has not had time to soften.

## The Terraces

People have built here, and built well, and built long ago by the look of the stone — but the building stopped. What stands is a town wrapped around the contour of a high valley, terrace upon terrace, each level a band of dressed stone holding back the dark wet earth. The walls are mortarless, fitted, each block shaped by hand against its neighbor. No metal split this stone; you can see the peck-marks of harder stone against softer, the patient bruising of rock by rock.

The terraces climb. From the valley floor they rise in long curving steps, following the land's own logic, and on each step there is growth — but sparse, deliberate growth, plots and beds with broad bare aprons of wet flagstone between them. Not crowded. Whoever shaped this place believed in space. A single broad-leafed plant, glossy and dripping, will hold the center of a terrace the size of a courtyard, and around it nothing but slick stone and standing water reflecting the pale sky.

The vegetation is lush where it grows — fat succulent leaves, mosses thick as carpet in the wall-seams, ferns uncurling from every wet crack — but it does not spread. It stays where it was set or where the water invited it. Between the green there is room to walk, room to see across to the next terrace, the next, and the next, the whole valley legible at a glance: settlement-scale, a place you could know.

## What Is Absent

There are no animals. No bird crosses the wet sky, no insect troubles the standing water, nothing rustles in the ferns. The mosses grow and the leaves drip and the water pools, and all of it is silent in a way that takes a while to understand. You keep waiting for the sound that completes a place like this — the hum, the call, the small movement at the edge of vision — and it never comes.

So the sounds that remain stand out, each one alone: the single drip from a cliff-seam landing in its basin, spaced and even. The thin slide of water finding a lower level. Your own foot on wet stone. These are quiet sounds and far apart, and between them is a silence the warm air seems to thicken.

## The Stillness

And nothing changes. The water weeps but the basin never overflows — it has found its level and holds it. The leaves drip but the plants do not grow larger; they are the size they are. The light stays even and full, neither rising nor falling that you can tell, a plain clear daylight without a sun to locate it. The mountains are young, freshly broken, sharp — but they are not breaking now. They have stopped at the moment of being new and stayed there.

This is the strangeness of the place, and it is a quiet strangeness, barely a strangeness at all: that everything looks caught mid-process — water mid-fall, stone mid-erosion, a town mid-life — and yet nothing moves toward its next state. The terraces will not crumble. The seep will not carve its channel. The valley holds itself in a single long breath that is never let go.

You walk the terraces in the warm wet quiet, between the scattered green, under the weeping sharp-edged cliffs, and the place does not need you, and does not change for you, and the water keeps its level in the stone.
