There is a room made entirely of plant, and it has been a room for longer than anything around it has had a name.

Start with the floor, because the floor is where the age shows. It is not soil. It is a single mass of root and stem and decayed leaf compressed over such an unthinkable span that it has gone smooth and dense, like the worn step of a temple that ten thousand feet have rounded — except no feet did this. Time did it. The plant grew, died into itself, grew again on its own corpse, and repeated the cycle so many times that the distinction between living and dead collapsed into one continuous green-brown substance, springy underfoot, cool, slightly damp. You could press a thumb into it and it would hold the print for a moment before slowly forgetting.

The walls are the same material risen vertical. Not built — grown, and then settled. They lean in the way very old things lean, having found over the eons the exact angles at which they need to do the least work to keep standing. Nothing here is straining. Everything has already arrived at its final shape and has no intention of changing. This is the most striking thing about the place once you've been in it a while: nothing is *becoming* anything. The room reached its equilibrium so long ago that the concept of "growth" feels like a rumor about the deep past. The plant still lives — it must, to stay this green — but it lives the way a glacier moves, the way a mountain breathes. Below perception.

The scale is intimate. A grove, a chamber, a single green room maybe twenty paces across. You can see the far side. There is no horizon here, only enclosure, and the enclosure is gentle rather than oppressive because the space inside is so *open*. This is the paradox you'll feel in your chest: the place is total plant, every surface alive, and yet it is nearly empty. The volume between the walls holds almost nothing. No undergrowth crowds your shins. No vines hang to catch your face. The plant has done all its living in the boundaries and left the middle hollow, a cleared interior, as if the room were the negative space the vegetation defines rather than fills. You stand in a green void with green edges.

Light comes in normally — that's the odd, almost reassuring part. Not the cathedral gloom you'd expect from total vegetation, but ordinary daylight, the kind that means it is simply afternoon. It enters through the high places where the wall-mass thins to translucence, and it lands on the smooth floor without drama. You can read by it. You can see the true colors of things. Whatever opening lets it in is somewhere overhead and out of sight, and the quality of the light never shifts much, because nothing here shifts much.

And then the sound.

The sound does not match the emptiness, and that mismatch is the whole character of the place. It is loud here. Industrially loud. Somewhere — within the walls, beneath the floor, *inside the plant itself* — there is a churning, a rhythmic mechanical labor, a deep cycling drone like the hold of a working ship or the floor of a mill. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. Press your ear to the wall and you feel it more than hear it: a turning, a pumping, valves of sap opening and closing on a schedule older than clocks. The plant is an engine. Not metaphorically — the moisture it pulls and pushes through itself, the gases it cycles, the slow combustion of its own metabolism, all of it has accreted over the immense stretch of years into something that *sounds manufactured*. Pistons of pressure. A great green factory that produces nothing but its own continuance, running at full volume in a room where almost nothing is present to hear it.

The water is part of this machinery. There is no rain, no river, only damp — condensation that beads on the cooler surfaces and runs in thin threads down into the floor, drawn back into the cycle. The walls sweat and drink their own sweat. The air carries a faint mist you notice mostly as a coolness on the lips. Everything is moist enough to live and dry enough to never rot freely. The dampness, too, is in equilibrium: it neither floods nor fails.

The fauna is the second surprise. For all the emptiness, there is *life moving* in here — abundant, even. Small things. Pale, soft-bodied creatures that live entirely within the engine of the plant, threading through its cycling channels, riding the currents of sap and warm air. They emerge onto the inner surfaces in numbers, cluster and disperse, feed on the secretions the walls produce. They are not loud; the plant drowns them out. But they are everywhere your eye learns to look — in the seams, in the high translucent places, crossing the smooth floor in slow purposeful lines. They have lived inside this one room for as long as the room has existed and they will never leave it, because there is nowhere else; the room is their entire world and it has never once changed on them.

Nothing was built here. No hand made this. There are no artifacts, no tools, no marks of intention — only the long patient self-organizing of one enormous ancient organism into a shape that happens to resemble a room with the acoustics of a factory. The resemblance is accident. Or rather, it is the resemblance that always emerges when a living thing gets old enough and large enough and stable enough: it starts to look like architecture, it starts to sound like industry, because those are simply what *function at scale* looks and sounds like, whether a mind designs it or not.

You could stand in the center a long time. The light would not change. The sound would not change. The small creatures would continue their cycles. The walls would sweat and drink. And you would slowly understand that you had walked into the inside of something that finished becoming itself before your species was imagined — a green machine at perfect rest, deafening and empty, cool and damp, lit by an ordinary sun, doing the only thing it has ever done, which is to keep being exactly this.

It is not waiting for anything. That's what the stillness inside all the noise finally tells you. It has already happened. You are inside the long, loud aftermath of its happening.
