# The Underlit Massif

There is no sun here, and there never was. This is the first thing to understand. The darkness is not nighttime — nighttime implies a day that left. This is the native condition. The place was assembled in the dark and has only ever known itself by other means.

So begin not with sight but with the wet.

---

Water is everywhere and always arriving. It does not fall so much as *condense out of the willingness of the air* — the atmosphere here is thick, warm, perpetually at the threshold of becoming liquid, so that walking through it you part curtains of suspended droplet, and your own breath comes back to you as rain. The rock weeps. Every surface is beaded, slick, running. There are no dry places, only places where the water moves fast and places where it pools and goes still and thinks.

The massif itself is old — *established* in the deepest sense. This is a high country, a great upthrust shoulder of the world, but its sharpness was filed off ages ago. The slopes are rounded, the ridgelines soft as the backs of sleeping animals. Erosion has had its long argument with the stone and the two have come to terms. What remains is a landscape of smooth troughs and basins, terraced pools, runnels worn so deep and so patiently that they curve like the inside of a throat. Water followed gravity for so long that gravity began to follow the water.

And it is *vast*. Vast past the point where the word means a large thing — vast to the point where it makes its own weather, its own pressure systems, its own rules. Walk far enough in one direction and the air thickens; the warm grows warmer, the condensing intensifies, until you reach regions where the atmosphere is essentially a slow rain that never finishes falling. Walk another way and you find the high cold drafts that the massif manufactures from its own breathing. There is no edge you could reach. The horizon, if you could see it, would be a concept rather than a place.

---

Now: how is it known, if not by light?

By **sound**, mostly, and the sound is enormous.

The water is the baseline — the drip, the trickle, the roar of distant falls that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, the suck and gulp of pools draining through hidden gullets in the rock. But layered over the water is something else, something that does not belong to a wilderness at all.

**Machines.** There is industry here, in the dark, and it never stops.

You hear it as a deep reciprocating pulse — *chunder, chunder, chunder* — vast engines somewhere in the folds of the massif, turning. Pistons the size of cathedrals breathing in the wet. Belts and gear-trains. The hiss of pressure released. A sound of *production* at continental scale, though you never see a single factory whole, only feel the heat-wash of it, smell the hot grease and ozone bloom into the saturated air, hear the clangor pass through the stone like blood through a body. Whoever or whatever built this works in the dark, by feel, by the logic of the dark, and has been working a very long time.

The structures themselves are **scattered** — and this is the strangeness of it. The industry is everywhere by sound but almost nowhere by hand. You come across them rarely: a half-buried housing of riveted iron, weeping rust into the runnels; a single pylon furred with luminous moss; a doorway in a hillside, warm air sighing out of it, a sound of distant turning within. The machines are *immense* and *few* and *connected* — as though the whole massif were one engine and these the only places where it surfaces. The builders left these marks the way a great fish leaves a fin breaking the surface. Mostly it is *under*.

---

The vegetation is lush and it does not need the light. It has made other arrangements.

Everything growing here is **chemical and tactile** — it eats the warmth, the dissolved minerals in the endless water, the leakage from the engines. Great pale fronds, soft as flesh, that turn toward heat instead of sun. Mats of moss thick as mattresses, draped over every weeping stone, that pulse very faintly with a cold internal light — *bioluminescence*, the only true illumination in the whole world, and it is dim, dim, a green-blue suggestion, enough to see your own hand if you hold it close and wait for your eyes to surrender all hope of more. Fungal towers rise in the basins, fed by the rot of older fungal towers, ringed with gills that breathe out spores you can hear ticking against your skin.

The plant-life is **dense, layered, near-touching** — you cannot move without parting it, brushing it, being brushed. It hangs in curtains. It carpets. It fills the volume of the air with hanging filaments that catch the condensing water and string it into trembling necklaces. Everything is soft and wet and alive against you in the dark, and the only way to know the shape of the world is to feel it crowd you.

---

The fauna is **abundant** and it owns the dark utterly.

These are creatures of sound and touch and chemistry, and they are everywhere, and they are loud. Things that click and answer-click across the basins, mapping the world in echoes. Things with long sensing whiskers that drink the warmth of you from a body-length away. Pale swimmers in the pools, eyeless, fat, that surface with a slap and submerge again. Hunters that *sing* before they strike — a rising tone that tells you, too late, what direction. Whole choruses of small things in the moss, a constant clamor of mating and warning and grief, so that the air is never silent, never still.

And there is a relationship — strange, settled, ancient — between the fauna and the machines. The creatures nest in the warm housings. They drink from the runoff. The largest of them have learned the rhythm of the engines and *time their calls to the pulse*, so that the whole massif keeps a single enormous beat: chunder, chunder — answered by a million small living throats — chunder. Ecology and industry have fused into one weather. Nobody arranged this. It simply settled, the way everything here has settled, over the unlit ages, into balance.

---

Here is the rule of this world, the deep weirdness underneath the rest:

**Light is what is missing, and the place is built around the shape of its absence.** Everything — the engines, the eyeless beasts, the heat-seeking fronds, the singing in the dark — is an organ grown to do the work light does in other worlds. To find. To grow. To know. The darkness is not empty. It is *full of substitutes*, dense with them, every niche that the sun would fill in another world filled here by something stranger that learned to do the job blind.

And the machines — the great turning engines no one ever sees whole — you begin to suspect, the longer you stay, the warmer you grow, the more the beat enters your own chest:

You begin to suspect they are not making a *product*.

You begin to suspect they are the thing that *keeps the dark warm.* That this whole world is one body, breathing in the wet, turning in the deep, singing to itself in a million voices because it cannot see, and does not need to, and never has — and that to be here is not to visit a place but to be briefly inside something vast and old and alive, that has its own pulse, and has never once opened its eyes, and is content.

Chunder. Chunder. The water runs. The moss glows its faint refusal of total black.

Chunder.

You are very far inside, now.
