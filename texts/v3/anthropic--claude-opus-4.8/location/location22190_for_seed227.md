There is a cleft in the rock, and inside the cleft there is a room the size of a modest house, and inside that room there is everything I am about to tell you.

You come to it low, near the bottom of the world, where the land has long since stopped arguing with itself. The hills here are old and rounded, sanded down by an unhurried patience — nothing jagged left, nothing raw. The cleft itself is a wound that healed crooked: two faces of grey stone leaning toward each other, not quite meeting, leaving a slot of darkness above. Rainwater has worked at the entrance for an age, rounding the lip, hollowing a smooth runnel where the drip comes down.

It is cold inside. Not the bitter cold of height — there's no thinness to this air — but the deep, mineral cold of a place the sun has never properly reached. The cold of stone that has held the same temperature for a thousand years and intends to hold it a thousand more. You feel it first on the backs of your hands.

And it is dark. Past the first few steps the daylight gives up entirely, and what little there is comes from low down: a faint phosphorescence on the wet walls, a greenish breathing glow where moss has learned to make its own light, or perhaps only seems to in the way the eye invents things to see when given nothing. The darkness is not empty of texture. It is velvet, close, total.

Now — the sound.

You must understand the sound before anything else here makes sense, because the sound is the master of this room and everything else is its tenant. Somewhere beyond the back wall, water moves in volume. Not a stream — a *river*, an underground torrent crashing through a throat of stone you will never see, and the cleft amplifies it the way a held shell amplifies the blood in your own ear, except magnified, monstrous. It is *deafening*. It comes up through the floor and down through the air and presses against your chest like a hand. You cannot speak in here and be heard. You cannot hear yourself think; thought has to be done with the body, with gesture, with the slow patience of someone who has learned to live below the threshold of speech. The roar is constant — that is the strange mercy of it. Because it never stops, it becomes a kind of silence. After a while the mind smooths it into a blanket and lays its smaller noises down on top.

The vegetation here is improbable and lush, and it lives entirely off the spray. The walls drip and weep, and the air holds a permanent fine mist thrown up by the unseen river, and in that mist a hanging garden has assembled itself across the centuries — ferns with fronds gone almost black for want of light, liverworts plastered in overlapping scales, long pale rootlets dangling from the ceiling like the hair of something patient. Moss has upholstered every horizontal surface. It is wet and cold and green-black and somehow tender, the way a thing is tender that has survived a long time by asking for very little.

But the room is *empty*. This is the thing that takes longest to feel. Despite the garden, despite the noise, the volume of the space is mostly nothing — vast spans of air between one living presence and the next. A fern here. Then a great reach of bare dripping stone. Then, far off, a single drip-pillar where a stalactite and stalagmite have spent geological ages reaching for each other. The life is real but sparse, scattered like a few candles in a cathedral. The room is mostly hollow, and the hollowness is what the sound fills.

There are signs that people came here. Long ago, and not many of them. On the smoothest face of the left-hand wall someone scratched a row of marks with a stone tool — tally, or prayer, or map, you cannot tell, and the carver could not have heard themselves work. Near the back, half-sunk in moss, a low wall of fitted stones, dry-laid, no mortar, knee-high — the remains of something built: a shelter, a barrier, an altar, a place to set something down out of the wet. A wooden haft, gone soft and dark, the metal head of whatever it once was eaten away to a rusty stain in the rock beneath. These are the marks of hands that worked slowly, with simple things, in the dark, by feel. People who came to a place that did not want them and made the smallest possible imprint and left. The cleft has nearly reabsorbed their work. Give it another age and there will be nothing.

Living things pass through, rarely. A pale cave-cricket, blind, antennae longer than its body, testing the moss. Once in a great while something larger — you find the print, not the animal: a clawed track pressed into wet silt by the back wall, already filling with water, already going. Whatever it was came for the river and left by a way you cannot follow. The fauna here are visitors. Nothing makes its home in the full roar.

And everything changes, but slowly. The water rises a hand's width over a season and falls again. The moss advances across the stone at the pace of a held breath. The drip-pillar grows the width of a hair in a human lifetime. The river, behind its wall, has carved this whole place out over a span of time the mind cannot hold, and it is still carving, patiently, indifferently, and one far day the back wall will give and the room will be a river and not a room at all. But not today. Today the balance holds. Today it is exactly what it has been: a cold hollow garden in the dark, full of a noise so large it has become a kind of peace, where a few green things drink the breath of an unseen flood, and the marks of old hands soften back into the stone, and there is space — so much space — between one small living thing and the next.

You stand in it a while. The cold settles into you. The roar holds you. And then you go back out into the thin grey light of the low old hills, and the quiet out there sounds, for an hour afterward, like the loudest thing you've ever heard.
