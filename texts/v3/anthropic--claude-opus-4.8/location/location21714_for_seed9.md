# The Terrace Works

High up, where the air thins enough to taste of metal and the clouds come to drink, there is a thing being built. Not a building — a place. It is small, the way a single courtyard is small, walled by structures that haven't decided yet whether they are walls.

## What it is made of

Everything here is fabricated. There is no native rock, no original soil. The ground is a lattice of cast iron and green-glazed ceramic, laid in interlocking tiles the size of dinner plates, and beneath the tiles run pipes, and beneath the pipes more tiles, and so down through a depth no one has surveyed. When you press a foot to the floor, it gives by a hair's breadth and then holds — there is water in everything, water in the joints, water sweating from the seams.

This is a made mountaintop. Someone, or something, decided that this elevation should exist and assembled it from below, course by course, and they have not finished. That is the most important fact about the place: it is *emerging*. There are scaffolds that are also the structure. There are pipes that end in the open air, capped with brass, waiting for the next length to be threaded on. A staircase rises three steps and stops at a platform the size of a table, and the platform's edge is unfinished — the tiles run out and show the raw iron armature beneath, bright and unrusted, recently poured.

Nothing is old enough to have decayed. Erosion has barely begun its work. The edges are sharp. The bolt-heads still wear their machining marks. This is a landscape with no past, only a present and an obvious, pressing future.

## The water

Water is the medium that makes the made-thing alive. It rises from below through capillary channels — you can hear it climbing, a faint chime as droplets traverse the pipework — and it weeps from every horizontal surface as a film, and it condenses out of the warm thick air onto the cooler ceramic and runs down in beaded threads.

The whole place is warm and wet at once: a greenhouse without a roof, a hot terrace in perpetual light drizzle that never quite becomes rain. The tiles are slick. Brass gutters carry the runoff to small square basins, and the basins overflow into lower gutters, and somewhere the water is recovered and lifted again. A closed system, mostly. You feel it on your skin within a minute — the warmth, then the damp, then the strange clean weight of being inside the breath of a machine.

## The green

Because it is warm and wet and bright, things grow. Lush, dense, crowded — but they grow on the made-thing, not in it. There is no earth for roots, so the vegetation has learned other holds.

Mosses fur the iron, jade-bright and thick as carpet, and out of the moss come ferns that have rooted in the pipe-joints where mineral water and rust make a kind of broth. Vines descend from the upper scaffolds, thick-leaved, their tendrils gripping bolt-heads and ladder-rungs. There are pale orchids growing directly out of valve-housings, fed on condensate and trace metals, their petals faintly green-veined as though tinted by the copper they drink. The plants press near each other — a hand's width between fronds, leaves overlapping leaves, three layers deep in places — so that the small courtyard feels overfull, a room stuffed with growing things. You move sideways. You duck.

The vegetation has begun to do the work erosion has not. Roots find seams. A young fig-like thing has cracked one ceramic tile clean across with the patience of its swelling. The builders, whoever they are, must contend with this: the place they are making is already being unmade by the life their water invited.

## The light and the air

The light is ordinary — daylight, diffuse through the constant mist, the kind of even silver-grey illumination you get on a bright overcast morning. It comes from above and from the side equally, scattered by water-vapor, so almost nothing throws a hard shadow. Everything is gently, thoroughly lit. The green glows. The brass does not gleam so much as smolder warmly. You can see fine: the place is generous with sight.

The air is dense with moisture and faintly sweet — chlorophyll, hot metal, the mineral tang of wet rust, and underneath all of it a clean ozone note, as of electricity passing through damp.

## The machinery

This is an industrial place, but a quiet one. Somewhere below, engines run — you feel them more than hear them, a deep even thrum through the soles of your feet, the pulse of pumps lifting water against the height. Occasionally a valve releases with a soft pneumatic sigh and a breath of warm vapor rises through the ferns. A chain somewhere advances one link, *tick*, and is still.

The technology is mature but not loud: belts, governors, the slow articulation of a mechanism doing maintenance on itself. Now and then a brass arm extends from a housing, sets a new tile, withdraws. The construction is partly automatic. The place builds itself in increments too small to watch, the way a coral grows.

Mostly, though, it is quiet. The loudest sound is water — the chime of it rising, the trickle of it falling, the drip from leaf-tips. Then the engine-thrum beneath. Then, rarely, the machine-sounds. You could speak in a normal voice and be the loudest thing present.

## The few living animals

Almost no fauna. The place is too new, too sealed, too strange. But there are a few.

Something small and bright moves in the upper vines — once, then gone — too quick to name, leaving a frond rocking. There are insects that have found the orchids: slow metallic-blue things, more like beads than beetles, that crawl the valve-housings and drink condensate. And in one of the brass basins, impossibly, a single pale fish, fingerlength and nearly translucent, circling. No one knows how it arrived in a closed system on a fabricated peak. It is simply there, a presence the place did not plan for, the way the cracked tile and the climbing fig were not planned.

## The rules of this world

- **It rises.** The default direction is up. Water rises, structures rise, the whole thing accretes upward into the cloud. Falling is the anomaly; only the runoff and the loosened tile go down.
- **Made and grown are at war, slowly.** The builders extend the iron; the green dismantles it. Neither is winning. The tension is the landscape's only weather.
- **Nothing is finished, and this is permanent.** The unfinished edge is not a stage the place will pass through. It *is* the place. Come back in a year and there will be more of it, equally unfinished, the boundary of completion having moved on like a tideline.
- **The water remembers what it touches.** It carries copper, iron, the dissolved breath of the engines, and it deposits these into the plants and the single fish, tinting them. Everything alive here is faintly the color of the metal it drinks.

## What it is like to stand there

You stand on a slick green tile at a tremendous unseen height, mist on your face, surrounded by crowding ferns and copper-veined orchids, hearing water climb the walls and an engine breathe below. A brass arm somewhere lays a tile that will not be walked on for a hundred years. The single fish turns in its basin. The air is warm and tastes of metal and chlorophyll. You are inside something that is becoming itself, very slowly, in a place where there was nothing and will, eventually, be more — and the green is already busy taking it apart, leaf by patient leaf.

It is small enough to cross in twenty paces. It feels, somehow, like the beginning of everything.
