There is a place near the top of the world where the air is thin enough that sound forgets how to travel.

Start with the rock. It is pale — not white, but the bleached grey of bone left in sun, with veins of something glassier running through it where the mountain cracked and healed and cracked again. The stone is not soft, but it has been worked by an enormous patience. Everywhere you look the edges are rounded, the surfaces scalloped, the spires worn into the shapes of melting candles. Nothing here is sharp anymore. The mountain has been here long enough to have given up its sharpness.

The scale is intimate, despite the altitude. You could walk the whole of it in an afternoon — a basin perhaps two hundred paces across, ringed by spires, a single feature folded into the larger anonymity of the range. But within that basin everything crowds. The spires lean inward, dozens of them, near enough to touch, a forest made of stone instead of trees. They rise in tiers, layered close, and between them the light does strange things.

Because it is so bright here. This is the first thing the body notices and the last it forgets. At this elevation the sun is undiluted, a white hammer, and the pale stone throws it back from every facet. The basin is flooded with light from below as well as above — the floor reflects, the walls reflect, until shadow becomes a rare and precious thing, hoarded in the lee of the spires like cold water in a dry land. You squint. Everything has a corona. The light has weight.

And it is cold. That is the paradox the place asks you to hold: brilliance without warmth. The sun pours down and the stone stays cold to the touch, and your breath fogs in the radiance. The cold is clean, mineral, without the rot-smell that cold carries in lower places because there is nothing here to rot. No moss. No lichen even — nothing has colonized this stone. It is a world before life, or after it, or simply beside it, untouched. The only colors are the pale of the rock, the glassy black of the veins, the violent blue of the sky pressed down close overhead.

Now the water. It is not abundant but it is present, and it is the engine of everything. In the cold of the long nights, moisture condenses out of the thin air onto the stone — a film, a sweat, a faint glaze of frost in the deep dark. And then the sun comes and the frost melts and runs, threading down the scalloped surfaces, pooling in the hollows it has spent ages carving. This is the active thing, the dynamic thing, the slow pulse of the place: freeze and thaw, condense and run, the daily breath of water moving across stone. You can hear it if you stay still — and you will stay still, because there is little else to do here, and the quiet invites it. A faint trickle. The tick of a droplet falling into a pool. The almost-sound of frost letting go of a surface as the light touches it, a sound like a held breath released. Otherwise: silence. The thin air swallows everything. Your own footsteps come back to you muffled and apologetic.

The pools are the secret of the basin. Where the water has gathered over uncountable years it has cut the stone into smooth-lipped cisterns, some no bigger than a cupped hand, some deep enough to drown a kneeling figure. They are very clear and very still and very cold, and when the light strikes them they hold it the way the rest of the stone cannot — a trapped, trembling brightness. They are scattered through the basin in their dozens, layered down the tiers, and at midday the whole place glitters with them, a constellation laid flat.

And here is the unusual thing, the thing that makes this place not quite obey the ordinary rules. The water does not always run downhill. Watch a melt-thread long enough and you will see it hesitate, double back, climb a hand's breadth against a spire's flank before settling. The pools, too, are not always level — some lie tilted in their basins, the water canted and patiently holding a slope it should not hold, the way a thought can hold a shape that doesn't quite make sense until you stop looking directly at it. No one has explained this. There is no one here to explain it. The water simply keeps its own counsel about which way is down, and the stone, scalloped by ages of this disagreement, bears the proof: ripple-marks that run uphill, frozen wave-fronts climbing the spires like tide-lines from an ocean that ran the wrong way.

There are marks of hands, too, though faint. Someone came here, long ago, and stayed long enough to leave a little. Near the center of the basin, low down where two tiers meet, there is a small structure — barely a structure, a waist-high enclosure of dry-stacked stones, the pale blocks fitted without mortar, rounded now by the same patient weathering as everything else, so that it is hard to say where the building ends and the mountain resumes. A windbreak. A shelter. Inside it the floor has been swept flat and a shallow basin chipped deliberately into the rock to catch the melt. And on the walls of the spires nearby, if the light falls right, you can find the marks: simple lines scratched into the stone, a tally, a spiral, a row of dots that might be a count of days or a map of the pools or nothing at all. The tools that made them were simple — a harder stone, a sharpened bone, a length of antler from some animal that does not live up here and perhaps never did. Whoever it was carried their tools up from below. Whoever it was understood that you do not bring more than your hands can carry to a place this high.

They are gone now. Long gone. The shelter holds no warmth, no ash, no bones. But the water still gathers in the basin they cut, faithful, every cold night, and runs out of it every bright morning, and this is the only ceremony the place observes — a small cupped offering of frost, made and unmade, to no one, forever.

Stand in the center of it at noon. The light is everywhere, and cold. The spires lean in, dense and close and patient. The pools hold their tilted brightness. Somewhere a droplet decides, against all reason, to climb. And the silence is so complete that you can hear your own blood, and the thin small song of water learning, slowly, over ages, to disobey.

This is a place that does not need you. It was finishing itself long before you came and will go on finishing itself long after. But it does not mind that you are here. It simply continues — bright, cold, slow, and quietly wrong about which way the water should fall.
