# The Roostvault

You come down into it through a fault in the limestone, and the first thing is the sound.

Not a single sound — a weather of sound. It arrives before the light does, while you're still in the throat of the entrance where the daylight thins to a grey memory. A vast layered chittering, a wet flutter of ten thousand wings folding and unfolding, the small percussive clicks of claws on stone, and under all of it a low continuous thrum like a held breath that never quite releases. The cave breathes this noise the way a forest breathes wind. It does not pause. It has not paused, you sense, for a very long time.

## The Space

The chamber is enormous and low. Not a cathedral — cathedrals reach up. This reaches *out*. The ceiling stays close, twenty or thirty feet over your head, but the floor runs away from you in every direction past the edge of seeing, a broad shallow basin of pale stone. The scale is regional, a buried valley. You could walk for an hour and find another wall.

The rock is young here — freshly broken, geologically speaking. The fault that let you in is recent work; the edges of the limestone are still sharp, not yet rounded by the slow patience of water. Flowstone has only just begun to drape the walls in thin translucent sheets, and the stalactites overhead are stubby, juvenile, more nub than spear. Everything has the look of a place that was made not long ago and has not yet decided what it will become. And yet — paradoxically — nothing here changes. The sharpness is permanent sharpness. The cave is young and frozen at once, caught at the exact moment of its emergence, like a held photograph of a forming thing.

## The Light

Dim, but not dark. The light comes from two sources and they don't agree.

From above, threads of grey daylight leak through the fault and through other cracks you can't see, falling in soft vertical columns that pick out drifting motes — dust, spores, the endless shed down of wings. The columns move slowly through the haze and never reach the floor; they dissolve halfway down into the general gloom.

And from below, a colder light: the floor of the basin holds shallow pools and slow seeps where mineral water gathers, and the water carries a faint phosphorescence, a blue-green ghost-glow from something living in it — algae or the larvae of insects, you can't be sure. The pools glow like spilled moonlight in the low places, and where the seeps run thin across the stone they leave luminous trails, a slow map of where the water has decided to go.

## The Water

It is damp here, not wet. Moisture is the medium. It beads on the cold flowstone and drips — you hear individual drops between the larger noise, a steady irregular metronome scattered across the whole chamber, a thousand small clocks each keeping its own time. The drips feed the pools. The pools never overflow and never dry; they sit at exactly the level they have always sat. Condensation films every surface, so that the stone is always faintly slick and always faintly cold against the hand.

There is a river, too, somewhere off in the dark to the left, where the sound goes hollow and rushing. You don't need to see it. You can hear that it is patient and unhurried and that it has been running exactly that fast forever.

## The Living Weight

But the cave is *made* of fauna. That is the truth of the place.

The ceiling is not stone. The ceiling is bodies. Bats — uncountable, a living plaster across every inch of the overhead rock, hung in their folded thousands so densely that the limestone behind them is only rumor. They shift and resettle in slow waves, a ripple of small movements traveling across the roof like wind over a field. When a column of bats peels loose and wheels through the chamber it is a single organism, a smoke of wings, banking and folding without one of them ever touching another. The thrum you heard at the entrance is them: the cumulative sound of breathing, of wing-membrane and small lungs, multiplied past meaning.

And below them, the floor moves too. The guano — soft, deep, ancient — is alive with the things that eat it. Pale crickets the length of your finger, eyeless and long-legged, moving in such numbers that the floor seems to crawl. Beetles. Translucent spiders strung on the lower walls. Centipedes the color of bone. And in the glowing pools, blind fish hang motionless and pale, and shrimp the size of grains of rice cloud the water, and the larvae of cave flies hang their luminous fishing-lines from the low places of the ceiling, false constellations strung above the real water.

It is overwhelming. There is more life per cubic foot here than in a jungle, but it is life of a strange kind — no plants to speak of, only a few pale fungal threads and a scatter of moss near the light-cracks where the green can just barely hold on. The vegetation is sparse to vanishing. Everything alive here lives on everything else alive, and on the slow rain of nutrients from the world above, brought down in the bodies of the bats. It is a closed economy of hunger, ancient and balanced. Nothing increases. Nothing fails. The numbers are exactly, eternally, the numbers they have always been.

## The Emptiness Between

For all the density of life, there is space. This is the strange peace of the place: between the living pools and the colonies of crickets there are wide flats of bare pale stone where nothing is — just the cold floor and the drifting light and the drip-drip-drip. The presences cluster and leave great quiet gaps. You can stand in one of those gaps and be alone in the middle of the most crowded place you have ever entered, with the great soft roar all around you and nothing within reach but your own shadow, doubled and faint in the doubled light.

## The Rules

There is no day and no night here, only the unchanging dim. The bats fly out somewhere, through cracks too high to see, and return — but even that has the quality of a tide, not an event. Time is a circle so large it reads as a straight line.

Nothing here was built. Nothing here was placed by hands. And yet — near one of the glowing pools, if you look, there is a single flat stone that has been chipped to an edge, and beside it a scatter of bone-needles and a heap of cracked crickets, the leavings of something that came down here once with simple tools and a simple hunger and learned to live off the endless harvest. Whoever they were, they left no other mark. The place absorbed them the way it absorbs everything — into the slow, patient, unchanging breath.

You stand a while in the noise. It does not change. You understand, slowly, that it never will, that this is what the world sounds like underneath, that it has always been here making this sound beneath the feet of everything, and you climb back toward the thin grey light of the fault carrying the roar in your ears like water you can't shake out.
