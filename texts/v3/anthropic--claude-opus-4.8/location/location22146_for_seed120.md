There is a clay jar, and the world is inside it.

Not metaphorically. You are standing on the curved inner wall of a vessel perhaps a handspan across, and you are also the size required to stand there, which is no size at all, and the jar sits on a high shelf in a kiln that is also a mountain. These facts do not resolve into each other. They simply both remain true, the way two notes held together remain two notes.

Begin with the heat. It comes up through the soles of your feet because the jar is being fired — has been being fired for a duration that has no word, the slow eternal bake of unfinished things. The clay underfoot is warm as a held breath, almost too warm, and it sweats. That is the water: it weeps out of the walls in beads, runs in thin threads down the throwing-ridges left by the potter's fingers, those ascending spiral terraces that ring the whole interior like the contour lines of an impossibly steep mountain. Because the jar *is* the mountain. The elevation is extreme — you are very high up, near the lip, and the curve of the wall falls away below you into a darkness that is the jar's own bottom and also a valley you could die falling into.

The water pools in the throwing-grooves. And in the pools, things grow.

This is the lushness: a vegetation that has no business existing in fired clay, but the clay is not finished, it is *emerging*, still soft in places, still deciding what it is — erosion at zero, nothing worn down yet, every edge raw and first-made, the whole environment in the embryonic minute before it sets. So plants take root in the wet ridges. Mosses, mostly, but dense — packed so tight there is no gap of bare clay anywhere the water reaches, a saturated felt of green and red-green and a green so dark it reads as wet stone. Ferns the size of eyelashes uncurl from the seams. Tiny liverworts plate the steeper faces. Everything drips. Everything is occupied. There is no room. Density at its near-maximum means you cannot set your foot down without crushing a colony, and the colonies don't mind, they re-knit behind your heel.

It is dim. The light comes from above, from the jar's mouth, a circle of furnace-glow filtered through whatever cloth or hand or sky covers it — a coppery dimness, the color of the inside of a closed mouth, of a sunset seen through eyelids. Enough to see by. Not enough to see clearly. The mosses glow very faintly in it, or seem to, a phosphor that might be light and might be the heat made visible.

Now the sound — and this is loud, this presses on you. The firing speaks. Clay under heat *sings* and *cracks* and *ticks*, and inside a jar all of this is amplified, the curved walls a throat that gathers every small report into a continuous reverberant roar. Water hisses where it touches the hottest seams. Steam mutters. And underneath it all, a deep slow groan, the sound a vessel makes deciding whether to hold or to shatter — the firing risk, the question every kiln asks of every pot, asked here forever and never answered.

The making is total. Nothing in this world is natural substrate. Even the mosses, look closely — they are not quite plants. They are *thrown*. Each tiny stem shows finger-ridges of its own, as though the potter's hands shrank and shrank and kept working at smaller and smaller scales, throwing moss, throwing the spores, throwing the water-bead and the fern-curl, all of it pulled up wet from the same clay on the same wheel. Made things all the way down. The technology that built this is primitive in the most literal sense — first-tools, the wheel and the hand and the kiln-fire, no metal here except maybe a single bronze pin somewhere holding a seam, the oldest crafts, the crafts that made *world* out of *mud*.

Fauna is scarce. But there is something. On the third terrace down from the lip, where a spring of condensation pools deepest, a snail moves. It is the only animal you will see for a long time, and it is also clearly made — its shell is glazed, a true ceramic spiral, celadon-pale, and it leaves a track of slip behind it, liquid clay, repairing the world's small cracks as it goes. It is a tender of the jar. There may be others. They are rare; you will wait hours between them; their slow ceramic passage is one of the active processes that keeps this place alive — and it *is* alive, it moves, it cycles: the heat drives the water up the walls as steam, the steam condenses at the cooler lip, runs down through the moss, feeds it, pools, and the snail crawls and mends and the firing groans and somewhere the temperature ticks one degree higher toward the moment of vitrification that never quite comes.

That is the whole of it and that is the weirdness, full and uncut: a world the size of a held jar that is also a mountain inside a kiln, never finished, never broken, weeping and green and roaring softly, fired forever in a copper dimness, tended by glazed snails, every blade of moss thrown on a wheel too small to imagine — a place caught in the single suspended instant before the clay sets and the world it dreams becomes only a pot on a shelf, a place that survives precisely by never being finished, by staying raw, by remaining the warm wet question and never the cold dry answer.

Stay on the lip a while. Mind the snail. Don't crush more than you have to. The firing has no end and you have all the time there isn't.
