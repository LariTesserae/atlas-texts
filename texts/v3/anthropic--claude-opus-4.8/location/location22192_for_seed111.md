There is a notch near the top of the world, and I will describe what lives in it.

Begin with the height, because the height explains everything else. This is a place at the extreme edge of elevation — not a summit, summits are too generous, too rounded by the patience of weather. This is a cleft between two young teeth of rock, a vertical wound where two mountains were thrust up against each other so recently that the stone still shows its fracture-faces, sharp as struck flint, unsoftened. Run a hand along the wall and it would cut you. Nothing here has had time to be worn down. The mountains are still becoming mountains.

The notch is small. You could walk its length in the time it takes to grow cold — and you will grow cold, because the air at this height is thin and cool and tastes of stone-dust and something faintly mineral, like blood or wet iron. Call it a room, then. A cathedral-narrow room with a floor of shattered scree and two walls leaning inward, and the open sky pinched to a ribbon overhead. But it is dark. This is the strangeness that takes hold first: the light does not arrive the way light should at such an altitude. The ribbon of sky overhead is the deep blue-black of very late dusk, never brighter, never moving toward dawn. Whatever sun this world has does not reach down into the notch. The slow gradient here is not day-to-night but something else, some patient cycle measured in the dimming and faint re-kindling of a glow that does not come from above.

It comes from the fauna.

Here is where the place stops being a mountain and becomes itself. The notch is overwhelmingly, impossibly alive. Not with one creature but with layers of them, near-touching, saturating every surface — and most of them carry their own small light. On the cut-stone walls cling colonies of soft-bodied things, palm-sized, that pulse a cold green-white from within like embers breathing under ash. They cover the rock in their thousands so that the walls themselves seem to inhale and exhale light. Between them move grazers — slow segmented animals the length of a forearm, armored in translucent plates, that crop the lichen and the few hardy plants and leave trails of fainter phosphorescence behind them, glowing snail-roads that fade across hours.

And the plants. There are not many — vegetation is sparse here, the cool and the thin air permit only the stubborn — but what grows is strange. Pale fronds, almost colorless, that do not photosynthesize light because there is no light, but that lean toward the glowing animals as if warmed by them. Cushions of dark moss in the rock-cracks, beaded constantly with condensation, because moisture lives here too: not rain so much as a perpetual seeping. The cold stone sweats. Water gathers in the fracture-lines and runs down in thin threads, pooling in the scree, and from those pools comes a sound.

The sound is the second thing that overwhelms. The notch is loud — not with wind, the air barely stirs, but with the animals themselves. The glowing wall-colonies make a sound when they pulse, a low collective *thrum*, felt in the chest as much as heard, and it rises and falls with their light so that the whole canyon seems to breathe aloud. Over this, the armored grazers click and rasp. And in the pools, things sing — long liquid notes, overlapping, ceaseless, the way frogs fill a marsh except there is no marsh, only these threads of seep-water and these singers crowding their edges, more singers than the water should hold, stacked at the pool-margins in living heaps. The noise has no silence in it. To stand in the notch is to stand inside a sound that has no outside.

The rules of this place, as I find them:

Light is generated, not received. It is currency, it is warmth-by-suggestion, it is the slow tide of the world. When the colonies dim together — and they do, gradually, over what might be hours or days, there is no way to mark time except by them — the whole notch slides toward true dark, and the singing pools grow louder, as if the creatures call the light back. And slowly it returns. This is the dynamic of the place: not weather, not seasons, but the long pulse of luminescence rising and falling, a tide of glow that the whole ecology rides.

Nothing has built anything. No nests of made things, no tools, no marks of intent — except, perhaps, one detail that troubles the eye. The glowing wall-colonies are not scattered at random. Over centuries, or whatever passes for centuries here, the grazers' fading trails and the colonies' growth have arranged themselves into patterns on the stone — spirals, branching lines, shapes that repeat at the same scale on both leaning walls. No hand made them. They are the residue of the slow process, the way frost makes ferns on a window. But they look made. They look like writing in a language of light that takes a lifetime to spell a single word, and the word is still being written, and you will be gone long before it finishes.

The cold deepens toward the floor. The scree underfoot shifts and clicks, half stone and half the shed plates of the grazers, indistinguishable now, the mountain and its animals composting into one another. Sharp rock above, soft pulsing flesh below, water threading between, sound filling all of it, light breathing slow.

A small place. A high, cold, crowded, singing place, very young, very strange, lit by its own inhabitants and indifferent to whether anything ever sees it.

It pulses on in the dark, writing its slow word.
