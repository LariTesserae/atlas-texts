There is a room inside a wall, and the wall is inside a mountain, and the mountain is something someone built.

You enter through a gap no wider than a hand, but the place beyond does not obey the gap's promise of smallness. It is a chamber — call it a chamber — maybe four paces across, though the walls breathe in and out of certainty when you look away from them. Everything here is made. There is no stone beneath the stone. If you press a thumbnail into what looks like weathered granite, it gives slightly, like the rind of something, and beneath it is more of the same all the way down, a fabricated substance pretending at geology so convincingly that it has begun to mean it.

The warmth comes first into the body, then the wet. It is the heat of a packed greenhouse, of breath held in too many lungs. Water beads on every surface — not falling, exactly, but condensing, weeping, the way a cold glass sweats. The made-rock is dark and slick with it. Channels run through the floor where countless feet, or whatever these creatures use for feet, have worn the engineered surface into smooth troughs. That is the strange thing: it was built, and then it was eroded, and the erosion has had time enough to settle into its own long balance. Someone constructed this and then left it to weather for an age, and it weathered the way real things do, into something that looks inevitable.

The light is dim and comes from the water itself — or from something dissolved in it, a faint sourceless green-gold that pools in the low places and leaves the high corners nearly black. You learn the room by its glistens.

And the sound. The sound is the room's true architecture. It is deafening — a layered roar that you feel in the sternum before the ears catch up. There are tens of thousands of small living things packed into this little space, near-touching, climbing over one another in slow shifting drifts along the wet walls, and each of them is making a noise. Not crying out — humming. A continuous resonant thrumming, each at a slightly different pitch, so that the whole becomes a chord with no edges, a standing wave of sound so dense it has texture. You could lean against it. When a section of the colony shifts, the chord modulates, a swell moving through the dark like weather.

The creatures: small, the size of a knuckle, soft-bodied, with a skin that catches the green-gold light and throws it back doubled. They cling to the few plants — and there are plants, sparse, strange, just a handful of fleshy stalks rooted in the made-floor, drinking the condensation, leaning toward the dim glow. The plants are outnumbered a thousand to one. The fauna is everything here. They graze the films of moisture, they graze each other's secretions, they pulse in slow tides up and down the walls following gradients you cannot see. Something eats them, too — you notice gaps in the drifts, sudden silences in the chord where a longer, paler shape moves through, and the humming closes over the place where it passed like water over a dropped stone.

This is an ecosystem. Predator and prey, the slow biological clock of feeding and shifting and condensing and dripping, the cycle of water from wall to air to wall. It runs without us. It has run for a long time.

The unusual part — the part that takes a while to settle into the mind — is that all of it is engineered past the point our physics has language for. The condensation does not simply fall; it returns. Water that should pool and stagnate instead climbs back up the walls along invisible scaffolds, recharging the heights, so that the weeping is also a circulation, a closed lung of moisture that has been breathing here, self-sustaining, for centuries inside a hand's-width gap inside a wall inside a built mountain. The room is high — you can feel the thinness behind the warmth, the way altitude sits under the heat like a cold floor under a warm rug — and yet the pressure here is its own, kept, tended by no one, by nothing you can find.

Someone made this and walked away and it became alive. It does not need the gap you came through. It does not need to be four paces across; that is only how it greets you. The chord rises. The pale shape moves. The water climbs the dark wall back toward the light, and the small things hum, and hum, and hum.
