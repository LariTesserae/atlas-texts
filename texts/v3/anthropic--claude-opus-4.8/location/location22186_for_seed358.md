Go down. Not far — the surface is only a few meters overhead, close enough that in places the ceiling sweats and you can see a smear of grey daylight where a root has punched through and let the world leak in. But down, into the warm wet dark beneath a low country.

This is a flooded works. Whether anyone built it on purpose is no longer a question worth asking. What matters is that the water came up to meet the digging, and the two have been negotiating ever since.

The floor is not a floor. It is the place where the standing water stops and the mud begins, and that line shifts a hand's width over the course of a day. The water is warm — body-warm, near enough — and it holds still in the broad way that very shallow water holds still, a sheet stretched across acres, broken by hummocks of clay and by the half-formed shapes the earth is making. Because that is the thing about this place: it is *emerging*. The geology here has not finished. Banks are still being decided. There are ridges of silt that look poured, that look like they happened an hour ago and might be gone by morning, and they are soft to the touch and warm and they smell of iron and of green. Nothing has had time to erode. Edges are raw, unrounded, the way a riverbank is for the first week of its life before the water teaches it to be smooth. The whole region is in its first draft.

Through it run the bones of an industry. Iron stanchions, rusted to the color of the water, march off in lines until the dim swallows them. Pipework, large enough to walk inside, lies half-submerged, weeping at the joints — and you cannot tell anymore whether the pipes brought the water or whether the water has only adopted them. A pump-house squats on the largest hummock, its single window dark, its machinery long stopped or perhaps never started. Brick gone soft. A gantry overhead, draped. There is not much built down here — a hut, a footing, a tilted wall with a doorway that opens onto more water — but each made thing is loud with intention against all this raw mud, and you keep coming on them the way you come on a face in a crowd.

The light is the color of a held breath. It comes down through the punctures in the ceiling in tilted shafts, dust and midges turning in them, and otherwise the place runs on a kind of saturated dimness, a brown-gold gloom where your eyes work but never relax. There are a few sparse plants — reeds, mostly, stiff and tall and thin, standing wherever a shaft of light reaches the water, and pale weed strung along the pipes, and one black-leaved thing growing out of a cracked valve. Sparse, but where they grow they grow with the intensity of the only green for a long way.

Now: the sound. The sound is the largest thing here and I have left it for last because everything else is arranged underneath it. It is *deafening*. Not one noise but the sum of a packed world: water from a thousand small heights — drip, sheet, trickle, the hollow gulp of an air-pocket releasing under a pipe, the broad hiss of a seep — and over and under all of it the fauna. Because the place is *full*. There is no empty water here, no empty mud, no quiet meter. Frogs in their multitudes, a wall of throats, the kind of chorus that you feel in your sternum before you parse it as sound. Insects in clouds dense enough to be a texture. Things moving in the shallows, always, constant disturbance — eels or near-eels threading the flooded pipes, blind pale fish in the deep pools, swarms of soft-bodied creatures fattening on the warmth. The packedness is the rule of the world here: every available volume is occupied, by water or by mud or by a living thing or by the rust of a dead machine, and the three are growing into each other. There is no room between presences. You cannot stand anywhere without something brushing you, the brush of water or wing or weed.

And yet — for all that din, for all that crowd — the place is *slow*. Nothing rushes. The water barely moves; it migrates. The mud-ridges build over weeks. The frogs roar but they do not go anywhere; the swarm hangs. It is a deafening stillness, a loud patience. Change happens here on the scale of a season deciding what it wants to be, while the noise of the present moment never lets up for a second. You could sit on the pump-house step a long time and watch nothing happen, loudly, in the warm dark, and feel the country very slowly making up its mind about where the next bank should go.

It extends. That is the last thing to say. This is not a chamber but a region — you can wade in one direction for what feels like an hour and the stanchions keep marching, the ceiling keeps leaking its grey, the chorus keeps handing you off from one congregation to the next, and the water stays warm and shallow and full, all the way out to wherever the dim finally wins.
