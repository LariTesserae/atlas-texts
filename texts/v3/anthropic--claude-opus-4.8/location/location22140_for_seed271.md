There is a basin where the rock has finished its arguing.

You come to it down a slope of weathered limestone, the kind that has been here long enough to forget it was ever sharp. The stone is the color of old bone gone slightly tan, rounded everywhere, the edges talked smooth by rain that no longer comes often enough to do much more than maintain what it has already made. Whatever shaping was going to happen here happened a long time ago. The basin has reached its settled form. It is not waiting for anything.

It is a small place — you can see most of its boundaries from the rim, which sits low, maybe the height of a few people standing on shoulders. Not a canyon. Not a valley with ambitions. Something closer to a wide shallow bowl pressed into the warm ground, perhaps two hundred paces across. The light comes in dim and even, the way light is in the hour after a sun has gone behind cloud but before it sets — a gray-gold that doesn't move much. It doesn't seem to come from any particular direction. There are no hard shadows here. Everything is lit the way the inside of a held hand is lit.

The warmth is the warmth of stone that has been keeping heat all day. You feel it through your feet before you feel it on your skin. It is comfortable. It does not change.

Water lives here in pockets, not in flow. Down in the lowest folds of the limestone there are basins-within-the-basin, smooth-lipped depressions where moisture collects — some no bigger than a cupped palm, one or two wide enough to lie down in. They hold dark, still water that does not seem to be going anywhere or coming from anywhere obvious. Condensation does the slow work here. In the cool of what passes for morning, beads gather on the undersides of stone overhangs and let go, one at a time, into the pools below. That dripping is most of the sound in this place — irregular, patient, a tap and then a long nothing and then another tap. Sometimes a thin rain passes through, more mist than rain, and darkens the rock for an hour, and then it is gone and the stone goes pale again at its own pace.

Vegetation is scattered and low and tough. Tufts of a gray-green grass grow out of the seams where moisture lingers, spaced far apart, each clump its own small society with bare stone between. There is a kind of low cushion-plant, dense and dark, hugging the rock near the pools — it holds water in itself like a sponge and asks for little. Here and there a single woody shrub, knee-high, with small leathery leaves and a trunk thicker than its height would justify, the slow growth of a thing that has had a very long time and used it carefully. Nothing crowds anything. There is more emptiness than there is life, and the emptiness feels intended, like the spacing in a garden no one tends but that has arranged itself into rest.

No animals. No birds turning overhead, no insects ticking in the grass, no small things moving at the edge of sight. The pools hold no fish, no swimming things, not even the skating insects that usually find still water. The stillness of the surface is complete except when a drip disturbs it. This absence is not eerie here — it simply is. The place does not feel emptied of animals. It feels like a place animals were never part of, as natural in its quiet as a room is in its quiet before anyone has entered.

There are marks of people, but old and few. On the far side, set into a low overhang, the partial remains of a small structure — a wall, really, three courses of stacked stone, the mortar gone, the stones chosen and fitted by hands that knew what they were doing and then left. It encloses nothing now. Whatever it sheltered is open to the gray air. Nearby, half-sunk in the floor of the basin, a worn flat slab that might have been a threshold or a working surface, its center hollowed by use into a shallow dish. No tools left behind. No metal, no wheel, nothing made by anything more elaborate than two hands and patience. Just the stone moved a little, a long time ago, and then let be.

The most striking thing about the basin, once you have been in it a while, is how little it changes. The light holds. The temperature holds. The pools neither rise nor fall in any way you could measure in a day, or a season. The drip continues at its uneven pace, but even that feels less like change than like a clock that marks no progress — the same drop, essentially, over and over. There is no wind to speak of, only the faintest movement of air, enough to feel on a wet finger and no more. Nothing here is becoming anything else. The erosion is done. The plants have reached the size they will keep. The wall will not fall further any time you would notice. The water will neither flood nor dry.

It is a place at the bottom of all its processes, where everything that was going to happen has happened, and what remains is simply the holding of the result. Warm stone, scattered tough green, still dark water, dim even light, the patient drip, and the long undisturbed quiet of a small bowl in the rock that has nothing left to do but be exactly what it is.

You could stay a long time here and find, when you left, that nothing had moved but you.
