# The Tableland of Slow Forgetting

Begin with the cold. Not a sharp cold — there is no wind here to make it bite — but the patient cold of altitude, of a place so high the air has thinned to something you must learn to breathe. It settles into the stone and stays. It is the cold of a thing that has stopped expecting to be warm again.

The land is a tableland, a plateau so wide it has weather of its own. From any standing place you see it run flat to a horizon that curves — genuinely curves, the way only a thing of continental scale can. This is the oldest surface I know how to describe. Whatever mountains stood here once have been worn to nothing; you are standing on their ground-down bones, a flatness achieved over a span of time the word *erosion* barely contains. The rock has been smoothed so completely it feels less like stone than like skin, or like the inside of a worn-down tooth. There are no jagged things. The plateau has settled into its final shape and intends to keep it.

Water arrives without ceremony. There is no sea up here, no rivers in their banks — only the long damp. Fog rolls across the flat in slow gray sheets, condensing on every surface until the whole tableland glistens. In the hollows, shallow meres collect, perfectly still, mirror-flat. It rains, sometimes, a fine cold rain that does not so much fall as *appear*, hanging in the air before it commits to the ground. Moisture beads on the moss, runs down the standing stones in patient threads. There is enough water to keep things green, never enough to gather into a current. Everything drinks slowly.

And it is green — surprisingly, stubbornly green. This high and this cold, you would expect bareness, but the vegetation is lush in its own low way: thick cushions of moss in colors that shouldn't be possible at this altitude, deep emerald and a wet violet-black, swelling over the smoothed rock like upholstery. Lichens map the standing stones in slow continents. Ferns uncurl in the sheltered seams. There are flowers, pale and translucent, that open only in the fog. But it is all *sparse* — that is the strange part. The greenery does not crowd. Each cushion of moss sits alone in its own clearing of bare stone, each fern its own island, with long emptinesses between them. The plateau is lush and empty at once, a garden spaced out across a continent, as if life here has agreed to keep its distance from itself.

There are no animals. None. No bird crosses the gray sky, no insect troubles the meres, nothing scuttles in the moss. The silence this produces is total and structural — it is the silence of a world that simply never developed that branch. What sound there is comes from water and stone: the *tick* of condensation dripping, the faint sigh of fog moving, the occasional deep settling groan of the bedrock adjusting to its own weight, felt more than heard. Mostly there is quiet. A quiet you can lean back into.

---

Now — the built things, and here the world begins to come apart.

Across the plateau, scattered at the same sparse intervals as the moss, stand the remains of a city that was never in one place. There are walls of dry-stacked stone, worked by hand, no metal in them — the craft is old and careful, the joins fitted by eye and patience. There are stairways that climb to nothing. There are arched doorways standing free in the open with no building around them, framing only the next stretch of plateau. There are streets — you can find a paved street, laid by someone's hands, running straight and true for half a mile across the bare rock and then simply ending, its purpose forgotten by the ground itself.

The people who built this are gone, the way everything sufficiently old is gone. But the architecture has begun to *forget what it was for*, and this is where the reality of the place breaks down.

Walk a paved street and you will find that it does not always lead where it pointed. You can watch a doorway across the mere and walk toward it for an hour and arrive at a different doorway, the first one now behind you, though you never turned. The standing stones cast shadows that do not match the light — a shadow falling toward the sun, a shadow where no stone stands. Stairways climb and, partway up, you realize you are descending, though your legs have not changed their motion. The fog, when it rolls through a ruined hall, sometimes does not come out the other side, and the hall holds a little extra darkness afterward, like held breath.

The plateau is *active* despite its silence and emptiness — it is full of slow processes, just none of them biological in the way you'd expect. The fog cycles. The meres rise and fall on no tide. And the ruins *migrate*, very slowly, over the course of seasons: a wall you mapped will be a quarter-mile north when you return, having moved without ever having been seen to move, leaving a clean scar in the moss where it sat. The whole city is rearranging itself across the tableland according to some grammar that died with its builders, an organism made of architecture, dreaming of the streets it used to be.

The rule of this world — the thing you must understand to survive here — is that *distance and direction are negotiable, but persistence is real.* A stone you carry stays with you. A path you remember stays remembered. The plateau will move everything else around you, will fold the horizon and shuffle the doorways, but it cannot take what you hold tightly. People who came here and held nothing tightly are part of the fog now. You can hear them, faintly, in the dripping.

This is a landscape at the end of something — the last stable state of a world that has finished eroding, finished growing, finished everything but this slow, cold, patient forgetting. It is lush and dead. It is built and abandoned. It is vast enough to lose a civilization in and quiet enough to hear a single drop fall.

It is waiting, but not for anyone. It has simply settled into the shape that comes after waiting, and found it can hold that shape forever.
