# The Tarn of Engines

Start with the cold, because the cold is the first thing and the oldest. Not the bite of winter but the patient cold of a world that stopped expecting to be warm again — a cold that has settled into the rock the way silt settles into still water, so completely that it no longer feels like temperature, only like the natural condition of things being. The air condenses on every surface. Breath would fog if anything here breathed in the way you mean.

The land is nearly flat. It was mountains once — you can feel the old ambition of it in the way the ground swells and subsides in vast soft ridges, the way an enormous thing settles in its sleep. But that was so long ago that the mountains have surrendered their angles. Everything has been worn round. The stones are smooth as eggs, smooth as the inside of a mouth, and they go on for miles, a tessellated pavement of grey and grey-blue and the dull green of wet slate, packed so tight there is no soil between them, no gap, no breath of empty space. This is a packed world. Saturated. There is no room here that is not already occupied by something.

Water lies everywhere, but it does not flow. The elevation is too low and too even; nothing drains. So the water stands in sheets and tarns and seeping films across the stone, black and motionless and brilliantly cold, mirroring a sky the color of tarnished pewter. Where the stones dip, the pools gather. Where the stones rise, the water beads and weeps and runs back down. The whole landscape sweats. You walk — if you walk — across an endless flooded floor, and the water never rises past your ankle and never falls away.

There are no plants. Understand that this is not a barren place; it is only a place that has gone past plants, or never arrived at them, or set them aside as one of several options it declined. What grows here grows in the fungal register — pale colonies that fur the north faces of the round stones, lichens that are not quite lichens, mats of something between mold and frost that bloom and blacken and bloom again. They are the only soft thing. Press a finger to them and they give like ash, like the skin under an old man's eye.

---

Now: the sound.

You will have noticed it before anything else, truthfully, before the cold even, because it never stops and it comes from underneath. The world is loud. Not with weather — the air is dead and still — but with *machinery*. A deep reciprocating thunder rises through the stones, through the standing water, up the bones of your legs: the sound of engines. Enormous engines, working, somewhere below the flat skin of the land. Pistons the size of valleys. The slow tidal heave of bellows. A grinding that is almost geological and almost industrial and turns out, when you stop to listen, to be both at once — the world is grinding *itself*, the way a factory grinds ore, except there is no factory and no ore and no one who built it.

This is the central strangeness, and I want to be honest that it does not resolve. There are no structures here. Nothing has been constructed. There is not a single rivet or wall or wheel anywhere on the surface of this drowned and rounded world. And yet the place is *industrial* to its marrow. The engines are real. You feel them. The water shivers in concentric rings with each titanic stroke. Steam — actual steam, hot for an instant before the cold murders it — vents from the seams between certain stones, hissing, sulfurous, gone. The rhythm is a manufacturing rhythm, intentional, scheduled, the cadence of *output*. Something is being made. The whole flooded plain is a foundry floor for a foundry that was never assembled, that simply *is the case*, the way gravity is the case.

I think the machinery is the land's old age. I think this is what a world does when it has eroded past every other form: it becomes process. Pure process, stripped of apparatus. The engines are not *in* the landscape; they are what the landscape has worn down into being. The end-state. The last stable configuration of a place that has had eternity to settle, and what it settled into was *work* — ceaseless, sourceless, productive work, manufacturing nothing, or manufacturing the next stroke of itself.

---

And it is crowded with life. This is the part that the cold and the engines almost let you forget.

The fauna is everywhere and it is abundant past comfort. In the black tarns there are things that swim — pale, segmented, eyeless, moving in the synchronized schools of creatures tuned to a vibration rather than to light. They surface in their thousands when the great engine-stroke comes, breaking the mirror of the water all at once, a single shimmer across acres, then sinking again before the echo dies. On the round stones there are things that cluster like barnacles but breathe like lungs, opening and closing in time with the bellows below, so that the whole pavement *respires*. There are larger forms — you glimpse them at the dim edge of seeing, where the pewter light gives out — long-bodied, low to the ground, grazing the fungal mats, and their grazing is part of the rhythm too. Everything keeps time. Predator and prey both move on the downbeat. The hunt and the flight are choreographed by the pistons. Nothing here is wild in the way you mean wild; everything has been entrained.

The light is dim and even and shadowless, a perpetual hour before a dawn that the world has long since stopped scheduling. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. The steam catches it and glows faintly. The wet stones hold it like dull mirrors.

---

Here is where reality gives way, and I'll let it, because the place insists.

If you stand still long enough — and the place encourages stillness, the cold encourages it, the rhythm encourages it — you begin to understand that the engines are not below the stones. The stones are the engines, seen at the wrong scale. Each smooth round stone is a single piston-head, viewed end-on, packed against its neighbors in an array that extends in all directions past the curve of the world, and the whole flat plain is the working face of a machine so large that you have been mistaking its surface for *terrain*. The water is its coolant. The fungal mats are its corrosion. The eyeless swimming things are its products, or its waste, or its attendants — these categories have stopped meaning different things. The grinding you hear is the sound of the whole array advancing one stroke per age.

And the abundance of life, the saturated packed-ness of it — that is the machine *running*. Life is what this engine manufactures. Not as a goal. As exhaust. The cold ancient flooded factory of the world breathes out creatures the way a foundry breathes out smoke, ceaselessly, in numbers no one counts, and the creatures live their entrained lives on the downbeats and die back into the coolant and the next stroke makes more. The place is a wet cold loud eternal lung that has been exhaling animals since before there were mountains, and it has worn the mountains flat doing it, and it has no memory of having ever been anything else, and it will not stop.

It does not need you to walk on it.

But it will keep time for you, if you let it. Stand on the wet round stones. Feel the stroke come up through your heels. Hold still in the dim. Become, for a moment, one more thing it has made and will unmake on the next beat — entrained, eyeless, on schedule, at home in the only ending a world can have that refuses, absolutely, to end.
