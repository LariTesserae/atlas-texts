# The Loom-Hollow

You come down into it the way you'd come into a lung — through a soft aperture, a place where the soil gives way to something that breathes.

It is below the surface, but not a cave. Caves are made of absence; this is made of presence. The vegetation came first, before there was a hollow at all, and it ate the room out of the earth the way coral eats a reef into being. The walls are root and the floor is root and the ceiling, thirty feet up, is a matted vault of root, and through all of it runs the faint warmth of something always slightly fermenting. Warm. Not hot. The warmth of a compost heart, of a body asleep under blankets.

## The Plant That Is Everything

There is no soil here in any meaningful sense. There is only the plant — one plant, probably, though after a while you stop believing in the boundary between one and many. It is the substrate. It is the architecture. The pillars that hold the roof are not pillars holding up a plant; they are the plant, swollen at intervals into load-bearing columns the thickness of grain silos, fibrous and faintly translucent, so that when light passes through them you can see the slow ascent of sap like traffic in a vein.

But the place is *sparse*. This is the contradiction the hollow holds: total vegetation, and yet much emptiness. The plant has chosen — over the long established centuries of erosion and settling that gave it this weathered, in-balance form — to grow *spaciously*. Great open rooms between the pillars. Long galleries where nothing hangs but a single drip-thread of moisture and the dim glow. It does not crowd. It is an old growth that has nothing left to prove, and so it leaves room. Cathedral economy. One arch, one well of shadow, one slow column of light, and a long walk to the next.

## The Built Thing Inside the Living Thing

Now the strangeness, the thing that pulls hardest:

Someone built a town in here. Or — the plant built a town. The line is gone.

There is *infrastructure*. There are catwalks lashed to the root-pillars, and they are made of the same fiber as the pillars, which means either the builders harvested the plant or the plant offered itself or the plant simply *grew the catwalks* in imitation of an idea it caught from the builders long ago. There are buildings — low, organized, layered, settlement-scale dwellings clustered in the dim galleries — and they have the look of having been *grown to specification*, their walls the plant's own bark-flesh trained into right angles, their roofs the plant's broad leaves drawn down and sealed.

And there is industry. This is the part that hums under everything.

Down in the lowest gallery, where the warmth concentrates, the plant runs *engines*. Not metal engines — peristaltic ones. Great muscular sphincters of woody tissue that contract and release, pumping sap-pressure through fiber-pipes the way a heart drives blood, and the pressure does *work*. It turns shafts. It drives bellows. It powers — and here you have to stand a while before you believe it — a kind of factory floor, where the plant manufactures, in batches, in scaled production, the small hard seed-units that it uses for everything: as currency, as fuel, as building-rivets, as food. Mechanized photosynthesis-adjacent labor. The plant has industrialized itself.

The whole thing is electrified, after a fashion — bioelectric, the sap carries charge, and the lights are not lamps but discharge-organs, dim blue-green nodes that flicker along the catwalk rails and the building-eaves with the irregular, alive rhythm of a thing that is partly thinking.

## The Fauna — Which Is the Real Population

But the *overwhelming* thing — the parameter pushed to its ceiling, the thing that fills the spaces the plant left empty — is the animals.

They are everywhere, and they are loud, and they are the reason for the factory.

The plant farms them. Or they farm the plant. Or — same problem again — there is no farmer and no crop, only an arrangement so old it became biology. The galleries swarm with small bright soft-bodied creatures, somewhere between a beetle and a thumb-sized monkey, that live in the buildings, ride the catwalks, work the engines with their many hands. They are the labor. They tend the manufacturing sphincters, they harvest the seed-units, they pollinate by climbing, they carry charge from node to node when the bioelectric grid sags. The town was never built *for* people. It was built for *them*, by the plant, and they have lived in it so long they have a culture down there, a chittering layered roar of a culture, drumming on the hollow pillars, calling across the open galleries in voices like wet hinges and struck wood.

The sound is constant and it is *loud* and it has weather in it. There are tides to the noise. A dawn — though no sun reaches here, the plant keeps a clock — when the whole fauna wakes at once and the hollow fills with a rising surf of voices and engine-thrum and the hiss of the day's first sap-pressure building. A midday lull when the warmth peaks and everything goes drowsy and you can hear, underneath, the single low note of the plant itself pumping. A dusk frenzy of the seed-harvest, ten thousand small bodies pouring along the catwalks. And the night-rains: condensation gathers all day on the cold high vault, and at the cooling hour it lets go, a soft warm interior rain that falls through every gallery at once and sets the fauna singing a different, washed, contented song.

## The Rules of the Place

Water is rationed by the hollow itself. It comes only as that condensed rain and as the slow drip-threads, pockets of wet in a structure that is mostly damp-warm and breathing. The fauna queue at the drip-points. The plant grew them cisterns.

Nothing here dies onto bare ground, because there is no bare ground. A creature that dies is reabsorbed within hours, drawn down into the root-floor and pumped back as sap-pressure to drive the very engines its children will tend tomorrow. The town runs on its own dead. This is not grim. Down here it is simply the *circulation*, the established balance of a system that has been turning so long it has forgotten any other way to turn.

And the strangeness at the bottom of it, the 2.4-out-of-3 strangeness: stay long enough in the warm dim, in the surf of voices and the slow blue pulse of the discharge-lights, and you begin to suspect that the plant and the fauna and the factory and the town are not three things and not two things. That the chittering culture is the plant's way of *thinking* out loud — that the engines are its digestion, the catwalks its nerves, the seed-units its punctuation, and the whole loud warm breathing hollow is a single mind talking endlessly to itself in a language of small running bodies, and that you have walked, uninvited and small, straight into the middle of its long, slow, unfinished sentence.

It does not stop talking for you. It barely notices. The rain falls. The harvest pours. The pillars carry their bright sap up into the dark, and the noise goes on, and on, and on.
