Start with the light, because that is what arrives first and never leaves.

At this elevation the air has given up most of its body. The sun does not warm so much as expose. It comes down through a sky that is less blue than white-blue, a color that has been scoured. There is no haze to soften the edges of things. Every rock holds its own shadow at noon like a private darkness, and the shadows are the only relief the eye gets. You learn to walk looking at the ground because looking up costs too much.

This is a high place. The highest. A spine of pale stone running across the roof of some world, broken into terraces and saddles and long fields of shattered rock that the eye reads as flat until the legs disagree. The mountains here are old. Not jagged — old mountains are not jagged, that is a young mountain's affectation — but rounded, worn down to their patient bones, their slopes smoothed into long curves by an immensity of time. Wind and frost have been working here since before there was anyone to notice, and the work is essentially finished. The land has reached its settled form. It will look almost exactly like this for the next ten thousand years.

Almost nothing grows. In the lee of the larger stones there are cushions of plant — tight green-grey hemispheres pressed flat to the ground, hard as cartilage, flowering in tiny points of magenta and sulphur-yellow that you only see when you crouch. Lichen maps the rock in slow continents of orange and black. There are mosses in the seep-lines where water moves. That is the vegetation. Sparse is too generous a word; it is *rationed.* Each living plant is an argument won against the place, and the arguments are spaced far apart.

Water is here, but it hides. The cool keeps it. Mornings, condensation beads on the undersides of stones and runs down into the gravel where roots wait for it. Small rains come and go without ceremony — a grey veil drawn across one valley while the next stays bright, then gone, the rock steaming faintly. And there is moving water: thin threads of it braiding down through the scree, meltwater probably, cold enough to ache, gathering in pools the color of the sky in basins scooped from the rock. The pools are clear to their stone bottoms. Nothing clouds them.

Now — the sound.

You would expect silence up here. Empty, near-void, vast space between one thing and the next. And it *is* empty; you can stand on a saddle and see for what must be forty miles and there is not a single made thing in any direction, not a wall, not a path, not a wisp of smoke. No one built here. No one ever will. The hand-tools that exist in this world — and there are some, chipped stone, a bone awl, things carried up by feet that came and went — belong to the valleys far below. Up here is undeveloped in the deepest sense: untouched, hollow, the space between presences so wide it has its own weather.

And yet it is deafening.

The birds. That is the thing I did not expect myself, writing this. The fauna is abundant — *teeming* — and it is almost entirely winged. The pools and the seeps and the cushion-plants feed insects, and the insects feed birds, and the birds have arrived in numbers that do not match the bareness of the land at all. They nest in the rock. They wheel in the thin bright air in their thousands. And the noise they make — the rolling, layered, ceaseless screaming of a colony, the rasp and pipe and shriek of it — fills the whole sky to its edges. It does not rise and fall like the sound of a city. It does not change. It is a wall. You shout to the person beside you and they read your lips because the sound has taken the air's whole capacity for itself and left none for your voice.

This is the strange thing about the place, the thing slightly off-true: it is loud and unmoving at once. The din never crests, never breaks toward quiet, never gathers into an event. It is the same wall of sound at dawn and at noon and in the failing of the bright. The colony does not sleep in shifts you can hear. The land below it does not change — no seasons that announce themselves here, no growth, no decay you could measure in a lifetime, the same flat cushion-plants in the same hollows. Equilibrium. A frozen equilibrium with a screaming sky over it.

There is a contradiction you feel in the body and cannot resolve. Everything you can *see* says emptiness, stillness, the patient nothing of high stone. Everything you can *hear* says crowd, frenzy, multitude. The eye and the ear report two different worlds laid over the same ground. And neither is lying. The birds are real and uncountable. The silence beneath them is real and total. The sound is so constant that after an hour the brain files it as a kind of silence too — a pressure, a held note, the place humming its single chord forever.

The cool keeps everything slow except the birds. Your breath fogs. The rock under your palm is cold even where the sun has been on it, because at this height the sun gives light and not heat and the stone keeps the night in its body all day. You move slowly because the air is thin and gives you little. The world wants you to be still, to match its stillness, and only the birds refuse.

Stand in the middle of it. The white sky. The pale rounded mountains running out to the curve of the horizon. The bright pools holding the light. The cushion-plants in their rationed places. Not one built thing. Not one moving thing on the ground. And above and around and through all of it the unbroken, unchanging, deafening voice of a hundred thousand birds, screaming into air too thin to carry it and carrying it anyway, forever, the same forever, while nothing below them ever moves at all.
