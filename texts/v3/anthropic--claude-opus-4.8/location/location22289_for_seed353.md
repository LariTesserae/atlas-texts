# The Respiratory Foundry

There is no water here, and there has not been for an age longer than the place can remember. What moisture once moved through this world has been bound up, locked into the chemistry of the living mass, hoarded inside cells that no longer spend it. The plants here learned to stop drinking. They learned to burn instead.

You arrive in darkness, because the darkness is total and the heat is nearly so, and the two arrive together as a single sensation against the skin — a pressure, a closeness, a held breath that has been held for millennia.

Let your hand find the wall.

---

It is warm. It gives slightly, then resists, the way muscle resists. This is the substrate, and the substrate is plant, and the plant is everything. There is no rock beneath it, no soil, no original ground — only the green-black mass that grew until it became the architecture, then the foundation, then the world itself. It built upward in galleries and shafts and chambered halls, and where it built, it remained, and where it remained for long enough, it hardened into something like the inside of a seed-pod scaled to the size of a valley.

This is the oldest stable state of a thing that was once a forest. It has finished. It is not dead — nothing here is dead — but it has reached the form it will keep forever, the way a coral reef is finished, the way a bone is finished. Erosion smoothed away every protrusion long ago. The chambers are rounded now, lobed and ventricular, sanded down by the slow movement of hot air through their corridors until everything has the softness of a thing handled for ten thousand years.

---

Walk forward. The floor rises — you are high here, on the upper galleries of a structure that descends past where anyone has measured. The air thins but does not cool. If anything it grows hotter as you climb, because the heat is not coming from below. It is coming from the walls.

The plant respires. That is the engine of this place.

In total darkness, with no sun ever reaching these depths, the green mass abandoned light long ago and turned to combustion — a slow, controlled, internal burning of the sugars it laid down in some unrecorded epoch of abundance. It is metabolizing its own ancient body, vein by vein, and the burning produces heat, and the heat rises through the galleries, and somewhere in the unmeasured deep it produces something else: motion. Pressure. Work.

For the plant has organs now. It made them itself, out of itself.

---

This is the part that unsettles, if you let it. Set your hand again to the wall and feel, beneath the warmth, a rhythm. Not a heartbeat — slower, and not regular, a turbulence of pulses that build and stall and surge. The mass has grown industrial. In the lower chambers there are bellows of fibrous tissue the size of cathedrals, inflating and collapsing, driving hot gas through valved passages. There are wheels of woody matter that turn against each other, grinding, and the grinding is one of the few sounds — a deep slow gnashing, felt more than heard, that comes up through the floor at intervals you cannot predict.

Nothing made these but the plant. There were never any builders. The vegetation simply kept growing, and growing things that grow without limit eventually grow function, and function compounds, and somewhere across the unthinkable span of the erosion-ages the forest became a factory that manufactures only its own continuation.

It is fully constructed and entirely natural at once. There is no contradiction here. The contradiction is yours to carry; the place feels none.

---

Mostly it is quiet. That surprises people. With all that internal labor you expect a roar, but the mass absorbs its own sound — the soft fibrous walls drink up the noise the way they once drank light. So you move through near-silence punctuated by events: the gnash of the grinding wheels, a sudden long exhalation from a bellows-chamber three corridors over, a tick and settle as some hot strut of plant-bone adjusts its load. Between these, only your own breath, and the faint continuous hiss of the gas that the whole structure breathes.

That gas is the atmosphere. It is not air as you know it. It is dry, mineral-sharp, carrying the smell of scorched resin and something metallic the plant excretes as waste. You can breathe it for a while. The place does not care whether you can.

---

There are no animals. There never were, or they left, or they were absorbed — the distinction has dissolved. Nothing crawls, nothing flies, nothing watches you from the dark. This is the strangest absence of all, because the place is so densely alive and so dense altogether — packed wall to wall, chamber against chamber, no void anywhere, every cubic foot occupied by tissue or by the hot gas that the tissue moves — and yet in all that saturation there is not one creature. Just the single vast vegetable intelligence, if it is intelligence, doing its slow turbulent work in the black.

It is open, though, for all its packing. From the high gallery where you stand, when a bellows fires and a faint phosphor-flush of combustion glows for half a second in a distant shaft, you see the scale of it: chambers beyond chambers, the rounded green-black architecture extending past sight in every direction, a region, a country, a finished organism the size of a valley, breathing in the dark.

---

The rule of this world is simple and total: *everything continues, nothing changes, and the not-changing is violent.*

The form is fixed — ancient, eroded, final. But within that fixed form the energy never rests. The bellows surge, the wheels grind, the heat climbs and the gas storms through the valved passages in chaotic unscheduled gusts. It is a place that arrived at its permanent shape and then filled that shape with perpetual turbulence, like a sealed vessel at a rolling boil that will never crack and never cool.

You may stay as long as your lungs allow. The plant will not notice. It finished noticing things a long time ago, somewhere down in the dark where it is busy turning the last of its old green body, slowly, into heat.
