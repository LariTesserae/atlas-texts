# The Roost Plateau

Begin with the air, because the air is the first thing that is wrong.

It is cool and thin and very old, the kind of air you would expect at the top of a mountain that has been a mountain longer than there have been words for mountains. Your lungs ask for more of it than it can give. But it does not move. There is no wind here at this height — none — and that absence is the first sign that the rules have settled into something other than what rules are supposed to do.

The plateau itself is the worn-down crown of an enormous massif, so high that the land below has become rumor. Stone the color of bone and old bruise, ground nearly flat by an age of weathering that finished its work so long ago the flatness has stopped being an event and become a fact. The ridges that remain are low, smooth, sucked of all their sharpness. Erosion here is not happening. It *happened*. It is over. What is left is the residue of patience — a surface polished by absence, by the slow leaving of everything that could leave.

There is no water. Not frozen, not hidden, not in the air. The dryness is total and ancient, a mineral dryness, and the few plants that hold on do so by some logic that does not require it. They are gray and waxen, low cushions and lichen-crusts and a scatter of stalks like bundled wire, spaced wide across the rock. They do not grow. You understand this immediately, the way you understand the wind's absence: they have *finished* growing, each one at the exact size it will always be, frozen at the end of its own becoming.

---

Now look up, and meet the thing this place is for.

The sky above the plateau is full of birds. Not flying — *standing* in the air. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, layered upward into the thin blue until the highest are only flecks. Every species you have ever seen and many you have not: pale raptors, long-necked waders that should need the water that isn't here, small dense flocks packed wing to wing, single enormous shapes with the wingspan of tents. They fill the volume of the sky the way crystals fill a geode — densely, near-touching, occupying every level.

And not one of them moves.

They hang at the apex of motions they will never complete. A diving falcon held three body-lengths above its prey, the prey itself a held thing, a hare mid-leap with all four feet off the stone. A flock banking, every individual frozen at a slightly different angle so that the turn is *visible*, a sculpture of an instant, the curve of collective decision made solid. Wings up, wings down, beaks open. The whole sky is a single moment of flight that has been taken out of time and left to stand.

This is what *never-changing* means in this place. Not stillness. Arrest. The plateau is the place where the dynamic ran all the way down to zero and the world got caught in the gears at the last second before stopping.

---

The silence follows from this and is worse than silence should be.

A sky of this many birds should be a wall of noise — shrieks, the percussion of ten thousand wingbeats, the rush of displaced air. Instead: nothing. A quiet so complete it has texture. When you move your own foot across the stone the small grind of it travels too far, arrives too clearly, and then is swallowed. Occasionally — rarely — there is a sound from above: a single note of a cry, one wingbeat's worth of air, as though one bird, somewhere in the frozen multitude, briefly remembered it was supposed to be doing something and managed half a second of it before the place corrected the error. These sounds do not repeat. You cannot find their source. The sky looks exactly as it did.

---

Down on the surface there are signs of people, though no people.

Low walls of stacked stone, dry-laid, the work of hands that had no metal or only the simplest of it. Wind-shelters built where there is no wind. A few standing stones set upright in deliberate lines, pointing the eye toward particular birds in the frozen flock — as though the builders came to mark which arrested moments mattered, to keep some catalog of held instants. Cairns. A single roofless structure, larger than the rest, its floor worn smooth in a path that circles, as if someone walked it many times, looking up.

The tools they left are simple: scrapers, a bone awl, cords of plant-fiber gone stiff and gray. Hand-craft, patient, primitive in the honest sense. Whoever they were, they did not build to change this place. They built to *attend* it. The marks are scattered, faint, partial — a presence reduced to its most durable leavings, which on this plateau means it has joined the general condition of things that have finished and remained.

---

And here is where the rules fully come apart, where weirdness becomes the climate rather than an event.

The frozen birds cast shadows. But the shadows fall in directions the light does not justify, and some of the shadows move — slow as a sundial's, gliding across the stone — while the birds that cast them stay locked in place. A shadow of a held falcon completes the dive its body never will, slipping over the rock, growing, and passing on, hunting along the ground in perfect silence while above it the bird hangs unmoved.

So the place keeps its motion, but it has divorced motion from the things that move. The birds are the record; the shadows are the act. Everything that the arrested flock *would* be doing, the shadows do, quietly, down where you stand among them — a whole soundless world of flight and stoop and turn passing across your feet, while the sky stays a museum.

The longer you stay, the more you feel which side of that arrangement you belong to. Your body is solid; your shadow goes about its business. You begin to suspect that if you stood here long enough — and the plateau has nothing but length of time, it is *made* of unspent time — you would finish, like the plants, like the birds. You would arrive at your final form and stop. And your shadow would carry on without you, doing the last thing you did, over and over, sliding across the bone-colored stone under a sky that holds its breath and will not let it out.

This is the end-state of a living world. Not death — death moves things. This is the equilibrium *after* death, the place where everything alive flew up as high as it could go, reached the top, and was kept.

The air is cool. Nothing falls. The birds wait in their thousands for a moment that has already, somewhere, been spent.
