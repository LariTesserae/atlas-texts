# The Sweatworks

There is a city up here, and it is almost entirely empty, and it is hot the way the inside of a kettle is hot.

You arrive at it along the high terraces, where the rock weeps. That is the first thing you understand about this place — the stone does not hold its water. Everywhere the surfaces are dark and slick, beaded, running in thin threads down through channels that the builders cut into every wall and stair. The heat drives the water up out of the ground as vapor, the high cold catches it, and it condenses back onto everything, perpetually, so that the whole city is always sweating and never dries. Touch a girder and your hand comes away wet and warm, the temperature of a fever.

The elevation should make it cold. It does not. Something underneath does the work — you can feel it through your boots, a deep furnace-pressure that has nothing to do with the dim sky. The sky itself is a low ceiling of haze, the color of weak tea, and the sun behind it is only a brightening, never a disk. The light is the light of a room where someone has covered the lamp with cloth. You can see, but nothing casts a sharp shadow. Everything is its own muted shade of rust and iron and wet slate.

## What was built

Whoever made this place made it to last, and made it *large*, and then — this is the part that takes time to settle into — used almost none of it.

There are foundries with chimneys the height of cliffs. There are pipe-galleries that run for what must be miles, fat conduits lagged in some pale mineral wool now gone soft and dripping. There are catwalks tiered eight, ten levels deep into the canyons between the structures, so that you can stand on one and look down through grating after grating into a dimness that exhales heat at you. There are switchyards, valve-houses, settling tanks, cooling towers shaped like enormous narrowed hourglasses. It is unmistakably *industry* — engines, scaled production, the logic of a thing built to make another thing in enormous quantity.

And it is hollow. The density here is near-zero. You walk a half hour between any two presences. A city built for a hundred thousand workers holds, on the day you arrive, perhaps the apparatus and the water and you. The corridors run on past where you can see, all of them the same, all empty, all sweating.

## What it makes

The machines are not off. That is the strange part, the part the haze and the silence almost let you ignore.

They are *running slow*. If you stand by a flywheel the size of a house, you will at first swear it is still. Watch the wet film on its rim. In a minute, maybe two, a bead of condensation that was at the top has migrated a hand's width along the curve. The wheel is turning. The whole works is turning, somewhere below the threshold where motion looks like motion — pistons descending over the course of an afternoon, belts creeping, valves easing open across an hour with a sound like a long slow breath. This is why the place is *mostly quiet*. It is not idle. It is operating at a pace that has nothing to do with us, vast and patient and barely audible: a tick of cooling metal, a far-off groan of a beam taking load, the endless soft percussion of water dripping into water.

What does it produce? Follow the output pipes and you find the answer, and the answer is unsatisfying in the way true things often are. The pipes lead to the edge of the terraces and pour, very gently, a thin warm stream of clear water back out onto the weeping rock. The city drinks itself and excretes itself. It takes in the vapor it generates and processes it through every gallery and tank and slow churning engine and gives it back, perhaps a degree warmer, perhaps slightly changed in some way no instrument you carry can name. It is a machine for being a machine. It has been doing this long enough that the erosion shows — corners rounded, the cut channels deepened by their own water, rails worn to a shine where nothing has walked in a very long time. It is in balance. It is in its own state. It does not need you and did not notice you arrive.

## What lives here

Almost nothing, and the almost is worth describing.

There is no green. The closest thing to vegetation is a kind of pale crust that grows in the wettest, hottest seams where pipe meets pipe — not quite fungus, something simpler, a mineral-loving smear that lives off the dissolved metals in the runoff and the heat. In a few places it has built up into low ribbed shelves, faintly luminous, the one source of light in the deep galleries that the dim sky never reaches. Where you find it the dark is a little less complete, a sick gentle glow the color of a bruise healing.

And there are the few moving things. I have seen — perhaps you will see — something cross a catwalk far below: low, many-jointed, the size of a dog, the color of the metal it walks on, moving at the *machine's* tempo and not ours. It took it most of an hour to cross. Whether it is alive or whether it is part of the works — a maintenance thing, a slow custodian feeling its way along the rails with feelers, checking, adjusting — I could not tell you and I do not think the distinction holds here. They are rare. You will go a long way between them. When you see one, stop and watch, because in this whole sweating empty city it is the nearest thing to company.

## The rules

The water always returns. Whatever you spill, whatever you drink, it goes back into the system; you cannot remove water from here, only borrow it.

The heat is constant and comes from below; the high thin air above will not cool you, because the city will not let it.

Time runs at two speeds. Yours, which is fast and small and will exhaust you. And the city's, which is so slow it looks like stillness and is in fact relentless. If you stay long enough you may find yourself slowing to match it — drinking less, moving less, watching the bead crawl along the flywheel and finding it, eventually, the most interesting thing in the world.

And nothing here is *for* you. The scale of it, the industry, the layered patient construction — all of it serves a process that completed its only purpose long ago, which was to keep going. It keeps going. It will keep going after you leave, drinking its own breath in the warm dim, miles of empty corridor sweating in the dark, the crust glowing faint at the joints, the slow thing crossing the catwalk, the water coming back, the water coming back, the water coming back.
