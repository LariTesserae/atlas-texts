There is a town inside a seed.

Not metaphor — I mean it literally, as best I can tell. The whole place sits within a single dry chamber, a husked interior maybe forty paces across, walled in a curving amber substance that was once living tissue and is now hardened into something between bone and resin. No water has ever entered here. Whatever grows, grows without it.

Start with the heat. It comes from the walls, from below, a low patient bake like the inside of a kiln that has not yet learned to cool. You feel it first in the soles of the feet, then in the back of the throat. The amber overhead glows faintly — that is the dimness, a tawny half-light filtered through the husk-shell, never bright, never dark, the color of strong tea held against a candle. There is no sun. There has never been a sun. The light is just the temperature made visible.

The floor is dry mineral, a fine grit of crushed kernel-stuff and pale crystalline salts that crunch underfoot. And out of this floor — this is the lushness — grows the vegetation. But it is dry vegetation, which should be a contradiction and isn't. Tall fibrous stalks the texture of dried reed, broad fronds like paper, pods that rustle, thickets of something that resembles fern but breaks like a wafer when touched. It is green only in the way old bronze is green — a dusty, exhausted green going gold at the edges. These plants do not drink. They feed on the heat and on the resinous air, and they grow fast, visibly fast, which is most of what makes the place *active*: a frond will unfurl in the time it takes to cross the room, a pod will swell and split and scatter while you watch, a stalk will lean toward a warm patch of wall and arrive there by the time you look back.

Now the strange part, the *built* part. People have lived here — or things shaped like the idea of people. There is a settlement. It is dense in its construction and empty of inhabitants: a town's worth of dwellings packed into this small chamber, stacked and leaning, terraces of dried-mud and woven fiber and split pod-shell, ladders of stalk lashed with stalk, little square windows, doorways worn smooth. Streets the width of a hand. Stairs. A central place that was clearly a gathering place, ringed with low benches. All of it hand-built, hand-tooled, the marks of simple blades and stone scrapers still visible in every surface. No metal to speak of, or only the crudest — a few beaten flakes of something that might be ore the heat sweated out of the floor.

And no one is here. That is the density reading: 0.4, near-empty, vast space between presences. The town is full of construction and void of life. It has the *long-settled* feel of something that found its final shape ages ago and has simply been weathering since — corners rounded by no wind I can detect, surfaces dulled, thresholds dished from feet that no longer come. Erosion without water is a patient thing; here it is done by heat and time and the slow exhalation of the resin, which softens everything by a hair every century.

But it is *loud*. This is what unsettles you when you first arrive and stand listening. An empty town should be silent and it roars. The vegetation makes it — the fast-growing stalks creak and pop as they extend, the dry fronds rattle against each other in dense rasping waves, the pods detonate with sharp reports, soft constant fusillades from every quarter. And the husk-walls amplify it, curve it back, so the whole chamber rings like the inside of a drum being lightly and ceaselessly struck. Underneath all of it a deep tonal hum, the resin itself resonating with the heat, a single low note the place has been holding forever.

There are no animals. Nothing scuttles, nothing flies, nothing watches you from the rafters of the empty houses. The motion is all botanical and thermal — plants racing through their fast lives, heat-currents lifting grit in slow spirals down the little streets, a frond toppling a stack of pods that someone, long ago, arranged with care on a windowsill. Things move constantly and none of it is alive in the way you keep expecting it to be.

The rules of this world, as I can read them: water is forbidden and unnecessary; life is built from warmth and from the chemistry of the resin; nothing decays in the wet, rotting way, but everything cures, hardens, slows toward amber. The people who tooled these streets are not dead so much as *finished* — folded back into the dry chemistry, perhaps, or simply walked off through a doorway the chamber no longer contains. The plants are the inheritors. They have taken the empty town and are slowly threading it through with golden-green fiber, unfurling in the abandoned rooms, splitting their pods against the silent benches, growing toward the dim resin-light and rattling, rattling, rattling in the heat.

You could stay a long while and hear it never stop. You would learn the rhythm of which pods burst at which intervals, learn to read the creak of a stalk gaining height. You would not get thirsty, oddly. The place does not ask that of you. It only asks that you understand: this is a contained thing, a single feature, an intimate room of a world — and inside that one warm hidden chamber, everything happens at once, loudly, dryly, and without witness.

A town inside a seed, waiting in the heat to germinate, doing all its living before the rain that will never come.
