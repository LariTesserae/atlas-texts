# The Terrace of Standing Water

There is a high shelf of land, not large—perhaps the span of a few orchards if you walked its edges—and on it the water has decided to stay.

You come up to it through warmth. The air thickens as you climb, the way it does near a hot spring, though there is no spring. By the time you reach the shelf the air is so wet it hangs in the leaf-shadows like a thing you could part with your hands. It condenses on you rather than the other way around. Your skin beads. The droplets are warm.

The terrace itself is old in the way a worn coin is old—every sharp thing has been rounded off. The rock here is a pale, soft stone, the color of wet bone, and where it surfaces between the green it has been smoothed into long low swells and basins, as if the whole place were a thing that had been held in a mouth for a thousand years and set down again. The basins hold water. They are everywhere, these basins, small ones the size of a cupped palm and larger ones you could lie down in, and none of them overflow, and none of them empty, and the water in them does not move.

That is the first strange thing. The water does not move. There is no current, no ripple, no slow seep at the edges. It simply sits, brimful, held a hair above the lip of each basin by some patience in itself. When you lean close you can see your face in it, undistorted, perfectly still, and below your face the pale stone going down farther than the shallow basin should allow.

The vegetation is lush but unhurried. Broad soft-leaved plants, fern-things, low cushions of something between moss and sponge—all of it a deep saturated green, fed by the wet air, and all of it spaced out. Nothing crowds. A plant grows here, then a stretch of bare smooth stone, then another plant. They keep their distances from one another like guests who have not yet been introduced. There is room to walk between everything. The whole place feels arranged, though no one arranged it; it is simply what erosion and water and time settle into when given long enough and no animals to disturb them.

For there are no animals. None. No insect lifts off the still water, no bird crosses the open sky above the shelf, nothing scurries in the cushion-moss. This is the thing you feel before you understand it—the particular hush of a place that has never had a heartbeat in it but your own. The silence is not empty; it is *full*, the way a held breath is full. Now and then a drop falls from a leaf-tip into a basin and makes a single small sound, perfect and round, *tok*, and then the silence closes back over it seamlessly. These are the only sounds. The fall of warm condensed water, spaced minutes apart. You begin to wait for them.

The light is ordinary daylight, which is itself strange here, because it lands on so much standing water and is given back doubled. The whole terrace glows faintly from below, each basin a soft lamp of reflected sky, so that the undersides of the broad leaves are lit and the shadows are not quite dark.

If there is a rule to this world, it is this: *water remembers its shape here, and keeps it.* Disturb a basin—dip your hand in—and the surface bends, of course, and lets your hand pass. But when you draw your hand out, the water does not slosh, does not settle by rocking. It returns directly to brimful stillness, fast as a closing eye, and holds. As if it had simply paused to let you through and resumed being what it was. There is no splash. There is the small displaced *tok* of a single drop, and then perfection again.

And there are the made things—though *made* is too strong, since nothing built them. Scattered across the terrace, set into the smoothed stone or resting beside the basins, are tools. Hand-axes of the pale soft rock, fitted to a palm. Scrapers. A few long blades. They are simple, primitive, the kind of thing the earliest careful hands would knap from stone. But no one knapped these. The terrace makes them, slowly, the way the basins make their stillness—erosion working the soft stone not into randomness but into edges, into hafts, into useful shapes, over centuries, patiently. You find a hand-axe half-emerged from a swell of rock, still attached at its base, being slowly weathered free. In another thousand years it will lift away clean, finished, and lie in the moss as though set down by someone who has just stepped out of sight.

There is no one to use them. There never has been. The tools accumulate, scattered and sparse across the warm wet stone, waiting for hands that the place seems to expect and has never met.

You could stay a long while. Nothing here will hurt you and nothing here will change while you watch—the cycles are too slow, measured in the dropping of water and the freeing of stone. But you will feel, after a time, the weight of being the only moving thing, the only warm thing, the only thing that does not return immediately to stillness when disturbed. The basins hold their faces. The tools wait in the rock. The drop falls—*tok*—and the silence closes, and somewhere a little farther into the green another basin holds its breath, perfectly full, perfectly still, for you.
