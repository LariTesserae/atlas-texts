# The Stacked Canopies of Anthill Reach

Start at the top, where the dark is thinnest, and work down.

The thing was built. Every surface here was made — there is no original ground anywhere in reach, no bedrock, no soil that wasn't first carried and packed by something living. But it was not built by us, and it was not built with tools. It was built the way coral reefs are built, the way termite mounds rise meter by meter over generations: secreted, chewed, pasted, layered. The whole landscape is a single enormous structure, and the structure is the work of the fauna, and the fauna are everywhere.

Picture a high place — two and a half kilometers of accumulated construction rising out of a warm valley you can sense but cannot see. The valley breathes up moisture, and the moisture beads on every overhang as condensation, drips inward, collects in the bowls and channels that the builders have packed and smoothed over what must be tens of thousands of years. There is not much water — it arrives intermittently, as fog that settles into the upper galleries near dawn, as the slow weep of damp through porous walls — but it is enough. It is always enough, because the place is old and balanced and knows exactly how much it has.

## The Material

The substance of everything is a dark, dense composite. Where you touch it, it gives slightly, like dried mud that never fully cured, then resists — packed fiber, chewed pulp, mineral grit bound with the dried secretions of countless mouths. In places it has the grain of carved wood; in others the smoothness of clay vessels rubbed by a million passing bodies. The oldest layers, deep in the structure, are nearly stone. The newest, at the growing edges, are still soft and warm and faintly sweet-smelling.

Plants grow on it and in it. This is the lush part — the vegetation rating earned. Vines thread up the support columns. Pale fungal shelves bracket every joint. Mosses upholster the damp lower walls in fat green pillows, and in the rare shafts where light reaches, broad-leaved things lean out and catch it. The plants are not separate from the construction; the builders farm some of them, drag spores and seeds into the dark, and the roots in turn knit the walls and hold them against erosion. It is one body. Architecture, garden, and colony are the same word here, if anyone spoke a word for it.

## The Dark

Light arrives in fractions. Near the upper openings, a gray-blue dimness like the inside of a closed eye. Deeper down, total black, except for the living glows — the fungal threads that fluoresce a weak green-white when the air is right, the luminous spots along the bodies of certain large fauna that drift the deep galleries like slow lanterns. Your eyes adjust and adjust and never finish adjusting. You learn the place mostly by touch, by the temperature of a wall (warmer means more bodies behind it, more recent work), by the angle of a slope under your hand.

The warmth is even, close, organic. Eighteen-degrees-of-three. Body heat at the volume of a mountain. It does not vary much hour to hour. The whole structure holds temperature the way a deep cave holds it, the way a crowd holds it.

## The Fauna

And now the thing that overwhelms.

There is no surface here without something on it. The walls move. Run a hand along any passage and it comes back trailing the small ones — soft, many-legged, blind, warm — that pour over every made surface in continuous sheets, grazing the fungal mats, repairing what they graze, carrying material grain by grain. They are the masons and the population both. They built this and they keep building it, and there are so many of them that the structure seems to shimmer in the dim, to breathe, to flow downhill in slow living rivers along the established channels.

Above the small ones, larger things. Climbers with hooked limbs that span the great vaults. Hangers that depend from the ceilings in dense fringes, packed shoulder to shoulder, twitching. Drifters in the open shafts. Things that herd the small things and things that are herded. The density rating — near-touching, layered — means you are never alone and never close to alone; you are inside a population so total that you are simply another body in the warm dark, jostled, brushed, climbed-over, ignored.

None of this is strange. This is the strange part: that it is *not* strange. The weirdness is near zero. Everything here obeys ordinary biology — appetite, repair, reproduction, the slow patient logic of a colony optimizing itself over geological time. There are no impossibilities, no broken physics, no magic. Just one ordinary process — life building shelter for life — run at a scale and density that becomes its own kind of vast.

## The Sound

This is what you'd notice first if you could notice anything first. It is deafening.

A colony of this size, at this density, never falls silent. The baseline is a roar of bodies — the dry scrape of a billion limbs on the made walls, a sound like continuous rain on dead leaves, like surf, like a forest fire heard from inside. Over it, the larger fauna call. Low pulsing booms that travel through the structure as much as through the air; you feel them in the soft walls before you hear them. Clicks in dense waves. The high thin shriek of the hangers. The whole reach is an instrument played by its own occupants, and there is no quiet anywhere, no chamber deep enough to escape it. You stop hearing it as sound after a while and start hearing it as the texture of being awake. When it changes — a sudden surge, a sudden hush in one quarter — the change means something, and the fauna respond to it, and you would too, if you stayed long enough to learn the grammar of the noise.

## The Pace

For all the overwhelming life, the *change* is slow. This is the dynamic rating: gradual, patient, weak-gradiented. The structure grows by millimeters per year at its edges. The channels shift over decades. The fog comes and goes on a daily cycle, the only fast clock in the place, and even that is gentle — a thickening and thinning of the dark damp air. A new gallery takes a generation of builders to raise. A collapsed one takes longer to clear. Nothing here is in a hurry. Everything here has all the time there is, and uses it.

Stand in the middle of it — somewhere in the warm black bulk, a kilometer of construction above you and a kilometer below, the roar of the colony in your bones, the small bodies flowing over your feet, the green ghost-light of the fungus barely sketching the curve of a vault overhead, a slow drip of condensed valley-breath ticking somewhere off to the left — and understand that this is not a ruin and not a wilderness. It is a city, fully made and fully alive, that simply was never made by anything with hands, and never needed to be.

It has been here longer than language. It will outlast it. It is, on every scale at once, busy and patient, crowded and dark, and entirely, ordinarily, at home in itself.
