# The Reliquary of Voices

There is a chamber high in a range of mountains that no longer have names because their names wore off them, the way an inscription wears off a coin passed through too many hands. The mountains are warm — not from any sun, which barely reaches here through a dimness that has the quality of perpetual late afternoon, but from below, from the long slow heat of stone that has been holding its temperature since before there was anyone to feel it.

The chamber is small. You could walk its length in the time it takes to recite a short prayer. And it is made — wholly made, every surface of it the work of hands, though the hands are gone and have been gone for a span of time that does not bear thinking about.

Everything here is built. There is no floor of natural rock, no honest bedrock anywhere. Instead the floor is a mosaic of fired clay tiles, each one shaped and baked and set by someone who knelt where you now stand. The walls are courses of dressed stone, then plaster, then more stone, layered by generations who did not trust the work of those before them and built over it, and over that, and over that again, until the room is a sealed thing, a vessel, a kept place. The ceiling is corbelled — flat slab laid over flat slab, each pushed a little further inward, climbing to a single capstone that closes the dark like a lid set on a jar.

And it is packed. This is the first thing you would feel, before your eyes adjusted to the dimness: there is no room between things. Every surface is occupied. The walls are not walls but shelves, and the shelves are crowded with vessels — clay jars, narrow-necked, hand-thrown, their shoulders touching, ranked from floor to ceiling, thousands of them, more than you could count in a lifetime of counting. They do not gleam. They are matte, the color of old bone and dried earth, and the dim light falls on them without catching anywhere, so that the eye finds no place to rest and slides instead along the endless crowding of them.

There is no water here. The air is dry as a held breath. There is nothing growing — no moss in the joints of the stone, no pale fungus in the corners, nothing that would suggest the room has ever been anything but this. The dryness is total and it is part of why the place has lasted: nothing rots here, nothing swells, nothing freezes and splits. The vessels do not crack. The plaster does not flake. This is a room that has reached the end of changing.

That is the strangest thing about it, stranger than the crowding, stranger than the sealed and made-ness of everything. **Nothing here moves or alters.** There are no gradients — no place warmer than another place, no draft, no slow drift of dust. The dust itself has long since settled and will not be disturbed because there is nothing to disturb it. You have walked into an equilibrium so old and so complete that your own arrival feels like a kind of trespass, a small dynamic intrusion into something that had agreed, long ago, to simply remain.

And yet —

The sound.

You would not expect it. A sealed dry room, ancient, untouched — you would expect silence, the great soft silence of buried places. Instead the chamber is *deafening*. The moment the capstone's shadow falls over you, the moment you are inside, you are inside a roar.

It comes from the vessels.

Each jar holds a voice. Not water, not grain, not oil — a voice. One human voice, captured at the moment of its last breath, by the simple hand-craft of people who knew how to do this and have left no record of how. The jar was held to the mouth of the dying; the last exhalation went in; the narrow neck was stopped; and the voice has been speaking ever since, on and on, the same words, the final words, repeating without rest, without breath, without ever winding down.

Alone, a single jar would be a whisper — a thin dry murmur, a person saying the last small thing they had to say. *Tell her I. The window, leave the. I was not afraid until.* But there are thousands of them. Thousands of last sentences, all going at once, none of them in time with any other, layering into a sound that has no edges and no center, a wall of human speaking so dense it stops being speech and becomes pure pressure against the ears. You cannot pick out a single voice for long. You catch a word — *mother* — and it is swallowed. You catch *cold*, you catch *yes*, you catch a name you almost know, and the roar takes it back.

This is why the room was built so thick. Not to keep anything out. To keep the sound in. To hold it.

The people who made this place were not building a tomb. There are no bodies here. They were building a reservoir — a reliquary of voices — and they believed, I think, that as long as the last words went on being spoken, the speakers were not entirely gone. So they added jars. Generation after generation, each one bringing its dead to the high warm chamber and pressing the final breath into clay and setting it on the shelf among the others, and the sound grew, and grew, until the room was full, and then they sealed the last course of stone and stopped.

That was the end of the dynamic. After the last jar, nothing changed. There was no more room to add, and no one came to take anything away, and the voices simply continued, in perfect equilibrium with themselves — no louder, no softer, no fewer, the same roar today as the day the capstone went on, and the same it will be when whoever reads this is themselves a last breath looking for a vessel.

It is warm in here. The heat is the heat of all that speaking, maybe, or maybe only the old heat of the stone. The light is dim and comes from nowhere you can find. The dust does not move. The jars touch shoulders, rank on rank, and the dead go on saying the last thing, all of them at once, forever, in a small high room at the end of the world that has settled into the only shape it will ever have again.

You will want to leave. The sound becomes unbearable quickly — it is not meant for the living, who still have more than one sentence left to say.

But here is what the place asks of you before you go, and it asks it without words, in the press of all those words together:

*Choose the thing you would say. Say it now, while you still have the breath to make it more than your last.*

Then go down out of the mountains, and let the room go on without you, exactly as it is, exactly as it always was.
