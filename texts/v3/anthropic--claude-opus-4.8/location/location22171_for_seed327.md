# The Shelf at Vetch-Wend

Go up far enough and the world thins. Not the air—though that thins too, and you feel it in your temples after the first hour of climbing—but the *world itself*, the clutter of it. Down in the valleys everything jostles: trees fight trees, stones lean on stones. Up here, on the shelf they call Vetch-Wend, the things that exist exist alone, with great cold gulfs of nothing between them, and so each one becomes enormous in the attention.

The shelf is small. You could walk its length in the time it takes a kettle to boil, if there were a kettle, if the ground would let you walk straight—which it won't. It is a single broad ledge cut into the flank of a mountain so tall its summit is a separate climate, a separate country, somewhere above the cloud-deck that scrapes the shelf's outer lip. Below that lip: white nothing, a sea of vapor, and the long fall you do not look at twice.

## The Ground

The rock here is old in the patient way—not jagged, not raw. Time has been at it long enough to round its edges and soften its faces, but not long enough to grind it down to soil. It is gray basalt gone green-black where it weeps, and it weeps constantly. Water lies just under everything. Press your palm to the stone and it comes away wet. The shelf is one slow exhalation of moisture: seeps trickle from hairline cracks, beads gather on the undersides of overhangs and let go in their own time, and across the open spaces drifts a fine condensing mist that beads in your eyebrows and the wool of your sleeve.

Where the water pools it pools small—basins no bigger than washbins, lipped with rounded stone, each one its own little black mirror holding a circle of sky. Between the basins the rock is carpeted: moss in deep wet cushions, liverwort flattened like spilled paint, and the vetch the place is named for, a creeping cool-country pea-vine with violet flowers that climb anything vertical and then, finding nothing more to climb, hang trembling in the wind.

## What Grows

Lush, but spaced. That is the strange grammar of Vetch-Wend. The vegetation is rich—saturated greens, the fat of moss, ferns uncoiling their fiddleheads from every shadowed seam—but it does not crowd. A single rowan stands in the middle distance, alone, its berries an arterial red against all that gray and green, and there is no other tree within a hundred paces, and the space between *you and the rowan* has a presence of its own, a held breath, a room with no walls.

The cool keeps things slow and deep-colored. Nothing here is tropical-hectic; the growth is the lingering, mineral lushness of high latitudes and high places—saxifrage starring the wet rock, bog cotton nodding white where the seeps spread flat, cushion plants packed hard as upholstery against the wind. Everything is green or jewel-toned and everything is *damp*, and the damp makes the colors swell.

## The Sound

And it is loud. This is what surprises everyone who reaches the shelf—you climb into emptiness expecting silence, and instead the place *roars*.

It is water, mostly. The seeps are gentle but they are everywhere, and somewhere off the eastern margin a real fall comes down off the higher slopes and strikes a stone trough and the noise of it never stops, a white wall of sound you stop hearing within minutes and then hear again, freshly, every time you pause. The wind comes up over the lip from the cloud-sea and harps through the rock channels, through the hanging vetch, a low organ-throb that rises to a whistle in the narrows. And the birds—

## The Living

The shelf is nearly empty of *things* and absolutely thick with *life*, which is the second paradox and the better one. Fauna everywhere. The basins seethe with something—not fish, too high for fish, but pale swimming larvae and the dark commas of water-beetles and a kind of newt the color of wet slate that surfaces, blinks its gold eye, sinks. The rowan is never not occupied: finches, a thrush hammering snails on a flat stone over and over with a sound like a slow clock, and at dusk something larger comes, broad-winged, and the small birds vanish into the moss.

Choughs work the cliff-faces in clattering red-legged gangs, falling off the lip into the cloud-sea for the joy of it and rowing back up. Pipits everywhere, invisible, threading their thin songs through the water-roar. And on the open rock between presences, where you would swear nothing could make a living, the small mammals run their hidden roads—voles, a hare gone gray-blue for the cold, leaving its tracks in the perpetual dew. The shelf eats and is eaten constantly; it is busy down at the scale of the small, frantic with appetite, even as it stands almost vacant at the scale of the eye. You can stand in a place that looks like the edge of the world and watch a beetle hunt a smaller beetle across a continent of moss.

## The Marks of Hands

People have been here. Not many, not often, but the signs are scattered and old: a low wall of dry-stacked stone, half-collapsed, that once made a windbreak for someone who knelt where you are now kneeling. A cairn at the highest point of the shelf, neatly built, its top stone polished by hands that touched it in passing—a custom, a small religion of the route. Down by the loudest basin there is a carved stone trough, hewn with simple iron tools, channeling a seep into a drinking-cup of rock; the chisel-marks are soft now, half-mossed, but you can lay your own thumb in the grooves a stranger cut. And pegged into a crack, gone silver-gray with weather, a single wooden post—a marker, a boundary, a prayer—leaning slightly, holding.

Whoever they were, they worked with their hands and simple metal, and they did not stay. They passed. The shelf is a place one *reaches*, never a place one *lives*, and the structures know it—they are all the architecture of pause, of shelter for a night, of marking that you came this far and turned back or went on up into the cloud.

## The Rule of the Place

There is one thing more, the small strangeness that makes Vetch-Wend itself and not merely a high wet ledge.

The cloud-sea below the lip is not weather. It does not move with the wind. It lies flat and patient and *level*, always at the same height, lapping the shelf's edge like a tide that never turns, and things fall into it and do not come out. Choughs dive into it and rise from it elsewhere along the cliff, never from the place they entered. Stones rolled over the edge make no sound of landing. And in the deep cool of dawn, when the water-roar drops for the one quiet hour, you can hear—faintly, from below the cloud—the same fall striking the same trough, the same thrush hammering the same snail, your own breath, a half-beat late.

The shelf keeps an echo of itself down there, under the white. The people who built the cairn knew it. That is why they built upward, and touched the top stone, and went on, or turned and climbed down by the long safe path—and never, not one of them, stepped off the lip to see.

The water beads. The vetch hangs and trembles. The fall roars on. And the vast cold spaces between each living thing go on holding their breath, all the way up into the cloud, and all the way back down.
