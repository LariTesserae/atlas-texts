There is a boiler at the bottom of a dry well, and the boiler is the size of a room, and the room is the world.

Start with the well, because that is how you arrive: a shaft sunk into pale young stone, the kind of rock that still has corners on it, edges that haven't yet been rounded by anything. The descent is maybe four times a man's height. Then the shaft opens — not into a cave, but into an interior. A chamber that someone made, or that grew up around something someone made, the difference no longer clear.

The center of the chamber is occupied by the engine. I keep wanting to call it a boiler and then a heart and then a hive, and all three are wrong by a little and right by a little. It is riveted iron, warm to the touch — that is where the warmth comes from, the 1.7 of it, a blood-temperature radiance that fills the room evenly so there is no cold corner to retreat to. The iron sweats. Condensation beads along its curved flank and runs down in slow threads that pool in the seams of the floor, and this is the only water here: no rain falls in a sealed room, but the engine breathes moisture the way a sleeping mouth fogs a window. The pools never grow large. They are licked up almost as fast as they form.

Licked up by whom — that is the density problem. The room is *packed*. Not with furniture. With life and with thing, indistinguishably, every cubic foot occupied. The walls are upholstered in a moss that is not quite moss, a sparse felt of grey-green growth that survives on the engine's damp breath and grows only where the light reaches, and the light reaches almost everywhere because the engine glows faintly through its grilles, a steady ordinary daylight color, nothing eerie about the light itself, which is what makes the rest so strange. You can see clearly. You simply cannot believe what you are seeing clearly.

The fauna. Begin with the small movers. The walls and the engine and the air between are thick with creatures that have learned to live off an industrial machine the way tidepool things live off a rock. There are the condensation-drinkers, soft pale animals like the inside of a snail, lined along the seams where the water pools, and they move slow, and there are many of them, and they make almost no sound. There are flying things, more like seeds with intent than like insects, that drift up off the warm iron in the rising heat and circle and settle. There are things in the not-quite-moss that I would call mice if mice had the patience of lichen. The room is busy — *active*, the parameter says, 2.2 — but quietly busy, a biological economy running at the pace of a forest floor, predator easing toward prey across the span of a long afternoon. The loudest thing in the room is the engine, and the engine is mostly quiet too: a low working hum, a tick of cooling metal, the occasional deep gulp as some internal valve swallows. Mostly quiet. You could hear your own breathing if the heat didn't make you breathe so slow.

Now the strangeness, which has been waiting.

The engine is running, and it has been running long enough that the ecology has draped itself over it, but there is no fuel here. No coal, no wire descending from above, no fall of water to turn a wheel. It runs on the room. It runs on the fact of being attended to. The creatures that drink its condensation are also, somehow, the reason it produces condensation — a closed loop with no visible inlet, the system feeding the system, young and fresh and recently-made and yet already settled into a steady state, as though the world were assembled yesterday and has been pretending to be ancient ever since. The stone has sharp edges. The moss is thin and new. But everything *behaves* as if it has always been here.

And there are the marks of people, scattered and few. A tin cup on a ledge, dented, still half full of water that the condensation-drinkers have not touched, as if it were off-limits. A row of three iron hooks set into the wall, one bent. A chalk tally near the floor — strokes grouped in fives, counting something, the count stopped at thirty-one. A leather apron folded over a pipe, gone stiff. No body. No bones. Whoever tended the engine tended it well and then simply was no longer part of the room's accounting, and the room closed over the gap the way water closes, and went on.

Here is the rule of the world, the one you feel in your chest before you can phrase it: nothing leaves. The shaft goes up, the cup stays full, the water that beads on the iron returns to the iron, the count holds at thirty-one. The room is a single held breath. It is intimate — you could cross it in ten steps — and it is complete, an entire ecosystem and economy and small lost human story sealed inside a space the size of a pantry, warm as a body, lit like an ordinary morning, packed to the rivets with slow patient life all quietly drinking the sweat of a machine that runs on being watched.

You are watching it now. That may be why it is still running.

If you climb back up the shaft, the engine's hum will fade behind you, and the strange thing — the thing you'll turn over for a long time after — is that you won't be certain, once you're out in the dry sharp ordinary stone again, whether the hum stopped because you left, or whether it ever made a sound at all, or whether the warmth on your hands was the iron or only the memory of having stood in a room that was, for as long as you stood in it, entirely full.
