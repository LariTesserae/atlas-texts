There is a city being born underground, and you have come at the moment of its making.

Begin with the ceiling, because that is what you notice first — not the floor. A vault of pale stone hangs above, and it is not finished. It is being finished. The rock is wet in long seams where water threads down from the surface, and along those seams the stone is still deciding its shape: blunt nubs of mineral, the first fingerbones of what will someday be columns and flowstone draperies, but for now only beginnings, soft-edged, glistening. Nothing here has eroded. Everything is accreting. The cave is growing the way a tooth grows, slowly, from the inside, depositing itself drop by drop. The drops fall. That is most of the sound there is.

Now the floor, and here is the strangeness that isn't strange — the parameters insist on calm, and so the wonder of this place is ordinary, domestic, human-scaled. People have built a town down here. Not in defiance of the cave's emergence but folded into it, hand-laid into the raw geology. The buildings are packed. There is no gap between them. Walls of cut stone and rammed earth and stacked, fitted blocks lean against one another along passages barely wide enough to pass, rising in storeys up the cavern's flanks wherever a ledge offered footing. Doorways open directly onto other doorways. A roof is someone else's floor. The density is total — every surface that can be inhabited is inhabited, and the town climbs the chamber walls until the construction loses itself in the dimness near the still-forming vault.

The light is good, and that is the second thing that should not make sense and does. Shafts come down through the ceiling — natural flues, widened by hand over generations, lined with smoothed pale stone that catches the daylight and passes it on, surface to depth, mirror to mirror, so that a clean ordinary daytime brightness pools in the streets. You could read by it. You could see a face across a square. The light arrives without sun in it, sourceless and even, the way light is in a room with north windows.

The town runs for a long way. This is not a single hollow but a region of them — chamber linked to chamber by worn passage, valley-sized voids beneath the world, and the building goes on through all of them, a settlement spread across an underground country. From a high ledge you would see the rooftops fall away into the next chamber and the next, lamplit thread connecting them, the whole of it cool and damp and quiet.

The people work with their hands and with simple things. There is metal but not much of it — a chisel, a hinge, a bronze bowl handed down. Mostly there is stone and rope and clay and patient repetition. They are masons more than anything. They cut, they fit, they raise. The dominant craft is the matching of human walls to the cave's slow self-construction, and there is a quiet etiquette to it: you do not build where the stone is still growing. You leave the wet seams alone. You let the flowstone come.

It is cool down here, the cool of constant moisture, and the air carries the smell of mineral and wet rope and woodsmoke held thin. Things grow sparsely — pale ferns and lank mosses in the lit places near the flues, cultivated in stone troughs; a few hardy crops under the brightest shafts; lichen crusting the older walls. Life is rare and deliberate. A few animals: cave-pale, soft-footed, kept or tolerated. A bird that has learned the flues. Something small that drinks at the seep-pools and is gone before you turn your head.

The sound stays low. Drip. The scrape of a tool. A voice, then quiet again. Water moving somewhere out of sight, the rivulet that feeds the town. The hush is not emptiness — the place is busy, fully alive, water cycling and stone accreting and people at their work — but it is busy the way a forest is busy, with no single noise rising over the rest. A dropped pebble would carry. Mostly nobody drops a pebble.

And under all of it, the slow active pulse: the water comes, the minerals settle, the cave thickens itself by a hair's width in a human lifetime, and the people build to keep pace, raising their packed stone town inside a room that is still, very gradually, making itself. Both are unfinished. Both are at work. The town will be old before the cave is done, and the cave will not notice.

This is the place. A bright, quiet, crowded town in a cave that has not yet decided what it is — water-fed, cool, hand-made, packed wall to wall, lit from a sky it cannot see.
