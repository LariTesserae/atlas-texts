There is a place at the bottom of cold water where machines run and no one came to build them.

Start with the water, because the water is everything here. You are below the surface — not the surface of land, but the surface of the sea, which lies somewhere far above as a faint grey ceiling you will never reach by looking up. The water is cool, not freezing; it holds the steady chill of a deep cellar, the kind of cold that doesn't bite but simply persists, the way stone persists. It moves only barely. There are no currents to speak of, no surge, no tide reaching this far down. The water here has the stillness of water that has finished moving.

And yet there is light. This is the first thing that doesn't make sense and then does. The light here is ordinary — the flat, honest light of an overcast afternoon — and it comes from the machines.

The machines are the thing.

They stand on a floor of pale silt and old rounded stone, spaced apart from one another with the patience of things that have stood a very long time. They are not arranged. No path connects them, no road, no logic of access. They simply occupy the floor at intervals — here one, then a wide stretch of empty pale ground, then another. Engines the size of small houses. Frames of riveted iron grown soft at the edges with the slow rust of saltwater, their plating worn to a furred, mineral roundness, every sharp corner long since eaten down to something that resembles river-rock more than metalwork. They have been here long enough to weather. They have reached the state weathering reaches when it is finished — not decaying anymore, just *settled*, the rust gone stable, the wear arrived at its own equilibrium.

They run.

Pistons rise and fall in their housings, slow, deliberate, full-stroke. Flywheels turn. Belts that should have rotted long ago move over their pulleys with no slack and no slipping. Inside glass tubes — clouded, but unbroken — small orange lights burn the warm electric color of old filaments, and it is these, multiplied across the machines, that fill the water with the soft daylight glow. Each machine hums. The hum is low and even and it carries through water differently than through air, arriving in your chest before your ears, a pressure more than a sound. Between the machines the water is nearly silent. Stand in one of the wide empty stretches and the hums layer into a single distant drone, like a city heard from a hill, and otherwise there is nothing — no creak of current, no click of crustacean, no movement of any living thing.

Because nothing lives here. That is the second thing. No fish. No drifting jelly, no crab working the silt, no eel in the dark between the engines. Not even the deep-sea things that learn to live where light never goes — and there *is* light here, so where are they? They simply aren't. The light falls on bare silt and bare iron and goes nowhere into any eye.

What grows is sparse and strange in its sparseness. Here and there, a stand of something like kelp — but it grows from the machines, not the floor, rooted in seams of rust and the crooks of old fittings. Pale green-grey, thin, unbranching, swaying only with the faint pressure-pulse the engines push into the water. A few fronds to a machine, no more. They are spaced like the machines are spaced — a little of everything, much of nothing. Between, the floor is clean and open, drifts of fine sediment laid down so undisturbed they show the long flat ridges of settling, smooth as a tide-pool floor at the moment the wave withdraws.

And here is the heart of it: nothing changes.

The pistons have risen and fallen at exactly this rate for as long as the rust has been this color, which is a very long time. The light has been exactly this bright. The plants have grown to exactly this length and stopped, holding, neither extending nor dying back. The silt has finished settling. There is no decay-clock running down and no growth-clock running up. The water does not warm with a day that doesn't reach here or cool with a night that doesn't either. This is a place that arrived at its condition and then held it — an engine-room reached by no stair, lit for no worker, kept running for no purpose anyone left a sign of, in a balance so complete it has the quality of a held breath that never has to be let go.

The scale of it is modest. You could walk it — wade it, drift it — in an hour. A field of standing machines, a few dozen, no horizon to them; the pale light fades off into blue-grey water at the edges and gives you the sense of boundaries without showing you a wall. A room, more than a country. A single quiet hall.

You will look for the cause. There is no cause to find. The machines have no inscriptions, no maker's plates legible through the rust, no cables running to a source, no fuel you can identify being consumed. They take in nothing and give out light, motion, and that chest-deep hum, and they have done so steadily into a cold and lifeless water that asks nothing of them and receives it anyway.

Stand still long enough and the hum becomes the only fact. Everything else — the cold, the light, the sparse swaying green, the clean unmarked floor — arranges itself around that single sustained note, the sound of work continuing in a place where work means nothing, will not stop, and has nowhere to arrive.

That is the place. It is running now. It was running before you came and the silt has already forgotten that you stood on it.
