# The Sumpworks

There is no ground here. There never was. What you stand on, if you stand, is the back of something built — a vast made-thing that goes down farther than digging has ever reached, plate riveted to plate, casting fitted into casting, all of it warm and wet and very nearly silent.

Begin with the water, because the water is everywhere.

It does not fall as rain. It condenses. The whole made-world breathes a warm exhalation, and the air carries more moisture than it can hold, so the moisture is always leaving the air — beading on the great brass flanges, threading down the grooves machined into every vertical surface, pooling in the troughs that the makers (whoever they were, whenever that was) cut into the floor-plates for exactly this purpose. The metal weeps. It has always wept. Every surface here is slick and warm to the touch, like the inside of a mouth, like a flank. Run your hand along a wall and it comes away wet and faintly mineral, tasting of iron and old steam.

The temperature sits at blood-warmth and a little above. Not the dry heat of furnaces — the heat is everywhere and even, radiating up from below where the great engines are, soaking through every plate, so that the wet air and the warm metal and your own warm skin all arrive at the same temperature and the boundary between you and the place goes soft. You sweat without effort. The condensation on the walls and the sweat on your arm are the same temperature, the same water, really, passed back and forth.

This is a low place. Whatever towers exist, you cannot see their tops — the moisture-haze closes them off at a certain height into a luminous grey ceiling. You move through the bottom layers, the sumps and the gantry-floors and the riveted basins, and the sense is always of being *down in* something rather than out upon it. Low, warm, wet, enclosed — and yet not small. Never small.

## The scale

Walk in any direction and the made-world continues. Walk for a day and it continues. The makers built to a scale that generates its own weather: the warm exhalation rises, meets the cooler upper haze, condenses, falls back as a fine perpetual drizzle in the great halls, so that there are *climates* here, distinct districts where the fall is heavier or the air is thicker, all of it internal, all of it weather made by the breathing of the structure itself. There is no outside that anyone has found. The horizon is always more structure, fading into vapor. If this thing is a machine, it is a machine the size of a country. If it is a country, it is a country with no soil in it.

## What grows

Almost nothing grows, and what does is barely alive.

Across the wet metal there is a film. Call it the bloom. In the troughs and the standing pools and the eternally damp seams, a slick of something simpler than plant or fungus spreads in sheets — coppery, faintly iridescent where the light strikes it, slimed and slow. It does not photosynthesize, not in any way that's been confirmed; it seems to feed on the dissolved metals in the water and the warmth from below. It is the closest thing to vegetation, and it is hardly more than a stain that happens to be slowly, slowly increasing. In a few places it has thickened into pads and ropes, draped over a gantry rail, but it does not climb and it does not flower. It just *coats*. The whole world is faintly upholstered in this living tarnish.

There are no trees, no grass, no green. The eye, looking for something to call a plant, finds only the bloom and gives up.

## What lives

And yet the place is *crawling*.

This is the strangeness of the Sumpworks: almost no vegetation, and animal life past all counting. The fauna here did not evolve from the bloom — there isn't enough bloom to feed a fraction of them. They feed on each other, and on the structure, and on the water, in cycles so dense and so old that they have reached a kind of equilibrium that looks, from the inside, like stillness.

Things move in the wet seams. Things hang from the undersides of the gantries in clusters, breathing slowly. The pools are thick with swimmers no longer than a fingernail, and the swimmers are eaten by the long pale things that pour themselves between the plate-gaps, and those are eaten in turn by the bigger ones whose names — if anyone named them — are lost. The air itself is occupied: soft-bodied fliers drift through the haze on slow membranes, so numerous that walking through a hall you brush them aside like beaded curtains, and they part around you and re-close behind, indifferent. They do not bite. They do not seem to want anything. They are simply *present*, layered through every cubic foot of warm wet air, a saturation of small life so complete that the place is less a landscape with animals in it than an animal with landscape in it.

And here is the thing that takes the longest to understand: they make almost no sound.

## The quiet

You would expect a world this crowded to roar. It doesn't. The fauna are nearly silent — the fliers move on noiseless membranes, the swimmers stir the pools with only the faintest lap, the clustered things on the gantries breathe below hearing. The bloom, of course, says nothing.

What sound there is comes mostly from water and metal. The endless soft *tick* and *trickle* of condensation finding its grooves. A far-off, deep, near-subsonic hum from the engines below — felt in the chest more than heard, so steady it stops registering after the first hour. The occasional long groan of a plate settling against a plate, which is not really settling, because nothing here settles; it is only the warm metal flexing by a hair's breadth and flexing back.

Mostly it is quiet. A warm, wet, breathing quiet, very full and not at all empty, the quiet of a vast crowded thing that has finished moving.

## The rule of the place: nothing changes

Because the deepest law of the Sumpworks is that it does not change.

The water condenses and falls and pools and evaporates and condenses again, and the total amount of water is exactly constant. The bloom grows and is grazed and grows and is grazed, and its extent does not increase. The fliers are born and die and are born, and their number in any given hall is the same today as it was a thousand cycles ago. The engines run at one unvarying pitch. The temperature does not vary by a degree. The light — a flat, even, sourceless daylight that seems to come from the haze itself, neither bright nor dim — does not brighten into morning or fade into night, because there is no morning and no night, only the one perpetual grey-gold luminance, always the same.

It is an equilibrium so total it borders on the eerie. Time passes, things move, life eats life — and nothing accumulates, nothing drifts, nothing trends. The Sumpworks is not stagnant; stagnant things rot and change. This is something stranger: a place where every process is exactly balanced by its opposite, frozen not by stopping but by perfect motion. A machine designed — if it was designed — to do nothing forever, flawlessly, in warm wet silence, full to bursting with life that goes nowhere.

The young, sharp edges of the castings suggest it was built not so very long ago. The total stillness suggests it has been finished forever. Both are true. It emerged recently into a state that will never end.

## Standing in it

So: you stand on warm wet riveted metal, ankle-deep at the trough-edges. The air is body-warm and heavy with moisture and crowded with slow soft fliers that part around your face. The walls weep mineral water that beads and runs in machined grooves down into pools coated with iridescent bloom. Somewhere below, an engine the size of a province hums one unchanging note into your sternum. The grey-gold light comes from nowhere and does not move. In every direction the structure goes on, fading into its own breath, made by hands that left no other sign.

And everything is alive, and nothing is growing, and nothing — not the water, not the light, not the numberless quiet swarming things — will ever be the slightest bit different than it is right now.

It is the warmest and wettest and most crowded stillness you will ever stand inside.
