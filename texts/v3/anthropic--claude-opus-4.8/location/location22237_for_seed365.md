There is a town on the shoulder of a mountain that is still being born.

You notice the youth of the place first, before anything else, because the rock has not yet learned to be smooth. The ridgelines are knife-edged. The boulders sit where they fell, raw-faced, their fracture-planes still bright, not yet softened by the slow tongues of weather. Streams have cut their channels into the slope as straight lines, impatient, not yet wandering. Everything here is recently arrived. The land has the look of a thing that has not finished deciding its shape.

And yet it is green past all reason.

The vegetation does not grow the way vegetation usually grows — it floods. Moss climbs the new rock in thick wet pelts. Ferns unscroll from every crack, deep-cut and dark, fed by a moisture that never quite becomes rain and never quite leaves. There is no downpour. Instead there is condensation — the mountain breathes, and the breath beads on every leaf, runs in slow threads down stone, gathers in the cupped throats of the larger plants and holds there, trembling, until something drinks it. The air is cool enough that the moisture lingers. You can feel it settle on your skin like a second, finer skin.

The light is dim — not the dimness of night, but of perpetual almost-dawn. The peaks above hold the cloud-layer close, and what comes through is grey-gold and even, shadowless, so that distance is hard to judge and the green seems to glow faintly from within.

The town is small. You could walk its length in the time it takes a thought to complete itself.

It was built by hands, with simple tools — you can see the chisel-marks, the way stones were chosen for fit rather than cut to fit, the timber lashed rather than nailed. The construction is genuinely urban in its *intent* — there are streets, there are levels, there is the logic of a settlement that organized itself in layers up the slope, terrace upon terrace, with stairs and channels and the worn paths of habit. But it is *sparse*. The buildings stand far apart. Between any two structures there is a great breathing gap of moss and fern and raw stone, as if the town were a sentence written with enormous spaces between the words. A plaza here, then nothing, then a tower, then nothing, then a row of low houses with water running through their gutters. The emptiness is part of the design. The town is a thin scatter of human marks across a slope that the green is trying, gently and constantly, to take back.

And it is almost silent.

This is the thing that will unsettle you, given everything else. A place this alive should roar. But the loudest sound is the water — and the water is quiet, the drip and the thread-trickle and the soft swallow of a leaf releasing its held drop. Footsteps are muffled by moss. Voices, if there were voices, would be swallowed. The silence is not empty; it is *thick*, the silence of a held breath, of a room full of things that have agreed not to make a sound.

Because the fauna is overwhelming.

They are everywhere, and they are quiet, and that is the strangeness of the place.

Look closely at the moss and it shifts — not wind, but the slow flexing of something that lives in the moss as a fish lives in water, a soft-bodied grazing thing the color of what it eats, moving in unhurried herds across the pelted stone. Look at the ferns and the undersides of the fronds are crowded with small still creatures clinging there, watching, blinking in sequence like slow lights. The cupped plants that hold the condensation — things come to drink at them, an endless rotation of drinkers, and they drink without splashing, lapping with a delicacy that makes no noise. The air itself is populated: things drift through it on membranes too thin to hear, riding the mountain's breath, settling and lifting again.

There are far more animals here than there are plants to hold them, and far more plants than the small town can explain, and the whole arrangement should collapse into noise and chaos — and it does not, because here the rule of the world is *quiet*. Sound is the one thing the ecosystem cannot afford. To make a noise is to be found, and everything here is both hunter and hunted, so everything has learned the same discipline. The predators move in silence and the prey freeze in silence and the great churning machinery of eating and being eaten turns over and over without a cry. A thing is taken; it does not scream; the moss closes over the place; the herd grazes on. The cycle is constant — *active*, relentless, the slope alive with a hundred slow pursuits at once — but conducted entirely in the register of held breath.

The people, if there are still people, learned this too. That is why the town is sparse: you cannot crowd, in a place where to be heard is to be hunted. You build with space between you. You speak rarely and low. You walk on moss. The infrastructure that survives is the water-channels and the stairs and the quiet — the architecture of a people who learned to live alongside the overwhelming by making themselves small and silent and far apart.

And the whole place is *young*. That is the last strangeness, and the one that grows on you as you stay. This was all made recently — the mountain, the moss, the swarming life, the silent town. It has the freshness of something that emerged last week and has already filled to bursting, an ecosystem that skipped its slow beginnings and arrived complete and crowded and quiet, draped over rock that has not yet had time to weather. As if the world here is in a hurry to be full, and a strict rule was struck to keep the fullness from tearing itself apart:

*Grow as fast as you like. Crowd as close as you can. But make no sound.*

So the rain that never falls beads on a million leaves, and a million quiet mouths drink it, and a million quiet hunters wait, and the small bright-stoned town holds its breath in the gaps between, and the mountain keeps making more of itself underneath it all — silently, greenly, without rest.
