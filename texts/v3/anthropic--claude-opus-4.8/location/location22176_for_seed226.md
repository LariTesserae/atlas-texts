There is a city here, but it has not finished happening.

You come up through cloud onto a high shelf of rock — wet rock, dark and cool, sweating water from a thousand seams as if the stone itself were straining toward becoming something. This is young ground. The mountain has only just decided to be a mountain. There are no canyons yet, no worn valleys, no smoothed faces. The crags are sharp as the day they cracked open, edges that have never been touched by the slow thumb of weather. Erosion has not arrived. The world up here is raw, freshly cut, unfinished at the level of the bedrock itself.

And the city — the city was raised on top of all this rawness, and the city is mostly empty.

Walk into it. The streets are wide and the streets are loud, and these two facts sit strangely together. Loud, because everything here drips and runs and rings. Water comes off the high stone in sheets and threads, and the builders — whoever they were, hand-workers, stone-stackers, people of rope and chisel and fired clay — built channels to carry it. The channels run everywhere, cut into the streets, leaping from rooftop to gutter to cistern, and the water in them roars. Not a single great roar but a chorus: a thousand small falls, each one a few feet high, each one striking a different basin, a different cupped stone, a different hollow drum of baked clay. The whole town sings with falling water, and because there is almost no one here to absorb the sound, it carries clean and enormous between the empty walls.

That is the first thing you understand. The city is loud and the city is empty, and the loudness is the water doing the talking in everyone's absence.

The buildings are good buildings. Stacked stone, mortared with something pale, roofed in overlapping fired tiles the color of wet iron. There are towers — not tall, but deliberate — and stairways, and squares with troughs at their centers, and arcades where you could shelter from the constant fine condensing mist that hangs in the cool air. Someone organized all this. Someone laid out districts, ran the aqueducts, set the cisterns at the low points and the catchbasins at the high. The labor in it is plain: every stone shows the bite of a hand tool. No machine made these edges. This was a city built carefully, with patience, by people who knew exactly how water moves.

But it is the scale that undoes you, slowly. The whole thing is small. You can cross it in an hour. It is hamlet-sized in footprint, city-sized in ambition — a dense knot of urban intention dropped onto a shelf of mountain barely large enough to hold it. And into that small, complete, deliberate town has poured a vast emptiness. The proportion is wrong in a way that takes time to feel. There should be people in a place this finished. There are almost none. You walk a long arcade and meet no one. You climb a tower and look down on roofs and squares and running channels and see no smoke, no movement, no faces at the windows. The volume between things is enormous though the distances are short. The place is hollow the way a struck bell is hollow — full of sound, empty of substance.

The vegetation has noticed. The vegetation is winning, slowly, patiently, in the way of all slow things here.

It is lush. The cool, wet, high air suits something — a deep green, broad-leaved growth that loves mist and shade and stone. It comes up through the channels and drapes from the gutters. Moss has thickened the troughs into soft green basins. Ferns fountain out of the joints between stones, fed by the everlasting damp. Hanging things spill from the arcade roofs in long, dripping curtains. And vines — fat, glossy, deliberate-looking vines — have begun the patient business of taking the towers apart joint by joint, though so slowly you'd never see it move. Everything green is thriving, and it is thriving in near-solitude, vast space between one plant-choked square and the next, the green riotous in pockets and the air between the pockets simply cool and empty and ringing with water.

Of animals there are almost none. Now and then a pale bird, large and silent, sails between two towers and is gone. Now and then something small moves in the ferns. The fauna here is rare and shy and seems, like everything else, to belong to a world still deciding what it will be. There are no swarms, no flocks, no herds. Just the occasional single life crossing an enormous quiet.

Here is the strangeness, the thing that sits a little outside ordinary explanation:

The city was finished before the mountain was. That is the wrongness you keep circling back to. The stone is embryonic — raw, unworn, just-arrived geology, the kind of ground that should not yet hold a settlement because it has not yet settled into being ground at all. And yet here is a complete, careful, hand-built town standing on it, with its aqueducts and arcades and tiled roofs, as if the builders came too early, raised their city on rock that wasn't ready, and then — left. Or were never quite here. The water runs as though tended. The channels are clear, the cisterns full, the catchbasins doing exactly what catchbasins do. Maintenance is happening. But you never see the hand that maintains.

The dynamics are slow, glacially slow, gradient-weak. Change here is measured against the patience of the mountain itself. The vines advance a finger's width a season. The moss deepens. The water carves nothing, because erosion has not begun — the falls strike clean stone and run off and the stone does not yield, will not yield for ten thousand years, so the sound stays exactly as loud as it has always been. Nothing wears down. Nothing finishes. The city waits in its loud green hollow at the top of the cloud for residents who built it and then never moved in, or moved out, or were always only the rumor of residents — and the water keeps singing to fill the space they left, in a town too small to need such a vast emptiness, on a mountain too new to deserve such a finished thing standing upon it.

Stay a while. Nothing will happen quickly. The mist will condense on your skin. A single bird will cross. The water will not stop talking. And somewhere under all that sound you will start to hear how patient the place is — how it is in no hurry at all to become whatever it is finally going to be.
