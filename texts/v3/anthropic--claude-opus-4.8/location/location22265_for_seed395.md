# The Sapwood City

There is no water here, and there never was. But there is sap.

Begin low, on the warm floor of a basin so wide its far rim hides below the curve of the world. The light arrives soft and full, the way light does on a long afternoon, except there is no afternoon — only this steady, generous brightness that seems to come from everywhere and cast few hard shadows. Warm air sits on the skin like a held breath.

And everything is built. Not by hands. By growth.

## The Material

The whole basin is one continuous living architecture of woody tissue — not trees exactly, not buildings exactly, but the thing that would exist if the two had never separated. The ground is bark. The walls are bark. They rise in fused trunks the width of streets, lean together overhead, knit into chambers, branch into the dense roofed lanes that thread the entire basin. There is no soil between structures because there is no *between*; the city is one organism, or one colony of organisms, grown so close that wall meets wall with no seam, no gap, no spare air. Packed. You could walk for a day and never touch open sky, only the dappled gaps where the canopy hasn't yet closed.

Where the wood is young it is pale green and soft, still extruding, still deciding its shape — this is the *emerging* part, the under-construction quarter, where the basin is busiest at becoming itself. Walls thicken visibly over the span of a season. Doorways narrow as their frames swell. Rooms appear that were not there, sealed pockets the growth has wrapped around empty space and held, like a knot in timber holding a bubble. The city is not finished and is not trying to be. It is in the slow middle of construction with no architect, only appetite for shape.

## Why There Is No Water

The sap does the work water would do, but it does not flow like water — it is thick, resinous, faintly amber, and it moves through the woody walls in channels you can see as raised veins along every surface. It is the city's blood and its weather and its plumbing. It does not pool. It does not rain. The chemistry here is sealed inside the living structure; nothing evaporates, nothing puddles, the air stays dry and clean and warm. When the wood is cut or cracks under its own growth, sap beads at the wound, golden, and seals it within hours. The whole place smells faintly of that resin — sweet, clean, a little like cut pine, a little like honey, all the time.

## The Vegetation

It would be wrong to call the wood the only plant, because *all* of it is plant, but inside the grown corridors a softer green has taken hold. Mosses upholster the lower walls. Vines that need no soil grip the bark and climb, flowering in small cool colors — bone-white, pale violet — that need no insects to find them, because here the flowers feed the city back, threading sugar into the veins. Broad fleshy leaves hang from the ceilings of the larger chambers like lamps, translucent, letting the steady light through filtered and green. Lush, but quiet lush — growth that fills space rather than fighting for it, because there is no scarcity to fight over. Everything is fed by the sap. Everything belongs to the one body.

## The Animals

They live inside the walls and along them. There are many.

Soft-bodied creatures the size of cats move through the bark corridors on suckered feet, grazing the moss, drinking from the sap-veins where the city permits small openings — and it does permit them; the animals are part of the body too, kept and used. There are gliding things in the high chambers, flat and pale, that drift between leaf-lamps. There are burrowers that keep the channels clear, dense colonies of them, working the way gut-flora work, the city's own digestion. Abundant, layered, every level occupied, predator and prey both present but slow — a hunt here is a patient, near-silent thing, because everything here is patient.

## The Sound

This is the strangest part to a visitor who has known other places: the quiet.

A city this packed, this alive, should roar. It does not. The wood absorbs sound. The mosses absorb sound. The animals have no need to call across distance because distance is always full and a neighbor is always near. So what you hear, walking the grown lanes, is almost nothing — a faint creak as a wall thickens, the soft suck of a grazer's feet, the all-but-inaudible tick of sap moving in the veins if you put your ear to the bark. A drop of resin falling and sealing. Your own breath. The silence is not empty; it is the silence of a thing so densely occupied that it has no room left over for noise.

## The Rules

Nothing is wasted and nothing leaves. The basin is a closed body. What grows, grows from what the body already holds; what dies is drawn back into the sap and built again into wall. There is no horizon-event, no storm off the sea, no flood, because there is no sea and no rain — the only weather is the weather of growth: a slow front of swelling that moves across a quarter over weeks, sealing rooms, opening others, the city breathing in geological time.

And it is vast — vast enough that the growth-front in the east is a different age from the growth-front in the west, vast enough that the air at the basin's center is warmer and stiller and older-smelling than the air near the still-emerging rim, vast enough to have its own gradients, its own slow internal seasons measured not in cold and heat but in fast-building and slow-building.

You could live your whole life in one of its rooms and call it the world. It would not be wrong.

It is one organism, grown into the shape of a city, the size of a country, going quietly about the work of becoming more of itself.
