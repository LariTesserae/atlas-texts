Below the surface, but not in darkness — that's the first thing to understand about this place. The light comes down through the water from somewhere above, but it doesn't dim the way you'd expect. It pools and brightens. The water here magnifies the light rather than swallowing it, so that everything is lit from above in a clean, even glare, the kind of brightness that leaves no shadow with edges. You squint at first. Then your eyes settle, and you see the room.

Because it is a room — a made thing, walled and floored, sunk just beneath whatever surface lies overhead. Someone built this. Hands built this. You can tell because nothing here is natural substrate. There is no rock, no native seabed, no silt that simply happened. Every surface is fired clay or cut stone or fitted timber, and all of it is warm-toned, terracotta and ochre and the pale gold of dressed sandstone, because the water is warm too — bathwater warm, near the upper edge of comfort, the warmth of a southern sea in late summer that has been heated further by the bright column of light falling into it.

The walls are tiled. Not perfectly — this is young work, recently laid, the edges of things still sharp, the mortar lines fresh and unsoftened. No barnacle has had time to round a corner. No current has had a thousand years to wear a doorway smooth. The chisel marks on the stone benches are crisp. You get the sense the place was finished not long ago, and submerged not long after, and the two events may have been the same event — that it was built *to* be flooded, built to live underwater, a chamber meant for this.

The scale is intimate. This is not a drowned city. It's a single contained space, a bathhouse perhaps, or a cistern, or a sunken courtyard — one feature, held complete. You could swim the length of it in a held breath if you needed to, though you don't seem to need to breathe here, and you don't question that. The walls hold you the way the walls of a small warm room hold you. There is much empty water between things. The space is sparse, generous with its emptiness — a few fitted columns rising to the bright ceiling of the surface, a long low bench, a basin set into the floor, and between them, water and light and not much else.

The vegetation is scattered. Sparse green has found the place since the flooding — strands of a fine soft weed rooting in the seams between tiles, a slow colonist working into the made world's joints. A few fronds lift and turn in the slow movement of the water. They are the only soft things here. Everything else is angle and surface and the deliberate geometry of construction. The green is the world beginning to take the room back, but only beginning — a thin first growth, hesitant, finding the cracks.

There is no animal life. No fish drift the columns. Nothing scuttles the floor seams or grazes the weed. This is a strange quiet for a warm bright sea, and it's the one thing that might make your skin prickle if you let it — that all this light and warmth and water, all the right conditions, and yet nothing has come to live here. The room is fertile and empty at once. The weed grows alone.

And yet it is loud. This is the thing that surprises you most, because submerged places are supposed to be hushed, and this one roars. The light-column above is also a *fall* — water is pouring in from the surface, a constant cataract entering the chamber from one corner, and the sound of it fills everything: a deep continuous rushing, the white drum of falling water meeting standing water, transmitted through the dense medium so that you feel it in your chest as much as hear it. The flooding is not finished. The room is still filling, or perhaps it is endlessly refilling, a held equilibrium between the cataract pouring in and some drain you cannot see carrying water out. The water moves. There are currents — the inflow drives a slow rotation through the whole chamber, so the weed-fronds all lean one way, and a leaf of something will lift off the bottom and travel the long circuit of the room and settle again. Active, working, in motion. The place is doing something, even with no one in it.

The technology of the building is simple and honest. Hand-tools made all of this — adze and chisel and the potter's wheel for the tiles. No metal beyond perhaps the blades that cut the stone. The drainage, the inflow, the careful slope of the floor toward the unseen drain — these are clever, but it's the cleverness of people who understood water and gravity and clay, not of machines. Channels and weirs and fitted pipes of fired earthenware. A primitive, capable craft. You could imagine the builders standing in this room when it was dry, dust in the bright air, deciding where the water would come and where it would go.

Now they are gone, and the water has come as planned, and the light pours down, and the cataract roars in its corner, and the slow current turns, and the thin weed reaches up out of the seams toward the brightness. The room holds it all — warm, loud, lit, alive with movement and empty of life, a made thing sunk just below the surface, doing endlessly the one thing it was made to do.

It is not strange, finally. It is only a room full of warm bright water, working. But you stay longer than you meant to, turning in the slow current, listening to the fall, watching the green find its way into the joints of the world.
