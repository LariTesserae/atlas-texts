There is a chamber under the world, and it is full of light.

This is the first wrong thing, the thing that takes longest to accept. You go down — and you are below the surface, no question, there is rock overhead, you can feel the weight of it in your teeth — and yet the place is bright. Not lit. Bright the way a noon field is bright, a flat hard radiance with no visible source, coming from everywhere and from nowhere, leaving almost no shadows. The walls glow faintly but not enough to account for it. The air itself seems to be the lamp. You stand in a downward cellar drenched in the light of an open sky that is not there.

The chamber is not large. You could walk across it in the time it takes to think a few slow thoughts — call it the size of a modest courtyard, a place with edges you can see, a place that holds you rather than losing you. And yet it feels enormous, because it is so empty. The volume of it is mostly nothing. Air and light and distance between the few things that exist. Whatever lives here lives far apart from whatever else lives here. The emptiness is the dominant material. Everything else is a guest in it.

The floor is old stone, worn into long smooth swells, the way a riverbed is worn though no river runs here now. You can read the water in the shape of the rock — the curves where current pressed for ten thousand years, the scoured hollows, the polished saddles between. The water has moved on but left its signature everywhere, like a language written by something that has gone. The whole place has this settled, finished quality. Nothing is sharp. Nothing is new. Erosion did its patient work long ago and then stopped, or slowed to the speed of geology, and the chamber arrived at a kind of agreement with itself. It is in balance. It is the shape it has decided to be.

There is still water, but it comes and goes. From the stone ceiling, beads gather and swell and fall — not a leak, more a slow breathing of moisture, condensation pooling and releasing on its own private schedule. The drops fall a long way through the bright air and land in shallow basins worn into the floor, and the sound they make is the loudest sound in the place: a single clear *tok*, and then a long silence, and then another. The silence between drops is enormous. You find yourself waiting for them. The waiting becomes a kind of clock. There is dampness on the walls, a sheen, a coolness to the touch, but no flowing water, no stream, only the memory of one in the stone and the patient drip that keeps the memory wet.

The warmth is steady and pleasant, the warmth of a body or a held stone, and it does not change. Plants grow here, but sparsely — they have to be the kind that can live on borrowed light and intermittent water. Pale grasses in the basins where the drops fall. A few low ferns in the damp seams of the wall, their fronds the soft uncertain green of things that grew toward a sun they never found. Here and there a patch of something between moss and lichen, spread thin across the rock like a stain. Between these scattered green presences: bare stone, bright air, distance. The vegetation does not crowd. It marks the places where the water lives and otherwise leaves the chamber to its emptiness.

The fauna is the strangeness.

For such an empty place, it is surprisingly alive — but the life is spread thin and behaves wrongly. There are pale moths the size of your hand, and they do not fly toward the light, because the light is everywhere; they drift instead, slow and aimless, settling on the wet stone to drink. There are small blind things in the basins, soft and translucent, that have never needed eyes and have grown them anyway here in all this brightness — vestigial eyes turned the wrong way, blooming uselessly. Long-legged creatures like cave crickets move across the floor in slow processions, far apart from one another, each one alone in its own acre of stone, and they make no sound at all, and they seem to be going somewhere, all in the same direction, on some migration that takes a lifetime to cross a room.

And there are the listeners. You will not see them clearly. At the edges, where the bright air thins toward the unreadable walls, there are shapes that hold still and seem to be attending to the fall of the water-drops. When a drop falls — *tok* — they shift, very slightly, all at once, like an audience acknowledging a line in a play. They have been listening to this same slow rhythm for longer than the stone has been smooth. They are not threatening. They are simply old, and patient, and tuned to something you can barely perceive.

There is one made thing. Only one. Near the center, where the floor dips into its largest basin, someone long ago set a row of small stones on edge — a deliberate arrangement, hand-placed, a low arc no higher than your knee. Beside it, a single tool: a shaped piece of antler or bone, worn smooth at the grip, abandoned mid-task. Whoever knapped the stones and held the tool worked by hand, with patience, with no metal and no haste, and then left, and did not come back, and the chamber has been keeping the arrangement intact in its slow unchanging way ever since. The stones are a little furred with moss now. The tool is dry. This is the only evidence that the place was ever known.

The rules of the chamber are few and absolute. Light without a source. Warmth without a season. Water that remembers more than it gives. A near-total quiet broken only by the falling drops, so that sound here is an event, a punctuation, something you mark rather than something that surrounds you. And time moving so slowly that the migration of the crickets, the swelling of a single drop, the spread of a moss patch by the width of a fingernail, are all happening at once and all imperceptibly — a place where change exists but you would have to stay a hundred years to see it.

You could sit on the smooth swell of the old riverbed in all that impossible light, in all that warm empty quiet, and wait for the next drop. And it would come. *Tok.* And the listeners at the edge would stir. And the moth would drift down to drink. And then the long silence again, holding everything, the way it has held everything for a very long time and intends to go on holding it.

That is the place. A bright hollow under the ground, nearly empty, very old, faintly wet, and patiently, strangely alive — keeping its few small lives far apart from one another, marking time by the falling of water, content to be exactly what it has become.
