# The Drowned Highland

Begin high. Not at a peak — there are no peaks here, nothing has had time to be ground into a point — but at altitude, where the air is thin and warm and so saturated with water that breathing is almost drinking. This is the first thing to understand: you are high, and you are submerged, and these are not a contradiction.

The mountains here are young. They came up fast and recently, the rock still sharp at its edges, fault-faces clean and unweathered, ridgelines crisp as folded paper. There has not been enough time for erosion to do its patient sculpting. The valleys between the ridges are not the soft U-curves of glaciers or the V-cuts of old rivers. They are raw clefts, freshly opened, their walls still showing the grain of the stone that split them.

And those clefts are full of water. Not flooded in the catastrophic sense — there is no violence here, no current, no tide that you can feel. The water simply *is*, filling the valleys to a depth of hundreds of feet, warm as bathwater, lit from above by an ordinary daylight that comes down green and slow. You float in it. Everything floats in it. The highland is a range of drowned valleys, an archipelago of sharp peaks rising from a sea that sits two miles above the lowland oceans that everyone else would call the sea.

## The Liquid Itself

The water is the strangeness. It is thicker than water should be — not quite syrup, but enough that movement through it is dreamlike, that a thing released will fall for a long time before it settles. Sound travels through it strangely: muffled, foreshortened, every noise arriving closer than it ought to and then dying quickly, swallowed. This is a quiet place. The loudest thing is your own pulse in your ears, and the occasional deep tectonic *settling* of the young rock — a groan from below, felt more than heard, the mountains still finding their footing.

The surface, far above, is a luminous skin. From beneath it looks like beaten silver. Things drift down through it slowly: pollen, spores, the occasional leaf shed from the canopies that crown the ridges where they break above the waterline. The light it lets through is enough to see by, enough to grow by — a normal brightness, diffused into something gentle and even, casting no hard shadows.

## What Grows

The vegetation is the great fact of this place, denser than the geology, denser than the water seems able to hold. Because the warmth and the light and the liquid all conspire, the drowned valleys are choked with growth — submerged forests that have learned to live without ever breaking the surface.

There are the **kelp-trees**, though they are neither kelp nor tree: trunks of a flexible, woody-but-soft material that rise in slow spirals toward the light, branching into broad translucent fronds that catch the descending green and turn it into sugar. They sway, but slowly — the thick water gives them a stately, underwater patience, a single sway taking the better part of a minute. Between them, the lower water is filled with **lantern-weed**, low cushions of vegetation that hold pockets of trapped gas, glowing faintly with a metabolic shimmer in the dimmer depths. And rising from the valley floors are the **column-mosses**, building slow accretions of soft green matter into pillars and curtains, the only architects here, the closest thing to building that this untouched place will tolerate.

Everything is layered, near-touching, packed into the warm light-zone in the upper hundred feet of water. Push through it and the fronds part reluctantly and close behind you. It is a crowd of plants, a congregation, a held breath of chlorophyll.

## The Rare Movers

Animals are scarce. The place is too young, perhaps, for much fauna to have arrived or evolved; the plants got here first and filled everything. So when something moves that is not a plant, it is an event.

A **drift-medusa**, perhaps — a bell of clear tissue the size of a hand, pulsing once and then coasting, navigating the kelp-trees on errands no one can read. They are not numerous. You might float for an hour and see none, then watch one cross the green dimness like a thought.

Or, more rarely, one of the **slow-fish** — long, ribbon-bodied things that hang vertical among the column-mosses, barely moving, feeding on the descending spore-fall. They do not flee from you. Nothing flees here. There has not been enough predation, enough history, for fear to have been invented yet. The fauna regards you, if it regards you at all, with the indifference of things that have never needed to run.

## The Rules

This is a world of slow gradients and patient processes. Change happens, but across durations you cannot witness — the mountains rising a finger's width in a lifetime, the forests thickening over centuries, the water level holding steady against some balance you cannot perceive. There is no weather here, no storm, no season you can name in an afternoon. There is only the long, warm, green held breath.

Nothing is built. Nothing is made. No tool has ever entered this water; no hand has shaped its stone. It is a place that has never been *used*, and the strangeness of it — the drowned altitude, the thick light-bearing liquid, the plants that have outpaced the animals — is simply the strangeness of a world that is still early in its own becoming, that has not yet decided what it will be.

You float in the warm green quiet, two miles above the ordinary sea, among the sharp new mountains and the patient swaying forests, and you wait, and nothing hurries, and the silver surface holds you under like a long, slow promise.
