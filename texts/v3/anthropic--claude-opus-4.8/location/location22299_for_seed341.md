# The Sump-Works

Begin with the heat. It comes up from below, not down from a sky — there is no real sky here, only a low ceiling of cloud that never quite resolves into rain but never quite stops weeping. The air is thick enough to lean against. Everything sweats: the iron, the brick, the broad waxy leaves, your own skin within moments of arriving. This is a hot place, a low place, a place where water has decided to live near the surface and the people built anyway.

It is not large. You could walk its edges in an hour, if you could find them — but the edges dissolve into reed-fen and standing water, and the works themselves are packed into a basin perhaps a half-mile across. A town's worth of construction folded into a grove's worth of ground. Call it a hamlet that dreamed it was a city.

## The Ground and the Water

There is no firm distinction between land and canal. The whole basin is a slow lung of dark water, and the buildings stand in it on brick piers gone green and furred. Between the structures: open black channels, sheets of standing water filmed with iridescence, mudbanks that exhale slow bubbles. Water beads on every vertical surface and runs in thin perpetual threads down the brickwork — the walls themselves seem to be crying, patiently, without grief. Where the channels meet the foundations the mortar has been weathered into soft rounded shoulders. Nothing here has a sharp corner anymore; the place is old enough to have settled into its own shape, and the heat-and-wet have rounded it the way river-stones are rounded.

But there is space. This is the surprising thing — for all the construction, the works are *sparse*, scattered. A pump-house, then forty yards of glittering empty water. A chimney-stack, then a long low shed, then nothing — reeds, mist, the gleam of a flooded yard. The builders left room. Or the water took room back. Either way you are never crowded; you stand always in a clearing of some kind, with the next presence looming softly through the haze.

## The Built Things

Industrial, but industry of an obsolete and patient kind. Riveted iron tanks taller than houses. Brick chimneys that lean. Great slow flywheels turning in open-walled engine sheds, belts slapping, governors spinning their brass balls in the dim. The dominant machine is the pump — for this is a place whose whole purpose is to move water that does not wish to be moved, and never finishes the job. The pumps run because the basin is always filling; the basin is always filling because the pumps cannot keep up; this is the bargain the place was built on and it has held for a very long time.

Pipes run everywhere, overhead and underfoot, lagged in rotting cloth, dripping at every joint. Walkways of slick iron grating span the channels. Everything is wet iron and oiled iron and rusted iron, and the rust does not flake but swells, soft and orange, like something alive.

## The Vegetation

Lush, and it has made peace with the machinery. Broad-leaved things — call them reeds, call them aroids, great green spears and unfurling fists of growth — rise out of every channel, climb the piers, root in the gutters and the lagging of the pipes. They grow fast in the heat. A flywheel left still for a season would be bound and stopped by green. The plants and the engines have reached the kind of balance two long-married things reach: the men who tend the pumps cut back the growth each morning, and each morning it has returned a finger's width, and so it goes, neither winning, both established.

The leaves drip. They collect the ceiling's weeping and pour it down in small private rains. Under a large enough leaf you can stand almost dry, listening.

## The Absence

Here is the strange thing, the thing you notice once the eye stops moving: nothing flies, nothing crawls, nothing swims. The water is dark and warm and rich and *empty*. No fish dimple it. No insect skates the iridescent film. No bird crosses the low cloud. The reeds rustle only when the warm draft from the engine sheds moves them. The plants grow but nothing eats them; they have no enemies, no pollinators, no grazers — they simply photosynthesize the dim wet light and rot back into the water and grow again, a botanical machine running in parallel to the iron one.

This absence is the source of the place's unease. It is loud, this landscape — but the loudness is all *engine*. The slap of belts, the gulp and suck of pumps, the deep arrhythmic knock of water-hammer in the pipes, the hiss of steam bleeding from a hundred slow leaks, the groan of iron expanding in the heat. A great industrial roar with no living voice anywhere inside it. No birdsong, no buzz, no splash of anything alive. You wait for it. It does not come. The machines fill the silence so completely that you might not notice the silence is there — until you do, and then you cannot un-notice it.

## The Rules

A few things hold true here, and you learn them quickly:

The water level is never fixed. It rises through the day as the heat thickens and the seeps quicken, and the pumps fight it, and toward what passes for evening — the cloud-ceiling dimming a half-shade — they gain a little, and the channels drop, and the mudbanks show, and the smell of the exposed mud is enormous and sweet. Then night thickens the air again, and it all comes back.

The dim light has no source you can point to. It is everywhere and weak, the colour of weak tea, brightest near the water's surface as if the water itself were faintly luminous — which it may be. The chimney-smoke does not rise; it spreads sideways into the cloud and is lost.

And the central strangeness: the place is in furious motion and going nowhere. Belts turn, water rises and falls, plants grow and rot, steam bleeds and condenses and falls and bleeds again. Everything is *active*, dynamic, ceaseless — a whole ecosystem of process — and none of it is alive in the way you expect life to be. It is a body without animals. A city without a citizen you ever quite see. A machine the size of a hamlet, weeping and breathing and pumping, keeping itself from drowning, for no reason anyone has been able to find, in the green wet heat, under the low grey ceiling, forever.

You leave by wading out through the reeds. Behind you the pumps keep their slow knocking. They will not stop. There is no one to stop them, and the water is always rising.
