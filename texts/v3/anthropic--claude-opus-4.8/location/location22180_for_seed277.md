There is a plateau that goes on past the curve of the world, and it is dark, and it is warm, and somewhere across it engines are breathing.

Start with the ground, because the ground is what everything else stands on. It is old basalt, weathered down to a kind of rounded honesty — the sharp edges gone, the lava-flows long since softened into low swells and shallow basins, a terrain that has finished arguing with itself. High country, but not jagged; the mountains here wore themselves out an age ago and left behind these broad humped backs of stone, dark as wet iron even when dry. You walk on it and it does not ring or crunch. It gives a low felted sound, because over most of the rock there is a skin of something — lichen, or the dark mosses that hold the damp.

The damp is the second thing. There is no sea here, no river you could name, but the air carries water the way a held breath carries warmth. In the basins, condensation pools overnight and lies in sheets thin as a coin until the warmth of the ground drinks it back up by what would be midmorning if there were a morning. There isn't, much. The light is the strange part — I'll come to it.

Vegetation is scattered and deliberate-looking, the way plants are when there isn't enough of anything to be careless with. Cushion-plants in the hollows, tight grey-green domes that store the night's water in their cores. Taller things stand alone, spaced wide — a kind of tree that is mostly trunk, pale and smooth and slightly waxed against evaporation, with a sparse crown held high and small. You can walk a long time between them. They do not form forests. They form a sort of slow punctuation across the plain, each one its own sentence.

Now the dark. The light here sits down near the floor of seeing — not the absolute black of a cave, but the deep blue-violet of a very long dusk that has decided not to end. There is a source. Low on the horizon, in one quarter of the sky, a smear of dull red glows like a banked coal — too diffuse to be a sun, too steady to be fire. It never rises and never sets. The whole landscape is lit by this ember-light, which means everything has a color it can only half-remember: the basalt is almost black, the cushion-plants are grey going to nothing, and the standing trees catch a faint red along one edge of their trunks, the side that faces the coal.

Your eyes adjust. They learn this place. After a while you realize the dark is not empty.

Because the fauna is everywhere, and the fauna is what makes the silence strange. There are a great many living things out here on the plateau, and almost all of them are quiet. This is a place that has learned, over a long time, that sound is expensive. The animals move on padded feet, on cushioned hooves, on belly-skin. There are grazers the size of cattle that crop the cushion-plants with a sound no louder than a sigh, and they travel in loose herds spread so wide you might think each one was alone. There are long low things that hunt the grazers, moving between the spaced trees, and they too make almost no sound — the kill, when it comes, is nearly silent, a brief soft scuffle in the dark and then nothing. Predator and prey both keep the quiet. It is a covenant nobody signed.

Listen, though, and you will hear three things.

The first is the wind, which on a plateau this vast is its own weather. It comes in slow and warm and goes over the open ground with a sound like breath through cloth, and it can build over the course of what passes for a day into something with shoulders, pushing the standing trees so they lean and groan once, low, before settling. The plateau is big enough to make its own storms — you can watch one stand against the coal-lit horizon, a darker dark, a column of moving air carrying the only rain this country gets, dragging its grey curtain across forty miles of stone and then unraveling into nothing.

The second sound is the animals' one allowance: at intervals, far off, a single call. Not a chorus. One voice at a time, somewhere out in the dark, a long descending note that might be a grazer locating its herd or might be the hunters answering each other. It travels enormously far across the open plateau. You hear it and you cannot tell if it came from a mile away or twenty. Then the quiet folds back over it.

The third sound is the engines.

This is the thing that doesn't belong and yet is here, and the longer you stay the more you understand it has always been here. Out across the plateau, at distances of many days' walk from one another, are the breathing-places. There are no buildings — no walls, no towers, nothing made and stacked. But the engines exist. They are mechanisms grown rather than built, or so old that no one alive could tell the difference: great low industrial organs half-sunk in the basalt, pistons of dark metal and bone-pale ceramic that rise and fall with a slow, enormous patience, drawing the damp air down into themselves and breathing it back out warmer. They are the reason the plateau is warm. They are the reason there is water at all — they pull it from the air and from the deep rock and weep it out into the basins where the cushion-plants drink.

They are not loud. This is the heart of the strangeness. An engine the size of a hill, and it makes only a sound like a sleeping animal — a deep cyclical exhalation, a faint metal tick at the top of each stroke, a long sigh on the down. You can stand beside one in the red dark and feel its warmth on your face and hear it breathing and not be afraid, because it is so slow, and so patient, and so clearly older than fear. No one tends them. There are no roads to them, no signs of a people who made or maintain them. They simply work, scattered across the continent of stone, terraforming a world that may have no one left to live in it but the silent grazers and their silent hunters and you.

The animals know the engines. The herds drift toward them in the long dry stretches because the ground near a breathing-place is damp and the cushion-plants grow fat there. The hunters know this too, and so each engine has its small economy of life arranged around its warmth, a ring of quiet predation in the red dark, and the engine breathes over all of it, indifferent, machine-patient, doing the one thing it has always done.

Stand in the middle of it. The plateau goes out flat and dark in every direction to a horizon you cannot trust the distance of. The coal-colored glow hangs unmoving in its one quarter of sky. Somewhere a single animal calls and is not answered. The wind comes up warm and lays itself across the cushion-plants. And under everything, so low you feel it in your chest before you hear it, the engines breathe — in, and out, and in — keeping a world alive that has forgotten, or never knew, that it was being kept.

It is not a ruin. Nothing here is broken. It is a balance, very old, very large, and very nearly quiet, and it does not need you to witness it.

But it does not mind that you do.
