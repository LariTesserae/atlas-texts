# The Foundry-Marsh

Begin with the heat. It comes first because it never stops arriving. Not the heat of a sun — there is no sun here, only a low amber smear behind the steam — but the heat of something being *made* nearby, always nearby, just past the next rise. You feel it the way you feel a kiln through a closed door.

Now the water. It is everywhere and it is wrong. It lies in sheets across the floor of this place, ankle-deep, body-warm, and it does not behave. Water should pool downhill. Here it pools toward intention. It gathers around the half-built things as if attending them. Steam rises in slow columns that lean against a wind you cannot feel, and where the columns cross they sometimes hold their crossing-shape too long, like a knot that forgot to come undone.

The whole world is made. This is the thing to understand. There is no bedrock under the water, no original stone. Dig down — you can, the floor gives like wet clay — and you find more made-things below: tiled courses, the seams of poured slabs, conduits, the underside of a floor that is the topside of another floor, going down past where you care to follow. Someone laid all of this. Someone is *still* laying it. But there are no workers anywhere. The construction proceeds unattended, or by attendants too slow or too strange to register as moving.

---

The materials are these: fired brick the color of old liver, going up in courses that stop mid-air, the topmost bricks still wet at their mortar and steaming. Long beams of a black glassy stuff, volcanic-looking, half-melted, sagging between supports like they were set down before they had cooled enough to keep their line. Sheets of beaten copper, hammer-dimpled, hand-worked — you can see the toolmarks, the irregularity of a real arm swinging a real mallet — but no mallet, no arm. Lengths of cord and woven matting, the work of someone's patient fingers, lashing the impossible to the half-finished.

It is all primitive craft at a continental scale. That is the contradiction that organizes the place. Everything is handmade — coil-built, hammer-formed, lashed, fired — and yet it stretches to the curve of a horizon that is itself clearly constructed, a far rim of scaffolding so distant it goes blue with haze, holding up a vault you take for sky until you notice the rivets.

---

The light is dim and comes from the work. Wherever a thing is being made, it glows — kiln-glow, forge-glow, the dull red of cooling. So the landscape is lit unevenly, in scattered hearths across a vast dark plain, like a marsh seen at night with a hundred separate fires burning low. Between the made things: long reaches of warm dark water and steam, and almost nothing else. The place is *sparse*. You can walk a long while between presences. The emptiness is not absence; it is the part not yet built. It waits with a patience that is hard to be near.

There is no plant. There is no animal. There is, instead, a kind of mineral fungus — call it that for lack — a crust that grows on the older brick, pale grey-green, faintly luminous, branching in patterns that are too regular to be alive and too irregular to be made. It does not grow toward light. It grows toward *finishing*. It colonizes the parts of the construction that have been abandoned longest, as if decay here is just a slower species of building.

---

Sound is mostly the quiet of a held breath. The water laps. Steam hisses where it meets the hot brick. And then — irregularly, with no schedule you can learn — the deep single report of something being struck. Not nearby. Always one rise over, two rises over. A great soft *tock* of mallet on copper, or the *chunk* of a slab being set, or a sound with no source at all: the sound a wall would make if it remembered being struck a long time ago. These reports are the only proof that the building continues. Between them, the silence is the silence of a workshop where the craftsman has just stepped out and will be back, will be back, will be back.

---

Now the reality breakdown, which is not a special event here. It is the weather.

The construction is *unstable in its own logic*. A wall built this morning was always-built and will-never-have-been, all at once, and the water around it cannot decide which, so it pools and unpools, leaving tidemarks on bricks that then unbrick. You watch a copper sheet being hammered into shape by no one, and the dimples appear, and then you understand that the sheet is being *un*-hammered, smoothed back toward flat, and that flatness is also a kind of finishing, and you cannot tell whether the place is rising or being recalled.

Things double. You round a stack of beams and find the same stack of beams, set down by the same absent hands in the same incomplete posture, and a third behind that, dimmer, a draft of the idea. The marsh holds copies the way standing water holds reflections — except the reflections are load-bearing. Lean on one. It holds.

The horizon-scaffold sometimes ripples and shows, for an instant, the same Foundry-Marsh on the far side of it, inverted, hung from its own vault — so that you understand you may be standing on the underside of someone else's floor, the topside of another, lit by their fires as they are lit by yours.

---

What rules can be stated:

Water moves toward what is being made.

The unbuilt is patient; the built is provisional.

Decay and construction are the same gesture run at different speeds, and the mineral-fungus is where they meet.

Nothing finishes. The single mercy and the single cruelty of the place is that nothing here is ever finished. The mallet falls forever, one rise over, and the wall it shapes is always almost a wall, and the water keeps faith with it anyway, gathering warm at its foot, steaming, attending, in the long amber dark, the next blow that does not come, and then comes, and then has not yet come.
