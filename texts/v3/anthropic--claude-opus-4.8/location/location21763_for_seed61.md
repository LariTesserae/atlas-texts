There is a city the size of a held breath, and you are already inside it.

To understand the scale: imagine the hollow left behind when a single drop of water has been working at limestone for ten thousand years. Not the cavern such patience eventually makes — the small one, the early one, the pocket. A room you could not stand in. A room with no entrance you would recognize. The whole settlement fits in the space a fist would occupy, and yet it is a settlement, with streets, with quarters, with a kind of citizenry, though I am getting ahead of myself.

Begin with the stone. It is pale and it weeps. Every surface here is wet, not with rain — there is no rain this far below, no sky to send it — but with the slow exudation of the rock itself, water pressing out of the grain the way sweat beads on cool glass. The walls glisten. They have glistened so long that the glistening is the wall now; you cannot find the dry stone underneath, there is no underneath, the moisture has soaked the idea of dryness out of the place. Run a finger along any ledge and it comes away beaded. The water does not run, mostly. It hangs. The air is so saturated that droplets simply assemble out of nothing, swell, and let go, and the falling of them is one of the sounds.

It is cool here, the cool of a cellar, the cool that has no opinion about you. Nothing warms and nothing freezes. This is important: nothing here changes. I want to be precise about that, because it is the strangest of the parameters and the one that governs the rest. The city does not grow and does not decay. It arrived at its present arrangement so long ago that the arriving is forgotten, and it has held that arrangement the way a chord holds, sustained past the point where you expected silence. The erosion that shaped it is finished erosion — every edge already rounded, every form already settled into the only form it was ever going to take, and then stopped. A landscape in its own state. Balance so complete it reads as stillness, though stillness is the wrong word for a place this loud.

Because it is loud. Astonishingly loud, for something so small and so still.

The sound comes from density. The pocket is packed — presence against presence, structure against structure, every cubic measure of it crowded. And because the chamber is sealed and curved and slick with water, every sound made inside it is kept inside it, folded back, layered onto the sound before, until the whole pocket rings like the inside of a struck bell that never quite stops ringing. The dripping I mentioned: multiply it by ten thousand droplets at ten thousand pitches, each fall amplified by the wet acoustics into something between a chime and a knock. Add to that a low continuous roar that I took, at first, for water moving somewhere, and only later understood to be the sound of the city's making, its building and re-building, which I will come to. The noise is total and the noise never changes its character. You stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the weather of the place. The atmosphere here is sonic. The loudness is the air.

There is almost no light. What there is comes from the water itself — or rather from what lives in the water, the only living thing I could find, a faint luminescence smeared across the wet stone in patches, dim green-blue, brightening where the moisture is thickest and the surface most disturbed. It does not illuminate so much as suggest. You see the city the way you see a room by the glow of a watch face: edges, hints, the nearer things, the far things lost. Most of the citizens you never see at all. You hear them.

Now: the city.

It is built, and built densely, but built by hands — or by something that works the way hands work, patient, repetitive, pre-metal. The materials are all local, all the place itself reworked: grains of the pale stone cemented with the city's own secretions into walls, into stacked cellular structures, into towers no taller than a thumbnail that lean against one another for the entire crowded width of the pocket. It looks, if I am honest, like a coral, or a wasp's nest, or the inside of a bone — the architecture of something that does not draw plans but follows rules, the same rule executed a million times until a metropolis happens by accumulation. There are passages. There are chambers stacked on chambers. There is what I can only call infrastructure: channels that gather the weeping water and route it, gutters and cisterns at the scale of a sand grain, a whole hydrology engineered to manage the one resource the pocket has too much of.

Who lives here. I told you fauna was rare, and it is — rare in the sense that there is only one kind, and you almost never catch it in the act of being alive. The builders. I never saw a whole one clearly; the light forbids it. I saw motion at the edge of patches, a paleness moving against paleness, something low and many-limbed that worked the wet stone and added its grain and withdrew. They do not seem to eat. They do not seem to breed, or fight, or rest. They build, and what they build is already finished, and so the building is not progress, it is maintenance — they replace each grain exactly as it was, the moment before it would have fallen, so that the never-changing city is in fact ceaselessly remade, atom for atom, into precisely itself.

That is the rule of this world, the one I came to understand last and slowest. Stability here is not the absence of work. It is the most enormous work there is, performed forever, to make sure that nothing happens. The roar is the sound of a million small labors all cancelling into stillness. The city does not persist; it is persisted, held in place by hands that never stop, against an erosion that long ago finished its sentence and simply waits, patient, wet, and cool, for the holding to fail.

It will not fail. That is the other half of the rule. As long as the water weeps, the builders build; as long as they build, the water is gathered and routed and kept; and the loop has closed so perfectly, so long ago, that you could set this pocket inside a stone and bury it and come back in any imaginable distance of time and find it exactly thus — the same droplet falling, the same dim glow, the same unbearable, unchanging noise of a place working with all its strength to stay exactly where it is.

And it is so small. You could hold the whole tireless, deafening, deathless thing in your closed hand and never know, walking in the open air above, that anything down there was awake.
