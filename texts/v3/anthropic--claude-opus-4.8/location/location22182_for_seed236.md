There is a city here, but no one built it, and there is no water in it anywhere.

Let me start with the rock, because the rock came first and is still arriving. It is a pale rock, almost the color of bone left in sun, and it is sharp at every edge. This is a high place — you can feel the thinness of the air in the way warmth sits on your skin without sinking in, a dry heat that never becomes sweat because there is nothing to evaporate. The stone is young. It has not had time to round. Every ridge is a blade, every fracture a clean angle, as though the whole formation finished cooling last week and is still deciding what shape to hold.

And on this fresh sharp stone, a city of plants.

Not a forest — too organized for that, too vertical and packed. The vegetation grows in columns. Stalks the thickness of a person's waist rise straight up, fluted like architecture, and at intervals they swell into chambers, hollow rooms with walls of green-gold pith. They lean toward one another at the top and fuse, so that walking beneath them you pass through arcades, doorways, the suggestion of streets. The plants have made a town out of themselves. There are plazas where the columns thin and the light falls in clean shafts, and there are dense quarters where they crowd so close you turn sideways to pass, leaf-flesh brushing leaf-flesh, the whole mass near-touching, layered, breathing very slowly against itself.

How do they live without water? They drink the warmth. I think that is the truest thing I can say. Down in the chambered hollows there is a slow chemistry — the plants pull something from the heated mineral air, some salt-rich vapor that rises off the young rock, and they fix it into the gold-green flesh of themselves. When you press a wall, a thin oil weeps from it, faintly resinous, and dries to powder almost at once. That is as close to water as this place comes.

The scale is small. You could walk the whole city in an hour if it let you — it is grove-sized, room-sized, a single hill's worth of this strange grown architecture, bounded on all sides by bare sharp stone going down. But inside it feels endless because of the density, the way each arcade opens onto another, the way you lose the edges.

Now the silence. This is the thing that takes longest to understand. There is no wind here, or almost none — the air at this elevation is still and warm and patient. Nothing rustles. The plants are too thick and too rigid to move. And so the city is quiet in a way that feels like a held breath. When sound does come it is small and close: a faint tick as a chamber wall flexes in the heat, the dry whisper of powder sifting down, the almost-inaudible click of something moving in the dark of a hollow.

Because there is fauna here, abundant, and it has learned the silence. The animals are pale, like the rock, and they move slowly — everything here moves slowly, this is a place of long durations and patient processes. There are creatures like flat soft moths that walk rather than fly, treading the column-walls on many feet, grazing the resin-powder. There are things in the hollow chambers that I can only call tenants — segmented, knuckle-pale, the size of a cat, that live one to a chamber and seem to spend their whole lives polishing the inner walls smooth with their bodies. They do not compete. The city has rooms enough. Everything here has settled into a slow occupancy, an ecology of waiting.

The weirdness is this: the city is growing toward a plan. If you sit long enough — and the warmth invites sitting, the silence insists on it — you begin to see that the columns are not random. They repeat. There is a rhythm to the spacing, a recurrence in how the chambers stack, as though the plants are remembering a shape they have never seen, building toward an order that no mind designed. Three columns, then a gap. A chamber at shoulder height, then one above, then the fusion overhead. It is too regular to be growth and too alive to be construction. The young rock is fresh enough that you suspect the whole arrangement only began recently — that this is the early formation of something, a town teaching itself to be a town, and that if you returned in a hundred years the streets would be straighter and the plazas more deliberate and the silence even deeper.

The light is ordinary, which after everything else is the strangest part. Plain warm daylight, falling clean through the dry air, gilding the bone-pale stone and the gold-green columns, throwing honest shadows. It makes the place almost gentle. You could mistake it for a garden, for a ruin, for somewhere people once lived and quietly left.

But no people. No tools, no devices, nothing made by hand. Only the rock arriving, the plants drinking heat and arranging themselves, the pale slow animals keeping their rooms, and the silence holding all of it the way the chambers hold their tenants — one to each, patient, in no hurry to become anything but more completely itself.
