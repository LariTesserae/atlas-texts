There is a hollow in the high country, no wider than a banquet hall, scooped from the side of a mountain so old it has forgotten it was ever a mountain. The peaks around it have worn down to soft shoulders, the granite gone to clay-colored rounds, the way a knuckle goes smooth after a lifetime of work. You come over the last rise expecting more of the same tired stone — and the ground simply opens, a green bowl held in the rock like water cupped in two hands.

The first thing is the noise.

It hits before you've taken three steps down. Not one sound but a wall of them, layered and unbroken — the saw-and-stitch of insects, the throat-rattle of frogs in pockets you cannot see, birdsong stacked so thick it stops being song and becomes pressure, a thing you feel in the small bones of the ear. The bowl is alive far past what its size should permit. By the arithmetic of any ordinary place there is room here for a few hundred living things. There are millions. They do not crowd — that's the strange part. The space between them stays generous. A beetle the size of a thumb sits alone on a leaf with a full hand's width of empty air around it, and yet the air is dense with motion everywhere you look. Sparse and overwhelming at once, as though the fauna had learned to occupy more sound and more presence than its bodies account for.

The warmth is constant, a held breath of warmth, the kind that lives in greenhouses and the undersides of stones. It does not come from the sun, or not only — the rock itself is warm to the palm, warm at the rate a sleeping animal is warm. Condensation beads on everything. There is no rain here, exactly, but the air sweats. Droplets gather on the broad waxy leaves, swell, fall, and somewhere below a frog answers each fall with a single note, so that the whole bowl ticks and chimes with falling water like a clock made of leaves.

Vegetation comes in slow, deliberate clusters. Ferns with fronds the color of old jade. A single tree, leaning, its bark fissured into a script no one set there, hung with epiphytes that breathe visibly — you can watch their small mouths open and close. The plants are lush but spaced, each one ringed by bare warm earth, and the earth between them is not dead but waiting, the way a stage between acts is not empty.

And then the marks of making.

They are scattered through the bowl, half-swallowed by green: a low arch of some pale material that is neither stone nor metal, worn to a comfortable nub. A panel set flush into the rock, its surface still faintly warm and faintly humming, showing — when you wipe the condensation away — a slow pulse of light beneath, like circulation under skin. Whoever built here built with an engineering that doesn't sit right in the eye. The arch does not cast quite the shadow its shape should cast. The panel's light moves *toward* your hand when you reach for it, the way a plant turns to the sun. These are not ruins in the ordinary sense. They have not failed. They have *finished* — completed whatever they were for and settled into a final, restful state, the way the mountain settled, the way everything in this bowl has reached its oldest and most stable form and simply continues.

That is the rule of the place, if it has one: nothing here is beginning and nothing is ending. It has all arrived. The fauna teem but do not multiply beyond the bowl; the water falls but the level never rises; the warmth holds; the structures wait without decaying further. Erosion has done its whole work already and now stands at rest. You have walked into the last steady state of something that was once enormous — a machine, a garden, a mind, a mountain — worn down across an unimaginable span to this one small green pocket where it keeps doing, faithfully and forever, the smallest version of what it always did.

The light is ordinary daylight, which is the strangest thing of all. Plain, honest noon, falling into a place this old and this loud and this impossibly full of life. It makes the bowl seem almost reasonable. Almost a meadow. Until a thing the size of a hand lifts from a leaf without wings and crosses the air in a straight line, and the chorus swells to greet it, and you remember that the meadow is a room inside something, and the something is listening, and has been listening, contentedly, since long before the mountains went soft.

You could stay. The warmth invites it. But the sound, after a while, begins to feel less like noise and more like a single held word, repeated — and you find you do not want to learn what it is saying. You climb back out over the soft stone rim. Behind you the bowl ticks and sings and breathes, sealed in its finished afternoon, needing nothing, wanting nothing, complete.
