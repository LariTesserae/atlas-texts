# The Roaring Tablelands

Start with the height. You are very high up — higher than makes sense for a place still soft enough to grow things. The air is thinner here but warm, a wet warmth that catches in the throat, and it never stops moving. This is the first thing you understand about the place: it is loud, and it has always been loud.

The terrain is a system of tablelands — flat-topped mountains worn down over time past counting, their tops broad as counties, their flanks dropping away in sheer faces of striped, rust-and-cream stone. These are not young, jagged peaks. They are the rounded stumps of something that was once enormous, ground down by epochs of weather into a stable old age. Where they crack, they crack along grain lines so old the rock has half-forgotten it was ever laid down in layers.

And between the tablelands: nothing. Or almost nothing. The gaps are vast — kilometers of empty air with a thread of distant valley floor too far down to see clearly. The place is mostly void, mostly the spaces between the standing rock. You could walk a whole day across one tableland's crown and meet no living thing larger than your hand.

## The Sound

The deafening comes from the water and the wind together. The tablelands stand in the path of a perpetual, turbulent weather — a sky that is never still. Updrafts scream against the cliff edges. Far below and all around, water that should be scarce is instead *thrown* — cataracts pour off the table-edges in wind-shredded ribbons that never reach bottom as water, only as drifting mist. The roar is constant and total. It is the sound a sea would make if you stood it on end.

You will not hear yourself speak. People who live here — and a few do — communicate with hands and with their bodies, leaning close, touching shoulders. They have lost the habit of voices the way deep-cave fish lose eyes.

## The Water

So: damp, but never wet underfoot. The moisture lives in the air, in the perpetual blown mist, in the condensation that beads on every leaf and stone at dawn. There is no standing water on the crowns — it sheets off the edges as fast as it falls, hurled outward by the wind so that the tablelands wear constant collars of spray-cloud at their shoulders. To drink, you collect what condenses. The plants know this too, and they are built for it.

## The Vegetation

This is where the place becomes lush against all its other odds. The crowns are forested — but a forest adapted to roar and wind and mist-fed thirst. The dominant growth is a low, dense, tough-leaved canopy, every plant streamlined, every leaf cupped and grooved to channel condensation down to the roots. The colors run deep — blue-greens, bruise-purples, the silvered undersides of leaves turned up by the wind so that whole hillsides flash like fish turning. Mosses and lichens crust everything, fattened on the constant damp. In sheltered folds, where the wind can't reach, things grow improbably tall and soft — gardens hidden in the rock's creases, almost silent there, the roar muffled to a pressure rather than a sound.

The vegetation is the loudest *living* thing, in fact. The wind plays the forest like an instrument — every leaf a reed, every stem a string. The strangeness lives partly here: the plants seem to *answer* the wind, to lean and rustle in patterns that arrive a half-second too soon, as though they hear the gust coming. Maybe they do. Maybe the constant turbulence has shaped them into something that listens.

## The Fauna

Rare. The animals here are few and strange and built for the conditions. There are gliding things — leather-winged, broad as cloaks — that ride the updrafts off the cliff-faces and almost never land, sleeping on the wing in the thermal columns. They are silent because nothing they could make would be heard. They communicate, when they must, by flashing the pale insides of their wings.

On the ground, almost nothing. A burrowing creature or two, ear-less, that reads the world through vibration in the stone. You may cross a whole tableland and see only one living animal, far off, and lose it again in the mist before you're sure it was real.

## The Built Things

Scattered, and old, and humble. There are people, or there were — a handful, living in the wind-sheltered creases of the crowns. They build with stacked stone and woven plant-fiber, low domes that hunker against the wind, weighted down with more stone. No metal to speak of — bone tools, knapped stone, fire kept alive in deep pits because no flame survives the open air. Their cleverest craft is in their water-catchers: nets and fluted channels and patient ceramic bowls set to harvest the night's condensation.

They leave marks. Hand-stencils on sheltered rock, blown there with pigment held in the mouth and exhaled — though here even that ritual is shaped by the wind, the stencils streaked and trailing where the gust took the spray. There are old cairns at the table-edges, purpose forgotten, perhaps marking where someone once stood and watched the spray pour off into the empty air. The people are few. They may be the last of themselves. They have settled, like the mountains, into a final, stable form.

## The Rules

A few things hold true here that may not hold elsewhere:

- **Sound does not carry meaning.** Meaning travels by sight and touch. This bends the people who live here into something quieter, more watchful, more inward.
- **Water moves only sideways and down, never collects.** Stillness of water is the rarest thing. A bowl of held, motionless water is sacred precisely because the whole world conspires against it.
- **The forest anticipates the wind.** Whether this is sense or mysticism is unclear, but the canopy moves to a rhythm slightly ahead of the weather. Some say if you watch the leaves you can know the next gust. Some have learned to.
- **Nothing here is young.** The rock is exhausted of geology, the people are dwindling, the animals are few and old-blooded. And yet the *air* — the wind and water and the turning leaves — is in perpetual violent motion, a furious, restless dynamism layered over a world that has otherwise reached its final, settled rest. That is the deep strangeness of the place: maximum stillness underneath, maximum turbulence above. An old, old land that will not stop shouting.

Stand at the edge. The wind takes the spray and the spray takes the light and somewhere below, too far to see, the valley floor is dark and unvisited. The roar is everything. The leaves behind you flash silver, turning, turning, a half-second ahead of the gust that is already coming for you both.
