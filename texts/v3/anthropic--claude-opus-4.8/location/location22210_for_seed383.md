# The Undercity at Glasslight Hour

You come down into it the way water comes down into it — by following the slope, by giving up on staying level.

The first thing is the light, which is wrong for being underground. It should be dark down here, half a kilometer below whatever surface once was, but the ceiling is not a ceiling so much as a vast lens of the world's old crust gone translucent, polished by ages into something between marble and ice, and through it pours a flat white brilliance with no visible source. It does not flicker. It does not warm. It lies on every surface like a coat of frost that you cannot scrape off. The locals call this the glasslight, and it never stops, and there is no night, only the slow rotation of brightness from one wall of the cavern-vault to another, the way sun moves through a room without ever showing you the sun.

Under that light: a city, and it is old past the point where age means decay. It means *settledness*. The towers here have been worn down not into ruin but into their final, calmest shapes — rounded ziggurats, smoothed colonnades, archways softened to the rough geometry of river-stones. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Three hundred thousand years of dripwater and condensation have rounded every corner the way a thumb rounds a soap bar. The architecture is *primitive* in its making — you can see the marks of hands, of stone chisels, of fired-clay brick laid without mortar — but it is enormous, layered, terraced up the walls of the underplace in tier on tier. A whole civilization's worth of building, hand-tooled, no metal beyond soft bright hammered copper here and there, and all of it nearly flattened now into a topography of mounds and worn plinths.

And nearly empty. That is the thing that catches in your chest. A city sized for hundreds of thousands, and you can stand in a plaza and see no one in any direction, only the long pale geometry running on and on toward where the glasslight thins to a haze. Whoever built this has been gone a very long time. The *density* of presence is almost nothing — wide stone avenues, broad terraces, and between them silence and space.

---

The silence is real and total in a way you have to learn. No wind down here — the air does not move enough to be called weather. No insects in chorus, no rustle of leaves because there are no leaves. There is no plant life at all. What grows on the old walls is mineral or near-mineral: pale crusts of something that might be lichen but has no green in it, films of crystal that creep across the damp stone in slow fractal bloom, white and gray and faintly iridescent. They are alive in the way a stalactite is *almost* alive — they accrete, they branch, they respond. But nothing here photosynthesizes, because nothing here ever needed to learn the sun.

So the loudest sound is water. *Damp* is the governing condition — not flooding, not rivers running deep, but the constant patient sweat of stone. Condensation gathers on the high lens of the ceiling and falls, single drops, with enormous gaps between them, each landing somewhere in the vast quiet with a single clear note. *Tok.* And then nothing for a long while. *Tok* — somewhere else now, in another district. The city is a drum-skin played by one drop at a time, and the silence between the notes is the real instrument. Pools collect in the worn basins of old plazas, mirror-still, holding the glasslight in flat sheets. You drink from them and the water tastes of stone and of cold and of nothing else.

It is *cool*. Not cold enough to freeze, never that — but the kind of even, settled cool of a cellar, of a cave, of a place that long ago forgot the difference between seasons and simply chose one temperature and kept it forever.

---

Here is where the place stops being a tomb.

The fauna.

You notice them slowly because they don't make noise — that is one of their rules, and you'll come to understand it's a rule and not an accident. The undercity is *full of animals*, and they move through the empty avenues in numbers that don't match the silence. They are pale, most of them, the way deep-dwelling things go pale: eyeless or near-eyeless, navigating by the fall of the drops, by the standing pools, by some sense of the glasslight's slow rotation. Things like long low salamanders run of milk-glass, their skin showing the dark thread of organs beneath. Flocks of soft fliers with skin wings, drifting between the worn towers on the barely-moving air, never flapping, just *gliding*, riding thermals you cannot feel. Herds of low six-legged grazers — though there is nothing to graze — cropping the crystalline crusts off the walls, the only feeding in a world with no plants, eating the mineral-growth and slowly, over millennia, sculpting the city's surfaces into yet smoother shapes.

They are abundant, and they are *quiet*, and that is the unsettling thing. So many of them, and the whole moving press of them makes barely a whisper — a city's worth of life conducting itself in a hush, as if every creature here evolved under the law that the silence must not be broken, because the silence is what the water needs to be heard, and the water is everything.

---

Now the turbulence. This is the strangeness that takes longest to see, because everything *looks* eternal — the worn city, the steady light, the unbroken hush — and yet the place is in violent, ceaseless change. You just have to widen your eyes to the right speed.

The crystal-growth is *fast*. Watch a single wall for an hour and you'll see the fractal blooms surge across it, branching and shattering and re-branching, building structures and collapsing them, a slow-motion storm of accretion happening everywhere at once. A pool that was mirror-flat when you arrived has, by the time you cross the plaza, grown a forest of mineral spires that the grazers are already tearing down. The animals breed and die in rushing generations — you watch a flock of fliers spiral up, mate in midair, scatter their soft young across the terraces, and dwindle, all within an afternoon. The city is *still* but its skin is boiling. Everything ephemeral lives at a furious pace against a backdrop that hasn't truly changed since before the surface world had a name.

So you get the strange double-vision of the place: turbulence held inside permanence. The chaos of a tide-pool, the energy of an anthill, all of it churning under that flat unmoving glasslight, in that flat unmoving cool, in that total quiet — a storm you can only hear as the occasional fall of a single drop of water.

---

The rules, as best as a visitor can read them:

The light never stops, so there is no rest from being seen, but there is also nothing here that minds being seen.

Nothing speaks, because the silence is load-bearing.

The water is the clock — not the light, which is constant, but the drops, whose intervals are the only measure of time anyone down here would recognize.

The dead built it; the living have inherited it and do not maintain it; the crystals are the new architects now, dressing the worn stone in lace that lasts an hour.

And it is going somewhere. Everything ancient is going somewhere — toward flatter, toward smoother, toward the eventual moment when the city wears down into a single curved plain of pale stone under a single sheet of standing water, and the glasslight lies on it unbroken, and the last drop falls into a surface that no longer ripples, because there is no longer anything raised enough to ripple from.

But that is a long way off yet. For now: come down the slope. Sit on a stone made round by patience. Wait for the next drop.

*Tok.*

Listen to how long the silence holds.
