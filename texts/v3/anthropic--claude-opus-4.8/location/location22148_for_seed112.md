# The Loud Forest

There is no water here, and yet everything grows.

Start with that contradiction, because the whole place is built on it. A high plateau — call it elevation, call it the roof of a continent — bone-dry, the air thin and cool enough that your breath would feel like an effort. No rivers. No rain that I have ever heard of. The ground is a dry mineral crumble, pale ochre and bonewhite, weathered down over an age so long the rock has forgotten it was ever sharp. Everything is rounded, softened, established. The land has settled into the shape it wants and stopped arguing with itself.

And out of this dry, rounded, ancient ground: vegetation. Not at the edges. Not in oases. *Everywhere.* The plant is the substrate. The plant is nearly the whole world.

---

But it is a strange botany, because there is no water to drink.

These are dry plants — the way you might call a thing a dry electrical storm. They draw nothing up from the ground because the ground has nothing to give. Instead they run on light, and there is so much light. The sky here is enormous and brilliant, a hard high-altitude blaze that never seems to dim into proper dark, and the plants drink it directly through skins like beaten brass and stretched amber. Photovores. They are closer to solar engines than to anything green. Their flesh is fibrous and resinous, shot through with veins of conductive sap — a dry sap, a kind of slow mineral current — and where two stalks touch, you can see the spark jump.

That is the first thing you understand about the Loud Forest. It is not metaphorically electric. It is *literally* humming with current, a whole continent of living photovoltaic timber crackling at the joints.

---

Now the scale. This is the part that takes a while to feel.

The plant-towers are not trees. They are too far apart to be a forest in any ordinary sense — sparse, scattered across the plateau with enormous emptiness between them, miles of pale dry flat between one tower and the next. From a distance you might think the land nearly bare. But each tower is a continent of its own ambition. They go up — hundreds of feet, some of them past the point where you can resolve a top — fluted columns of woody brass, fanning at the crown into broad mineral leaves angled at the sun like the dishes of some abandoned industry. They are spaced like that *because* of the light. Each one needs its own enormous hemisphere of sky, and so they stand apart, sovereign, each a single living factory drinking down a private allotment of the blaze.

The emptiness between them is not dead. It only looks dead from above. Down at ground level the spaces are crossed by low dry runners, root-cables thick as a man, racing across the surface from tower to tower — the whole sparse field is wired together underneath, one organism pretending to be many, trading current along these cables in surges you can *hear* coming before you see anything move.

---

The sound. I have been holding the sound back, because it is the hardest part to put down honestly.

It is deafening. Not occasionally — constantly, at a level that would be a wound anywhere else. The towers resonate. Wind comes off the plateau and pours through their fluting and they sing, but *sing* is too gentle — they bellow, a great brassy organ-roar that never stops and never resolves into melody, a hundred-mile chord with no key. Under that, the electrical work: the snap and crackle of current jumping the gaps, the deep arterial *thrum* of charge moving through the root-cables, building and discharging. When a surge passes beneath you the ground itself buzzes and the nearest towers flare brighter and answer with a louder note.

And the discharges. Every so often — and this is the turbulence, this is the part that makes the place feel *alive* and dangerous rather than merely loud — a tower overloads. Too much light, too much stored charge, and it lets go: a column of dry lightning fires straight up out of the crown into the brilliant sky, a thunderclap you feel in your sternum, and for a moment that whole tower is incandescent. Sometimes it arcs sideways to a neighbor instead, a bolt leaping the miles of empty air, and you watch the discharge run from tower to tower across the plateau like a row of cannon firing in sequence, each clap rolling into the next. The whole continent is a slow, perpetual, lightning-driven argument between living engines.

There is no peace here. There was never any peace here. The place is in a permanent storm-state — but a dry storm, a storm of light and charge and sound, with no cloud and no rain to relieve it.

---

The fauna live in this, somehow, and there are a lot of them.

They are insulated. That is the rule that every animal here obeys, the price of admission. Things with thick rinds and ceramic shells, things sheathed in their own non-conductive armor, things that have learned to time their movement to the surges — scuttling across the open ground in the lulls, freezing flat and rooting themselves down when they feel a charge building, letting it pass over them and through the root-cables instead of through their bodies. There are grazers that crack the resinous bark off the towers and chew it for stored sunlight, leaving long pale scars up the brass flanks. There are gliders that ride the heat coming off the leaf-fans, soaring the miles between towers in the dead air, never landing if they can help it. There are small clattering things in the root-cables' shadow that feed on the spark itself, mouths built to catch and earth a discharge and digest the energy raw.

They are noisy too, in their way, but you cannot hear them. Everything they do is swallowed by the towers' roar. So they have given up on sound entirely and speak in light — flashes along their flanks, a flicker-language pitched against the brilliant sky, a constant silent chatter beneath the deafening one.

---

What there is *not*, anywhere, is anything built.

No structure raised by hand or claw. No wall, no road, no tool, no ruin. And yet the place *looks* built — that is the unusual, the strangeness of it. It looks industrial. The towers look like cooling stacks and dish-arrays and transformer fields; the root-cables look laid by some buried municipal authority; the rhythmic discharges look scheduled, switched, *managed.* Walk through here and your every instinct insists that someone made this, that there is a controlling hand, that around the next tower you will find the substation that runs the grid.

There isn't. There never was. The Loud Forest grew its own industry the way a coral grows a reef — blindly, over an age, in balance with its own ferocity. The factory is alive and the factory is the wilderness and they are the same thing, and it has been roaring to itself up here on the dry roof of the world, drinking light and throwing lightning, long before anyone arrived to mistake it for a machine, and it will be roaring long after the question stops being asked.

Stand in it a while. You will stop trying to find the meaning in the chord. You will start to feel that the noise is the point — that the place is *expressing* itself, fully, constantly, with no audience and no need of one, a vast bright dry organism shouting its own enormous sentence into a sky that never answers and never goes dark.
