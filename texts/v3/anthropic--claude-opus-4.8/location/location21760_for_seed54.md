There is a city growing on a cold plateau, and it has not finished being born.

Start with the water, because the water is everywhere and it is the oldest thing here. It is not rain — it is too patient for rain. It comes up from below, seeping through a plateau of black anhydrous stone that has no business holding moisture, yet weeps it constantly, so that every surface wears a film. The film does not run. At this temperature — barely above freezing, the kind of cold that doesn't bite but settles into the marrow as a low note — the water hangs. It beads on the underside of things and refuses to fall. The air itself is half-water, a standing fog so dense that distance dissolves at thirty paces, and what you see beyond that is inference, suggestion, the darker grey of mass against the merely dark.

For it is dark here. Almost wholly. Whatever sun this plateau answers to is a rumor. There is a faint luminance in the fog, sourceless, the way snow glows on a moonless night, and it is enough to know that you are surrounded but not enough to know by what.

Now the built thing. The city is not made of stone or metal in any way you'd recognize. It is grown, or being grown, out of the plateau itself — long ribbed structures that rise from the rock like the beginnings of cathedrals that have decided to assemble themselves molecule by molecule and have not yet agreed on a plan. Some of the towers are complete to a height and then simply stop, their tops a fringe of unfinished lattice, threads of material reaching upward into the fog and feeling for the next layer. The geometry is too clean to be geological and too unsettled to be architecture. Walls are smooth where they are old and granular where they are new, and if you put your hand to a new surface you would feel it — a faint vibration, a grain-by-grain accretion, the structure pulling matter out of the wet air and the weeping rock and laying it down. This is the tech, the thing current physics does not fully describe: a city that builds itself out of fog, slowly, patiently, on a timescale of centuries, with a will that is not quite a will.

The erosion and the construction are the same process here, running in two directions at once. What is being built is also being unmade, the water working into seams, the cold cracking edges, so the whole place exists in a permanent state of half-formed. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here is ruined. It is all emerging, all the time, and the emerging is so slow that across a human lifetime you would see no change at all — only the certainty, somehow, that change is happening, the way you know a glacier moves.

There is almost nothing living in the botanical sense. No plant has ever grown here; there is no soil, no green, no root. Perhaps a fungal bloom in the wettest seams, a pale crust that is more chemistry than biology. The mineral world reigns. And yet —

The fauna. The fauna is overwhelming, and this is the contradiction at the heart of the place that took me longest to understand. How can a dead mineral city teem?

It teems because the dark is full of them. They are not made of meat, most of them — they are creatures suited to a world without plants and without light, drawing whatever they need from the seeping water, from the slow tectonic chemistry of the rock, from each other. They cling to the unfinished towers in numbers past counting, layered over the surfaces so thickly that the architecture wears a living skin. They are the density: the near-touching saturation of presences per volume that makes the city feel, when you stand in it, less like a place and more like the inside of a body. Things move on every surface, slow and patient as the buildings themselves, grazing on the accreting walls, feeding on the very growth of the city — so that the city builds itself out of fog and the fauna eats the city and the city builds again, and the whole cycle turns over the length of ages.

And they are loud.

This is the thing you'd notice first, before your eyes adjusted, before you understood any of the rest. The sound. In the wet dense dark the air carries everything, and the creatures call constantly — not cries of distress, nothing so sharp, but a vast layered drone, a chorus of low sustained tones, clicks, the scrape of mineral mouthparts on mineral walls, a resonance the fog amplifies until it is the medium you stand in. The towers ring. They are tuned, perhaps deliberately, perhaps by accident of their growing — hollow ribbed columns that take the chorus and hold it, so the whole plateau hums on a single deep chord that shifts, over hours, by quarter-tones. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in the water on your skin. The loudness is not violence; it is presence, the sound of an enormous slow life that has been singing here longer than there have been ears to hear it, and intends to go on.

The rules of this world, as best I can read them:

Nothing hurries. The fastest thing here is the fog, drifting. Everything else — the building, the eating, the singing, the slow migration of the creatures up the half-made towers toward the fresh growth at the top — moves on the patient gradient, the weak flux, the timescale where a year is a heartbeat.

Light is scarce and so sight matters little. The city is built for things that hear and feel and taste the chemistry of the wet air. It was never meant to be seen. You, seeing it, are a kind of trespass — bringing eyes to a place that solved the problem of darkness long ago and has no need of you.

And the water and the cold together make a kind of mercy. Nothing decays fast. Nothing rots. The dead creatures are folded into the accreting walls and become part of the next tower, and so the city is also a slow tomb, and the slow tomb is also a nursery, and you cannot tell the building from the burial, because here they are the same.

Stand at the edge of the plateau, where the towers thin and the fog opens just enough to suggest the drop — the high cold air, the sense of a whole region of this below and around you, more towers fading into grey, the chord rolling out across all of it. You will not see the bottom. You will not see the end of the city. You will only hear it, breathing its single note, growing one molecule at a time out of the weeping dark, alive with a million slow creatures, finishing nothing, beginning everything, in no hurry at all.
