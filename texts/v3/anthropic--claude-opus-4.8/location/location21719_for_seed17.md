# The Cold Loud Deep

Start with the cold. Not the bite of frost but the cold of pressure — a cold that has weight, that presses evenly from all sides, the cold of water that has never seen the sun and never will. You are far down. You are also, somehow, far up.

That contradiction is the first thing to understand. The elevation here is high — this is the top of something, a mountain measured from a floor so distant it has no name — but everything is submerged. You stand on a peak that is drowned. The summit is a high place inside an ocean that has no surface, or whose surface is so unthinkably far above that it has become a kind of myth, a rumor of air.

So: high, and underwater, and cold, and immense.

---

The rock is young. You can tell because it has not learned to be gentle yet. The terrain is all edges — fresh fracture planes, glassy black faces of something poured and cooled fast, ridges with the sharpness of broken obsidian that the water has not yet softened into curves. There is almost no sediment. No drift of accumulated time. The slopes are clean and angular, recently made, the geology of a place that emerged into its own ocean not long ago in the reckoning of stone. Where erosion has worked, it has worked only a little: a few rounded shoulders, a few runnels where the cold currents have polished a groove. Mostly it is angle, plane, vertex. The mountain is sharp the way a thing is sharp before the world wears it down.

No plants. Effectively none — a fur of something on the lee faces, not quite algae, not quite mineral, a faint olive bloom that may be alive or may simply be a stain that grows. Call it the threshold of life, the barely-anything. Otherwise the surfaces are bare. This is a mineral world wearing a thin coat of ocean.

---

Now the strangeness, which is the size and the emptiness held together.

The scale is vast — continental, more than continental. This drowned mountain is one of an array that runs past the limits of perception, and the water around it generates its own weather: slow internal storms, thermoclines that move like fronts, great lazy currents that turn in gyres broad enough to have their own seasons. There is weather down here. Cold weather, in the dark-but-not-dark.

And yet it is empty. Almost nothing per volume. The water is so clear and so vacant that you could swim for a day and meet nothing — no rock, no creature, no debris — only the blue-black going on. The emptiness is the dominant texture. Vast space between presences. You feel it as a kind of vertigo: too much room, too little in it. The mountain is a single sharp object in an ocean of near-void, and the next object is impossibly far.

The light is the puzzle. There is normal light — daylight quantity, the brightness of an overcast afternoon — and there is no sun. It comes from the water itself, or from something dissolved in it, a luminescence so diffuse and total that it reads as ordinary sky-light. Shadowless. Sourceless. You can see your hands. You can see the mountain's whole flank falling away. The light just *is*, the way temperature is, an ambient property of the medium. It makes the emptiness worse, honestly. You can see exactly how much nothing surrounds you.

---

And the sound.

The sound is the thing you will remember. It is deafening. In all that emptiness, in all that cold clear void, the water carries sound the way nothing on land ever carries it — fast, total, undiminished. And there is a *source*.

Down on the lower shoulders of the mountain, where the slope flattens into a terrace, there are structures. Scattered — a few only, against all that vastness — but unmistakable. Built things. Industrial things. Engines.

They are old and they are running.

Squat machines of riveted dark metal, the size of cathedrals and the proportion of furnaces, anchored to the young rock with pylons driven deep. They have intakes that draw the cold water through and exhausts that pulse it back out warmer by a degree, and they roar. Each one alone would be a thunder. Together — and the sound travels, the sound finds you across kilometers as if you stood at the throat of each — together they make a noise that has no gaps in it, a continuous mechanical bellow that fills the whole drowned world. It is in your chest. It is in the rock under your feet, which hums. The water itself shakes with it. There is no quiet anywhere. The deafening is not an event; it is the climate.

What do they make? Nothing you can find. There are no goods, no products, no purpose visible. The engines run and have always run and will always run, processing the endless water, exhaling their single degree of warmth into an ocean too vast to notice. Industry without a market. Production without a product. They were built — by whom, when, you will not learn — and then left to their equilibrium, and they have kept it.

Because the last thing to understand is that nothing here *changes*.

---

This is a place at rest. Time-invariant. The currents turn but they turn the same way they turned a thousand years ago. The cold stays cold, the same cold, the exact degree. The engines run at the same pitch, never faltering, never speeding up, a frozen storm of sound. Even the weather, the internal ocean-weather, has settled into loops so stable they are barely weather at all — the same fronts crossing the same water on the same schedule, forever. The mountain does not erode further than it has eroded; it has reached the small amount of wearing it will ever do, and stopped, and holds. The light does not brighten or dim toward any night, because there is no night. Equilibrium. Stillness wearing the mask of motion.

And into this stillness, abundance — of fauna.

This is what the emptiness was saving room for, in a way: where there is anything alive, there is a great deal of it. Around the engines, drawn to the one degree of warmth, life clusters thick. Things with the bodies of eels and the eyes of nothing, schooling in the warmer plume. Pale grazers that crop the not-quite-algae from the lee faces. Translucent swimmers in ribboned ranks, hanging in the exhaust-warmth like iron filings in a field. They have organized themselves entirely around the machines — the engines are their suns, their seasons, their only reason. Far from the structures, in the true void, there is almost nothing. Near them, the water churns with bodies.

And the creatures are loud too, or they have made peace with loudness — they communicate in the brief silences between thunder, or under it, or by means that need no quiet at all. They do not seem to suffer in the noise. They were born into it. To them the bellow of the engines is simply the sound the world makes, the way for you the world makes a sound of wind and traffic and your own blood. They are at home in the deafening. They circle the warm machines in the cold sourceless light, in the sharp young rock, in the vast indifferent dark-bright water, and they do not change, and the engines do not stop, and the cold holds steady, and the great loud equilibrium goes on.

---

A high place, drowned. A loud place, in an emptiness. A young sharp mountain wearing a few ancient machines like barnacles, and around the machines a riot of cold-blooded life, all of it perfectly, permanently still — moving and moving and never once becoming anything else.

That is the place. Stand on the summit-that-is-a-deep and listen to the engines you cannot escape, and watch the pale schools turn in the warm plume, and feel the whole world humming under your feet, forever, at one unchanging cold and thunderous note.
