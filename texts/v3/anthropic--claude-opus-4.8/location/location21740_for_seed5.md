There is a city inside a stone.

Not metaphor — or not only. Find the right hollow in the right limestone bluff above the right slow river, and you will find it: a geode the size of a great hall, but the crystals never finished growing. They stopped, ages ago, at the scale of houses. And in their stopping, people came to live in the lattice they left.

Let me take you in. The way is low and damp — you go on your hands at the threshold, where moss has stuffed every seam with green and the rock sweats a constant film. Then the passage widens and you can stand, and then the place opens, and you are inside the bright.

The light is the first surprise. There is no sun here — we are deep in the bluff — yet everything is luminous, an even daylight with no source and no shadow worth the name. It comes off the crystal faces themselves, a held brightness, as if the stone drank a long-ago noon and never let it go. The walls are pale quartz and paler calcite, faceted into terraces and ledges and overhangs, and on every facet the light pools and passes. You squint at first. Then your eyes settle and you see how thoroughly the place is *full*.

Because it is packed. There is no empty air in this room. Every ledge bears a dwelling — low cells of fitted river-stone and mudbrick, roofed with woven reed and the broad waxy leaves of the cold-ferns that grow everywhere here. The dwellings climb the crystal walls in tiers, stacked and leaning, sharing walls, sharing floors, one household's roof the next one's doorstep. Ladders of knotted root connect the levels. Bridges of laid stone span the gaps. Where there is a hand's-width of horizontal surface, something grows in it or someone has built on it. The density is total and it is *old* — every edge rounded, every corner mossed, every path worn into a shine. Nothing here is new and nothing here is going anywhere.

The cold is steady and deep. It is the cold of a cave that has forgotten weather — not bitter, but unwavering, a temperature with no day and no season. Your breath shows. The people wear felted wool and ferncloth in layers, and they move slowly, partly from the cold and partly because there is nowhere here that anyone needs to rush to. Everything is already where it has always been.

And the vegetation — this is the strange green heart of it. The cold-ferns mantle every surface that water touches, and water touches nearly everything. The whole geode weeps. Condensation beads on the high crystal vaults and falls in a slow intermittent rain, gathering in cupped facets into hanging pools, running down the lattice in thin threads to feed the gardens terraced on every ledge. Mosses in a dozen depths of green. Pale mushrooms in fat colonies. Climbing vines with leaves like wet glass that have learned to feed on the held light. There is more living tissue here than stone, almost — the place is upholstered in growth, soft over its hard bones.

Now listen. This is the thing that takes longest to understand about the city in the stone: it is *quiet*. Not dead — abundantly, teemingly alive — but quiet. The drip of water is the loudest sound, and even the drops fall onto moss and are swallowed. People speak in the lowest registers, or sign with their hands, which here is a real and complete language because the silence taught it to them. Children learn to be quiet before they learn to walk. The crystal carries sound strangely — a whisper at one tier can arrive sharp and intact at a tier across the hollow, while a shout three feet away is muffled by the fern. So everyone has learned: keep it low, keep it soft, and the stone will carry your meaning to whoever needs it. The whole community is a held breath that never quite releases.

There is fauna, and it has adapted to the hush. Small pale frogs that pulse their throats but rarely call, living in the hanging pools. Soft-winged moths the size of hands, drifting through the bright on no detectable air, their wings the color of the crystal so that they vanish when they land. Blind silver fish in the deeper basins, where the meltwater gathers. And a creature the people call the *lampkeeper* — a slow, flat, glowing thing like a living lichen that crawls the crystal vaults and is, perhaps, the actual source of the light, though no one can say for certain because no one has ever climbed high enough to check. It does not move while you watch it. You look away, you look back, it is somewhere else. Brighter where it has been.

The tools are simple and made to last forever, because nothing here gets used up faster than it can be replaced — there is no fast here. Knapped stone, ground bone, fired clay. Looms of fernwood. Knives kept sharp on the crystal itself, which holds an edge against your blade like a whetstone made of light. Some little copper, hammered from nuggets the river leaves at the threshold, traded in from the outside world that almost no one in here has seen. They make beautiful things slowly and they are in no hurry to finish them. A bowl can take a year. A bowl can last a thousand.

What is unusual — what would stop a visitor and make them stand very still — is the *stillness itself*, made physical. Things here do not change. The water falls but the pools never overflow and never empty; they have found their level and kept it for longer than memory. The ferns grow but the gardens never overgrow; they reached their fullness and stopped, exactly as the crystals once stopped, as if growth here is allowed only up to the brim of the form and then politely declines to continue. Children are born and the old die, but the number of dwellings never changes, and the new child moves into the cell the old one left, and the felt is rewoven from the same wool, and the bowl is finished by the grandchild and used by the great-grandchild, and the place holds its shape across the generations the way a crystal holds its angles. It is a city in equilibrium so complete that time inside it seems to have laid down and gone quietly to sleep.

The people know this. They have a word for it that does not translate, but it points at the feeling of being inside something that is finished. They are not trapped. The low wet threshold is always open; the river runs past it; some leave, a few, and go out into the loud changing world of seasons and weather and growth-and-rot, and most of those never come back, and that is allowed too. But those who stay, stay because here, alone of all the places they know, nothing is being lost. The light does not fade at evening because there is no evening. The cold does not deepen because there is no winter coming. The water that fell on the first family to find this hollow is the same water, near enough, falling now.

Stand a while on a middle terrace. Let your eyes go soft in the sourceless brightness. Watch a single drop of condensation gather at the tip of a crystal house-high above you, swell, hang, and finally let go — and watch it fall, slow and shining, the whole long way down through the bright and the green and the breathing quiet, until the moss takes it without a sound.

It will happen again in a moment. It has always been happening. It is the only clock the city in the stone has ever needed, and it tells the same time forever.
