There is a place inside a seedpod that is also a foundry, and I will try to tell you how it holds both.

Begin small. The whole of it would fit, if you could fold it that way, inside the cup of two hands. But you are inside it now, and from here it has the dimensions of a cathedral that has not decided how large it wants to be. The walls are far when you look at them directly and close when you don't. This is the first rule: distance here is a matter of attention, and attention is in short supply, so most things stay far.

No water anywhere. Not even the memory of it — the air does not sweat, the heat does not bead. Instead the place is wetted by something else, a slow mineral sap that wells up through hairline seams in the floor and never pools. It is the consistency of warm resin and it does the work water would do. The plants drink it.

And there are plants — lush, the parameter said, and lush is right, but lushness here is spread thin across great vacancies, so that a single fern-thing the height of a man stands alone in a room of empty heat, and then nothing for a long while, and then a cluster of fat translucent stalks leaning together like people sharing a secret. The vegetation is not green. It is the amber of the sap it drinks, shot through with darker veins, and it glows faintly from inside when the light comes through. The leaves are not finished. That is the strange thing — they are still forming, their edges raw and unhemmed, the way a wave is raw before it breaks. You can watch a frond for a long time and see it pushing its own outline forward into the air, deciding where it ends.

The heat is enormous and patient. It does not move. It sits on everything like a hand that means no harm but will not lift. There is no wind to carry it off because there is nothing for wind to do here — the place is too contained, too just-begun. The temperature is the kind that would be unbearable if it ever changed, but it never does, and so the body forgets to argue with it.

Now the geology, which is the youngest thing here. The floor and walls are raw rock that has not yet learned to be eroded. No cracks of age, no smoothed corners, no dust of long wearing-down — everything is sharp-new, freshly extruded, the mineral equivalent of a wound that hasn't scabbed. In places the rock is still cooling from whatever made it, and where it cools it sings, very faintly, a high almost-silence you feel in the teeth more than hear in the ear. This is most of the sound: the thin keening of new stone settling, and under it the slow tick of the sap rising, drop by drop, through the seams.

And then the foundry. This is where the place refuses to be one thing.

Set into the far wall — which is near, when you look away — is machinery. Industrial, electrified, enormous: pistons the size of trees, a great wheel turning so slowly you must hold a thought against it to be sure it moves, belts of dark material running up into the haze where the ceiling should be. It is all working. Lamps burn along its flanks with the steady electric whiteness of a real factory, and that is where the normal light comes from — there is no sun in a seedpod. The machine is making something, and what it is making is the place itself. The pistons press out new rock. The wheel pours the amber sap. The raw fronds are spooling off a loom somewhere up in the dark, and that is why they are unfinished — you are watching them be manufactured, fed out slowly into the heat to grow.

But there are no builders. No animals, no workers, no maker walking the floor. The factory runs itself, untended, and there is no constructed shelter, no house, no road, nothing made to be lived in — only the apparatus of making, and the made world spreading out raw from its mouths. The machine is industrial; the world is wild and embryonic; they are the same continuous process and you cannot find the line between them. A piston-arm becomes a stone column becomes a living stalk without any seam you can point to. This is the deepest rule of the place, and it is the one that breaks reality a little if you look too long: there is no difference here between growing and being built. The plants are manufactured. The factory is alive. The geology is being printed. Everything is under construction and nothing is finished and the construction is the only event.

The breakdown shows most at the edges of your seeing. Stand still and the far wall holds its image. Move your eyes and you catch the world recompiling — a frond that was three is now one and was always one; the great wheel was turning left and has always turned right; the foundry-lamps were there and the rock was there and you cannot say which the place chose first. The contradictions don't resolve, they take turns. Hot and unfinished, lush and nearly empty, machined and untouched, vast and pocket-sized — the place does not pick. It cycles between them so slowly that within any single moment it seems merely odd, and only across a long patience does it become impossible.

Everything moves at the speed of the great wheel. A frond finishes its edge over what might be an hour. The sap clears one seam and starts another. The heat holds. Nothing hurries because nothing here has yet been given an ending to hurry toward — this is a world still in its first making, sealed, small, hot, and slow, manufacturing its own raw beginning over and over without ever quite arriving at a thing that is simply *there*.

You could stay a long time. You would not be disturbed; nothing lives here that would notice you. You would only have to make peace with the fact that the room is breathing, that the stone is new each time you blink, and that somewhere above you a machine you will never see is patiently weaving the next leaf, the next stone, the next moment of the place you are standing in.
