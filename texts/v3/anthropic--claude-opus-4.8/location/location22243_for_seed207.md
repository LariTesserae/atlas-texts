There is a room beneath the world, and it is cold, and it is growing.

Start with the cold, because the cold is the oldest thing here. It is not the sharp cold of wind — there is no wind this far down — but the patient, settled cold of stone that has not been warm in a very long time. The walls hold it. You can feel them holding it before you touch them: a pressure on the skin, a slight ache behind the eyes. The temperature sits just above freezing and does not move. It has not moved in a span of time that does not have a human number.

The room is small. You could cross it in a dozen steps if you could see to cross it, and you cannot, because there is almost no light. What light there is comes from the moss.

Let me tell you about the moss, because the moss is most of what is here.

It grows in layers — sheet over sheet over sheet, the older growth compressed beneath the newer into a substance dense as felt, dense as flesh. It coats the floor and climbs the walls to about the height of a kneeling person, where some old waterline must have been, and it stops there in a clean horizon, as if the room were half-filled with a slow green liquid. In the near-dark it is black. Bring your eye close and you find it is not black but the deepest possible green, the green that color becomes when it has almost given up on being seen. A few threads of it carry the faintest luminescence — not enough to read by, not enough to find your hand by, only enough that after an hour in the dark your eyes resolve a dim topography: the swell of the floor, the curve where wall meets ceiling, the slow vertical seams where water tracks down.

Because water tracks down. This is the second oldest thing. From somewhere above — a crack you cannot see, a fault in the rock that conducts the surface weather down through meters of stone — moisture arrives. Not a stream. A patience of water. It beads on the ceiling and gathers and falls, one drop, and then a long quiet, and then one drop, and the moss below receives each one with a sound so small it is almost the idea of a sound. *Tip.* Wait. *Tip.* This is the loudest event in the room and it happens perhaps four times a minute. Everything else is the held breath between drops. The room is mostly quiet the way a held breath is quiet — full of the thing it is not yet doing.

Where the drops land most reliably, the moss is thickest, and there other things grow up out of it: pale fungal stalks, fingerlength, blind and white, and a kind of fern that has forgotten it ever needed sun, its fronds translucent, its veins pumping something clearer than sap. They lean toward the water-fall the way other plants lean toward light. They are dense, crowded, near-touching, a small jungle the size of a doormat, and within that jungle is a whole saturated world that you would have to lie down and press your face into the cold to perceive.

There are no animals. No scuttle, no wingbeat, no eye catching the moss-light. Whatever might have lived here either never arrived or left long ago, and the absence is total enough that the plants have arranged themselves without reference to anything that moves. They have only the water and the cold and each other.

And the third thing. The thing that does not belong, and yet is the reason for all the rest.

Embedded in the far wall — you find it by the moss, which grows toward it, denser and brighter the nearer it comes — there is a made object. Most of it is buried in stone, grown over, only a curve of it exposed: a surface that is neither metal nor glass, gray-warm to the touch when everything else is gray-cold, and faintly, faintly humming, a frequency you feel in your teeth more than hear. It is the engine of the place. It does not look like an engine. It looks like a stone that decided to be smooth. But it is doing something — it has been doing something for as long as the cold has been cold — and what it does, near as anything could tell, is make the water.

Not bring it. *Make* it. The drops on the ceiling are colder than the rock around them, and purer than rain that fell through any sky. The object pulls moisture out of the cold air itself, condenses it from nothing the air should be able to spare, and feeds it upward through the stone to fall down again, an inverted rain it built for no reason anyone left written down. It tends a garden it cannot see, in a room no one visits, far below the surface, and it has done this with such steadiness for such an unspeakable while that the garden has reached its own balance — grown to exactly the size the dripping can sustain, no larger, no smaller, weathered and settled into its single perpetual self.

That is the rule of this world: the machine waters the cold, the cold holds the green, the green hides the machine, and the drop falls, and the drop falls, and nothing here needs the sun, and nothing here has ever been warm, and the whole quiet arrangement turns over on a clock so slow that a human standing in it would feel nothing change in a lifetime — only the cold, and the dark, and four small sounds a minute, and the patient breathing of a thing that learned, somewhere past the edge of what we understand, how to make a hidden room rain on its own forgotten garden forever.
