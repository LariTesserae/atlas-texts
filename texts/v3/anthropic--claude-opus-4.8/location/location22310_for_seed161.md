# The Pinnacle Town

Start at the top, where there is no more up.

The mountain here is so old it has forgotten it was ever sharp. What remains is a stub, a worn molar of stone pushed against a sky the color of weak tea. Three vertical kilometers of erosion have rounded every edge the world once gave this place; the cliffs that drop away on all sides are not jagged but smooth, scalloped, soft as something licked by a slow tongue for an age. Rain did none of this — there is no rain. There has been no rain for longer than the rock remembers. The wearing came from wind, from grit, from the patient sanding of the high dry air, and from time itself, which is the strongest erosive force and the only one that never tires.

So: a flattened summit, no wider than a market square. A single feature, an area with edges, a place you could walk across in the time it takes to grow restless. And on it — packed, layered, leaning into each other — a town.

The town is made of the mountain. There was nothing else to make it from, so the builders took the rounded stone and stacked it: dry-laid walls, no mortar, because mortar wants water and there is none to spare. The houses climb over and into one another, terraced, corbelled, each roof someone else's floor, each wall shared three ways. Ladders of bone-pale wood lean everywhere. The density is the density of a coral head — every cubic meter occupied, near-touching, layered with the residue of long habitation. You cannot stand anywhere here without something built rising close on at least two sides.

The light is dim and stays dim. At this elevation you would expect a hard brilliance, but the sky carries a permanent high haze, a veil of suspended dust that has never been washed down because nothing washes here. The sun is a smear. Shadows are soft-edged and reluctant. Lamps burn in the doorways — small clay dishes of rendered fat, wicks of twisted fiber — and the town glows from within itself more than from above, a low amber pulse in the gray.

Now the strangeness that isn't strange, only unexpected: it is *lush*. Despite the dryness, despite the height, things grow here in abundance. They grow because the people grow them, and because the things that grow have learned to live without standing water. Fat-leaved succulents crowd every ledge, swollen with stored moisture pulled from the night air. Vines with waxy skins drape from terrace to terrace, fruiting in dim purple clusters. Moss-things, gray-green, velvet the north faces of every wall and exhale a faint resinous smell when you brush them. The whole town is furred and tangled with vegetation, hanging gardens stacked a hundred high, and the green is dark and deep because the light is poor — leaves grown wide and greedy to catch what little sun gets through.

The water comes from the air and from the plants. The people slit the fat leaves and drink the cool jelly inside. They string nets of fiber across the gaps between houses overnight, and in the morning the dust-veil has condensed on them, and they wring the threads into bowls. It is enough. It has been enough for a very long time. The chemistry of this place runs on sap and dew and patience, not on rivers.

The town is *loud*. This surprises everyone who climbs to it expecting the thin silence of high places. But the abundance of life makes its noise: there are animals everywhere. Birds first — not flying birds, the air is too thin to fly well, but walking birds, climbers, with hooked feet and long necks, that swarm the terraces by the hundreds, croaking and clattering and squabbling over the purple fruit. They nest in the wall-gaps. Their voices echo off the stone and never quite die down. Beneath them, smaller things: a profusion of insects in the moss, a hum and tick and rasp; lizards that bark; some soft furred animal kept in pens that lows in long mournful chords at dusk. The fauna here is dense as the town is dense, every niche stuffed, every ledge a habitat. People shout to be heard over it. The town speaks in a roar of life and the answering roar of its keepers.

But the *pace* of all this is slow. That's the thing to understand. The noise is constant but the change is not. The birds croak the same croak their ancestors croaked. The vines fruit on a cycle of years, not seasons — there are barely seasons here, only a long slow swing of warmer and less-warm, the temperature steady at a kind blood-warmth that the stored stone heat holds through the dim nights. Nothing here happens *fast*. A wall takes a generation to lean past its tipping point; a child watches the same vine reach the same window its grandparent watched. The town has reached a settled state, the oldest-stable-state, and it simply persists, loud and green and dim, on top of its worn-down mountain, going around and around in its long gentle gradients.

There is no road up. The cliffs are too sheer and too tall. People are born here and die here and the only traffic is the seedlings carried by the climbing birds and the dust that settles and the slow erosion that, in another age past counting, will finally wear even this last molar down to nothing — and the town will go with it, gardens and ladders and barking lizards and all, sifted gently down the smooth flanks into the dry void below.

But not soon. Here, nothing is soon. Here it is always now, and now is warm, and the leaves are heavy, and the birds will not be quiet, and the lamps will burn until the fat runs out and then be filled again.
