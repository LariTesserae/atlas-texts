# The Sweatroom

You come into it the way you come into a held breath.

The descent is gentle — not a plunge but a leaning. The ground tilts you down through a throat of pale limestone, worn so smooth by old water that it feels like the inside of something rather than a passage into it. By the time the surface light gives up, you are perhaps thirty feet below the world, and the world above has stopped mattering.

This is a small place. You could walk its full width in the time it takes to grow restless — a chamber, or a series of chambers that pretend to be one, the size of a generous courtyard. It does not sprawl. It crowds.

## The Heat

The first thing is the heat, and the heat never leaves. It sits at the high edge of bearable, a wet animal warmth that comes up through the floor — there is something deeper still beneath this cave, some slow geological furnace, and the stone remembers it constantly. You feel it in your shins, in the soles of your feet, a patient upward pressure of warmth. The air is thick enough to lean against.

And so everything sweats. The walls bead and run. Condensation gathers on the limestone overhangs and lets go in slow, irregular drops — not rain, but the cave's own weeping, a moisture that is always arriving and never quite a downpour. *Tk. Tk. Tk-tk.* Drops finding the pools. A surface that is never fully dry and never fully flooded; the wet lives in pockets — a still black pool in one hollow, a glistening sheet down a wall, a vein of trickle threading between roots — while a yard away the stone is merely damp, merely warm.

## What Grows Here

There should not be this much life underground. There is.

The dimness is not darkness. Light comes from above through fissures and the descending throat, filtered green and dust-gold, arriving sideways and weak, and it is enough — barely, generously enough — for a vegetation that has learned to make the most of almost nothing. Ferns crowd the wetter ledges, fronds broad and dark and faintly translucent, layered three and four deep so that the lower ones live entirely in the breath of the upper. Mosses upholster every surface that will hold them, deep and springy, beaded with the perpetual condensation. Pale vines descend from the fissures like the cave is being slowly stitched to the surface, and along them grow leaves the size of hands, oriented flat to catch the thin light.

And there are the others — the ones the dimness invented. Fungi the color of old ivory, rising in shelves and towers. A low ground-cover that holds a faint cool luminescence in its tissues, not bright enough to read by but bright enough to see where you step, a soft blue-green that does the work the absent sun won't. Where the moss and the glowfern meet, the floor seems to hold its own dim weather.

The density is the point. There is no empty volume here. Everything touches something. Roots braid through moss, fungi sprout from rotting fronds, vines knot, water films across leaf and leaf-shadow. To move is to part curtains.

## The Quiet

And through all of this — silence. Or what passes for silence in a living place: the *tk* of dropping water, the faint sigh of warm air rising along the walls, the almost-inaudible rustle when something small shifts its weight in the ferns. No wind to speak of. No roar of any kind. The heat seems to muffle sound the way it muffles motion, and the abundant life here has learned to be quiet — there are no birds to fill the air with song, and the things that do live here keep their counsel.

Because there is much that lives here.

## The Abundant Small

The fauna is everywhere once your eyes adjust, and it is patient, and it is mostly small. Translucent things in the pools — blind shrimp the color of glass, tracing the rim with their antennae. Pale amphibians, smooth-skinned and slow, that sit in the moss with their throats pulsing, eyes either large and dark or grown over with skin. Insects in clouds and constellations: glow-gnats that the luminous fern seems to call, beetles ferrying fragments of leaf, things with too many legs threading the root-mesh.

And quietly, the predators. A spider whose web spans an entire fern, holding condensation in a thousand droplets so that it looks like jewelry until it catches something. A long pale salamander-thing that hunts the shrimp pools, unhurried, certain. The cave is not still — it is busy, ceaselessly busy, but at a pace that asks nothing of urgency. Eat, be eaten, breed, decay, feed the moss. The whole place is a single slow stomach, digesting itself and growing.

## The Marks of Hands

And here is the unusual thing, the thing that makes you stop:

Someone built here. Long ago. Not much — a low wall of stacked stone across one of the wetter hollows, half-collapsed, completely mossed, the work of hands so old it has nearly rejoined the rock. A niche carved into the limestone, deliberately, with a shape inside it worn past recognizing. A line of flat stones set into the floor as a path, now sunk and slick. Three or four such marks, scattered, no more.

No tools remain. No metal, no device, nothing that hums or holds a charge. Whoever they were, they came with their hands and their patience and they made these small still things — a wall, a niche, a path — and then the cave took them back, slowly, the way it takes everything. The erosion here is not violent; it is the long settled erosion of a place that has been exactly this place for a very long time, water doing in millennia what it cannot do in a year.

You find yourself wondering what the niche held. You find yourself, in the green dim and the patient heat, lowering your voice though you have not spoken.

## The Rule of the Place

There is one law here, and the cave teaches it without words: *nothing dries, nothing dies entirely, nothing leaves.* The warmth keeps the engine running. The water keeps it soft. The light is just enough and never more. Everything that enters — a fallen leaf from the surface, a drowned insect, a wall built by the forgotten — is taken into the slow wet green and made part of it.

You are in it now too. You can feel the warmth come up through your feet. You can feel the air settle on your skin like a second skin. The drops keep their slow time.

The place is becoming, very gently, more itself around you. It is in no hurry. It has all the time there is.
