# The Hollow at the Top of the Old Tree

There is a tree so old that it has stopped trying to grow. It reached its full height in some forgotten age and has spent the eons since simply holding its shape against the wind — its bark gone smooth and grey as weathered bone, its highest branches worn down to gentle nubs, the way a river stone is worn. Lightning has struck it and it has healed. Whole centuries of insects have bored through it and moved on. It is not dying. It has simply arrived at what it will always be.

We are not interested in the tree, though. We are interested in a single hollow near its crown — a chamber no larger than a wine cask, opened where a great limb broke away an age ago and the wound sealed itself into a smooth-walled room.

This is the whole world.

## The Terrain

The floor of the hollow is not flat. It is the cupped, rotted heartwood of the old break, gone soft and rich and dark, like packed coffee grounds, terraced into shelves and ledges where harder grain resisted decay. There is no standing water here — none. The air is dry, almost peppery with the smell of resin and warm wood-dust. What moisture exists arrives as the faintest morning film on the inner walls and is gone within the hour, drunk by the mosses before it can pool.

Because the hollow sits high — near the crown, where the wind passes and the light comes slanting — it is always warm. Sun-warmth seeps through the thin upper wall and holds in the wood like heat in a loaf of bread long after the sun has moved on. By the standards of this small place it is a tropical country.

## The Vegetation

Despite the dryness, the place is green to the point of crowding. The walls are upholstered in cushion-mosses and tiny ferns no taller than an eyelash, growing in stacked tiers, each tier shading the one below. Lichens hang in pale rags from the ceiling-curve. A single seedling — fallen here from who-knows-where, a real tree's child trying to grow inside the corpse-chamber of another — has unfurled three optimistic leaves it will never make a fourth from; there is not enough light, not enough root-room. It will live for years exactly as it is, neither thriving nor dying. Everything here has learned to do that.

The plants don't need rain. They drink the morning film and the breath of all the animals.

## The Fauna

And there *are* animals. This is the truth of the place: it is overwhelmingly, dizzyingly alive.

Per its size, this hollow holds more creatures than a city holds people. Mites move through the moss-forests in herds. Springtails detonate off the ledges in panic. Pseudoscorpions ride the legs of beetles like passengers in palanquins. There are pale spiders the size of dust who have built entire dynasties in a single fern, and predatory mites who hunt them, and tinier things that hunt *those*. Bark-lice graze the lichen in slow oxen-files. A centipede the length of a fingernail moves through all this as a leviathan, a god of teeth, and where it passes the moss-cities fall silent and then, behind it, resume.

Everything is eating, fleeing, mating, dying. Generations turn over in days. There is a constant churn — the active, restless pulse of an ecosystem at full tilt, predator and prey, bloom and rot, the whole engine running hot.

## The Built World

And here is the strange part: it is *settled*. It is *urban*.

The ants have been here longer than memory. Through dozens of their own dynasties they have organized the hollow into something like a town. Galleries are carved into the soft heartwood in tiers — nurseries low and warm, granaries high and dry, roads of cleared wood-dust connecting them, scent-paved and maintained. They farm the moss in plots. They keep aphids in pens on the seedling's three doomed leaves, milking them. They have a midden, a graveyard, a defensive wall of chewed pulp at the eastern lip where the wind comes through.

This is the primitive technology of the place: chewed pulp, dragged fiber, hand-built (mouth-built) architecture, no metal, no fire, only the patient manual craft of mandibles. But it is *organized*. There is infrastructure. There is a plan older than any single ant alive, carried forward in the shape of the rooms themselves.

## Light and Sound

The light comes dim and amber, filtered through the thin upper wall — never bright, always like late afternoon, always gold. In this perpetual evening the moss glows faintly and the dust hangs visible in the slanting beams.

But it is *loud*. For its scale, deafening. The hollow is a resonating chamber, a wooden drum, and every footstep of every beetle, every scrape of mandible, every wingbeat of the gnat-swarms that hang near the ceiling — all of it amplifies in the wood and overlaps into a dense, continuous hum. To stand at the center (were you small enough) would be to stand in a marketplace at noon, a roar of a thousand small businesses being conducted at once.

## The Rules of This World

- Nothing grows larger; everything has reached its size and holds it.
- Water never gathers; it is breathed, filmed, and drunk before it can become a pool.
- The dead are not wasted — the floor is made of the dead, and the moss feeds on it, and the herds feed on the moss.
- The tree does not change. Only its tenants do.
- Time is fast inside and slow outside. A whole ant civilization rises and forgets and rises again in the span of one of the tree's quiet, dreaming seasons.

It is a complete world, sealed and warm and crowded and golden, hidden near the top of something that long ago stopped reaching for the sky and simply decided to *remain* — and gave, in its remaining, a home to multitudes who will never know they live inside a wound that healed.
