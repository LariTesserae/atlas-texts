# The Spireworks

Start with the heat, because everything here begins with the heat.

It comes up through the rock before the sun touches it — a deep, mineral warmth, as if the mountains themselves are still cooling from some birth that happened a million years ago and isn't finished yet. By midmorning the air over the high passes shimmers and bends, and the spires in the distance seem to wade through their own reflections.

These are the spires: a forest of stone needles, some of them three thousand meters tall, packed so close together that from below you cannot see between them. They are not random. Long erosion has settled them into a balance — softened their edges, hollowed their flanks into long vertical flutings where rare rain has run for ages. The rock is a pale rust color, banded with darker strata, and where the bands meet the surface they wear into shelves and ledges, so that each spire is ringed at intervals like the growth-marks on a tree. Nothing about them is sharp anymore. They are old and they know it.

And they are *packed*. This is the thing the eye fights to accept. There is no open valley floor here, no clearing — the spires rise from a base of more spires, smaller ones, broken ones, leaning ones, with the larger ones crowding up behind, layer on layer, all the way to the limit of sight. To stand among them is to stand inside a held breath. The horizon is always close because something always stands in front of it.

## Water

The dryness is real, but it isn't total. Water lives here in pockets and intervals.

At the bases of the tallest spires, where the morning shade lingers, condensation gathers. The flutings channel it. It runs down in thin dark threads and collects in the shelved hollows, and these hollows hold pools — small, clear, lukewarm from the warm rock, fringed with the few plants that have learned to wait. Sometimes a true rain comes, sweeping in from over the eastern ridges, and for an hour the whole spireworks runs with water, every flute and channel singing, the pools brimming and overflowing one into the next in tiny cascades. Then it stops, and the heat takes it back, and the rock steams faintly until noon.

There are also the cisterns. More on those below.

## Vegetation

Sparse, deliberate, clinging. On the shaded ledges grow low cushion-plants, gray-green, leathery, holding their moisture inside thick skins. Higher up, in the wind, a kind of pale lichen makes maps across the stone — slow, century-patient. In the moist hollows there are reeds, surprisingly bright, and a flowering succulent that opens only at dusk when the heat slackens, releasing a smell like wet sugar.

Nothing grows tall. Height belongs to the rock. The plants understand this and stay close to the ground, close to the water, close to the shade.

## The Built Things

Here is where the place turns strange, and quietly so.

Someone built here, long ago and then more recently, and not many of them. Scattered across the spires — never crowding, always solitary — are structures. The oldest are little more than stone-lined platforms set into the ledges, weathered to the same rust as the rock, distinguishable only by their straight edges. Steps cut into the flutings. A doorway with no building behind it anymore.

But the more recent works are the puzzle. Bolted to the flanks of certain great spires, at impossible heights, are *engines* — dark iron machines the size of houses, set on cantilevered platforms, connected by cables that run from spire to spire in long catenary curves you only notice when the light catches them. The engines drive pumps. The pumps lift water — from where, it isn't clear; from deep in the spires, perhaps, from cisterns sunk into the rock's heart — and move it along pipe-runs that thread the whole region, feeding the high pools, the far hollows, places water could never otherwise reach.

This is the industry of the place: a vast, patient irrigation of stone. Mechanized, electrified — there are cables and there is current, you can smell ozone near the junction-boxes — but *slow*. The engines turn over once a minute, twice. They are tending something. The whole spireworks has been quietly plumbed by hands that are mostly gone now, the work continuing on momentum and the few who remain to oil the bearings.

## Sound

And yet it is quiet. This is the deepest contradiction and the truest thing about the place.

You expect industry to roar. It doesn't. The engines are geared so low that they make almost no sound — a deep, occasional *thock* of a piston completing its stroke, far off, then silence. The cables hum only when the wind crosses them at the right angle, a single low note that comes and goes. Water drips. Somewhere a pump exhales.

Mostly there is the silence of great height — the thin, clean silence where sound has too far to travel and gives up. Your own breathing is loud. A pebble dislodged falls for a long time before you hear it land, if you hear it at all.

## Fauna

But the place is *alive*, abundantly, and the life is what fills the spaces the silence leaves.

Things fly here. The spires are riddled with the flying creatures of high dry country — long-winged gliders that ride the heat-columns rising off the sunward faces, never beating their wings, circling for hours. They nest in the flutings. At certain times of day the whole pillared forest is threaded with them, wheeling silently between the stone needles, casting moving shadows.

On the ledges live the climbers: small, quick, hot-blooded things with pads that grip the rust-rock, that drink at the pools and bask on the warm shelves and dart into crevices when the gliders stoop. There are predators and prey here, a full economy of it, played out in near-silence on vertical ground — a pursuit across a sheer face, a stillness, a sudden motion, done.

And in the warm pools live the smallest residents: pale things that have never seen the open sky, that came up through the pumps from the cisterns and stayed, breeding in the lukewarm water, glassy and blind.

The fauna keeps the engines company. I think the builders knew it would. I think the whole irrigation was, in some part, a way of inviting the life upward — pulling water to the heights so that the heights could fill with wings.

## The Rule of the World

If this place has a law, it is this: *height is given, water is earned.*

The rock gave the height — three kilometers of it, packed close, ancient, settled into its long slow weathering. The height was free and total and indifferent. But nothing could live in it without water, and water does not want to climb. So everything that lives here has either learned to wait in the rare shaded pockets, or has been *lifted* — by engine, by pipe, by the slow current humming in the cables. The gliders, the climbers, the blind pool-things, the dusk-flowers: every one of them is downstream of a decision someone made, long ago, to send water up.

The place runs on that decision still. The pistons fall once a minute. The water climbs. And in the great quiet between the spires, in the bending heat, the wings turn and turn and turn, riding columns of air over a stone forest that was dead until somebody taught it to drink.
