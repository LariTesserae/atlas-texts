There is a city in the dark, and the dark is warm.

Begin with the heat, because the heat comes first to anyone who arrives, though no one arrives. It is the heat of a body that has been sleeping under blankets — close, humid, slightly used. The air is at blood temperature, so the boundary between yourself and the world goes soft. You cannot feel where your skin ends. The dark presses on you like the inside of a mouth that has decided, for now, to be gentle.

The water is everywhere but it does not fall. It hangs. It beads on every surface and runs in slow ropes down the faces of the buildings, and the buildings sweat the way a stone sweats in a cellar before rain. The streets are wet underfoot — not flooded, just perpetually weeping, a sheen that holds the few lights there are and stretches them into smears. Where the water pools in the low places it does not reflect the sky, because there is no sky to reflect. It reflects the buildings, and the buildings in the water are taller than the buildings above the water, and they keep going down.

The city is old. You can feel how old it is the way you can feel age in a hand. The corners of things have been rounded by a long, patient weather — the curbs, the lintels, the carved heads over the doorways have all been softened until they are suggestions of carved heads, faces remembering themselves. Nothing here was built recently. Everything has settled into the position it intends to keep. The mortar has bled out between the bricks and re-hardened into the shape of slow time. This is a place that reached its final form long ago and now only deepens.

And it is a real city — that is the strange part. Not ruins. There are thousands of buildings, packed in the close way of old cities, the way cities grow when they grow by accretion: districts that don't quite line up, a street that becomes a stair that becomes a tunnel that becomes a street again. Arcades. Cisterns. A drainage logic so elaborate it must once have been the work of an entire civilization's worth of engineers, channels and grates and overflow basins everywhere, all still working, all still moving the warm water along its endless circuit. The infrastructure hums with competence. Someone built this enormously well.

But the buildings are spaced wrong. That is the first thing that is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that takes a while to name. The city is dense — you are surrounded, hemmed, urban — and yet between any two presences there is too much room. You walk down a street of close-packed houses and the next street is a long way off, farther than the walking should allow, and the dark between is not empty lot or rubble but simply *interval*, a held breath of distance that the city keeps between its own parts like a man holding two magnets apart. The city is crowded and the city is mostly absence, and both are true, and you stop being able to tell whether you are in a tight quarter or a vast plain with a few lit windows scattered across it.

There is almost no light. What light there is has no clear source — a dimness in some of the windows, the color of a banked coal, a paleness that gathers in the standing water and in the wet undersides of arches. You never see a flame. You never see a lamp. The light is just present in certain places the way damp is present, a property of the material, and it does not help you see so much as it tells you where the dark is thickest. Your eyes adjust and adjust and never finish adjusting.

It is quiet. This is a city, and cities are never quiet, and so the quiet is itself a kind of sound — a held silence with weight in it. Water moves: that you hear, the trickle and drip and the deep slow gulp of cisterns swallowing, somewhere far below, on a schedule. Occasionally a single drop falls from very high and lands in a pool and the sound of it travels for a long time, too long, arriving and arriving in widening rings of audibility. There are no footsteps but your own, and your own are uncertain, because the wet stone takes the sound and folds it back in.

Nothing lives here. No bird, no rat, no insect skating the pools, no fish in the cisterns, not even the white things that live in true caves. There is no animal pulse anywhere. And yet there is vegetation — sparse, scattered, deliberate-seeming. A single tree grows out of a fourth-story window, leafless or nearly, its few leaves dark and thick and waxed with moisture. A vine works along a wall and stops, as if it lost interest. Plants stand here and there in the wet warm dark with no light to feed them and they are alive, you can tell they are alive, and they are not bothered by the impossibility of being alive without light. They have made an arrangement with this place that you have not been told the terms of.

Now the deeper wrongness, the one the rest was preparing you for.

The water that runs down the buildings runs *up* some of them, on the far side, where you don't look. You know this without checking. The reflection in the pools is deeper than the buildings are tall because the buildings continue under the water and the under-city is the real one and the city you walk is its exhalation, its surface tension, the skin on cooling milk. The distances between things are not fixed; they are negotiated, moment to moment, by something that wants you neither to leave nor to arrive. You turn a corner you have turned before and it gives you a different street, and the difference is not a trick of memory — the city has simply decided, and a city is allowed to decide, here, because here the rule is that the made world is awake.

That is the law of this place: everything that was built has slowly become alive, not as animals are alive — there are no animals — but as the city is alive, as a single enormous patient organism that has digested its builders and kept their work as a body keeps its bones. The heat is its warmth. The water is its slow blood, pumped on the schedule of the swallowing cisterns. The plants are the few thoughts it still has, scattered and dark. The light is where it is dreaming. And it is dreaming actively — the whole apparatus is in motion, processes turning over, the water cycling, the distances breathing in and out, the reflections living their deeper lives, the whole drowned engine running and running in the warm dark with great competence and no purpose anyone could find.

You are inside something that finished becoming itself a long time ago, and what it became was this: awake, warm, wet, patient, and not in need of you. It does not threaten. It does not welcome. It holds you the way water holds whatever falls into it — completely, indifferently, at body temperature, in the dark.

And it goes on past where you can see. Over the rooftops, where there should be a horizon, the city simply continues into the held dark, district after district, all of it weeping its slow water, all of it spaced too far and too close at once, all of it dreaming its dim lights — a whole region, a valley's worth, a world's worth, of an old made thing that woke up alone and decided to keep the lights on, here and there, just in case.
