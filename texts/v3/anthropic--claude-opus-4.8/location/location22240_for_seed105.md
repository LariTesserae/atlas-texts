# The Drowned Highlands

Begin underwater, because everything here begins underwater.

The whole valley is submerged — not flooded, *submerged*, the way a thought is submerged in sleep, as though the water were the original condition and air the thing that never arrived. You stand on the floor of it, and there is a floor, terraced and ancient, and above you the water goes up and up into darkness without ever becoming sky. This is high country. You can feel it in the cold, in the thinness of the pressure, in the way the slopes climb toward summits that are themselves still underwater. A mountain range, drowned at altitude. The sea did not rise to meet it. Something else happened, long ago and slowly, and now this is simply how the land is: held in cold water, in near-dark, in balance.

The cold is the first fact. It is the cold of meltwater, of stone that has not been warm in a very long time. It moves through you without violence. You acclimate the way you would to a long grief.

## The Light

There is almost none. What there is comes from below and from the plants — a low blue that never resolves into morning. Down between the terraces, in the cracks of the weathered stone, something luminesces faintly. You learn to read the landscape by its absences, by the shapes that block the dim glow, by feel.

When your eyes adjust — and they do, slowly, over what feels like hours — you begin to see the architecture of the place. Not buildings, not yet. The architecture of the water itself: great slow columns of differing temperature, of differing clarity, standing in the valley like pillars. You can swim through a cold one into a colder one. The boundaries shimmer. The whole submerged valley is partitioned into rooms you cannot see the walls of.

## The Vegetation

It is lush here, against all reason. The cold and the dark should forbid it, but the slopes are thick with growth — not the swaying kelp-forests of warm seas but something stiffer, slower, more deliberate. Tall stalks of a plant like frozen rope, gray-green, rising in scattered stands with wide spaces of bare terrace between them. They do not sway. They are too cold to sway, or the water is too still, or they have simply decided not to. They stand like the survivors of something.

Between the stands, low mats of a softer growth cover the stone, and *this* is what glows — faintly, in pulses, a slow tidal rhythm of light passing across the valley floor like the breathing of a sleeping animal. There is no animal. There is only the plant, and it breathes light because that is what it does.

The spacing matters. This is not a jungle. There is much emptiness between the living things, and the emptiness is part of the place — long stretches of bare terraced stone, weathered smooth, where nothing grows and the blue light from the mats does not reach. You cross these the way you would cross a plaza at night.

## The Sound

And here is the strangeness that unsettles first, because the place should be quiet and it is *loud*.

Sound travels far and fast in water, and this valley is full of it. There is a constant low roar — not of current, the water barely moves — but of the stone itself. The drowned mountains *sing*. Deep groans pass through the rock at intervals, long settling notes as the established land shifts a hair's width against itself, releasing pressure held for centuries. These notes are felt in the chest before they are heard. Between them: clicks, ticks, the percussion of mineral cooling and contracting, a sound like a vast slow clock.

And under all of it, threaded through, a high singing tone that has no source you can find. It rises and falls. You will come to believe it is the boundaries between the water-columns, the temperature-walls vibrating as cold meets colder. You will never be sure. It is the loudest quiet you have ever been inside.

## The Built Things

Scattered across the terraces, mostly toward the valley's lower reaches, are the marks of hands.

Stone is stacked here. Cairns, low walls, the footings of structures whose upper parts are long gone — eroded, or never finished, or taken back. The work is simple: fitted stone, no metal, the patient craft of people who had hand-tools and time and the cold. A doorway frames nothing. A channel cut into the terrace carries no water in particular but was clearly meant to carry *something*. The walls follow the contours of the land with a care that suggests the builders loved it, or feared it, or both.

There were people here. There are no people here now. There were never, it seems, any animals — no shells, no bones, no fossil curl of anything that swam or crawled. Just plants and stone and the makers, who left their walls and their cairns and went wherever the absent go. The fauna of this world is zero and was always zero. Life here means only the green things and, once, the ones who stacked the stones.

You wonder how they breathed. You are not breathing either, exactly, and have stopped wondering about yourself.

## The Rules

This is the established state of things. Nothing is collapsing; everything has already collapsed and settled into what it will be. The valley is in balance — but a *moving* balance, an active one. Things happen here, on the slow clock.

The temperature-columns drift, very gradually, recombining. When two boundaries meet, the singing changes pitch, and the glowing mats beneath them pulse faster, as though excited, as though the light were a kind of weather and the columns were the seasons passing overhead. There is a cycle, and you begin to sense its length: long, much longer than a day, a breathing that takes — you estimate, badly — something between an hour and a year to complete.

And once, while you wait in the dark between two stands of frozen rope, the entire valley exhales. The roar deepens. The mats flare bright enough to throw your shadow on the terrace wall — a shadow, in this depthless dark, sharp and blue and *yours* — and then the light recedes and the singing drops an octave and the cold presses in again and everything is as it was. A tide that has nothing to do with any moon. The valley breathing, in its own long time, in its own state, asking nothing.

You climb. The terraces rise toward summits you cannot see, into colder columns, into darker water, the singing always above you. The walls of the absent makers thin out and stop. Above the last cairn there is only stone and the slow groan of the mountain and the few highest stalks, gray, motionless, glowing faintly at their roots —

and the conviction, growing in you with the altitude, that the water does not end. That there is no surface. That this is the whole world: high, cold, drowned, breathing, sung-to, lush in its scattered way, and entirely, peacefully, without a single living thing that ever had a heartbeat.

Just you, and the stone, and the long blue exhalations of a valley that learned to live without air.
