# The Town That Climbs

Start with the rock, because the rock came first and will outlast everything.

These are old mountains — not the jagged adolescents that tear at the sky, but something past that, worn down toward a kind of patience. The summits here have been rounded by an unthinkable span of weather into broad shoulders and saddles, slopes that fold and refold like cloth that has been worn soft. And yet they are *high*. That's the strangeness of the place: you stand at an elevation where the air goes thin and cold, where breath comes shallow, and the land around you is not the bare scoured stone you'd expect at altitude but the gentled, rounded contours of something ancient and nearly flattened — flattened from a starting point so far above you that even its end-state stands at the roof of the world.

So: a high, cool, weathered country. The light is dim, perpetually — not night, but the muffled silver of cloud sitting on the peaks. Up here you live inside the weather rather than beneath it.

## The Damp

Water moves through this place sideways more than down. Cloud drifts through the streets like a slow tide, beading on every surface — the iron railings, the moss, the eyelashes. Condensation is the dominant hydrology. Things drip. The stone weeps. Walls grow dark with permanent damp and the green-black sheen of liverwort. Now and then a real rain comes, fine and cold, and the gutters fill with the brief music of running water before the mist closes back in.

There are no rivers up here at the top — the water hasn't gathered yet. But you can hear it beginning: a thousand small seeps and trickles in the rock, the sound of the future rivers being born grain by grain, far below where the slopes finally pool their gathered moisture.

## The Green

For all the cold and altitude, the place is *lush* — densely so. The damp feeds it. Moss is the founding citizen: it sheathes the rooftops, the steps, the dead and the living wood alike, in cushions inches deep that exhale water when you press them. Ferns uncurl from every crack in the masonry. Stunted, contorted trees — alpine things, rhododendron and gnarled juniper and something with leaves like dark wet leather — grow wherever they can find purchase, which is everywhere, because over the long ages roots have found every flaw in every stone.

Vegetation and construction have stopped being separable. A wall is half plant. A staircase is a slope of moss with treads beneath it. The town is being slowly, gently eaten by the green, and the green is being slowly trained by the town, and neither minds.

## The Town

And there *is* a town — that's the thing that takes a moment to arrive at, because at first you'd swear the place was wilderness. But look: organized construction, terraced into the rounded slopes, layer on layer. Houses of dark wet stone with steep roofs to shed the cloud-rain, stacked up the mountainside in tiers connected by stairs and switchback lanes and the occasional iron bridge slung across a gulf. A settlement built vertically because there is no flat ground to build on, only the long worn shoulders of the mountain.

It is a fair-sized place — not a single hamlet but a regional thing, a valley-and-its-walls' worth of habitation, spilling over multiple ridges, with districts that can't quite see one another through the mist. You sense the *scale* of it more than you see it: lights at different elevations, the suggestion of more streets above and below the ones you're standing in.

## The Machines

Here is where it turns strange.

There is industry here — engines, the mechanized hum of scaled production — but the technology has the same ancientness as the rock. It is *worn-in* industry, the oldest stable form of the machine. Great iron funicular cables run up the slopes, drawing cars laden with — what? Water, mostly. The whole town's economy is the gathering of the damp. Brass and copper condensers stand on every high roof, fan-shaped collectors that comb moisture out of the passing cloud, and pipes carry the gathered water down through the tiers to cisterns, to wheels, to the slow turning of machines.

The machines run on the mist itself. Cloud-engines: chambers where the temperature differential between the cold outside and the warmer interior drives condensation, and the falling water turns wheels, and the wheels do the patient work of the town. You hear them as a low, ever-present, mostly-quiet hum beneath the dripping — the breathing of a settlement that has learned to live on weather. Electrified, dimly: weak yellow bulbs strung between the tiers, halos in the fog.

Smoke would be wrong here, too obvious. The factories exhale steam, which is only the cloud being briefly borrowed and returned.

## The Quiet, and the Life Within It

It is a quiet place. The mist swallows sound. Footsteps go soft on the moss-furred steps. Voices don't carry. You can stand in a street and hear only the drip, the distant engine-hum, the occasional groan of a funicular cable taking up tension.

Animals are rare — the cold and altitude keep most life small or absent. But what is here is precise and memorable. A particular bird, slate-grey, lives in the condenser-towers and drinks from the collection trays; you'll hear its single thin note before you see it. There are pale moths the size of a hand that move through the lit fog at dusk, drawn to the dim bulbs. And something larger that the townsfolk speak of carefully — a creature that moves on the empty upper slopes above the highest tier, where the green thins and the cloud is constant. They leave water out for it. No one says quite why.

## The Rules of This World

The active part — the *dynamic* — is the weather, always. This is a place in constant slow motion, though the motion is atmospheric rather than geological. The mountains are finished; they've settled into their final, oldest form and will not change in any human span. But the air over them never stops working. Cloud tides come in and go out. Pressure rises and falls. The condensers run faster on damp days and slow on the rare clear ones, so the whole town has a *metabolism* tied to the sky — busy when the mist is thick, drowsy when it lifts. The people have weather-bodies; they feel the engines' pace in themselves.

There is one rule everyone obeys: you do not waste water, even though you live inside a cloud. Because the cloud is not the same as the cistern. The cloud is wild and the cistern is earned, drop by gathered drop, and there is a difference between standing wet in the mist and holding water you can drink. The whole town is an argument, patiently won every day, that abundance and scarcity can be the same thing wearing two faces.

A place at the worn-down top of the world, cool and green and quietly humming, that drinks the sky for a living and is being slowly, lovingly reclaimed by moss — and waits, in the dim, for the thing on the high slopes to come down for its water.
