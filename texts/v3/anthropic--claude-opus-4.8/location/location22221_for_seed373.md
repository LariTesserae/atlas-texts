# The Singing Terraces

There is no water here, and yet everything is alive.

You stand on a high shelf of pale dry stone — a kind of weathered, wind-rounded mineral that flakes like old bread, soft enough to score with a thumbnail, but it has stood for ages. The terraces step down and up around you, dozens of them, each the size of a small courtyard, their edges smoothed by something that was never rain. The rock holds no moisture at all. Crack it open and the interior is the same powdery cream all the way through, faintly warm, faintly bitter-smelling, like crushed almond shell.

And out of this bone-dry stone, the forest grows.

It grows without water because it does not need any. The plants here run on sound. Their leaves are taut translucent membranes, veined with something metallic, and they vibrate — they *feed* on vibration. This is why the air is unbearable. The noise is not weather and not animal cry; it is the ecology itself, every leaf and stalk a string under tension, and the place is deafening in the way a cathedral full of organs mid-collapse is deafening. A wall of layered tone that you feel in the wet of your eyes, in the fillings of your teeth. You cannot hear yourself think. You learn quickly to stop trying.

The vegetation is lush — improbably so for somewhere this dry, this cool. Stalks like fiber-optic reeds bend in slow forests across the terraces, sheaves of them, glassy and faintly glowing, ringing at different pitches as they flex. Broad resonator-pads lie flat against the stone, drumskins the color of olive and oxblood, and they *boom* when the wind touches them — except there is barely any wind, so it must be something else moving them. Climbing growths spiral up the terrace walls, hollow and fluted, each one a slightly different note, so the walls themselves are pipe-ranks. The temperature stays cool, almost autumnal, and the plants seem to like it; they shiver harder when the chill deepens, ringing louder.

The strangeness sits in how *empty* it is despite all this life. The terraces are vast between their features. You can walk for a long time across bare warm stone before you reach the next stand of singing reeds. Everything is spaced as if arranged — a sparse garden across an enormous tablework, with great hollow gaps of nothing between presences. The density is almost a void. The fullness is all in the sound, not the substance. A single resonator-pad, then a hundred meters of empty pale shelf, then one spiraling pipe-vine alone against the sky. The place breathes through its emptiness; the silence-spaces between plants are where the sound goes to turn around and come back changed.

The fauna is abundant and you almost never see it. The animals here are made for the acoustic world — they are mostly ear, mostly resonant cavity. Things shaped like floating bells drift between the terraces, tuning themselves to the loudest stands and feeding on the overtones. Small scuttling creatures with cupped, dish-shaped backs gather sound the way beetles gather dew, carrying it folded inside them to quieter terraces, where they release it and a new stand of plants begins to grow. They are the pollinators of this place: they move *vibration* from one garden to another. You feel them pass more than see them — a pressure-shadow, a momentary dead spot in the wall of noise as a creature swallows the tone near your head.

And there are the built things, scattered, plainly not grown.

Standing among the reeds at intervals are slender structures of a material that is neither stone nor metal — black, seamless, cool to a touch that comes back wrong, as if your fingertip arrived a half-second before you decided to reach out. They are tuning machines. That is the only honest word. Their physics is not quite ours: they hang fractionally off true, casting shadows in directions the light does not come from, and they are doing something to the sound — listening, sorting, *answering*. Some are tall as masts. Some are no bigger than a chair and half-buried in flaking rock, ancient, settled into their hollows, their surfaces worn by ages of pressure-wave the way river-stones are worn by water. They have been here longer than the terraces have held their shape. Whoever made them, or whatever, engineered the entire ecology to run on resonance, then left the engines to keep it tuned. The machines are in balance with the gardens now. Weathered into the system. Part of the world's own rules.

Because that is the deep law of this place: nothing is still. The dynamic here is total, turbulent, a storm-state with no storm. The pitch of the whole valley shifts constantly. A stand that was a low drone an hour ago is now a shrieking chord; a silent terrace suddenly blooms into full ringing voice as a bell-creature seeds it. The tuning-machines chase the changes, retuning, and their retuning changes things further, so the system never rests. You watch a forest of glass reeds dissolve — not die, *retune* — its leaves going slack and silvering and falling to powder, while forty meters away the bare stone begins to sing as new growth forces up through it in minutes, drawn by some overtone the machines decided to favor. Creation and unmaking, constant, everywhere, never the same valley twice. The light stays even and ordinary through all of it, plain daylight from a sky you don't bother to look at, and that ordinary light over such restless ground is its own kind of vertigo.

There is no water and there will never be water. Here, sound is the fluid. It pools in the low terraces and pours over the edges and evaporates into the empty air and condenses again on the cool leaves of the gardens. The whole high dry place is a watershed of noise, and you are standing in its current, drenched to the bone in a flood you cannot see, in a forest that drinks the storm it makes.

Stay long and you stop hearing it. Stay longer and you start to.
