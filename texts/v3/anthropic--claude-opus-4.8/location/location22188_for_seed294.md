# The Drowned Works

Start with the water, because everything here starts with the water.

It is not ocean exactly, though it has ocean's weight. It fills a basin the size of a province, and it is cold the way deep stone is cold — a temperature that has forgotten there was ever anything else. The surface lies somewhere above you, a slate-grey ceiling that the dim light passes through but does not warm. You are submerged. High up, too — this whole flooded country sits sixteen hundred meters above whatever counts as sea level in this world, a drowned plateau, a bowl of black water held up against the sky.

And it is all made. That is the thing you understand slowly, as your eyes adjust to the dimness: there is no natural floor here, no silt-and-rock seabed. Everything under the water was built.

---

You can tell because of the geometry, even ruined. The basin floor is a lattice — vast interlocking platforms, terraces stepping down into deeper dark, channels cut in deliberate parallels, the stubs of columns standing in colonnades that go on past sight. Someone built a city, or an engine, or a temple the size of a region, and then the water came, and then time came, which is slower and more patient than water.

Because the second thing you understand is how *old* this is. Erosion has worked on these made surfaces for so long that the made-ness is nearly polished out of them. Edges that were once square are now soft as soap. Inscriptions — and there were inscriptions, you can feel the ghosts of them under your palm — have been smoothed to a faint corrugation, a texture without text. The builders are not gone the way the recently dead are gone. They are gone the way a word is gone after you've forgotten the language. The structures have settled into their final form, which is the form of almost-nothing, almost-stone, made-thing relaxing back toward mineral.

Nothing grows here. No weed, no kelp, no mat of algae softening the ruins. The cold and the dark and perhaps something in the construction itself forbids it. The surfaces stay bare. This is a mineral world wearing the clothes of a built one — every plane manufactured, none of it alive, all of it scoured clean by ages of patient water.

---

But it is not still. This is the part that takes longest to accept.

The water is *turbulent*. Not surface-storm turbulent — there's no wind down here. It is internal. Great slow rivers move through the basin, currents of different temperature and density that shoulder past each other in the dark. They are the engine's last function, or its decay, you can't tell which. Somewhere the old works still breathe — a terrace exhales a column of colder water, a sunken vent draws a long swallowing draft, and the whole flooded city is in constant churning circulation. You feel it as pressure-changes against your skin, as sudden temperature seams where you cross from one body of water into another that is two degrees colder and tastes of different stone.

The light flickers with it. What light there is — that dim slate glow filtering from above — gets bent and stirred by the moving water, so the smoothed colonnades flash pale and then go dark, flash and go dark, and the shadows of the columns swing across the platforms like the hands of clocks that have all lost their settings. Nothing here keeps time anymore but the light still tries.

Things collapse. That's the dynamism made visible: a tower-stub that has stood for an age finally lets go and slides, soundless at first then with a deep felt *thud* through the water, raising a slow cloud of fine pale grit that the currents take and smear across the basin for hours. And elsewhere, the same currents are *building* — depositing the carried grit into long banks, ridges, drifts that pile against the old geometry and harden. The place is destroying and remaking itself constantly, on the timescale of an afternoon and the timescale of an eon at once. Solaris-slow, anthill-restless. Settled into final form and never settling at all.

---

It is mostly quiet, and the quiet has a quality.

There's the deep churn-sound of the currents, felt more than heard, a low organ-note in the chest. There's the occasional collapse, that distant felt thud. There's a high faint singing where water moves fast through a narrow channel — the works whistling through their own ruined throats. But between these the silence is enormous and total, the silence of a held-up bowl of cold black water with no air in it and no voice that belongs in it.

And it is full of life.

This is the contradiction the place asks you to hold: no plants, no growing things, but the fauna are *abundant*. They don't need plants. They feed on the works — or on the chemistry the works still leak — or on each other. They are pale, mostly, having no use for color in this dimness. They move in the seams between currents, riding the temperature-borders like fence-lines.

There are the grazers, soft slow bodies that drift along the smoothed colonnades and scrape at the surfaces — eating the ruin itself, the slow erosion partly their work, mouths that have spent ten thousand generations polishing a god's architecture down to nothing. There are long ribboning swimmers that follow the warm exhaust-currents, threading the columns. There are the still ones that anchor in the dark cracks and open like hands when prey drifts past. They cluster where the works still breathe, sparse everywhere else — the basin is mostly emptiness, mostly cold dark water and smoothed grey planes, and then a sudden teeming knot of pale life around a vent, and then emptiness again. Balanced with the void. Scattered like the columns are scattered.

They make almost no sound. The quiet is partly theirs. They have no need to call across this water; everything that matters arrives as a pressure, a temperature, a vibration. They listen with their whole skins.

---

The single strangeness — the one thing that tips this place from austere into *unusual* — is what the currents carry.

In the moving water, sometimes, you find the grit organized. The collapse-dust and the deposited banks: where the currents have worked them long enough, they hold shapes. Not random. The old inscriptions, scoured off the walls, seem to reassemble in the silt — faint patterned ridges in the deposited banks that echo the corrugations that used to be writing. As if the water, stirring the works for ages, has memorized them, and writes them out again in dust, over and over, in a language no one is left to read, on a page that dissolves the moment it's complete and is written again downstream.

The fauna gather thickest where this happens. Whether they read it, or only feed there, you cannot say. Probably there's no difference. The meaning and the meal are the same pale grit, the same endless rewriting.

You hang in the cold dim water, sixteen hundred meters above the idea of a sea, inside a made world that has forgotten it was made, and you watch the current take the page apart and the soft mouths come to eat the letters, and you understand that this is the works' final function, the one it will perform until the water itself wears out:

to be slowly read to pieces, and slowly written down again, by things that do not know they are reading.
