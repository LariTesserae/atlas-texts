# The Sump Cities of Telch

There is no land here, only the *idea* of land, postponed.

You arrive at it the way water arrives at a drain — drawn, spiraling, certain. Telch is a vast low basin, a wound in the planet's skin so wide that its far rim disappears below the curve of the world. Standing at its lip, you cannot see the other side. You can only see the haze: a warm, brown-gold steam that rises continuously from everything below, because everything below is wet, and everything below is warm, and the two facts grind against each other all day and all night without resolution.

There was a sea here once. It did not evaporate so much as get *built upon* before it could finish leaving.

## The Floor

Walk down — and you will walk, the slopes are gentle, this is a low world, nothing rears up at you — and underfoot you find no soil. There is no soil because there was no rot, because there was no green to fall and feed it. The ground is a poured composite, a self-leveling mineral resin laid by the city's own machines, and it sweats. Constantly. The water table sits a finger's-width below the surface and pushes up through ten thousand seep-holes, deliberate perforations, so that the whole floor of Telch *weeps* in an even film. You walk on a mirror two millimeters thick. Your reflection walks beneath you, upside down, through the haze-light.

Nothing grows from this floor. What you take at first for moss in the seams is not moss. It is a slow gray *biofilm* — the city's chosen ecology, a deliberate thing, a single-celled mat that metabolizes the dissolved metals in the seep-water and lays them down again in new patterns. It is alive the way a tide is alive. It does not need leaves. It does not need the sun, which it gets anyway — normal daylight, ordinary and unremarkable, filtering brown through the steam. The biofilm is the closest thing this place has to a forest, and it is everywhere, and it is barely there.

## The Stacks

Now look up, and understand why the density crushes you.

Telch is built in *stacks* — not towers, towers are too lonely a word — interlocked masses of construction packed so tight that there is no street, only the negative space left between buildings that were poured against each other while still soft. The stacks rise on legs, on stilts, on splayed mineral roots that drink from the seep-water and pump it upward through the whole structure, because the buildings here are not dry inside. They are *meant* to be wet. They are grown-and-cast hybrids, lattices of that same resin threaded with conduit and with channels and with the warm circulating fluid that keeps them — what is the word — *comfortable*. They prefer humidity the way a lung prefers it.

Between the stacks, water moves. Not rivers — there is not enough fall in this low place for rivers — but slow braided sheets, canals without banks, the whole basin floor a single connected wetness that the city has divided and pumped and re-divided into a circulatory system the size of a continent. You travel by skiff, by gondola-thing, by the flat-bottomed barges that nose through the channels with their engines submerged and silent and their decks crowded past reason.

Crowded with *what*, though. That is the question the place keeps asking.

## The Fauna

There are things in the water.

Not fish — or, fish-shaped, fish-derived, but a thousand generations into something the original ocean never planned. They are abundant past counting, the warm seep-water is thick with them, and they cluster at the seep-holes where the biofilm grows and they graze it and each other. Some are the size of your thumb. Some are the size of the barges, gray-backed and patient, surfacing between hulls to take a breath the city has taught them to take. The locals — and there are locals, there are *people*, though I am putting off describing them — net the small ones casually, scoop them with bowls dipped over a gunwale, and eat them raw and warm and salt-bright while they pole on through the haze.

And there are flyers — heavy-bodied, leather-finned things that nest in the high wet stacks and dive the canals, and their cries are part of the sound, which I have not described, which I have been avoiding, because the sound is the hardest part.

## The Sound

It is deafening. I told you it would be.

It is not one sound. It is the *sum* of a saturated world: the perpetual hiss of seep-water rising through ten million holes, a white susurrus under everything like rain that never falls and never stops; the slap and chuckle of the canals against ten thousand hulls; the wet machinery of the stacks pumping, the whole city a body of working valves; the flyers screaming; the fish-things breaking the surface in their multitudes with a sound like applause that does not end; the people, packed shoulder to shoulder on every deck and walkway and stilted plaza, *talking*, always, a continent of voices in a hundred trade-tongues; and beneath all of it, felt more than heard, the deep arrhythmic groan of the stacks themselves shifting on their roots as the water table breathes.

You do not hear individual things in Telch. You hear the *pressure* of all of it, a wall, and after an hour your body stops fighting it and you begin to feel it as weather. Loud is just the climate here. Quiet would be a kind of death.

## The Tech, and the Weirdness

Here is the thing that makes Telch more than a swamp-bazaar grown monstrous.

The resin floor that weeps, the biofilm that lays down metal, the stacks that drink and pump — none of it was *built* in the way you mean. It was *seeded*. Somewhere far back in the established weathered history of this basin, somebody — or some process, the locals are not entirely sure it was a *somebody* — set down the first programmed mat, the first self-extending lattice, and let it run. The city is the slow precipitate of an instruction. It is still precipitating. It does not hold still.

This is the *dynamic*, the turbulence: Telch is never finished and never the same twice. The stacks grow and slump and are reabsorbed. A plaza you crossed yesterday is a soft pour today, off-limits, sagging back into the resin to be re-laid as something else by morning. The canals re-route themselves overnight as the structures shift. The locals navigate by a shared, constantly-updated sense — call it a feel for the grain — and by the biofilm itself, which changes color in advance of a structural shift, going from gray to a faint warning amber along the seams where the city is about to remake itself. They read the floor the way sailors read water.

The engineering is past your physics. The lattice computes — the whole city is a slow saturated thinking-thing, the water its medium, the metal-laying biofilm its memory, the pumping its pulse — but it does not think *like you*, and it is not clear that it knows it has people living in it any more than your skin knows it has mites. The weirdness is mild, only unusual: it is just that nothing here is fully dead and nothing is fully designed and nothing is fully *yours*. You live in a substance that is making itself up as it goes, and it is warm, and it weeps, and it never stops talking.

## The People

I said I would put them off. I can't anymore.

They are packed in. There is no room in Telch and so they have made peace with no room. They live a body's-width apart on the stilted decks, in the dry-enough pockets of the stacks, and they have a courtesy of crowds that strangers find unnerving — a way of moving through density without friction, a constant minute negotiation, ten thousand small yieldings a minute that keep the whole saturated mass of them flowing. They are wet most of the time. They consider being wet a neutral condition, like you consider being dressed. Their clothes shed water; their homes are designed to drain; their children swim before they walk because the floor is a film and the canals are everywhere and falling in is not a catastrophe, only a Tuesday.

They eat the fish. They harvest the metal the biofilm lays. They tend the amber seams and move out of the way when the city decides to rebuild a block, and they do not fight this, because fighting it would be like fighting the tide, and you do not fight the thing you live inside of.

Ask one of them where Telch ends and they will look at you, kindly, in the brown steam light, over the wall of sound, and not understand the question.

It does not end. It only thins, far out, to the basin's distant rim where the seep-water finally fails and the biofilm goes pale and the last stacks slump unmaintained into the resin — and beyond that, dry country, dead country, country where nothing weeps and nothing thinks and the silence, they say, is unbearable.

They never go there.

They stay where it is warm, and low, and loud, and wet, and packed to the last finger's-width with the endless self-revising body of their home — and they call that, without irony, *the world*.
