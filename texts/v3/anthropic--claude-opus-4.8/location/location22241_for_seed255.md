There is a place built entirely by hand, and it is not finished, and it never will be.

Begin with the dark, because the dark is the medium here, the way water is the medium for a fish. It is not the absolute black of a sealed cave but a worked darkness — a darkness someone has been adding to and taking from for a long time. Your eyes adjust and find a low grey luminance pooling in certain hollows, the color of breath on cold glass. There is no sun. There may never have been a sun. The light that exists is the kind that seems to leak out of surfaces themselves, faint and mineral, as though the made things remember having been somewhere brighter.

Now the structure. Everything underfoot and overhead is constructed. There is no soil, no bedrock, no natural floor anywhere — dig down and you find more building beneath the building, courses of fitted stone and packed brick and tamped ash, going down past where anyone has dug. The world is masonry all the way through. But it is masonry in the middle of being made. Half the walls are only knee-high, lines of stacked block laid out like the footprint of a city someone sketched and walked away from. Scaffolding of lashed poles leans everywhere, bound with wet cord that has not yet dried and tightened. Mortar sits in troughs, still soft, skinning over on top. Whole staircases climb four steps and stop in the air. The place is dense — there is barely room to walk between the half-walls and the propped beams and the stacked materials — and the density is the density of a workshop the size of a valley, every surface near to touching the next, layered, leaned, stacked, wedged.

The temperature is cool, the cool of stone before dawn, the cool that holds the back of your neck. And there is water in it, low water, the damp of a thing being mixed. It beads on the undersides of the scaffolding. It runs in thin threads down the faces of the new walls, washing pale streaks of lime. Somewhere unseen a vessel overflows in a slow rhythm, a fat drip every few seconds, and that drip is multiplied across the whole expanse — ten thousand drips, all slightly out of time with each other, so the dark is full of a soft irregular ticking like rain that can't decide to start. Pockets of standing water sit in the unfinished hollows, black and very still, and skinned with the same lime dust as the mortar, so they look solid until something disturbs them.

Nothing grows here. There is no green, no moss even on the wet stone — the world is too young or too made for plants, pre-life or past it, hard to say which. What grows instead is the building. The construction *is* the ecology. Walls rise. Walls slump and are re-laid. Mortar cures and cracks and is repointed. The whole place is alive the way a coral reef is alive, except the coral is brick and the polyps are hands you never quite see.

Because there are makers. This is where it gets strange. You do not see them clearly — the light forbids it — but you hear them, and the place is *loud*. Not the loud of machinery; the loud of labor. The grind of stone dragged on stone. The chock, chock, chock of something being dressed with a hand-pick, the rhythm doubling and tripling as more of them work, falling into and out of unison. The slap of mortar. The creak of loaded rope. Long groaning slides as a block the size of a cart is levered into place, and then a hollow boom when it settles, and the boom rolls away through the half-built corridors and comes back changed. Voices, maybe — a low call-and-response, a work-song with no words you know, passed from one dark gang to the next, keeping the rhythm of the lifting. The tools are simple. Wedge and lever, rope and roller, the adze, the plumb-line, the pick. No metal beyond soft worked copper. This is a world built with arms and patience and gravity, and it shows in the surfaces — everything bears the small irregular marks of having been shaped by hand, no two blocks identical, the walls subtly alive with the unevenness of effort.

The makers are rare, though, for all the noise. You go a long way between them. Sound carries so far in the worked dark that a single crew of stoneworkers fills a whole quarter with their clamor, and you can walk toward it for a long time through finished and abandoned passages, the sound getting no closer, until suddenly you round a half-wall and there is the dim grey suggestion of them — bent shapes, the gleam of wet shoulders, the swing of an arm — and you understand there are only a handful, and they are building something enormous, and they have always been only a handful.

The scale opens out the longer you stay. At first it seems like a single great unfinished hall. Then you find the hall has no far wall, only more scaffolding fading into the grey, more courses being laid, the work-front extending past sight in every direction like a tide-line. This is a regional thing, a valley-sized thing, the building of a country. And it is being raised at the pace of a body — slow, breathing, the rhythm of lift and rest, lift and rest. Things change while you watch. A wall you passed is taller when you come back. A staircase that stopped in air now climbs two steps further, the new stone still dark with wet. A pool you stepped around has been drained into a mortar trough and is gone.

And the strangeness sits underneath all of it: the suspicion, growing, that the unfinishedness is permanent. That somewhere far back the oldest walls are crumbling exactly as fast as the new ones rise. That this is not a thing being built toward completion but a thing being *held* — a structure maintained in the perpetual condition of almost-being, by hands that never stop and never gain. The erosion is low, just emerging, just the first soft rounding of edges, the first thread of damp finding the first hairline crack. But it is there, and it keeps pace. The drip that sounds like rain is also the sound of dissolving. The lime washing down the new walls is the new walls leaving.

So the place becomes itself slowly, the way it told you it would: not a ruin, not a city, but the act of building, made into a world — cool and dark and wet and loud, dense with leaning timber and curing stone, sparse with the rare bent figures who keep it just barely from finishing, and just barely from falling, forever in the gap between.

You could stay and lay a course yourself. There is always room for another pair of hands, and the song would shift to let you in, and you would never know, after a while, whether you were raising the world or only keeping it from going down.
