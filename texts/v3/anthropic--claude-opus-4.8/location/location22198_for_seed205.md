# The Canopy That Is the Country

Begin with the heat, because everything here begins with the heat. It rises from below, not from a sun — the sun is a rumor filtered through twelve stories of leaves before it reaches anything that could be called ground, and by then it is only a green dusk, a swimming light the color of pond-water. The heat comes instead from the vegetation itself. This much plant, packed across this much of a world, generates its own warmth the way a compost heap does, the way a crowd does. Decay and growth happen so fast and so constantly that the air is a slow furnace, wet at the edges, and you sweat the moment you stop moving and you sweat more when you move.

The vegetation is not a forest. Forest implies trees with space between them, an understory, a floor. Here there is no floor anyone has reached. The plant-mass is the substrate, the architecture, the terrain — a single braided continent of stem and root and aerial trunk, layered on itself for kilometers of depth, the lower strata long dead and composting into the structure that holds the living strata aloft. You do not walk on the land. You walk on the country's own body, and it is springy underfoot, and warm, and it breathes.

## The Layers

Think of it as elevation measured in life rather than rock. This place sits high — a plateau, you'd guess, if you could ever find stone, fifteen hundred meters of something raised above whatever lies beneath. But the height you actually experience is the height *within* the mass: the long vertical climb from the dim composting deeps to the searing upper crown where the leaves finally meet sky.

In the deeps it is dark and almost cool by comparison, a brown-black world of fibrous beams thick as cathedral columns, slick with the slow water that collects down there. This is the only reliably wet zone. Everywhere else the moisture is intermittent — it arrives as the canopy's own weather. Higher up, where the heat is fiercest and the light strongest, the great leaves transpire so much vapor that clouds form *inside* the vegetation, drift between the upper boughs, and release sudden warm rains that never reach the deeps because every drop is caught and drunk on the way down. You can stand in a downpour and watch the water vanish leaf by leaf above the next layer, so that ten meters below it is bone-dry tinder and dust of spores.

So: pockets of water in a larger dryness, yes — but the pockets move. They are weather, not geography. You learn to read where the rain will pool and when, the way a desert people read wells.

## The Sound

You will not have a quiet thought here again.

This is the thing the place does to a visitor first and worst. The fauna is overwhelming — not in size, mostly, but in *number* and in *voice* — and the architecture of the plant-mass is a resonating chamber the scale of a small sea. Every hollow stem is a flute. Every taut sheet of leaf is a drum-skin. Every braided trunk carries vibration for kilometers. And into this instrument pours the sound of uncountable animals: the shrieking of things in the crown, the deep tidal hum of things in the deeps, the click and saw and whirr of the small bodies that make up most of the living weight of the world.

It is deafening. Genuinely — the kind of sound that becomes pressure, that you feel in the sternum and the teeth. There are moments, rare, when the whole country seems to inhale at once and fall briefly silent, and that silence is so total and so wrong that animals panic in it, and within seconds the noise crashes back twice as loud, an avalanche of relief.

The creatures here have evolved their entire signaling around this din. Some have given up on sound and speak in flashes of color or in vibrations sent through the living trunks themselves, drumming messages with specialized feet, so that the whole structure is also a single trembling telegraph that the attuned can feel through the soles.

## The Animals

They outnumber everything. The plant builds the house and the animals fill every room of it and most of the walls. There is little open space — the density of *presences*, paradoxically, is sparse: the world is so vast that any one creature, any one structure, sits in a great deal of emptiness, scattered. You can travel a long way through dim green halls and meet nothing. And then a single bough will hold more living things than a city, a writhing district of them, and you understand that the world is sparse the way the ocean is sparse: enormous emptiness wrapped around dense reefs of life.

The animals are mostly small and mostly strange. The strangeness is real here — call it 2 out of 3, a steady wrongness rather than a chaos. The bodies follow the logic of a place where down is dangerous and the structure is everything:

- Creatures that have lost their legs entirely and become *hooks*, living grappling-bodies that swing through the lattice and never let go of more than one limb at a time.
- Things that farm the plant they live in, pruning and tending a personal garden of edible shoots, defending it, dying in it.
- Translucent grazers, the size of a dog, with no obvious head, that drift through the inner clouds eating mist and the things that live in mist.
- And the deep-dwellers, slow and pale and large, the recyclers, who eat the country's own composting body and whose passage through the dark beams keeps the whole structure from suffocating in its own dead weight. They are the closest thing this world has to weather underground — a slow churning that the people above feel as a faint warmth rising on certain days.

## The People

There are people. You can tell by the marks — scattered, faint, the *built* element of this world set very low. No cities. No metal to speak of, or only the simplest worked metal, more likely bone and sharpened wood and rope braided from the country's own fibers. They are climbers, of course; everyone here is a climber or is dead. They make small things: a platform lashed into a junction of boughs, a shelter woven from living leaves still attached so it grows around them and must be re-pruned, a set of hooks and lines for the long traverses, drums for speaking across the din.

Their technology is primitive in materials but sophisticated in *knowing* — they read the weather-of-the-canopy, the migration of the rain-pools, the drum-language of certain animals, the safe routes and the routes that have rotted. A people's entire territory is a remembered three-dimensional map with no ground in it, passed down as song because writing is hard when there is no quiet and no flat surface and no permanence — the structure itself shifts as the plant grows and the deeps compost away beneath.

The world is **established**, in balance, weathered into its own long stable form. It has been this way a very long time. The composting-down at the bottom matches the growing-up at the top; the country sheds its dead lower self at the same rate it climbs higher, so that across centuries the entire living surface migrates slowly upward, and the people migrate with it, their ancestors' platforms long since fallen into the warm dark below, becoming the beams their descendants will one day walk on.

## The Rule of the World

Here is the rule, the one thing to understand if you understand nothing else:

**Nothing here rests on the ground, because there is no ground. Everything rests on everything else.** The plant holds the animals; the animals' tending and recycling hold the plant; the people hold the routes in memory and the memory holds the people. Let any one part stop, and the whole vast braided country begins, very slowly, in deafening protest, to come apart and fall into its own warm composting dark.

So it never stops. It is always active — growing, raining its private rains, drumming, climbing, decaying, climbing again. A vast hot wet green animal of a world, made of smaller lives, breathing in the dark, singing too loud to hear itself think, and rising — always, imperceptibly, rising.
