# The Cistern Works

It is a room the size of a small church, and it is entirely underwater, and it has been here longer than anyone can name.

Begin with the water, because everything begins with the water here. It fills the space completely — there is no surface, no air pocket overhead, no place to come up and breathe. The water is cool, the temperature of a deep cellar, and it moves. Not violently, but with the steady purposeful current of something pumped. You can feel it press against you, a slow lean from one wall toward the far corner, where the old intake throats have been working without rest for longer than the concept of rest had meaning.

The walls are made. All of it is made — there is no natural floor, no bedrock breaking through, no sediment that wasn't poured by hands. This was a cistern once, or a holding tank, or some sub-municipal organ of a city that may or may not still exist above. The walls are masonry: brick laid in courses, the mortar long since softened and rounded at every edge. Erosion has been at work for ages, but gently, patiently, the way only water can erode — no sharp breakage, just the slow rounding-off of everything that was once a corner. Run your hand along a ledge and you feel no edge at all. Everything has been sanded smooth by the centuries. The bricks have gone soft as bread crust. The iron fittings have bled long rust-shadows down the walls, ochre plumes fixed in place like the ghosts of fountains.

## The Machinery

The industry is still running. That is the strange dignity of this place — it was built to do a thing, and it has never stopped doing it.

Along the long wall, set into recesses, are the pumps. Great cast housings, drum-shaped, fed by pipes thick as a man's body that run up into the dark of the ceiling and vanish. They turn. You can hear them before you understand them — a deep mechanical churning that fills the whole volume of water, a sound that is less heard than felt in the chest and the teeth. The water is loud. Underwater, sound has no manners; it arrives from every direction at once, the grinding of submerged gears, the periodic *thunk* of a valve seating home somewhere in the wall, the long groaning resonance of metal that has held its load for a very, very long time. Bubbles rise from a leaking gland-seal in a constant silver thread, and they make their own small chiming as they go.

The machines are electrified, after a fashion. Somewhere conduit still carries current; a few of the fixtures still hold a dim filament glow behind glass gone green and furred with growth. The light is low — the color of late dusk, of a room lit by one window at the wrong end. Most of it comes from those few surviving lamps, smeared and softened by the water and by the slow drift of suspended matter. Shafts of it cross the room at angles, and within those shafts you can see the water is never truly still: motes turning, the wakes of small swimming things, the shimmer where the pumped current shears against the slack water near the floor.

## The Living Things

For a made place, a flooded machine, it is astonishingly full of animals.

The fauna here is abundant — not crowded, but present everywhere you look, threaded through the architecture as if the architecture were always meant for them. Pale fish with reduced eyes hang in the lee of the pump housings where the current is gentlest, holding station with the faintest fanning of fins. They are the color of the water, the color of old milk, and they move in loose congregations, scattering when a shadow passes and reassembling without urgency. Eels have taken the dead pipes for dens; you see a head withdraw into a mouth of rusted flange, watching. Crustaceans work the soft mortar between bricks, grazing whatever grows there. Things with too many legs ripple along the smoothed ledges.

And there is the vegetation, sparse but holding on — weed in the lit zones, thin green tresses that stream out flat in the current and go limp where the water slacks. A few cushions of something darker, mossier, in the corners that catch the lamplight. It is not a garden. It is what manages to live where light reaches and where the surfaces have grown old enough to soften and hold a root. Most of the room is bare smoothed brick. The life is scattered — clustered where the conditions allow, absent across wide cool stretches of nothing.

## The Rhythm of It

The place is not static. There are cycles. The pumps surge and ease on some long internal schedule — every so often the current strengthens, the whole population of fish reorients into it, the weed lies flatter, the bubbling intensifies, and then it relaxes again, and the slack returns, and the swimmers drift back out into open water. Predation happens in the gaps: a larger pale fish runs down a smaller one in a brief silver scramble that scatters the school and is over in a moment. The eels emerge at the low-current intervals to hunt the crustaceans off the walls. There is a tide here, but it is a mechanical tide, a tide made by engines, keeping a beat that no longer answers to any sun.

There is nothing strange about it, finally. That is what settles over you, hovering in the dim churning cool of it. It is only a flooded old machine that kept working, and the animals that came to live inside the work. Everything is exactly what it is. The water is cool and it presses you gently toward the corner. The pumps grind. The lamps hold their tired green light. The fish hang in the current's shadow, patient, at home, in the small dark church that was built for water and is now nothing but water, and the things water keeps alive.

It will be like this tomorrow. It was like this a thousand years ago. The machine does not know it is old. It simply continues.
