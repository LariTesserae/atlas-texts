# The Sill-Town

It is small enough to walk across before the morning fog burns off — which it doesn't, not fully, not here. Call it a town the size of a held breath.

The ground is new. You can tell because the rock hasn't learned to round itself yet. Everything is edged: the stone comes up in slabs and shelves, fresh-split, the grey-blue of a mussel's inner shell, and where the slabs meet they meet at angles that haven't been softened. This is recently-emerged land. Sometime not long ago — a season? a generation? — it lifted out of the shallow water, and the water has not agreed to leave.

So the town sits in a place that is half-drowned and half-just-born. Between every building runs water. Not rivers — there's no slope to make a river — but a webwork of channels, films, standing skins of it, finding the seams in the fresh stone and lying there. The rock weeps. Press your hand to a wall and it comes away wet. Every vertical surface has a dark waterline and above the waterline a paler band where the damp climbs by capillary patience.

## The Building

They built densely. This is the thing you notice once the fog thins enough to see more than the nearest doorway: there is no room between the structures. None. The town is packed to its edges and then packed further, walls sharing walls, roofs leaning on roofs, the channels of water running not *beside* the houses but *through* them, under them, in stone gutters cut down the middle of what would otherwise be a floor.

The construction is all hand-work. No metal beyond the simple — copper pins gone green, a few iron clamps weeping rust-tears down the stone they hold. Mostly it's the fresh slab-rock itself, dry-stacked and wedged, fitted by eye and by the long memory of hands that have done nothing else. The makers learned to use the stone the way it wants to break — in flat sheets — so the walls are layered like the pages of a wet book, course on course, with reed-bundles and packed clay sealing the seams. The clay never quite dries. The reeds stay green at their tops and rot at their feet and are replaced, constantly, an endless small labor you can hear if you listen: the *whisk* and *tamp* of someone, somewhere, re-bedding a course before the wet gets in behind it.

Because the wet always gets in behind it. The town is a thing built against water by people standing in water. That contradiction is the town's whole character.

## The Quiet

It is mostly quiet here, and the quiet has texture. The fog eats sound the way wet wool eats it. What you do hear arrives close and small: the *tick* and *plink* of water finding its way down through the stacked stone, dozens of tiny separate droppings, a percussion with no rhythm. The suck of a foot lifted from a film of water. The faraway tamp of the reed-setters. A bird, occasionally — but only occasionally, because the fauna is thin here. This is too new a place to be crowded with animals.

What lives here lives sparsely and on purpose. A long-legged wading bird the color of the fog itself, standing in a channel between two houses, perfectly still, then gone. Pale fish, finger-length, in the deeper gutters — translucent, you can see the dark thread of their spines, and they school through the town's plumbing as if the channels were a single nervous system. Some kind of soft snail that climbs the weeping walls at night and leaves a track of cleaner stone behind, grazing the thin green film that grows on everything that stays wet, which is everything.

That green film is most of the vegetation. The land is too young and too cool for forests, too saturated for grass that needs to dry its roots. So the plants here are the ones that don't mind: the algae-skin on the stone, beards of moss in the angle of every roof, a few reed-stands at the town's wet edges, and — this is the surprise — small hard flowering things that have colonized the rooftops, where the stone bakes slightly in the pale day. White flowers, mostly closed, that open only in the hour the fog lifts. Stonecrop and its cousins. The roofs of the town, seen from above, would be the only bright thing: a scatter of white on grey, blooming for one hour, closing again.

## The Light, the Cool

The light is ordinary daylight and that's almost strange given everything else — no gloom, no glare, just an even grey-bright illumination diffused through the standing fog. It makes the whole town shadowless for most of the morning. Things have no edges of darkness, only the edges the fresh stone gives them. In the hour the fog clears, shadows arrive suddenly, hard and blue, and the town looks briefly like a different and more solid place. Then the fog comes back off the water and softens everything again.

It is cool. Not cold — your breath doesn't smoke — but cool enough that the wet never feels comfortable, cool enough that the people here wear layers of felted reed and undyed wool and keep small fires of something that burns slow and smokes white, which is hard to tell from the fog. Cool enough that the water doesn't evaporate fast enough to ever drain the place. The cool and the wet are partners. They keep the town drowned.

## The Rule of the Place

Here is what emerges as I keep looking: the town is in a slow argument with the water, and the argument is the town's life.

The land rose. The water stayed. So the people who came to the risen land have to keep choosing, every day, to be above the water rather than in it — and the only way to stay above is to keep building up. That's why it's so dense, so packed, so layered. They don't expand outward, because outward is the wet edge where the reeds give way to open shallow water. They build *upward and inward*, course on wet course, stacking the new land's stone into a town that is always growing taller and never wider, like a coral doing the only thing a coral knows.

And underneath, the old courses sink. The fresh stone is heavy and the ground beneath is young and unsettled, so the town's lowest rooms are slowly drowning — being reclaimed by the water that never agreed to leave. There are flooded floors under the lived-in floors. There are stairs that go down into still, clear, fish-threaded water and stop at a surface that mirrors the ceiling. The people know which ones. They do not go down them. Sometimes the pale fish come up the drowned stairs at night and graze the green film on the bottommost dry step, and that step's edge is the cleanest stone in the whole town.

So the active process here — the thing in motion — is a vertical conveyor of stone and water and human patience: building added on top, old building lost to the wet below, the waterline holding steady through it all while everything else slides slowly through it, upward into use and downward into the drowned dark.

## What Emerges Last

If you stayed long enough — past the fog-clearing hour, into the cool blue evening when the rooftop flowers shut and the water's tick grows louder in the dimming — you would notice the strangest small thing.

The water in the channels does not flow downhill, because there is no downhill. It flows *toward the center of town.* All the films and threads and gutters lean inward, gathering, until at the town's middle there is a single round stone-rimmed well that is always exactly full, never overflowing, never low — a held surface, dark and still, into which the entire town's weeping quietly drains and from which, somehow, nothing visibly leaves.

Nobody drinks from it. They draw their water from the rain and the clean upper channels. The center well is for the water to go to. It is the place the town has decided the wetness belongs — a single feature, a heart, a drowned coin at the bottom of a stacked grey purse. The waders stand near it. The pale fish circle it and won't enter it.

And in the hour the fog lifts, if the light is right, the surface of that central well holds a reflection that is just slightly wrong — too deep, too blue, holding more sky than the small round mouth should be able to hold.

That's the town. New stone, old water. Built up against the wet by people standing ankle-deep in it. Quiet, cool, crowded, weeping, blooming for one hour a day on its roofs and drowning by inches at its roots, and keeping its whole patient argument gathered around one still dark eye in the middle that nobody looks at too long.
