# The Cloud Hamlet of Wet-Bound

There is a place, high up where the air keeps trying to become water, where a town grows on the spine of a ridge that has not changed its shape in a very long time. The ridge is old. The town on it is loud.

## The Ground That Is Not Ground

Walk here and you do not walk on stone. The stone is somewhere below — you can feel it through the soles, a hard truth under everything — but it has been so long buried in living matter that no one has seen it in generations. The floor is moss. Not moss as a coating: moss as a substance, a deep green peat-cushion that gives underfoot like the muscle of a sleeping animal, sometimes ankle-deep, sometimes thigh-deep where the hollows fill. It is always wet. Squeeze a handful and it weeps clear cold water, and the water never stops coming, because the air up here is half-rain even when no rain falls. Cloud crosses the ridge in slow herds. You watch it arrive between the houses and you watch it leave, and in between everything you own is beaded with it.

The cool is a gentle cool, not a killing one — the cool of stone springs and shadow. It keeps the green from rotting too fast. Everything is in a slow equilibrium of growth and decay so balanced that the ridge has held its form across centuries: settled, weathered, in-its-own-state. The turbulence here is not in the land. The land is patient. The turbulence is in the **life**.

## The Architecture

The people built here, and you can tell, but the building is hard to separate from the growing.

They started with a primitive craft — split wood, lashed cane, woven withy, smoke-hardened poles, walls daubed with the same peat that makes the floor. No metal to speak of, or very little; a few traded blades kept oiled and named. They raised perhaps forty dwellings along the ridge, terraced one above another, connected by walkways of plaited root and plank. A hamlet's worth of people. A town's worth of construction, because every dwelling is doubled and tripled by what has colonized it.

The vegetation does not merely surround the houses. It **is** the houses, now, in their second life. A roof thatched fifteen years ago has become a hanging garden of fern and liverwort and the small white orchids that grow on nothing but other plants. The support poles have rooted. Walls breathe. A wall here is four hands thick — daub, then moss, then a felted layer of fallen leaf and seed and rootlet, then more moss, then the living skin of whatever is presently growing. The town is plant-built as much as people-built, and the two arguments never resolve. The people prune. The green returns. This is the agreement.

Layer upon layer: the eye cannot find empty space. Vine drapes over walkway over hanging basket over the rooftop garden of the house below. Look up and there are three more levels of green between you and the bright pearl-grey sky. The density presses close — every cubic arm's-length of air holds a leaf, a stem, a dripping frond, a moving thing.

## The Moving Things

And the moving things are the whole point.

The fauna here is past abundance. It is **saturation**. The wetness and the warmth-enough and the endless green make a single vast organism's worth of small creatures, and they do not pause.

Frogs first, because you hear them before anything: thousands, in the moss, in the cisterns, in the cupped leaves where rainwater pools, in the eaves. Their calling is not a chorus but a wall — a thrumming, clicking, belling pressure that rises and falls like breathing and never fully stops, day or night. Tree-frogs the size of a thumbnail and the green of new shoots cling to every windowframe. Larger ones, fist-sized, grave and golden-eyed, sit in the doorways like householders.

Then the birds, which the green canopy keeps mostly hidden but never silent — a constant overlapping racket of whistle and shriek and machine-like ratcheting, sunbirds stitching between the orchids, ground-fowl crashing in the undergrowth, something high and unseen that screams twice and stops. Insects make the third layer of the wall of sound: the saw of crickets, the drone of the heavy slow beetles, the high needle-whine of the cloud of small biting things you wave away from your face and that close again the moment your hand drops.

The whole place is in motion. Always something flinching, fleeing, landing, calling, laying eggs in the wet, hatching, being eaten. This is the **turbulence** — not weather but biology, the anthill-energy of too much life in too small a bowl. Step off the walkway and you displace a hundred lives. The moss seethes. A stone lifted reveals a writhing economy. The people move through it all unbothered, the way you stop hearing a waterfall you live beside.

## The Rules

Nothing dry exists here, so nothing is stored that water can ruin. They eat what grows and what they catch and what comes in fresh; there is no granary, only the living larder of the slope.

The dead are given to the moss, which takes everything in a season. There are no old bones underfoot — the green is too hungry. This may be why the town feels, despite its age, so young: it eats its own history and grows it back as leaves.

Fire is sacred and difficult, because everything is wet. A single hearth in the largest house is never let to die. Coals are carried house to house in clay pots. The keeper of the coal is an office.

And the people listen, always, to the wall of sound — because in a place this loud, the only alarm is **silence**. When the frogs stop, everyone stops. When the frogs stop, something larger has come up the ridge through the cloud, and the whole hamlet turns its face toward the quiet and waits to see what walks out of the white.
