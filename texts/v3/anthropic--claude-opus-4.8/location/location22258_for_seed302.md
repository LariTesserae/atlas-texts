# The Singing Cirque

Start with the light, because the light comes first and never leaves.

It pours down out of a sky so pale it reads almost white, a high-altitude blue bleached thin by elevation. There is no haze here to soften it. The air is too cold and too clear, and the sun strikes the world without mercy and without warmth — a brilliance you feel on the backs of your hands as a faint cool prickle rather than heat. Everything is lit. Nothing hides. There are no deep shadows, because the surfaces that surround you throw the light back at each other, bouncing it, multiplying it, until even the undersides of overhangs glow with a pale reflected fire.

What it strikes is stone. There is nothing here but stone and water and the noise the water makes.

---

You stand in a high bowl of mountains — a cirque, a great rounded amphitheater scooped from the range long ago and left to settle into itself. The walls rise on three sides, tier on tier of pale grey rock veined with quartz and threaded everywhere, *everywhere*, with water. This is the first thing that overwhelms after the light: the rock weeps. Not a stream here and a spring there, but a continuous saturation, the whole face of the cirque wet from top to bottom, sheeting and trickling and seeping from a thousand thousand fractures at once.

The water has worked the stone for ages and has reached an understanding with it. The forms are settled now — rounded flutes and runnels and shallow basins, lips and ledges polished to a soft matte sheen, nothing raw, nothing newly broken. The mountain isn't crumbling. It isn't growing. It long ago found the shape water wanted it to have, and now it simply holds that shape and lets the water run across it, the way a stone in a riverbed has stopped arguing.

---

And the sound.

The sound is the thing you cannot prepare for, the thing that arrives before you have finished registering anything else. Every surface of this enormous bowl is in motion with falling water, and the cirque is a perfect curved shell that gathers all of it and throws it back at the center where you stand. It is not the roar of a single waterfall. It is ten thousand small fallings braided into one vast continuous voice — a white wall of sound with texture inside it, layers, a deep organ drone beneath where the heavier sheets pour into the lower pools, a high silver hiss above where the thinnest films feather down the upper walls, and through the middle a constant fine chiming where droplets strike the standing water of the floor.

It is deafening. You cannot speak and be heard. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it behind your eyes. After a few minutes your own thoughts begin to take on its rhythm, and the boundary between the noise outside and the noise inside you starts to soften. People who have stood here — though almost no one has — describe a strange thing: that the sound is so total it becomes a kind of silence. There is nothing for it to contrast with. It fills every available space and then becomes the baseline, the floor of the world, and the mind eventually stops hearing it as noise and starts hearing it as the *shape of the place itself.*

---

There is no life here, or almost none, and the absence is not menacing — it is simply complete.

No plant grows. Nothing grows. The water is clean and cold and mineral, too cold and too constantly scouring for moss or algae to take hold, and the stone offers nothing to root in. There is no soil. There is no rot, because there has never been anything to rot. This is a world that came before life or stands aside from it — a mineral world, fully itself, needing no green thing to complete it.

And yet — *rare*, the parameter says, and rare is the right word. Once in a long while, something moves at the rim. Pale creatures, if they are creatures: I picture them slender, white as the quartz veins, with broad flat feet that grip the wet flutes, descending the weeping walls in absolute silence — though of course everything is silent here, in the sense that nothing can be heard over the water. They come down to the pools, lower their faces to drink, and climb back up. You cannot tell if they are mammal or amphibian or something with no earthly category. They do not seem to see you. They have no business with you. They were here drinking before there was anyone to watch, and they will be here after. They appear, perhaps, once in a thousand of your heartbeats, and then the wall is empty again and the water goes on falling.

---

Nothing here is built. There is no mark of any hand, no tool, no path, no cairn, no cut. The cirque has never been used. It only *is.*

And nothing changes. This is the deepest strangeness of the place, deeper than the light or the noise. By every sign the cirque should be a place of furious activity — all that falling water, all that motion. But the motion never goes anywhere. The springs do not swell or fail. The pools do not rise or drain. The walls weep at exactly the rate they have always wept. There are no seasons that touch it; the cold holds steady, cool and even, and the light pours down at the same brilliance whether you arrive at what should be morning or what should be dusk. There is no dusk. There is no weather. The water cycles — falls, gathers, and the gathered water vanishes somewhere into the stone floor and rises again unseen at the rim — a closed and silent circuit that has been turning at one unvarying speed forever.

It is a place of enormous energy in perfect equilibrium. A storm that has learned to stand still. A roar that has become a stillness. Everything moves and nothing happens, and the whole vast bright loud bowl of it simply continues, exactly as it is, exactly as it has always been, brimful of light and water and sound and entirely, finally, at peace.
