# The Tableland of Slow Weather

Begin with the height, because the height is the first fact and the last one. You are nearly three units up — not up a mountain but up onto something. A plateau that has no edge you can find by walking. The ground here is the ceiling of the world, a flat that was once a range of mountains and has been worn, over a span the rock itself has lost count of, down into a single continuous floor. Erosion at its maximum is not violence; it is patience that has finished its work. Nothing here is jagged. Everything has been rounded, sanded, brought to rest. The stone underfoot is the deep red-grey of iron that has forgotten why it was ever iron, and where you scuff it the powder lifts and hangs — there is so little air-weight to it — and drifts sideways for a long time before settling.

There is no water. Not the dryness of a desert, which still dreams of rain. This is a place that completed its chemistry long ago and signed off on it. Whatever was wet has bonded into the mineral and stayed there. The cool comes not from moisture but from altitude and emptiness — a clean, edgeless cool that does not bite, only persists. Your breath, if you breathe, makes no fog. There is nothing for the cold to condense.

## What grows

Vegetation, sparse. Across the flat, at intervals you could measure in hours of walking, stand the **standing growths** — I hesitate to call them plants, but they are the closest thing. Each is a single dark spire, waist-high to head-high, made of something between lichen and crystal, dry to the touch, faintly warm. They grow toward no sun in particular. They have learned to live on the slow trickle of static that the dry air carries, and where two of them stand near enough they sometimes pass a thread of pale light between them, soundlessly, once an hour or once a year. Around the base of each, a small disc of grey moss-stuff, brittle as ash, marks the only soil that the spire has managed to make in all this time.

Between them: distance. The density here is almost nothing. You can stand at one growth and see the next as a dark vertical mark against a horizon that curves with the planet's own size. The scale is continental and then some. This tableland has its own weather, its own day-length to the way the light leans.

## The light

Normal light, but from where? There is a sky — a high pale sky the color of skimmed milk — and it is bright enough to read by, bright enough to throw soft shadows from the spires. But you will look for the sun and not quite find it. The brightness is distributed, as if the whole dome glows a little, evenly, and somewhere up there the source is diffused past locating. Shadows fall, but they fall *gently*, with edges that blur, because the light comes from everywhere slightly more than it comes from anywhere.

## The silence

This is the held quality of the place. Sound at almost nothing. Your footsteps land and the sound dies the instant it is born, swallowed by the thin air and the soft powder. You can hear your own pulse. You can hear the small dry tick when a spire passes its thread of light. Beyond that — a vast, attentive quiet, the quiet of an enormous room that has been empty so long the emptiness has become a kind of presence. When the wind moves — and it does move, slowly, in long laminar sheets across the flat — it makes almost no noise, only a faint dry hiss at the very edge of hearing, like the world inhaling through its teeth.

## The contradiction, which is the heart of it

Here is where the place becomes itself. Everything I have described is *settled* — ancient, eroded, empty, quiet, finished. And yet the air above the tableland is **turbulent at the maximum**, a storm-state, chaotic, in constant violent reorganization.

Resolve this by looking up.

The ground is at rest. The sky is not. High above the plateau the weather is alive in a way that has nothing to do with rain — there is no water to make rain. Instead the upper air churns with **forms**: bands of light that fold and refold, sheets of charged haze that gather into towering structures and collapse and gather again, faster than weather should move. Whole architectures of glowing vapor assemble themselves in minutes, rotate, shed daughter-storms, dissolve. The sky is a Solaris-sea turned on its side and hung over a dead-calm floor. It makes almost no sound — that is the eeriest part — this enormous churning happens in near-silence, a storm you watch the way you'd watch a fire behind thick glass.

The two layers do not touch. The plateau has finished changing; the sky has never stopped. They share a world and ignore each other, and you stand at the boundary between the most patient thing imaginable and the most restless.

## What was built

Scattered through the flat, the marks of makers. Not ruins exactly — nothing has *fallen*, because nothing here falls without being asked. Instead: low rings of set stone, perfectly circular, half-buried, each ring centered on nothing. Occasional slabs lying flush with the ground, their upper faces worked smooth and inscribed with lines so fine you only see them at a slant of light, and the lines are not language but *diagrams* — the same churning sky-forms, recorded, as if someone catalogued the storm.

And then, rarely, the **regulators**. These belong to the tech, which is past explaining. They are columns, taller than the spires, made of a material that does not weather — the only things in this whole worn-flat world that the erosion has not touched, and they are not touched because they are doing something to the air around them. Up close you feel it: a faint pressure, a sense of the world being *managed*. The turbulent sky does not descend to the floor, and now you understand why. The regulators hold the boundary. They were built — by whom, the diagrams don't say — to keep the storm above and the rest below, to let the plateau finish dying in peace while the sky rages on forever, harmlessly, overhead. The engineering is beyond what the place's own physics seems to allow. The columns are slightly *out of focus*, as if they extend in a direction your eye won't follow.

## The fauna

Rare, and it took me a while to find them. There is one kind of moving thing here: the **drifters**. They live in the boundary layer, the thin zone where the calm meets the turbulence, riding the slow shear. Each is a creature of folded membrane and faint internal light, the size of a held breath, and they school in loose ragged clouds along the underside of the storm. They feed, I think, on the charge the regulators leak. They make no sound at all. When a drifter dies it simply stops glowing and falls, very slowly, all the way down through the cool quiet air, and lands among the spires, and becomes one more thing the patient ground will take a million years to forget.

## The rule of the world

It is this: *above is becoming, below is become.* The floor is the answer; the sky is the question still being asked. And someone, once, cared enough to build the thin line of columns that keeps them apart, so that the finished world could rest, and the unfinished one could go on churning, and neither would ruin the other.

You stand on the line. You can walk for a lifetime and never leave it.
