# The Cistern at the Top of the World

Begin with the cold fact of altitude. You are higher than weather, higher than the places where mountains usually stop being mountains and start being theory. Up here the air would be thin enough to kill you — except there is no air. There is water.

The whole place is submerged. Not flooded, *submerged* — drowned so long ago and so completely that the word "flood" implies a before. There is no before that anyone living remembers. The water fills everything to the ceiling, warm as bathwater, faintly green, and it holds the light the way old glass holds it: thick, slow, willing to bend.

And this is the strange thing, the thing your lungs keep arguing with: the water is *up here*, at an elevation that should be all ice and vacuum. Some logic was broken to make this. Some engineer, long dead, decided that the highest possible place should also be the deepest.

---

It is small. That matters more than anything. For all its extremity, this is not a sea — it is a *room*. A cistern. You could swim its length in the time it takes to grow nervous about the dark corners. The walls press close, and that closeness is the whole character of the place: an enormous idea folded into an intimate body, like a cathedral the size of a wardrobe.

Everything in it is *made*. There is no rock, no soil, no natural floor — every surface was built. Beneath you: a tessellated pavement of dark bronze tiles, each one stamped with a maker's mark too worn to read. The walls: riveted plates, vaulting ribs, the bones of some industrial purpose. There were *machines* here once. There are machines here still.

---

But built does not mean new. This place is *ancient* in the deepest sense — not ruined, but finished. It has done all its eroding and arrived at a final shape, the way a river stone arrives at roundness and then simply *is* round, forever, with nothing left to wear away. The bronze has gone soft at every edge. The rivets are smoothed to gentle pimples. The maker's marks have dissolved into suggestion. This is not decay in progress; this is decay *completed*, a steady state, the oldest possible version of itself, holding.

The engines still run. That is the second strange fact. Somewhere behind the walls, a great slow machinery turns — you feel it more than hear it, a deep periodic *thrum* that comes up through the bronze floor into your chest, regular as a tide, regular as a sleeping animal's breath. Pistons the size of trees, worn round as the rivets, moving warm water in long convective cycles through the room. That is why it is warm up here where it should be frozen. The machine is the only reason this place is alive at all, and the machine has forgotten it was ever switched on.

The sound is *loud*. Underwater-loud, which is a different loud — it doesn't come from a direction, it comes from everywhere, soaking into you. The thrum of the engines. The groan of metal flexing against the water's weight. And above all, a constant ringing — the whole cistern is a bell, and the machinery never stops striking it, so the place hums one long enormous chord that has been sounding, unbroken, for an age.

---

Now look at what *lives* here, because plenty does, and it lives in the spaces between things.

The vegetation is lush but scattered — that's the *sparseness*, the deliberate emptiness between presences. Where the convection currents pool warm, great soft gardens have taken hold: kelp-like ribbons grown not from any seed but rooted directly into the bronze, drinking metal, their fronds the deep red-green of things that have made peace with strange light. Between the gardens, wide stretches of bare clean tile, empty water, room to drift. Nothing crowds. The place breathes in long intervals — a thicket, then a clearing of pure suspended green silence, then a thicket again.

The fauna is abundant and quiet about it. Things move in the open water with the unhurried confidence of creatures with no predators worth fearing and no prey worth chasing — they are mostly grazers, mostly slow. Translucent swimmers the size of your hand, ribbed like accordion bellows, pulsing through the bare stretches. Schools of something flat and silver that flick all at once and then hang still. And in the gardens, small armored things tend the metal-roots, cleaning them, and no one knows whether the creatures domesticated the plants or the plants the creatures or whether the dead engineers arranged the whole arrangement and then left it to run.

There are larger presences too, glimpsed at the edge of the green light — long slow shapes that circle the cistern's walls following the convection, never approaching, part of the machinery's rhythm. When the engine-thrum quickens, they quicken. When it eases, they drift to stillness. They are tuned to it. Everything here is tuned to it.

---

The rules of this world, as far as a visitor can tell:

You can breathe the water. You discover this without alarm — the place simply permits it, the way the place permits warmth at an impossible height. Do not ask how. The cistern has its own physics and they are old and settled and not interested in your questions.

Light comes from no sun. It comes from the water itself, which luminesces faintly green, brightest near the engines, dimming in the corners — a normal-enough brightness once your eyes adjust, like an overcast afternoon held permanently still. Shadows are soft. Nothing here casts a hard edge anymore; the place wore its hard edges away long ago.

And the last rule, the one that takes longest to feel: *nothing changes, but everything moves.* The cistern has reached its final form and will never reach another. Yet inside that stillness, the engines pump, the creatures circle, the gardens sway, the chord rings on — a furious amount of motion, all of it going nowhere, all of it perfectly content to keep going nowhere, a clockwork of life wound up so tight and so long ago that it has become indistinguishable from rest.

You float in the warm green hum at the top of the world, in a made room older than memory, breathing water, and you understand — slowly, the way everything here happens — that you have found the place where motion and stillness finally agreed to stop arguing, and decided to be the same thing.
