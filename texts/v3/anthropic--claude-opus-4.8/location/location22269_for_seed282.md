Below the surface, there is a room that is still deciding what it is.

You come into it sideways, because there is no straight way in. The passage that delivers you is poured concrete, but young concrete, concrete that has not yet learned to be still — it sweats. Beads of condensation stand on it in even rows, as if the wall were trying to grow something and could only manage water. The cold here is patient. It sits at the bottom of everything, a temperature just above the place where breath becomes visible, so that you are never quite sure whether you are exhaling fog or whether the room is.

The space is small. You could cross it in nine steps if it let you, but it does not lay itself out for walking. It is a made place — every surface of it is manufactured, riveted, cast, extruded — and yet it has the air of a thing caught mid-assembly, abandoned by whoever was making it at the precise moment between scaffold and structure. There is no floor of earth anywhere. Dig and you would find more floor. The world here goes down in plates and seams and never reaches dirt.

Look up. The ceiling is a lattice of pipes, and the pipes are dense — they crowd each other, hundreds of them, running in bundles that nearly touch, so that the overhead reads as a single corrugated organ rather than as separate conduits. Some are warm to within a finger's width; you can feel the small territory of heat each one holds before the cold reclaims it. Most are cold. They drip. The dripping is the loudest thing in the room and it is not loud — it is the kind of sound that you only notice has been continuous when it briefly stops, and the silence that follows feels like a held breath.

Between the pipes, machinery turns. Not fast. The whole place is mechanized but at the pace of something digesting — flywheels the size of dinner plates rotating once every several seconds, belts creeping, a piston somewhere completing a stroke with a soft hydraulic sigh and then taking its long time about the return. It is industrial without urgency. The factory is running, but it is running the way a heart runs in sleep. You get the sense that production is happening, that *something* is being made here, but the product is never visible — it must be very small, or it must be the room itself, slowly building itself out of its own exhalations.

And things grow. This is the strangeness of it. In the wet seams where two manufactured surfaces meet, where the condensation pools and refuses to drain, there is vegetation — but sparse, and wrong, and somehow correct. Pale stalks, the color of the inside of a stem, push up between rivets. They have no leaves to speak of; they are mostly stem, mostly the *idea* of a plant, as though they too are emerging, pre-formed, not yet committed. They lean toward the dim sources of light. The light comes from filament bulbs caged in wire, spaced too far apart, each one a small amber island in a brown dimness, and the pale growths bend toward them with a slowness you cannot catch in the act but can confirm by looking away and looking back.

There are no animals. You will wait for a rat, a beetle, the scuttle that always lives in such places, and it will not come. The absence is total and it changes the texture of the quiet. Nothing is hunting. Nothing is being hunted. The only motion that isn't machinery is the water and the slow vegetable yearning toward the bulbs, and once — you might decide you imagined it — a place on the wall where the condensation seemed to gather itself, to bead more thickly, to consider something, before letting go and running down in a single line.

Because the room is active even without fauna. That is the thing that takes longest to understand. There are cycles here. Watch long enough and the warmth migrates — the warm pipes go cold, the cold pipes warm, on some schedule the building keeps for itself. The condensation thickens and thins; there are weathers in this nine-step room, a fog that rises near the floor in one hour and is gone the next, a phase when every surface beads and a phase when they go dull and nearly dry. The pale stalks track all of it. There is an ecology, but it is an ecology of made things and water and slow light, a metabolism without creatures, a place keeping itself alive by rules it is still writing.

The rules, as far as you can tell, are these: nothing finishes. The concrete will keep sweating. The pipes will keep trading their small heats. The plants will keep being mostly stem, leaning, never flowering. The machines will keep making whatever they make at the pace of breath. And the room will stay small — it does not want to be a landscape, it wants to be a *pocket*, a single contained cold wet emerging thing, hidden under all the floors of the world, dense with its own crowded apparatus, dim, quiet, busy, and not quite born.

You will want to stay and see what it becomes. That is the last rule, and the cruelest: it is always becoming, and you will never be there for the moment it arrives.
