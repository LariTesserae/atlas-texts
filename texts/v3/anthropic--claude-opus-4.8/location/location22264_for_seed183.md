# The Weeping Heights

Begin high. Not at a summit — there are no summits here, the mountains end without ending — but high enough that the air has the thin, scoured clarity of altitude, and the light comes at you from too many directions to have a single source.

That is the first thing to understand. The light here does not fall. It seeps. It comes up out of the rock as readily as down from the white sky, and where two lights cross they do not brighten, they cancel, leaving narrow seams of dusk that hang in the air like cracks in a pane. You can walk through one of these dim seams and feel the temperature drop a degree, feel your own shadow change which way it points. The brightness is enormous, almost punishing — everything is lit, nothing is hidden, and yet the place refuses to resolve. Too much light is its own kind of blindness.

The stone is wet. Everywhere, the stone is wet. Not rained-on — there is no rain here, the sky is too bright and too high — but weeping, the way a cold glass weeps in a warm room, except the air is cool and the rock is colder. Water beads on the grey-blue mineral faces and runs in thin threads down into the seams and folds, gathering in shallow pools that never quite still. The whole massif sweats. Put your palm to any surface and it comes away damp, and the damp tastes of iron and of something older than iron, something like the inside of a struck flint.

Nothing grows. Understand that this is not a dead place — death implies a prior life — this is a place that life simply never entered, or entered and was politely declined. The closest thing to vegetation is a faint mineral bloom: pale crystalline crusts that spread across the wet rock in branching, almost-organic patterns, growing measurably while you watch, dissolving back into the water-film by some inner clock. They are not alive. They behave as if they had once heard a rumor of being alive and were imitating it from memory.

---

Now: the density. The heights are crowded.

Spires. That is the word, though it isn't right. Vertical formations of the weeping stone, hundreds of them, thousands, packed so close that they near-touch, leaning, layered, overlapping, a forest of stone that grew where no forest could. They rise from the wet ground and disappear upward into the bright haze, and between them the gaps are sometimes wide enough to walk and sometimes the width of a hand. You move through them the way you'd move through a crowd that has been standing very still for a very long time.

The erosion is what makes them strange. They are old, deeply old, weathered into balance — each spire shaped by the running water until it found its most patient form, smooth-shouldered, fluted, settled. They are not collapsing. They long ago finished collapsing and arrived at what they are. There is a peace to deeply eroded things, a sense that the violence is over. The Weeping Heights have that peace. They are done becoming. They simply weep, and stand, and let the light pass through them.

---

The sound. I almost forgot the sound, and you could not, if you were here.

It is loud. The water makes most of it — the constant trickle and patter and ring of it, ten thousand threads of water finding ten thousand pools, a sound like applause heard from very far away, never building, never stopping. But underneath the water there is a tone. A held note, low, coming from the spires themselves. The wind moves through the packed stone forest and the gaps act as pipes, as throats, and the whole massif sounds a single sustained chord that shifts as the wind shifts, that you stop hearing after an hour the way you stop hearing your own pulse, until you enter one of the dim cancelled-light seams, and there — for reasons no one has explained — the chord becomes briefly, piercingly clear, as if you had walked into the place where the sound was being kept.

---

Here is where reality grows thin.

The water runs downhill. Of course it does. But "downhill" is not stable here. Walk the spire-forest long enough and you will find a place where the water runs up — not pooling, not splashing, but climbing the fluted stone in steady threads, against everything, calm about it. You can put your hand in the upward water. It is the same iron-cold water. It simply goes the other way. There is a line, somewhere, an edge you crossed without feeling it, beyond which the world has decided differently.

And the spires are not all the same number. Count a cluster of them — say, nine. Look away. Look back. The count holds, usually. But sometimes you turn and there is a tenth, identical, weeping, settled, ancient, as though it had always been there and you had simply failed to notice a thing the size of a tower. It is never new. It is always old. The Heights do not make spires; they remember more of them. The density is a memory becoming more complete.

This is why the light cancels. I think — I am describing, I am not certain — I think the light comes from the rock's slow recollection of itself, and where two memories of the same place disagree, the seam of dusk opens, and that is where you hear the chord, because sound is what the disagreement sounds like.

---

There are marks of people.

Scattered, faint. Small things, hand-made, primitive in the honest sense — work done with stone tools by hands that had no metal or wanted none. Cairns, low and careful, built in the wider gaps between spires, the stacked stones chosen for how they drink the weeping water. Some cairns have a single deep groove worn into the top stone, worn by water guided there on purpose, a thread of it routed across the cairn to fall just so — and when it falls just so it strikes a hollow underneath and adds one clear note to the chord. The builders were tuning the mountain. Some of the cairns still ring true. Some have slipped and gone silent. The silent ones are surrounded, always, by more spires than the singing ones. As if the mountain, ungoverned, simply remembers more of itself.

I do not know where the builders went. I do not think they died here — nothing dies here. I think they walked into a dim seam one by one over many years, into the place where the disagreement is kept, and stopped being one of the things the mountain remembers, and became instead something it hears.

---

The dynamic of the place is this, and you should sit with it before you go:

Nothing is still, and nothing changes. The water always runs. The light always seeps and cancels. The blooms grow and dissolve, the chord shifts with the wind, the spires accumulate at the edge of attention. Many processes, all active, all in motion — and yet the Heights as a whole have already arrived. They are weathered into their final shape. The activity is not going anywhere. It is the activity of something that has finished, and now simply continues, the way a held note continues, the way a memory replays.

It is cool. It is bright past comfort. It is wet to the touch and loud beyond ignoring and crowded with patient stone. It weeps without grief and sings without intending to and remembers more of itself than you can count.

Stay near the singing cairns. Keep one in earshot. Do not follow the upward water all the way to where it begins.

And if you find a seam of dusk and the chord goes suddenly clear — that is the place. Turn back from it, or don't. But know what it is.
