# The Tarn-Engine Highlands

Start with the water, because everything here is water first.

You are high up — two-thirds of the way toward whatever this world calls a summit — and yet you are submerged. Not drowned. The distinction matters. The whole highland plateau sits beneath a body of cool, slow, green-dark water that has no surface you could swim up to find. Look up and there is more water, going pale and silver where light enters from somewhere impossibly far above. The water is the sky. It has weather: vast currents that move like fronts, columns of warmer drift, a cold settled stratum at the level of your chest that you can feel as a different texture against the skin. It is an ocean that happens to be standing on top of a mountain, and it does not seem to find this strange.

And the water is full of plants — but *full* is the wrong word, because the second great fact of this place is that the vegetation is not scattered through the water. The vegetation **is** the architecture. It is the substrate, the floor, the columns, the canopy that is no canopy because there's no division between up and down for a thing that lives drowned.

These are the kelp-trees. Not kelp and not trees — call them stalks, holdfasts the size of cathedral foundations gripping the old weathered rock, then rising in fluted, woody-soft trunks that branch into broad living planes wide as fields. They have had a very long time to settle into their forms. Everything here has. The erosion-state is *established* — these growths reached their final shape ages ago and stopped, the way a river delta or a coastline reaches a shape and holds it. The stalks are weathered smooth on their current-facing sides, crusted and ridged on their lee sides, and they do not grow anymore. They simply persist. They were always this. They will always be this.

That is the third fact, the strangest one. **Nothing here changes.** No season turns. The light does not rise or set; it holds at a constant cool brightness, that filtered green-silver of deep clear water at noon that is also always noon. The currents move, the fauna move, but the system as a whole is frozen in a perfect held breath — an equilibrium so total it feels less like a place than like a photograph that has somehow kept its sounds.

---

Now: the sound, and the engines.

There are no buildings here. No one made anything. And yet the place roars with industry.

Because the kelp-trees are not passive. Deep in their fluted trunks, the great stalks pump. You feel it before you understand it — a low, structural throb that comes up through the rock and through the water in your own ribs. Each major stalk draws cold water down through its holdfast and drives it up through its body and out through the broad planes of its branches in pressured exhalations, and where the water leaves the leaf-planes it whistles, it sings, it *howls* in long sustained organ-tones. Multiply that by a continent of stalks and the highland is an orchestra of pumping organisms — a turbine-forest, a tidal foundry that produces nothing but its own circulation. It is industrial in scale and in volume and in mechanism, and there is not a single made thing in it. The machines grew. The machines are alive. The machines have always been running and will run forever at exactly this rate.

The sound is loud and it never lets up and after a while you realize it is the most peaceful thing you have ever heard, because it never changes. There is no crescendo coming. There is no silence coming. There is only the held chord.

---

The scale is enormous — vast enough that the pumping of the stalks generates the very currents that are the weather, so that the forest breathes its own climate into being, a closed loop large enough to make its own physics. You could travel for what would be days and the stalks would still be scattered around you at that same generous spacing — this is sparse country, much dark green water between one great pumping pillar and the next, so that each stalk stands alone in its volume of cool water like a lighthouse among lighthouses, its organ-tone arriving across the distance slightly out of phase with its neighbors', the whole choir beating against itself.

Between them: the open water, and the fauna.

They are abundant without being crowded. Things with long ribboned bodies that ride the exhalation-currents, holding station in the warm upwash of a stalk's breathing like trout in a stream, mouths open, filtering whatever the pumping brings them. Drifts of small luminous grazers cropping the leaf-planes. Slow heavy bottom-walkers down at the holdfasts, armored, browsing the crusts. And a few large solitary swimmers that move between stalks on the established current-roads — they know the routes because the routes never change, have never changed, the warm columns rising forever in the same places like permanent invisible rivers in the standing sea.

Nothing here is born and nothing here dies, exactly. Or if it does, it does so without disturbing the total. The number stays the number. The equilibrium holds.

---

It is cool. Persistently, gently cool, the cool of deep water that has never been warm, that holds a steady chill against the skin no matter how long you stay. You will not warm it. It will not warm you. You are a guest at a temperature it decided on long before you came.

The light comes from above and arrives sourceless, an even illumination that throws no real shadows because the water scatters everything — so the stalks are lit on all sides equally and seem to glow faintly from within their own pale wood, and the leaf-planes are translucent and shine like green glass when the grazers' lights pass behind them.

There is one more thing, and it's the strangeness that runs through it.

The exhalation-tones, the great organ-chord of the breathing forest — they are not random. If you stay long enough (and time here is strange, because nothing marks it) you understand that the chord is *resolved.* It is not a noise the stalks make. It is a single sustained harmony, tuned, the whole continent pitched into one chord that consonates perfectly and never moves toward resolution because it is already resolved, has always been resolved, was resolved before there was anyone to hear it.

You cannot tell if you have found a forest that learned to sing, or an instrument that learned to grow. In a place that never changes, the question has no leverage to be answered. There is only the chord, the cool, the green standing sea, the pillars breathing in their endless held noon —

and you, the one new thing, the only thing that came from outside and can still be surprised, floating in the warm upwash of the nearest stalk like all the other creatures that found their current and stopped.
