# The Lung-Engine at the Top of the Air

There is a machine the size of a small chapel, and it breathes at the very edge of where breathing is possible.

Begin with the height. You are higher than weather wants to go — almost three measures of elevation, the kind of altitude where the sky stops being blue and starts being something thinner, a violet-black at the zenith even at noon. The air here is too sparse for most things, and yet everything here is green. That is the first contradiction the place asks you to hold.

## The Made Thing

Everything is built. There is no rock under your feet that the rock didn't have help becoming. The floor is riveted brass gone the color of old pondwater, plated over with a substrate of fired ceramic tiles, each one stamped with a maker's mark too worn to read. Where the tiles end, there are pipes — and the pipes are the architecture, the geology, the entire bones of the world. Copper mains as thick as tree trunks. Iron flues. Glass conduits in which something pale moves slowly, like the inside of a stem you've sliced open.

This was made. All of it. Someone — long ago, judging by the verdigris and the way the seams have crept apart with thermal cycling — assembled a single great apparatus up here in the impossible air, and then the apparatus was left alone to weather into itself. That's the *erosion* of the place: not natural decay but the long settling of a manufactured thing into equilibrium. The bolts have found their final tightness. The gaskets have wept and dried and wept and dried until they made their own black resin. Nothing is breaking anymore. It has all arrived at the shape it intends to keep.

## What It Does

The machine breathes. This is the function, near as anyone could tell, and the function is total.

Enormous bellows-lungs of riveted leather and ribbed steel hang in the central volume, and they swell and collapse on a slow tidal rhythm — one full breath every ninety seconds or so, the *dynamic* of the place being gradual, patient, a thing you measure with the back of your neck rather than your watch. On the inhale, valves the size of cartwheels haul thin mountain air down through intake throats, compress it, warm it. On the exhale, the warmed dense air floods out across the tile floors and through the green.

And the green drinks it.

## The Green

Vegetation everywhere — nearly the maximum of it — but you must understand *how* it grows here. There is no soil. The plants have learned to root in machinery. Vines spiral up the copper mains and fatten where the pipes run warm. Cushions of moss colonize the condensation collars, the places where the air gives up its moisture against cold brass. Ferns unfurl from junction boxes. There are flowers — fat waxy things, deep red and bruise-purple, the colors of high-altitude survival — that grow only from the exhalation ports, opening when the warm wet breath comes and closing in the cold pause between.

The *water* is exactly this: damp, intermittent, condensed. The machine breathes moisture out and the green catches it before it can fall. Beads of it run down the glass conduits. Pockets gather in the cupped tiles and grow a film of algae bright as paint. Nowhere is there a pool, a stream, a body of water you could call by a water's name — there is only the cycle of breath laying down a film of wet and the green lapping it up, over and over, slow as the bellows.

## The Emptiness

Here is the strange part — the part that takes the longest to feel.

The place is *small*. You could walk its boundaries in a quarter hour, a chamber the size of a modest church, a single great room with the machine in it. And it is *empty*. Not empty of things — it is crammed with pipe and leaf and engine — but empty of *occupation*. There is enormous space between any one presence and the next. The volume is hollow at its core. Stand in the middle and the bellows hang far above you, the floor stretches far below, and between is just air and the slow breathing and nothing. A near-void wearing a coat of lushness.

You feel watched and you feel alone and both are true, because the things that are here keep their distances.

## The Fauna

There are animals — abundant ones, more than you'd expect for a place this barren and high — but they have arranged themselves into the empty spaces and so you mostly hear them rather than see them.

Pale moths the size of your two hands, with wings like wet paper, riding the exhale currents. They never land except on the flowers, and only during the warm breath. Long-legged things, part insect, part something that has decided machinery is a kind of forest — they walk the pipes upside down and graze the algae and the moss. And birds, or the descendants of birds, who have grown thin and metallic-feathered to survive the air, and who roost in the dead flues and shriek.

They shriek because the place is *deafening*.

## The Sound

This is the sense that overwhelms all others. The machine is loud almost past bearing — *sound nearly at the top of the scale.*

The bellows do not breathe quietly. Each inhale is a vast wet groan of leather and a shriek of intake valves, a sound like a cathedral organ being slowly drowned. The exhale is worse — a roar, a gale-howl through a thousand ports, and under it the constant industrial clamor: the clank of compensating weights, the hiss of escaping warm air through the worn gaskets, the deep arrhythmic *thud* of some governing mechanism far below, the rattle of loose ceramic, the squeal of the metal birds answering the machine in a language built from its own noise.

You cannot speak here. You cannot think in words. The sound enters your chest and breathes you along with everything else. It is total and it never stops, only swells and recedes on the ninety-second tide, and somehow within that monstrous noise the place still feels *empty* — because the sound fills the hollow without populating it. A roaring void. A deafening solitude.

## The Light

The light is ordinary, which is the cruelest mercy of the place — *middling, normal light.* Whatever sun reaches this altitude pours in through high glass panels gone milky with mineral scale, and it falls clean and undramatic across the brass and the green. No mystery to it. You can see everything. The verdigris, the wet leaves, the moths, the slow heave of the lungs. The place withholds nothing from your eyes. It is all simply, plainly there, breathing and roaring at the roof of the world, growing flowers out of an engine, alone.

## The Rule of the Place

Here is what holds it all together, if you stay long enough to feel the logic:

The machine was built to breathe, and the green came to live on its breath, and the animals came to live in the green, and now none of them remembers there was ever a builder or a purpose. The breath is the only law. Everything that lives here lives on the schedule of the lungs — opens on the warm exhale, closes in the cold pause, ninety seconds, forever, patient and enormous and slow.

It is a small place, and it is the whole world, and it is nearly empty, and it never stops shouting that it is alive.
