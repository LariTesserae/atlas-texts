There is a city here, but no one built it in the way you mean.

Start with the cold. It is the first fact and the last one — a cold that doesn't bite so much as settle, a high-altitude cold that has been here long enough to become the natural state of the air. Breath would smoke if anything breathed. Water that should freeze instead hangs, half-committed: beading on every surface, gathering in the hollows of leaves, weeping in slow threads down the green walls. This is a wet place that never quite rains. The moisture lives in the air itself and condenses where it can.

Now the green. Everything is green, and the green is the architecture.

Imagine a hamlet's worth of structures — call it a town square and the close streets that feed it, an area you could walk across in twenty minutes if walking were possible, which it mostly is not. Every wall is moss. Not moss growing *on* a wall — the wall *is* moss, compacted over uncountable seasons into something dense enough to lean a hand against, a peat-firm vertical substance that gives slightly and springs back. The buildings are not ruins reclaimed by plants. There were never any other buildings. The plant did the constructing.

This is young terrain, freshly raised. You can read it in the edges — the moss-towers have sharp shoulders, clean ridgelines, the geometry of something recently pushed up rather than long worn down. Cushion plants the size of rooms have domed themselves into hard pale-green hummocks, each one a fortress, each one packed so tightly with its own million tiny rosettes that you could stand on it and leave no print. They have the structural confidence of new stone. Between them: lanes. Channels. The negative space where the cushions and moss-towers haven't met yet, and through these run the threads of water, cold and clear and slow, finding the low ways down the high ground.

Density is the rule that governs everything. There is no gap. Where you might expect air between two presences, there is liverwort, there is a third smaller cushion, there is a hanging curtain of something filamentous and dripping. The packing is total. A dropped stone would not find ground; it would lodge somewhere in the green and stay, slowly upholstered. The fullness is what makes the place feel built — not emptiness organized by walls, but matter so complete that the few open lanes read as deliberate streets, as plazas, as the rooms and corridors of a settlement whose every brick is alive and faintly, perpetually damp.

The light comes down dim and even, the light of a sky thick with the same suspended water that beads on the leaves. No direct sun reaches the lanes. Everything is lit the gray-green of underwater, of late afternoon under cloud, a light that doesn't cast shadows so much as let the deeper hollows go dark. The tops of the moss-towers glow slightly brighter, catching what diffuse glow there is, so the high city has an upward gradient: dark at the foot of every wall, luminous-pale where the cushions crown.

It is mostly quiet, and the quiet has texture. The dominant sound is water — not rushing, never rushing, but the constant accumulation and release of drops. A bead grows on a leaf-tip, gathers weight, falls, ticks against the cushion below. Multiply that by the whole packed town and you get a soft uncountable percussion, a rain that is always landing and never falling, the place watering itself drop by patient drop. Under it, sometimes, a deeper sound: the slow creak of saturated moss compressing somewhere, settling its own weight. The structures are still moving. This is an active place, though its pace is geological-slow and biological-patient. The cushions are expanding by a hairsbreadth a year. The water is rerouting itself through the lanes by increments. Weather cycles through — the suspended moisture thickens toward fog, thins toward a clearer cold, thickens again — and each cycle leaves the green a little fuller.

Life that moves is rare here, and shy. Something small and pale lives in the wet seams between cushions — a flat soft thing, slug-adjacent but not quite, that grazes the liverwort and is the color of the dimness. Now and then a smaller motion: a springtail-cloud rising off a disturbed patch of moss like a puff of green smoke, settling again. Higher up, you might once in a long while see a bird — but it doesn't sing here, it only passes, and the silence after it is somehow deeper. The fauna are guests. The plants are the citizens.

And the one thing that is strange, the thing that tips this past an ordinary cold high bog into something else: the structures keep the *forms* of a settlement. Not because anyone shaped them. The cushion plants, generation over generation, have grown into the same recurring geometries — the squared shoulder, the arched lane-mouth, the regular spacing of the towers around the open central low place where the water pools. As if the plant, with no maker and no mind, has been remembering an architecture. As if the idea of a town is something the green discovered on its own, the way it discovered photosynthesis, and now reproduces it faithfully in living tissue, cold and wet and dim, drop by drop, in a high place that only recently rose into the air.

You could live in such a city. You would only have to learn to stand very still, and let yourself be slowly, gently, upholstered in.
