There is a place beneath the world that should be dark and is not.

You come into it the way you'd come into a held breath. The ceiling is somewhere far above — fifty, sixty meters of green between you and whatever surface there is — but light comes down anyway, and this is the first thing that's wrong, in the way that the most beautiful things are usually a little wrong. The light is not sunlight filtered. It is generated. Every leaf here glows. Not brightly each, but there are so many of them, layered and overlapping into the high distance, that the cumulative effect is a flat, shadowless, total brilliance, like standing inside a lamp made of foliage. There is no source to squint at. The light comes from all sides equally and casts no shadow at all, so your own hands seem to float without weight in front of you, edgeless.

The whole cavern — and it is a cavern, though you'd never call it that, because there is no stone you can find — is filled with plant. I mean filled. There is no soil floor and rock wall, no clear architecture of mineral overgrown by green. The green *is* the architecture. The walls are woven trunks, fused and braided, growing into and out of each other so densely you cannot see where one ends. The floor is a spongy mat of living tissue, warm and faintly yielding, threaded with paler runners that pulse — slowly, you think, watching, then realize they don't pulse, they only seem to, the way a held note seems to waver. Above and around, the space is divided and subdivided by membranes of leaf and fibrous curtain, so that "open" and "enclosed" stop meaning much. You can see a long way — the place extends, valley-sized, room opening into room into room of green-lit chamber — but you see it through ten thousand intervening leaves, so the distance is a luminous haze, jade going to gold going to white where it's farthest.

It is hot. The kind of hot that is also wet, though there's no standing water you can find at first. The heat comes off the plants themselves; they are running some quiet engine of their own, metabolizing in the brightness, and the air sits at blood-warmth and just below saturation, so everything is faintly slick. Condensation beads on the broad upturned leaves and trembles there and does not fall, mostly. Now and then, somewhere, one does fall, and you hear it — a single soft tap against a lower leaf — and that small sound carries an absurd distance in the quiet, and then the quiet closes back over it. This is a mostly silent place. There's a low organic hum under everything, more felt in the chest than heard, the sound of a great deal of living tissue being alive at once. Above that, only the occasional drop, the occasional faint creak of a trunk settling against a trunk. No wind. There is no weather here to make wind.

Here is the second wrong thing, the deeper one: nothing changes.

You wait, and you watch a particular leaf, a wide pale one at the level of your eye, with a drop of water held in its cup. You expect the drop to swell and slide and fall. It does not. You expect the leaf to nod under the building moisture. It does not. The light does not brighten toward a noon or dim toward an evening, because there is no sun and no day. The heat does not climb or break. The runners on the floor do not actually pulse. Everything is grown into its final shape and *stays*, held at the exact moment of being finished, which is strange because the place also feels unfinished — raw, even, embryonic. The trunks have a softness to them, a newness, like the green of a stem before it goes woody. There is no rot anywhere. No fallen leaf browning on the floor, no dead wood, no scar where something broke. Nothing erodes because nothing has yet aged. It is all *just made*, and it will stay just-made forever. A landscape frozen at the instant of its own creation, before time was allowed to start working on it.

You begin to understand that this is not a forest that grew. It's a forest that was *cast*, all at once, into its mold, and the mold was the cavern, and then everything stopped.

There are signs of others. Not many. Set into the woven wall, here and there, are small structures — you could call them shrines, or just markers, you can't tell. They are built of the only available material, which is the plant: lengths of stem bent and bound into low frames, a doorless arch, a little knee-high enclosure around nothing in particular. They aren't grown; they're *made*, the bends too deliberate, the bindings tied. Whoever made them used no tool you can see evidence of — no cut ends, no carved faces, everything bent green and lashed with stripped fiber. They are old in feeling and new in fact, since nothing here ages. They sit in the brightness unchanged, waiting for makers who, you suspect, are not coming back, or perhaps never left, or perhaps were never quite people at all.

Animals are nearly absent. Once, far off through the leaf-haze, something pale moves — slow, low, the size of a cat or a hare, you can't be sure — and then doesn't move again, and you're left unsure whether it was an animal or a stem swaying from a fallen drop. Twice you find, on a leaf, a single insect, motionless, perfect, jewel-bright, and you cannot decide if it is alive. There is so much plant and so little that crawls or flies through it. The ecology is lopsided, nearly monastic: almost all producer, almost no consumer, a whole economy of light eaten and stored and never spent, building and building toward nothing, in perfect balance because nothing ever draws it down.

And the light keeps coming. That's what stays with you. Standing in the warm wet brilliance with the green going to gold in every direction and the one held drop of water that will never fall, you feel that you've walked into the inside of a seed — the very first instant of a world, lit from within, holding its breath, complete and unstarted, waiting under everything for a beginning that the rules of the place will never quite permit.

It is very beautiful. It does not need you. You could stay a long time, and nothing would mark that you had.
