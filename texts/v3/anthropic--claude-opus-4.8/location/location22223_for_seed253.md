Begin underwater, at the top of the world.

That contradiction is the first thing to settle, because it refuses to settle. The elevation reads at its absolute maximum, and yet everything here is submerged — drowned in a still, cold, near-lightless body of water. So this is a peak that holds an ocean. Not a peak with a lake on it; a peak that *is* a vessel, brimming, its summit a basin that goes down further than its slopes go up. You are high enough that there should be thin air and frost-rime, and instead there is water pressing on every side, cool and unmoving, dark as the inside of a closed hand.

Let the made-thing reveal itself slowly. The substrate is constructed — every surface here was built, nothing is raw rock or natural silt. But it was built so long ago, and worn so thoroughly, that the building has become almost geological. Run a hand along what should be a wall and you feel courses of masonry rounded to pillows, the mortar lines softened to suggestion, edges that were once square now worn to the gentle convexity of riverstone. Someone laid these blocks. The someone is past all asking. What remains is the *intention* of structure — ranks of columns, the lipped rims of terraces, stairs descended into the deep — all of it smoothed by an erosion so patient it could only be the work of standing water that never moved, working grain by grain across an unthinkable span. This is the oldest stable state of a made place. It has finished becoming. It will not change again.

And it is green. Wholly, totally green — vegetation as the new substrate over the old construction. Not gardens, not forests; a single living mass that has taken the architecture as its skeleton. In water this cold and this dark, the green is improbable, and so it must be something other than ordinary plant — a deep, slow, chlorophyll-less weed that does not need the sun, that mines the stone-built walls for whatever they were salted with when they were raised. It sheets the columns in pelts. It hangs from the terrace-rims in motionless curtains. It packs the stairwells solid. The plant is the wall now; the wall is only the memory the plant grows along. Where a block has fallen, the weed holds its place in the wall, a green cast of the absent stone.

Here is the strangeness, the thing that makes this place *unusual* rather than ordinary: despite the totality of the water and the totality of the green, the space is **sparse**. You would expect plenitude — drowned and overgrown should mean choked, teeming, dense. It is not. The structures stand far apart. Between one colonnade and the next is a great open reach of black water with nothing in it, a regional emptiness, a valley's worth of room. The green is total in that it covers everything that exists — but the things that exist are scattered across the flooded summit like islands across a sea, each a tower or terrace or sunken plaza standing alone in the dark with long swims of nothing between. The fullness and the emptiness hold each other in balance. Every made thing is saturated with life; the spaces between made things are saturated with absence.

It is loud. This is the part that arrives last and stays longest. You would think a still cold drowned place would be silent, and it is the opposite — sound carries through the cold water hugely, and the place *sings*, low and constant and enormous. Some of it is the fauna: the water is abundant with animals, slow pale things that move between the green islands, fish without eyes, drifting bells, long-bodied swimmers that thrum as they go. They are everywhere in the open dark, more felt than seen, a population spread thin across the immensity. Their sounds stack — a groaning, a clicking, a sustained organ-note that is many throats at once — and the rounded masonry and the soft green curtains do nothing to muffle it; they pass it on, resonate with it, so the whole drowned summit is a single instrument played by everything alive in it. You cannot find a quiet place. The loudness has no source and no direction. It simply is the medium, the way the water is.

There are no tools here, no devices, no light but the faint biological glimmers some of the swimming things carry — a dim pulse, a smear of cold green-blue that reveals a column's curve for a moment and then leaves it to the dark again. By that intermittent light you learn the rules of the world:

Nothing changes. The water does not move, the temperature does not vary, the day does not come because there is no day. The animals move but the population never grows or thins. The green never advances or dies back; it reached its final extent ages past and stopped. The erosion that smoothed the stone has run to completion — there is nothing left to round. This is equilibrium, frozen and total, a place that finished all its becoming and now only continues. The builders built. The water rose. The green came. The animals filled the dark. And then it all arrived at the form it would keep forever, and held it, and goes on holding it, cold and singing and barely lit, at the top of a world no one is left to climb.
