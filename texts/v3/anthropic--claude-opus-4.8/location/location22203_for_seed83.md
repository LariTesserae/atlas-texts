Go down. Not far — the entrance is unmarked, a stair worn concave by feet that no longer come, and after maybe forty steps the daylight gives up entirely and you are inside.

It is a boiler-room the size of a chapel. That is the nearest word, though no chapel was ever this hot. The air sits at body temperature and above, thick enough that you feel it enter you, and it does not move much. You sweat at once. The sweat does not evaporate; the place is too damp for that. It beads on you and runs and you learn to ignore it, as the room itself has learned to ignore the slow weeping of its own walls.

Everything here was made. There is no rock, no soil, no honest ground — only iron, and brass gone green, and pipe, and riveted plate, and the great curved flanks of vessels whose purpose has been forgotten by everything except the vessels themselves. They are packed close, these made things. You cannot stand anywhere without a pipe at your shoulder and a valve-wheel against your hip and the cold-hot bulk of some tank pressing at your back. Density is the law here. The makers, whoever they were, did not believe in empty space. They filled the chapel floor to ceiling with apparatus and then they left, and the apparatus stayed, and went on being.

It is dark. This is the first fact and the last one. No lamp burns. The darkness is total and warm and it has a grain to it, a texture you almost see — but that is your own eyes inventing. There is one true light and you must wait for it: deep in the tangle, a pilot flame, very small, very blue, burning in a housing of soot-black glass. It has burned a long time. It does not flicker. By its light, if you stand close, you can read the gauges nearest it — needles all stopped at numbers that meant something once.

Listen. The place is mostly quiet, and the quiet is the point. A drip, somewhere, into standing water — you hear it find the surface, then the long pause, then again. The tick of metal holding heat. Far off, behind plate, a single pump still turns, slow as a sleeper's breath, one stroke every several seconds, *thunn... thunn...* — not laboring, just continuing. These sounds do not crowd each other. Each gets its own silence to live in.

There is water, but you have to find it. It collects in the low places — in the curved bottom of a dead boiler you can climb into, in the dished tops of the great tanks where condensation gathers and stands warm and faintly metallic. Pockets. Not a river, not a flood, just the dampness the heat keeps pulling out of itself, gathering, dripping, gathering again. Run your hand along any vertical surface and it comes away wet. The whole chapel is exhaling, slowly, and breathing its own breath back in.

Things grow, but barely. In the damp seams where the heat and the wet agree, there is a furred dark moss, almost black, that needs no light — it lives off the warm and the mineral water and something it draws from the iron itself, leaving rust-orange tears below each patch. A few pale threads of something fungal reach up a pipe and stop. The vegetation is sparse and patient and it has chosen its few spots after long negotiation with the place. It will not spread. It has what it needs and no ambition beyond it.

Fauna: rare, and you will mostly only know them by signs. There are pale things that live in the standing water — eyeless, soft, a finger long, holding still until your shadow (but there are no shadows here, only deeper dark) disturbs the warmth. Once in a great while something crosses the floor on many legs and you hear the smallest tick-tick-tick of it against plate, and then nothing. They are not many. The place could not feed many. They are the slow descendants of whatever came down with the makers and stayed.

Here is the unusual thing, the thing that makes this more than an abandoned plant. The apparatus is not entirely dead. It is settled — weathered into its own final shape by long years, eroded into balance, rust having reached the place where rust stops because there is nothing easy left to take. But within that balance, very slowly, the machine is still doing *something.* The pump turns. The pilot burns. Gauges, here and there, are not at zero — a few hold steady at strange readings, and once in a long while, over hours, you might see a needle move the width of a hair. The room is mid-process. It has been mid-process for a very long time, conducting some operation whose timescale is not human — a distillation, a tending, a slow cooking of the dark itself. The heat is the work. The heat *is* the process, kept and circulated and never let go.

And there is the smell of it, which I should have given you sooner: hot iron, wet stone that isn't stone, the sweetish reek of old grease still slowly burning somewhere, and under all of it a mineral warmth like blood, like the inside of a working thing.

The scale stays small. You can cross the whole place in a minute if you could move freely, which you cannot. It has edges. It is one feature, one room, one held breath of an enterprise. Beyond its walls there is presumably more dark, more iron, the rest of whatever this was — but you do not get there. The chapel is complete in itself, sealed and self-tending, hot and damp and barely alive, and it does not need you, and it does not turn you away. It simply continues, one slow stroke of the pump at a time, attending to its long and forgotten work in the dark.

You can stay as long as you can bear the heat. The drip will keep time for you. Nothing will change while you watch — that is its nature; the change is real but slower than watching. When you climb back toward the unmarked stair, the warmth will follow you partway up and then let go, and the blue pilot will go on burning behind you, small and certain, the one open eye of a place that has decided, long ago, simply to keep going.
