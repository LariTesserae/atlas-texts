There is a place where the land has finished arguing with itself.

You arrive at it the way you arrive at any flatness — gradually, with a sense that the horizon has stopped pretending to hide anything. This is old country. Not old like ruins; older. Old like a thing that was once mountains and has spent so long becoming smaller that the becoming is essentially complete. The hills here are not hills. They are the rounded knuckles of something that used to be granite and is now mostly a memory of granite, worn to long low swells, the gradients so gentle that water doesn't run off them so much as consider them. Everything here is a few meters above the water that lies just beneath it. There is no high ground worth naming.

And the water is everywhere. Not as lakes — though there are sheets of standing water, broad and shallow and warm as breath — but as a condition. The ground is saturated to within a hand's depth of the surface. Step anywhere off the firmer swells and the earth gives, and your weight calls up a dark mirror of water around your foot. The rocks weep. The old bones of the bedrock surface here and there as flat pale slabs, and they are never dry; a film slides constantly down their faces, beading at the lower edges, dropping. In the warm air everything is faintly steaming. The atmosphere itself is half water — a thick, soft, breathing humidity that hangs in the low places as actual mist in the early light and never fully burns away.

It is warm. Not the sharp warmth of desert but the heavy enveloping warmth of a place that has held the sun's heat in its water all night and gives it back slowly. The air is close. Sweat does not evaporate; it just joins the general wetness of being here.

The vegetation is sparse, and this is the strange thing — you would expect a place this wet and warm to drown in green. It doesn't. The soil is thin over the worn rock, and what soil there is has been leached by ages of water moving through it, carried off whatever it once held. So the plants are scattered, deliberate, spaced wide apart across the flats: clumps of stiff dark reed standing in the shallow sheets, and on the firmer ground occasional low palms — or things like palms, broad-fronded and patient — set so far from one another that each casts its own private island of shade. Between them, bare wet rock and pale waterlogged ground and the silvered shine of standing water reflecting an enormous sky. Mats of low fleshy plant cover some of the slabs, the kind of growth that drinks from the surface film and asks nothing of the soil. Everything is widely spaced. The eye travels a long way before it meets the next living thing.

That spaciousness is the dominant feeling: openness. The land extends. You can see across a whole shallow valley's worth of it — kilometers of low wet flat under a high bright sky — and the scattering of palms and reed-clumps and weeping slabs reads as punctuation across an enormous, mostly empty page. There is balance in it. Emptiness and presence in even measure.

And then there is the sound, which is the thing you do not expect from emptiness.

It is loud. The place roars, in its way. Not from weather — the air is slow and the change is slow, the whole world turning over its cycles across durations you can't feel from inside a single day. The sound is *alive*. The fauna here is abundant, and most of it you hear long before — or instead of — seeing it. The standing water is full of voices: a continuous, overlapping, layered chorus of things that croak and chirr and boom and trill. Frogs in their thousands, or whatever fills the frog-shaped role in this place — fat amphibious singers crowding the warm shallows, calling across the spaces. Insects sheet the air with a high sawing drone that never stops, that becomes the silence after a while because you can no longer hear under it. Birds — long-legged waders stalking the flats, throwing up harsh single cries; flocks that rise off the water all at once with a sound like applause and a hard collective shout. Things move in the reeds with sudden splashing rushes. Far off, low and rolling, something large calls — a deep resonant note that travels across the open water and arrives at you softened, like the land itself clearing its throat.

You realize the loudness and the emptiness are the same fact. There is so much space *because* the life here is not vegetable but animal — it moves, it spreads itself thin across the flats, it occupies the open water and the open air rather than crowding into thickets. The richness is in motion and voice, not in standing biomass. A heron-thing every hundred meters. A great wallowing shape out in the middest sheet, sending slow ripples to the edges. Clouds of small flying things over every still pool. The ecology has organized itself horizontally, across the broad shallow warmth, the way life does where the limiting thing is not water and not heat but firm ground and clean light — and there is light enough, ordinary good daylight, the sun high and the water throwing it back doubled.

Nothing is built here. Nothing has ever been built here. There is no edge, no cut stone that someone cut, no straight line that isn't the accidental straightness of a long water-margin or the lean of fallen reed. No tool has touched it. The only shaping is the shaping of unimaginable time and patient water, and that shaping is nearly done — this is what a landscape looks like when it has stopped reaching for any other form, when the mountains are spent and the rivers have wandered themselves flat and the whole region has subsided into a final, warm, half-drowned equilibrium and will stay this way, more or less, for an age, changing only by the slow drift of a channel or the slow creep of a reed-bed, a hand's breadth a century.

It is not a sad place, though it is an ended one. Endings can be loud. This one is. It teems. It steams. It sings without stopping under a wide and unobstructed sky, the old worn rock weeping quietly beneath all that voice, the water lying everywhere just below the surface, patient, and the warm air holding everything close — the whole flattened, finished country, at the bottom of its long descent, more crowded with living noise than it has ever been.
