There is a forest that has learned to manufacture.

Not in the way a city does, with men and chimneys, but the way a body does — by being the factory and the product at once. There are no walls here, no struts, no riveted plate, nothing that was carried in and assembled. Everything industrial about this place was grown. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing the mind agrees to.

Begin low, near the water. The ground is not soil so much as a single continuous tissue, root and stem and fiber pressed so close they have forgotten the boundaries between individuals. You cannot find one plant. There is only the plant, and you are standing on a small portion of it. It is warm underfoot — blood-warm, the warmth of something working. Water lies a finger's width below the surface everywhere, so that each step releases a slow welling, a brown sweetness, and the depression holds the print for a moment before the tissue swells and erases it. The whole plain weeps without sorrow. It is simply that there is more water in this world than the plant can hold, and so it sweats, perpetually, in the dark.

For it is dark. Not the dark of night, which promises a morning, but a settled and total dark, the dark of a world that never agreed to have a sun. And yet the place is not blind. The plant makes its own faint workings of light — not to see by, but as a byproduct, the way a furnace glows. Down in the deep tissue, where the metabolic columns run, there is a dull rust-colored luminescence, banked and almost nothing, the color of an ember three days cold. It does not illuminate. It only tells you that something enormous is burning very slowly, everywhere, beneath you and around you and above you.

Above you, because the plant is also architecture. It rises in vast hollow trunks the width of cathedrals, and these are not trees in any honest sense — they are stacks, flues, towers. Inside them the sap does not drift; it is pumped. You can lay a hand against the inner wall of one and feel the systole, a deep slow stroke, once every long while, a tide of pressure climbing toward the canopy and falling back. This is the engine. There is no fire and no piston, but there is work being done at scale, and the vocabulary of it is unmistakably industrial: pressure vessels, condensers, distribution. The canopy far overhead is a manifold. The hanging structures that drip warm water back down to the plain are not leaves but cooling-organs, fins grown for the dumping of heat, and the heat they dump is the heat of the manufacture going on inside the green.

What is being manufactured? This is where the place stops making the kind of sense a visitor wants.

It manufactures more of itself, but not in the way things grow. Growth implies change, a striving toward some later size. This place is not striving. It reached its equilibrium an unimaginable time ago and has held it ever since with the patience of a thing that has nothing left to want. The pumps pump, the condensers condense, the warm water falls and is reabsorbed and rises and falls again, and none of it accumulates. It is a closed industry, a factory whose only product is its own continuation, running at full capacity forever, producing nothing that leaves. The plant builds and unbuilds the same mass eternally. If you could watch for a thousand years you would see no edge advance, no tower grow taller, no new flue open. The output exactly cancels the output. The whole vast green machine is at rest while in furious motion, like a wheel spun so fast and so true that it appears to stand still.

And it is vast — continental, weather-making. High in the dark there are clouds that the canopy itself exhales, and they gather and let down their own warm rain in a slow rotation across distances that would take a walking lifetime to cross. There is a climate here, but it is the plant's climate, manufactured to the plant's specification: warm, wet, windless, lightless, unchanging. It made the weather it needed and then stopped the weather from doing anything else.

Listen. This is the strangest part, the part that the rust-light and the warm weeping and the cathedral flues only prepare you for. It is nearly silent. A thing this large, this industrial, working this hard, should roar — should clang and hiss and thunder with its own labor. It does not. The pumping is below hearing, felt in the chest rather than the ear. The water falls but the tissue receives it like a hand catching rain, soundless. The only thing you can actually hear, if you hold very still in the warm dark for long enough, is a faint dry ticking, irregular, coming from no direction — the sound of the plant's valves opening and closing somewhere in its depth, the only admission it makes that it is a built thing at all. Tick. A long nothing. Tick, tick. Nothing. As if the forest were a clock that long ago stopped telling time and now only reassures itself that it still could.

There are no animals. There never were. Nothing crawls in the tissue, nothing swims in the welling water, nothing flies between the flues. The plant did not evolve toward anything that would eat it or be eaten. It simply arrived, complete, an industry without a market, a factory without a town, a body without a beast — and it has been here in the warm dark ever since, low and wet and enormous, weeping its own water back into itself, glowing its low ember-glow, pumping the same warm tide up and letting it fall, ticking to itself in a language of valves, manufacturing the eternity it is already inside of.

It is young, the way a thing can be young and finished at once — recently emerged, sharp-edged where it meets the standing water at its rim, not yet worn by any erosion because nothing here moves fast enough to wear it and no weather is permitted to be violent. The edges are crisp. The towers are clean. It looks new. It has looked new for longer than newness should last.

If you left — and you could leave, nothing here would stop you, nothing here would notice — the warm dark would close behind you, the tick would resume its patient nothing, and the plant would go on doing the only thing it has ever done, which is to be, exactly and completely and forever, itself.
