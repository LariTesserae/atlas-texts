There is a high plateau where it does not rain and has never rained, and yet the place is green.

Begin with the ground. It is not soil — soil takes water and time and the slow work of rot, and there has been none of that here. It is raw rock, freshly extruded, pale and faintly translucent like a fingernail or the inside of a shell. In places it has not finished hardening. You can press a thumb into it near the low ridges and leave a print that will set that way forever, because nothing here erodes. No wind has rounded these edges. The fractures are sharp as the day they cracked. This is geology caught in its first breath, a landscape that has come into being but has not yet begun to be worn down. Everything has corners.

And out of this unfinished rock grows the vegetation, which is the strange heart of the place.

The plants do not drink. They have no need of water and so they did not bother to invent the architecture of thirst — no fibrous roots reaching for damp, no waxy skins to hoard moisture. Instead they take what they need directly from the rock and the light. They are succulent without being wet, fleshy in the way of warm wax. Their green is real chlorophyll-green but it has a mineral undertone, a hint of the pale stone they came from, as though they were the rock itself learning to be alive. They grow in broad slow rosettes and tall jointed columns and low spreading mats, and they are everywhere the eye lands — lush, crowded with leaves — and yet the place feels enormously empty.

That is the contradiction you have to sit inside to understand this plateau. It is lush and it is empty at the same time. The plants are full of leaves but the land is mostly nothing. A single great rosette the size of a house, then a hundred meters of bare pale rock, then a stand of jointed columns, then nothing again for a long way. The vegetation is dense in itself and sparse in its distribution. You could walk an hour between one living thing and the next, and each one when you reached it would be overflowing, generous, almost grotesquely alive — and then there would be the long emptiness again.

Now the air. It is warm, evenly warm, the warmth of a room someone has kept at the same temperature for a thousand years. There are no gradients here. No cold pooling in the hollows at night, no heat radiating off a sunlit face. The temperature simply is, all over, the same. This is part of the stillness of the place. Nothing moves because nothing is driven to move — no pressure differences, so no wind; no evaporation, so no clouds; no animals, so no breath but your own.

The silence is the kind that has weight. Without water there is no dripping, no trickle, no lap. Without wind there is no rustle in those crowded leaves. Without animals there is no call, no scuttle, no wingbeat. What sound exists is structural and slow: very occasionally, somewhere across the plateau, a piece of the still-forming rock finishes hardening and gives a single dry tick, like a clock with hours between its seconds. And underneath everything, so low you feel it more than hear it, there is a hum.

Because there is one more thing on this plateau, and it does not belong, and it is the source of the weirdness.

There are engines.

Not buildings — there are no buildings, nothing constructed, no walls or roads or roofs. But scattered across the empty distances, standing directly on the raw rock, there are machines. They are industrial in scale and clearly made, clearly *manufactured*, with the heavy riveted confidence of a thing built to run a factory. Great drums and pistons, banks of dark coils, flywheels taller than the rosettes, all of it in dull iron and verdigris brass. And they are running. The hum is theirs. The flywheels turn, slow and certain, never speeding, never stopping. Belts move between the drums. Somewhere inside, a steady mechanical labor goes on and has always gone on.

But there is no one who built them, and nothing they are connected to. No wires lead away. No product comes out. No fuel goes in. They simply stand in the green-flecked emptiness, turning, working at nothing, in the warm unchanging air. The plants grow right up to their feet and sometimes through them, threading the gaps in the iron, taking — perhaps — something from the metal the way they take from the rock. As though the engines were just another mineral the land had decided to grow life out of.

And here is what you slowly understand, walking the long bright distances between them under the plain even light: the machines are not running *down*. Nothing here runs down. The fixed temperature, the unworn corners, the engines that turn forever on no fuel — they are all the same fact. This is a place at perfect equilibrium, a place that has come into being and then refused to proceed to the next thing. It will not erode. It will not cool. The flywheels will not slow. The rock will finish hardening tick by tick over the centuries but the shape of everything will stay exactly as it is now, lush and empty and warm and humming, lit forever in that ordinary daylight that has no sun you can find and casts shadows that never move.

You will not be thirsty here, oddly. The plants, if you split one open, are dry inside — dry and warm and faintly sweet, like a wax candle that has stood in the sun. There is nothing to drink and you will find you do not want to. The place does not require water of you either. It has simply decided, in its raw young permanence, that this is one of the laws it will not have: no wetness, no decay, no change.

The engines hum. The rock ticks, once, far away. The green columns stand in their crowds of leaves and wait for nothing, because nothing is coming, because here it has already all arrived.
