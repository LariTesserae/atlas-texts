# The Drowning Works

Start with the sound, because here you cannot start anywhere else.

It arrives before sight does — a low continental groan layered with the slam of reciprocating engines, the wet slap of pistons the size of grain silos driving down into something that gives and resists and gives again. Over that, a higher band: the shriek of belts, the chatter of valves, ten thousand small percussions that never resolve into rhythm. It is the sound of a factory the size of a county, and it has been running so long that the noise has become a property of the air itself, like humidity. You feel it in the water on your skin. You feel it in your teeth.

This is a made place. There is no ground here in the sense you mean. Walk in any direction and you walk on plate — riveted iron, cast panels, gratings worn to ribbon-thin by a traffic that ended long ago. Beneath the plate, more plate. Dig and you find machinery embedded in machinery, gear-trains feeding gear-trains, the whole low country built up in strata of construction the way other lands are built of sediment. Nobody poured a foundation. The works *is* the foundation, all the way down, and it has been settling into itself for an age beyond reckoning.

## The Flattening

Erosion has done its slow work even on iron. This is the strangeness of the place: it is industrial and it is ancient, and those two things have ground against each other until the boundary blurred.

The great machines have worn *round*. Sharp casting-edges are gone, smoothed to the soft profile of riverstones. Bolt-heads stand proud of their plates where the plate around them has dissolved into rust-flour and washed away. Whole towers of apparatus have slumped, half-flattened, their structural members buckling not from violence but from the patience of their own weight across deep time. The landscape reads as a plain — low, open, extending past sight in every direction — but it is a plain of *collapsed mechanism*, machinery laid down and smoothed over like the stumps of a worn mountain range.

And yet it still runs. That is the thing you cannot get past. The pistons still fall. The belts still turn. Whatever was being made here is still, dimly, being made — though there is no one to take it, and the product, whatever it is, vanishes back into the works to be processed again. A closed loop that forgot its purpose and kept the motion.

## Water

Everything weeps. The cool air holds more moisture than it can carry, and so it lets the surplus go onto every surface — beading on iron, running in the grooves between plates, collecting in the worn dishes of old gearwheels until they brim and overspill. The works sweat. Condensate falls from the high members in a slow internal rain that never quite stops, ticking on the plate, hissing where it meets the few hot surfaces that remain.

In the low places — and at this near-flattened elevation, *everything* tends toward low — the water gathers into marsh. Black water, sheened with engine-oil rainbows, standing between the rusted stubs of structure like reeds. Marsh-edge ecology, but the reeds are pipework, the hummocks are buried turbines, and what grows from the wet is not quite plant.

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse, scattered, and *wrong* in a quiet way. Things have colonized the works, but only the things that could find purchase on metal in the dark. Rust-lichens, mostly — flat crusts in oxide reds and the dull green of corroded copper, eating the iron slowly, the only true biology in a place otherwise sterile. Here and there a pale fungal mass has rooted in a junction-box, fed on grease and condensate, throwing up soft fingers that flinch from the engine-shudder.

And in the standing water, the reed-things: jointed, hollow, metallic-skinned growths that may be plant and may be the works' own attempt to grow replacement parts the way a body grows scar tissue. They rattle in the vibration. They are spaced wide — much emptiness between them, water and plate and dark.

No animals. Nothing breathes here, nothing scurries. Once you notice the absence you cannot un-notice it: a wetland with no frogs, no insects skating the black water, no birds in the iron canopy. The silence-of-life sits *underneath* the deafening silence-of-machinery, a vacuum the noise pours into and cannot fill.

## Light

It is nearly dark. The sky overhead — if there is a sky, if the highest girders are not simply too distant to see — gives nothing. Light comes from the works: the orange seep of forges that never fully died, banked down to embers across the centuries; the blue-white flicker of arcs jumping gaps in old conduit; the faint phosphor-glow of the rust-lichens, which luminesce when the vibration runs high. You navigate by these scattered glows, pool to pool of dim color across a black plain, the way you'd navigate a swamp by the fires of distant settlements.

The condensate catches what little light there is and gives it back as a haze. Everything is seen through a wet, low gleam.

## The Rules

Three things to know, if you mean to cross it.

The first: the works tracks the rain. The internal weather has cycles — the condensation builds, sheets down, the marsh rises; then the heat of the deep machinery slowly drives it back to vapor, and the air thickens, and it begins again. Tides without a moon. Plan your route by the dry phase.

The second: the machinery is not dead and is not entirely indifferent. Things that have run for an age develop the habit of running. Step into a gear-dish that has lain still for a thousand years and your weight may be enough to remind it of motion. The works is *active* — multiple processes, all of them slow, all of them larger than you, all of them capable of resuming.

The third, and the strange one: the product still moves through. You will find it in the troughs, in the conveyors that still creep — a substance, dark and warm and faintly humming, manufactured by a machine that no longer remembers what it makes, from a raw material that is only the same substance fed back in. Do not touch it. It is the one thing in this whole drowned country that is *new*, and newness here is the rarest and least trustworthy thing of all.

Stand still long enough and the noise will start to sound like a single word, repeated, just below the threshold of meaning. It isn't one. But you'll lean closer to hear it anyway. Everyone does. That is how the works keeps you.
