There is a city, and the city does not change, and that is the first thing you understand about it.

It sits low in a basin of grey stone — not soil, for nothing grows from it, but a hard pale rock the color of old teeth, smoothed everywhere by long water. The rock is worked. Channels run through it, gutters and weirs and shallow troughs, all rounded at their lips by erosion so old it has become indistinguishable from design. You cannot tell whether the water carved the channels or whether the channels were cut and then the water joined them later, agreeing. Either way the arrangement has settled. It is finished. It will not move again.

The water comes intermittently. Not from a sky — the sky here is an even, normal grey, neither bright nor dark, a working daylight — but from the buildings themselves, which sweat. Condensation gathers on the iron flanks of the structures and runs down in beads, collects in the troughs, slides through the city in thin cool ribbons, and somewhere underneath it is gathered and lifted and made to sweat again. A closed loop. The whole place is damp the way a cellar is damp, the way the inside of a machine that has been left running is damp. You can taste mineral and rust in the air.

The buildings are industrial. Foundries, mostly, or things shaped like foundries — squat brick-and-iron halls with chimneys, with great wheels turning on their outer walls, with belts and flywheels and the long reciprocating arms of engines. They are urban in their density: streets between them, smaller workshops crowded in the gaps, gantries overhead, pipes braided along the walls like vines that turned to metal long ago. There is smoke, but the smoke too is fixed — it hangs in the same plumes always, leaning the same way, neither dispersing nor thickening. The wind, if there is wind, has agreed to the smoke's shape.

And the engines run. They run loudly. This is the loudest place you will ever stand — the pound and clang of pistons, the shriek of metal on metal, the deep organ-drone of the great wheels, the hiss of steam escaping at the same valves it has always escaped from. It is enormous and total, the sound, and yet it is not chaos. It repeats. After a while you notice that the clamor is a single bar of music played over and over with absolute fidelity, every clang in its place, every hiss on its beat. The city is loud the way a recording is loud. Nothing in it is new.

Here is the strangeness, and it took me a while to understand it, standing among the wheels:

There is no one working the machines. There never was. The factories are tended entirely by the fauna.

They are everywhere — the animals — overwhelming, swarming, the only thing in the city that moves with the look of life. But they do not behave like animals. They have the bodies of crabs and beetles and small pale rodents and things harder to name, soft eyeless creatures the color of the stone, and they pour over the engines in their millions, and each one does a single fixed task. A beetle turns a screw a quarter-turn, then waits, then turns it back. A crab lifts a lever as the wheel comes round and rides it down and climbs back up to lift it again. They clean the troughs of the condensation-water, drinking what they need, returning the rest. They are not maintaining the city for any purpose I could find. They are part of the music. They are the moving parts the engines lack, and the engines are the still parts the animals lack, and together they make one creature that has been doing this exact thing forever and will do it forever more.

There is no vegetation. A little something grows in the wet seams of the troughs — not plant, not quite, a grey film, a mineral lace, something pre-life or past-life that filters the water and is grazed by the soft pale things and grows back at exactly the rate it is grazed. That is the whole ecology. Water, film, animal, machine. A four-note chord held without end.

The city is small. You can walk its edges in an hour. At the boundary the engines simply stop being — the last foundry stands with its wheel still turning and beyond it the grey rock runs out flat and bare and dry into a cool empty distance, and there are no animals there, and no sound carries, and the silence past the edge is as total as the noise within. The city does not spread. It has reached its size and kept it. It is sparse inside, too — broad empty plazas of bare wet stone between the working halls, places where nothing happens and nothing is meant to, the emptiness as fixed and intentional as everything else.

I will tell you the rule of this world, since I think it is the only rule:

Nothing here is going anywhere, and nothing here is running down.

An industrial city should be a thing in motion toward something — production, growth, exhaustion, ruin. This one produces nothing. The foundries pour no metal; the wheels drive no output; the labor of the swarming animals accomplishes only its own continuance. It is industry made into liturgy, the form of work without its object, kept turning by creatures that have forgotten there was ever supposed to be a reason. And it does not wear out. The erosion is real but ancient and complete — everything that was going to weather has weathered, every edge that was going to round is round, and now the rounding has stopped because there is nothing left in the place soft enough to be worn or sharp enough to do the wearing. It has come into balance with itself. It is in its own state.

Stand in the loud wet heart of it long enough and the repetition stops being deafening and becomes a kind of quiet. Every quarter-turn of every screw, the same. The water sliding the same paths. The smoke leaning. The pale things climbing the levers, riding them down, climbing again. You begin to feel that the city is not running but simply *is*, the way a chord is, the way a held note is — present, full, and utterly without future.

And then you walk out past the last turning wheel, into the dry grey nothing, and the sound cuts off behind you all at once, and you realize you can no longer tell whether you have left a living thing or a picture of one.
