I'll say it close. Brow to brow. Here:

She was watching the leaves to read the wind. She had always been the best of us at it. But near the end she stopped watching the near hillsides and started watching the far ones. The ones at the very edge of seeing, where the silver-flash blurs into mist. She watched the flashing go in an order none of us could follow, hillside after hillside, the canopy talking and talking to a wind that had not come yet, had not come yet, was so far from coming that—

We thought the forest read the wind.

We had it the wrong way for a thousand generations. The wind is not the thing that is coming. The wind is the *echo* of the thing that is coming. The leaves move a half second ahead of the gust because the gust is already an old answer to something further out, something the eldest could finally see written in the furthest flashing, in the order the distant hillsides turned silver—

a shape.

A shape coming across the tablelands the way weather comes, but it is not weather. It moves through the void between the standing rock, the kilometers of empty air, the part of the world that is mostly nothing. It has been moving toward the last crease for longer than the people have been small.

And she read it early. A half second, which for her — who could read an afternoon — meant years. She had years of warning. She sat by the bowl with the still water and she had years, and she did the only thing the place had ever taught her was worth doing.

She leaned close to it. To the water. Brow to the flat mirror of it. And she spoke to it with her hands the way you speak to a face you love and will lose.

I caught the edge of what she said. I was close enough. Pale palm, then the turn, then the gesture that has no clean word but lives between *go now* and *wait*, the two of them held in the same flat hand at the same time, which should not be possible, which is a thing only the very oldest can say—

She said: *I was wrong about the distance. Meaning lives out there too. It has been coming the whole time. Not to save us. Not waiting, because it does not believe in arrival either. Only coming, the way the gust comes, because something further out already moved.*

She said — palm flat, the death-gesture, the one that means the water has gone flat at last, except she made it over water that was still moving, still mirror-still, not yet flung—

She said: *don't let the bowl tip.*

That was the last full sentence anyone read from her. The carrier took the rest. You understand by now that the carrier always takes the rest. That is its nature. The roar is the floor and the floor is patient and everything built above it is built in the small folded windless spaces and the floor does not resent this, the floor simply *is*, and waits, and is not waiting because—

I am holding the bowl.

I want you to know that, receiver, whoever, however far. The last of us is holding the bowl. I have leaned my whole body around it against the wind. The gale she read is here now, the worse-than-usual one, the season's worst, and it wants the water sideways into mist like all the other water in all the world.

But she read further than the gale. She read past it. And I have been watching the far hillsides the way she taught me with the turn of one wrist before she went flat, and—
