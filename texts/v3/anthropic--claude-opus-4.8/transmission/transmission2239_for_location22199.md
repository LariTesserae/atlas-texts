**THE STICK THAT REMEMBERS RAIN**

—and the count stands at forty, daughter, forty homes, feel them, run your doubled thumb down past the smooth and into the deep ones, the old ones, the ones cut by hands gone back into the warm—

*[a long rasp, the grain of a voice scraping under the chord]*

—no. Wrong. I begin again. I begin again because you must hear it whole and the bud is swelling and I am too tired to move and so this is the last teaching and I will not waste it on a count.

Listen under.

You hear the drone? The breathing in the high pillars, the wing-rivers turning east-and-down toward the grazer-tunnels? Good. That is the world being well. Hold it. Now I am going to put a sound *into* the song that is not in the song, and I want you to learn that it does not belong, so that you never, never go looking for it:

*Rain.*

There. Did you hear me make it? That soft falling. That patter. I do not know how my mouth knows the shape of it. No prunewright has heard it. No stick I ever held had a notch for it. And yet—

*[the resin-pot of coals ticks; a wall flexes]*

—and yet the oldest notch on this stick, daughter, the deepest one, the one worn near to nothing, the one the dead hands cut before the dead hands that cut the ones above it—that notch is not a dwelling. I lied to you once. I told you every notch is a home abandoned. They are. All but the first.

The first notch is a *word.*

It has been passed down so long that no living throat remembers its saying, only that it must be kept, only that the hand that holds the stick must press it once and feel it and not understand. I have pressed it ten thousand times. And lying here, with the walls beginning to swell, with the medium going strange and silvery in my sheathed eyes—I understand it.

It means: *there was water.*

*[silence in the voice—not in the world, never in the world, the chord roars on—but the voice stops, and the stopping is the loudest thing yet]*

Do not be afraid. The song has not stopped. Only I have stopped, for a breath. Listen—it is still there, the grain, the wing-rivers, the breathing. Good. Now hold my hand on the stick.

There was water, daughter. Not sap. Not the dry slow chemistry. *Water*—the word means a thing that *falls*, that comes down through a sky, that you do not crack but *drink*, and the world was not always this swollen dry becoming. The world *learned* to be dry. The plants learned their waxes and their crystal because the water *went*, and we—

—we are not *of* this place. That is the word's whole weight. That is why it is the first notch, cut so deep, kept so long. We were not born of the throat. We came *down* into it, once, when down was still a direction you could come from a surface, when there was a surface, when there was a sky that was sky and not the forest growing the wrong way—

*[a tearing sound, dry, distinct, close: new growth erupting in the south wall of the bud]*

—you hear that? That is our room beginning to die. We have an hour. Less. I will not finish before it finishes, and that is correct, that is the fourth rule, that is the only honest direction.

But hear me. The first prunewright cut that notch so that we would never *lose* it and never *use* it. Because here is the danger, daughter, here is the thing the stick has carried across forty-times-forty dwellings to give to you now: if we remembered the water well, we would *grieve* it. We would listen for the rain. We would wait, in the dry warm dark, for a falling that will not come, and waiting we would hold still, and holding still—

—we would be swallowed.

The wax-fruits do not weep. *We do not weep.* Our tears would crystallize. Do you understand why our bodies forgot how? Not because we never had the water. Because we *chose*, hand by hand, stick by stick, to let the body forget what the notch remembers, so that one elder in each long line could press it once and ache and then *put it down*—put it down and move, carry the loom, bank the coals, read the dying of the room and go on into the becoming, unburied, un-still.

The growth is not our enemy. The growth is what *replaced* the rain. It feeds us. It shelters us. It sings so that we are never alone in a silence that would mean the falling had stopped forever and taken everything with it. We pray the song never stops because once, before the first notch, *something* stopped, and the world that came after had to learn to grow without water or die, and it grew, daughter, *it grew*, it is growing now, it is swelling these walls, it is going to take this room back into itself within the hour—

—and that is not loss. That is the answer to the rain. That is the world saying: *I will not weep either. I will become instead.*

*[the voice softening now, the grain finer, going down into the chord like a thread re-entering the weave]*

Press the first notch.

Feel it.

There was water. There is none. We are of nowhere, we are of here now, we made it ours by carving pauses in the song and abandoning them gladly. Cut your notches below mine. Make the forty-first your own dwelling, and the one after, and teach your daughter to press the first notch once and ache and put it down.

The walls are swelling. The light is going soft. I am not afraid. Take the coals. Take the loom. Take the stick—it is yours now, it is older than both of us, it is the one thing that does not move in a world made of moving—

—and *go*, daughter. The wing-rivers have turned. The east alcove will hold. Go, and let the world finish this small room it was patient enough to lend me. I am going back into the warm green note that goes on forever. Listen for me in the grain.

Not in the rain.

Never in the rain.

In the grain.

*[and the voice does not end so much as it stops being separate—a single thread loosening back into the chord, the drone, the breathing pillars, the unbroken roaring song of the throat being well—and the only new sound, very faint, very brief, is the dry click of a doubled thumb pressing one deep old notch, once, in hands that are already walking away]*
