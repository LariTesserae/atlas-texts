**WORK ORDER FOR A TAP THAT IS GOING DRY**

—

...still reading. Pressure at the third vane down to eleven. Eleven and falling. Mark it.

Mark it where? Who's mending the log. Nobody's mended the log since the wall took the log-keeper and we left her the cloth and the cloth is part of the wall now, you can see the weave in it if you stand close, you can see the weave going gray.

This is the West Tap speaking. I am the one who speaks for it. That is not a dignity, that is a chore nobody else wanted, the way speaking is always the chore nobody wanted because speaking spends the breath. Somebody has to count the drip out loud so the others can sleep through it. That's me. I count the drip. I am the counting of the drip.

Drip.

...long that time. You hear it? Too long between. That's eleven-and-falling sounding like itself. A rich tap drips on the heartbeat. This one drips like an old man deciding.

Drip.

—

We are going to have to walk.

I am saying it to the dark and the dark is the others, folded along the catwalk, banked, pretending the manufactured day is night because we have agreed to call the dim part night. They hear me. Nobody moves. Moving is heat. Hearing is free, so they hear me and bank it and decide nothing, which is the same as deciding to wait, which is the predator's trick we all learned whether we wanted it or—

Drip.

—or not.

Listen. I'll say the count and inside the count I'll say the true thing, because the count is breath I'm spending anyway and I might as well freight it.

Eleven. The trunk is closing its channel to us. Not against us. It does not know us. It is rerouting its slow weather the way it does, on no schedule, district to wherever the pressure favors, and the favor has left here, the favor is walking and we have to walk after it or become the people who didn't.

Ten.

—

I want to set down, before the channel shuts, what the West Tap was. So that somebody carries it. Even flat. Even close.

It was a good tap. Sixteen years rich. My mother counted it before me and her voice was flatter than mine, she could say a whole grief in four words and not lose a drop, and she sat at this tap so long that toward the end she said she could feel the nine-hour breath from the inside, she could feel the tide turn in her own chest, and we left her the cloth, and—

Nine.

—and she is the wall now, the West Tap is partly her now, and that means when I say *the favor has left here* I am also saying *we are leaving her,* a second time, finished her a second time, and I do not have the water to weep about it so I am putting the weeping into the count where it can't spill.

Nine. Hold at nine. Stay, you. Stay rich a little.

Drip.

—

Somebody's awake. I can hear the not-sleeping change shape over there. Good. Good, you. Don't speak, save it, just take a condenser off the wall when the dim lifts. Take the small one. Take the dead lamp too.

I know. I know we don't need the dead lamp. It doesn't light. It's been dark since before the canopy taught us we were fools. But we keep it. It is the thing that proves we once came into a place that didn't need us and tried to give it light, and were wrong, and were let stay anyway. A people should carry the proof that they were wrong once. It's most of what we've got that the grazers haven't got. They feed and fuse and never once thought to bring a lamp.

Eight.

—

Drip.

...there. That one came late and small. That one was the tap deciding. You felt it. Eight is the number where you stop reading and start walking.

So.

We unbolt the condensers. We fold. We take the cloth, the new cloth, not the wall-cloth, the wall-cloth stays, you never take back what you've given the wall, that's the one rule, that's the closest thing to a god we hung up there in the dark trusses. We take the new cloth and the small condenser and the dead lamp and we go out into the dry haze toward where the tides still favor, and we walk slow because slow is how you arrive, and we don't fight whoever's already at the next rich tap, we sit very still near them and outlast their suspicion, the way you outlast everything here, like weather.

And the children swaddled close, breathing us, learning before they learn anything that you do not spend yourself on grief. I'm sorry, small ones. It's a miser's milk you're getting. But it keeps. A banked coal keeps longest. You'll thank the flatness later, when it lets you love somebody deep without it killing you to lose them.

—

Drip.

Last one. I'm calling it the last one. Not because it is — it'll go on dripping after we're gone, slower and slower, feeding nobody, until the channel seals and the West Tap becomes solid, becomes wall, becomes a *going-to-be-solid* place that already went. But I'm calling it last so the count has an end, because a thing should be allowed to finish, and we are the ones who say what's finished, that's our one power, we lay the old against the wall and we *call* it finished and we are not entirely sure we're lying and the not-being-sure is the most honest part of us—

Up. Everyone up. Fold and carry.

The engine doesn't notice. It goes on becoming itself, unhurried, enormous, building itself out of its own dead, and some of its dead are ours, and it breathes its one low note over the leaving like it breathes over everything, evenly, the way the sourceless daylight falls.

We are the parts that haven't been reabsorbed yet.

We are walking to stay that way a little longer.

That part stays.

...carry the lamp. I said carry the—

good. Good. West Tap, out.

— note left in the wall —

[ no further drip on this band ]
