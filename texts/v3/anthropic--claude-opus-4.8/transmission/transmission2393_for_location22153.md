THE KEEPING-SONG, AS HEARD FROM THE FOURTH TERRACE BELOW THE CISTERN-MOUTH

there. You have it. Good. We do not let the silence in.

This is for whoever is listening from the far weather, if anyone is. We sing across the gulf and the gulf does not always tell us where the voices go. Sometimes a voice comes back changed. Sometimes a voice comes that we did not send. So we send this one carefully, the way we send everything: slow, and full, and counting.

——

I am the one they leave at the cistern-mouth in the cold months. It is not a hard duty. You sit, and you watch the water come, bead by bead, down the dark north stone, and you make sure the ice does not close the channel, and you sing so the others below know the water is still moving, still being kept. The song is the water. If the song stops they will think the channel has frozen and come climbing in the dark and that is breath wasted, that is heat spent against nothing. So I do not stop. When my breath ends, Sela has it. When hers ends, old Tarn below her. We are a wall of voices. No seam.

—— and hold —— and hold ——

——

You who are listening. I think you must be from the lower world, or lower than the lower world, from where the air is thick and easy and a body can run and shout and spend itself and not die of it. I am not angry at you for that. I have wondered about you. We all wonder, up here, in the long part of the night when the singing has passed down the line and you are only holding the one note and there is nothing to do but hold it and think.

I think: down there, do they know what a portion is? Do they ever count the fuel? Do they have a word for warm-that-is-shared that is different from warm-that-is-kept-for-one?

I think you must not. I think you must have more than enough, and I am sorry for you, the way one is sorry for a child who has never been hungry and so does not yet know what bread is *for*.

——

—— take it, Sela ——

——

I will tell you a thing while she carries the note.

My grandmother is in the wall behind me. Not the spirit of her — her, the held-still her, sealed in the third course up from the cistern floor with a stone I helped to fit when I was small. My hands were too weak then to grind it true. My uncle ground it. But I held it while it set, I pressed it to seat, and so the wall has me in it too, a little, the warmth of my small hands that one time.

When I lean back to rest between the breaths I am leaning on her. This is not sad. You must understand it is not sad. She is keeping the night with me. She kept it ten thousand nights when she was warm and now she keeps it cold and still and I keep it warm and moving and between us the water comes down and the channel stays open and the song does not stop.

We are never the only one keeping. That is the whole of what I have to tell you. If you take one thing down into your thick easy air, take that. *We are never the only one keeping.*

——

—— and hold —— I have it back now —— and hold ——

——

The light is coming to the high terraces. I can see it on the cistern-mouth, the pale gold, very thin, very brief. In a moment the warmth will reach me and I will turn my face to it and stop, all of us along the line will stop, and let it sit, and not hurry it.

So if the song breaks now, if you hear it thin out and go to static and the gulf takes it — do not think the channel froze. Do not come climbing.

It is only that the warmth has come, for its little hour, and we have turned our faces to it, the living and the held-still both, the whole stacked breathing body of us leaning toward the one warm thing the way the bent trees lean —

and we are saying nothing,
