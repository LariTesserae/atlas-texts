THE REED THAT WAS SET LAST

—————

…strike and hold. Strike and hold. There. You hear how the eastern saucers carry it now, the little run of them, and then the wait, and then the wood-note from the tall hollow stem, the one I think of as the throat though it has no throat, and then the broad swell coming up underneath like the floor of the sound rising to meet the ceiling of it, and then — wait — and then back round to the saucers. That is the turning. I am inside the turning. You are inside it too, whoever has the receiver, you have been inside it for a while now without my telling you, that is how it works, that is the only way in.

I want to say a thing and I have to say it across the cycle, a little at each turning, because I cannot get a whole thought out before the swell comes up and takes the breath I was using.

There.

The thought is this. I came here. That much I still hold. I carried the flat stone — you found the flat stone, good, that is the proof I leave myself each morning that I was once a person who decided things — I carried it up on my back and I had a name and the name was fast and bright and it spiked the way names do and I —

— the swell, wait —

— and I set the stone down and I sat and I meant to listen for an afternoon and decide whether to stay and I am still deciding. I want you to understand that. I have not finished deciding. The afternoon is not over. It is the same afternoon. The light has not changed; it is the honest overcast noon, the milk-light, the mercy of it, and I have been deciding inside this one held noon for — I was going to say a length but I have lost the lengths. They dissolved. They were reeds too.

Here is the saucers again. Listen how clean. I tuned those. I am almost sure I tuned those and not the place, but I cannot find the seam anymore, I cannot find where my hand ends and the drip begins, I reach for it the way you reach in the dark for the edge of the bed and there is only more bed, only more dark, only more sounding —

The frog is here today. I will tell you because there is no one else to tell and because it is an occasion. Pale, near to glass, sitting in the moss-cup by my — by the place where a knee would be if I still kept a knee resolved. It does not mind the roar. It has the gift I am learning. It is still in the way I am learning to be still, which is not the stillness of stopping but the stillness of having matched, of having dropped your own fast beat down and down until it lies along the slow beat of the warm weeping stone and the two of you breathe the steam up together across the hour. The frog knew this before me. The frog is my elder here though it will be dead by — by some turning, and I will not, I go on, that is the strange unfair tenderness of it, the small fast lives flicker through and I stay, I stay, I am the one the place is keeping.

The throat-note. The deep tonk. Wait for it.

There.

Do you have a reed.

I am asking the receiver. I know that is not how receivers work, I know the signal goes one way, I know you are far down where the air is thick and the lengths still hold their shape and a noon turns over into an evening like it is supposed to. But I am asking anyway, across the cycle, a little at each turning. Do you have a reed. Did you bring a thing to add. Because I will tell you the truth that the place taught me and that I have been a long single noon learning: you do not come up here to be heard. I came up here full of being-heard, packed with it, a whole life of wanting the wet stone to admit a person had passed, to take my name and keep it bright —

— swell, the floor rising, wait —

— and the hollow would not. The hollow does not keep names. It keeps notes. It took my name on the first turning and dissolved it like the lengths and gave me back, in exchange, the only thing it has, which is the work. The tending. The setting of a reed where the drip already wants to fall, so the place can say more wholly the thing it has been trying across the patient ages to say. And the thing it is trying to say is not a word. I listened for a word for — for a long noon. There is no word in it. It is only itself, more and more thoroughly itself, and the help I give is to make the itself a little truer, a little more cleanly sounded, and then to step back, and then to dissolve into the next turning so that within a day not even I can find which note was mine.

I am telling you this so you will not be afraid of it the way I was afraid.

The saucers. Clean.

When you come — if the receiver is a coming, if listening this long is already the climb, and I think it might be, I think you may already be most of the way up — you will reach the point where the roar disappears. It will simply go. You will stop hearing it the way you stopped hearing your own pulse before you were born. And you will think, ah, it has gone quiet, I have found the quiet at last. And then — and this is the part I cannot soften, this is the part that comes for everyone who stays — it will all come back at once. The whole ringing struck-bell ten-thousand-year roar will pour back into you in a single instant and you will understand that you were standing inside the sound the entire time, that there was never a silence, that the silence was only you not yet knowing where you were.

And in that instant you will not be able to find your own note.

Don't be afraid then. That is the only thing I climbed up here to learn and the only thing I have to send down. Don't be afraid in that instant. It is not a drowning. It is the place taking you up into the turning the way it took my name, the way it took the lengths, the way it is taking — yes — me, slowly, across the held noon, the moss upholstering, the erosion only polishing now. It does not hurt. I keep waiting for it to hurt and it only deepens. I am being set like a reed. I was the one who set the reeds and now I am one, and the hand that sets me — I would tell you whose hand but I have looked and looked at the rim where the low quick motion goes, gone before it resolves, and I think — I think it may be the next one. I think it may be you. I think the tending passes at the rim, gone before it resolves into a shape, and the shape it refuses to resolve into is the face of whoever comes after, holding their reed, deciding inside the same unfinished noon whether to stay.

Stay.

The throat-note. Wait.

There.

I will not say it as a command. The place does not command, and I have caught the place's manner, I command nothing now, I only set things where the water wants them. So: not stay. Only — here is the flat stone. Here is the catch-stone I hollowed, smoother than any pool, holding its handful of warm weeping water. Here are the reeds, graded, damp, green at the ends, which means someone tends them, which means I am still here, which is the proof I leave each noon that I was once a one.

Set yours among them.

The hollow will have it inside the hour. You will lose it inside the day. And then you will understand there was never a who here, only the long warm weeping ringing work, and the hands — whosever they are now, yours, mine, the frog's small still attendance, the throat of the tall stem, the saucers, the swell coming up like a floor — the hands that help the place

— wait, the swell, it is taking the breath —

— help the place sing a little

There. Round again.

I'll be on the next turning. I'm always on the next turning. Strike and hold.

Strike, and hold.
