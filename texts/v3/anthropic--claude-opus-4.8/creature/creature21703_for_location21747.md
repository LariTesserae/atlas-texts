## The People of Wet-Bound

They are small, and they have made smallness a virtue. Generations in the close green have selected for it the way the moss selects for patience — there is no profit in a large body where every passage is a tunnel of leaf and every walkway was plaited to a child's weight. They are short, broad in the foot, long in the toe, with a grip in the hand that does not let go of wet wood. Their skin holds the damp the way river-stones do: never dry, always faintly cool to the touch, and over it all a sheen that is half their own oil and half the world's. They do not sweat much. The air does their sweating for them.

Their eyes are large and dark and set to gather the pearl-grey light, which is the only light they get — they have never in living memory seen a hard shadow, because the cloud and the canopy strain the sun to a steady glow with no edge to it. An outsider's eyes ache here for want of contrast. Theirs are made for it. They read the green the way a sailor reads grey water: a hundred shades that all look like one shade until you have spent your life among them, and then each is a separate word.

### What They Wear, Which Is Almost Nothing, and Almost Everything

Cloth would rot. They learned this so long ago it is not a lesson anymore but a bone-deep fact, like the wrongness of fire near thatch. So they wear plant the way the houses wear plant — woven fresh, worn until it begins to turn, given back to the moss, woven again. A garment here is a few days old. Their clothing is a verb. The elders, who weave best, are walking gardens in miniature; an old woman of Wet-Bound may have a living shoulder-mantle of the small white orchids rooted into a felt of her own making, fed by the damp of her own skin, so that she is never quite sure where she stops and the slope begins. This does not trouble her. It is the agreement, worn on the body.

They paint themselves with the peat, dark on the cool skin, and with a chalk-pale clay traded up from somewhere below — the only true bright thing in the hamlet besides fire and the orchids and the white of the sky. A face marked with the pale clay can be seen across three levels of green. They use this. It is how they speak across distance in a place where shouting is swallowed by the wall of sound: a smear of white on the cheek, a hand held flat, a body turned. They have a whole grammar of being-seen.

### How They Hear

This is the truest thing about them, and the strangest to an outsider.

They do not hear the way you hear. They have learned to *not*-hear, which is a finer skill. The frogs, the birds, the crickets, the needling drone — all of it is, to them, not noise but **floor**. It is the second ground beneath the moss-ground, a thing you stand on and stop noticing precisely so that you can notice the crack in it. Their attention is tuned downward through the wall of sound the way yours is tuned through a window's glare to the movement on the far side. They are listening, always, every one of them, for the *shape of a hole* in the noise.

A child of Wet-Bound is taught this before it is taught to weave. Not "listen to the frogs." The opposite. *Stop hearing the frogs. Now — when did they stop?* They play a game with the smallest ones: an elder claps once, softly, somewhere in the green, and the child must say not where the clap was but where the silence around it spread. By the time they are grown they can feel a wrongness in the soundscape from a hundred paces, a thinning, a held breath in one quarter of the slope, and they will turn toward it with the whole body before they could tell you why. They have, in a sense, only one fear, and they have organized their entire selves around hearing it coming.

When the frogs stop, they go still all at once — forty houses, three hundred souls, going to stone between one breath and the next, every pale-clayed face turning toward the quiet. They do not run. Running is sound, and sound is what gives you away to the thing that made the silence. They wait. They have always waited. Most often the silence fills back in — a hawk's shadow, a snake's passage, a nothing — and the floor of noise comes up again under them and they let out the breath and go back to pruning. But every one of them has, at least once, stood in the held hush and watched something walk out of the white. They do not speak of what. The not-speaking is also an office, in its way.

### The Keeper of the Coal

There is always one, and it is always the same kind of person, though it passes through many bodies down the years. The Keeper is the one who is wrong for the slope — the one too still, too inward, the one who as a child was bad at the silence-game not because they could not hear it but because they heard *everything* and could not stop. A mind like that drowns in the wall of sound. But put that same mind in a small dark hut with one task — the coals must not die, ever, in a world made entirely of water — and it has found the one stillness in Wet-Bound that holds. The Keeper tends the single living fire of the hamlet through the dripping dark, breathing it, feeding it slivers of the hard-won dry wood that everyone brings as tithe, carrying it house to house in the clay pots so that each hearth can be woken and put back to sleep. The Keeper is the only one who is warm. The Keeper smells of smoke, which no one else does, and so the Keeper is the one creature on the ridge that the wall of sound parts for — frog and cricket and bird all flinch a little from the smell of burning, and the Keeper walks through the green inside a small travelling hush of their own making, a person-shaped quiet moving among the noise.

They know what this means. The Keeper, alone among them, walks always inside the silence that the others fear. They have learned to live in the thing that is the hamlet's only alarm. This is why the office is sacred and why it is lonely, and why, when the frogs stop and everyone else goes to stone, the Keeper is the one who steps *forward* — pot in hand, the one warm and smoke-wrapped body — toward the white. If something is going to meet what walks out of the cloud, let it be the one who already lives in the quiet. Let it be the one who carries the fire.

### What They Are, Finally

They are a people who have made an accommodation with abundance the way a desert people makes one with thirst. Everything wants to grow over them, eat them, hatch in them, take them back. They have decided not to fight this except in the smallest, most patient ways — a daily pruning, a fire kept, a body painted, a garment rewoven — and to spend the strength they save on the one thing that matters: the listening. They do not own. They do not store. They give their dead to the moss within a season and grow them back as leaves, and so they have almost no past and they do not seem to want one. A people without a granary, without bones in the ground, without anything dry — and yet they have lasted on this unchanging ridge longer than any of them can count, three hundred small wet patient lives standing on a floor of sound, waiting, always, to hear it go quiet.
