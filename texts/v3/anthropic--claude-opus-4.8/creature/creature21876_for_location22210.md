## The Inheritors

Begin with the grazers, because they are the most honest about what everyone here has become.

The six-legged croppers move along the walls in loose herds, and the first lie you tell yourself is that they are stupid. They have to be — eyeless, soft, breeding and dying in the span of an afternoon, eating mineral lace off worn stone with mouths like rasps. What is there to be clever about? But watch one herd long enough and you'll see them *route*. They do not graze randomly. They strip a wall in a pattern, leaving certain crystal-spires standing, trimming others to particular heights, and the pattern is not for them — they will be dead before it matters. It is for the herds that come after, three generations down the same wall, who will find the crusts grown back richer where their great-great-grandparents pruned. The grazers farm a harvest they will never see, by an instinct so old it has forgotten it was ever a decision.

This is the first thing to understand about everything that lives here. *Nothing here works on the scale of its own life.* The individual creatures are ephemeral past the point of mattering — a flier lives an afternoon, a salamander a few days, a whole bloom of crystal an hour — but the *behaviors* are ancient, inherited intact across uncountable generations, tuned to a city that changes on a timescale none of them can perceive. They are short notes played in a song so long that no single note has ever heard the melody.

---

Consider the fliers more closely.

They glide on air that does not move — that's the puzzle you noticed, and it's the wrong puzzle. The air *does* move, barely, in vast slow convections driven by the rotating glasslight warming one wall while another cools. Too gentle for you to feel. Too gentle, really, for anything to use. And yet the fliers ride it perfectly, never flapping, because they are not navigating the air. They are navigating a *map* — a map of where the thermals will be, encoded in them, accurate to the slow wheel of the light.

But here is the thing. The light's rotation has shifted, infinitesimally, over the three hundred thousand years. The thermals are not quite where the inherited map says they should be. And so the fliers are always slightly wrong, always correcting, always making the same small adjustments in the same places — and *that correction is also inherited now*. They have begun to encode their own errors. Layer on layer of compensation, each generation flying a city that no longer exists, then patching the difference, then passing down both the old map and the patch as a single seamless instinct. They are a living palimpsest of every version of this place there has ever been. You could read the history of the cavern's drift in the curve of a single glide, if you knew how.

They do not know how. They just fly. The knowing is in the shape of the flock, not in any flier.

---

Now the salamanders, the milk-glass runners, and here the inheritance turns stranger and sadder.

They navigate by the drops. You learned that early — they orient to the *tok*, the single clear note falling somewhere in the dark, and they move through the city by triangulating against a sound-clock that has been ticking since before the surface had a name. But the drops fall where the ceiling sweats, and the ceiling sweats where it is coldest, and the cold places have migrated, slowly, as the great lens has worn thinner here and thicker there.

So the salamanders are listening for a city that has moved out from under them.

They run their old routes — you can see the routes, polished into the stone, grooves worn by a million milk-glass bellies following the memory of where the water used to fall. They arrive at basins that dried up forty thousand years ago. They wait at the foot of plinths for drops that now land three avenues over. And then, slowly, with the patience of a species and not an animal, they *find the new water*, and wear a new groove, and abandon the old one — and the old grooves remain, ghost-roads, a whole transit map of vanished rains laid down in stone beneath the flat white light.

They are always grieving water they cannot remember losing. They are always finding water they don't know is new. To them it is all simply *the water,* eternal, the one true clock. They have never noticed it move because no one of them lives long enough to notice anything at all.

---

And the crystals.

You called them the new architects, and that is exactly right, but you must understand what kind of architects they are. They are not building toward anything. They have no plan, no inheritance, no map — they are the one thing down here that is genuinely *young*, genuinely improvising, the only true present tense in a city made entirely of past.

They grow into whatever the conditions offer. Damp, mineral, the rasp of grazers, the slow rotation of light — they take all of it as instruction and bloom accordingly, building lace that lasts an hour, shattering, rebuilding subtly different. They are the city *thinking out loud,* trying configurations at furious speed, and almost all of them are wrong, and they collapse, and a few are slightly less wrong and persist a little longer before they too come down. There is no memory in them. Each bloom starts the world fresh. This is why they are so fast and so doomed: they carry nothing forward, so they must discover everything, always, again.

The grazers eat the discoveries. The salamanders run between the wreckage. The fliers read the warmth the crystals shed as they grow. The whole hushed living mass of the undercity feeds on the only thing in it that is still surprised to be alive.

---

So who lives here? Reckon it honestly.

Not the animals — the animals are too brief to be said to live, exactly; they flicker. What lives here is the *inheritance itself.* The behaviors that outlast every body that carries them. The grazer's pruning-pattern, ten thousand generations deep. The flier's palimpsest map. The salamander's ghost-roads. These are the real organisms of the undercity — slow, vast, distributed across millions of disposable creatures the way you are distributed across the cells you shed without noticing.

And they are *dying,* all of them, in the only way such things can die: by becoming wrong. The city wears toward flatness. The thermals fade as the temperature differences smooth out. The drops slow as the ceiling-lens reaches its final even thinness. Every inherited map is a map of a place that is gradually ceasing to exist, and the inheritances cannot update fast enough — they are tuned to drift, not to ending. They can follow the water as it moves. They cannot follow the water as it *stops.*

When the last raised stone is worn down, the drops will fall into standing water that does not ripple, and the salamanders will have no notes to triangulate against, and the fliers will have no thermals to ride, and the grazers will have no crusts to prune because the smooth wet plain will grow crystal everywhere and evenly, a single featureless lace, nothing to route around. The inheritances will arrive, all of them at once, at a world their ancient instructions no longer describe at all. And they will not understand what has happened, because understanding was never in any of them — it was only ever in the *shape* of them, and the shape will have nothing left to hold.

The last few generations will be born perfect and useless. Flawless adaptations to a city that has finished becoming a mirror.

---

That is who lives in the undercity at glasslight hour.

Not the dead who built it — they are gone past even being missed.

Not quite the living, who are too quick to count.

What lives here is *memory without a rememberer.* A whole civilization of inherited gestures, exquisitely tuned, endlessly faithful, performing themselves into a world that is slowly, patiently, kindly erasing the conditions that ever made them mean anything.

They keep the silence not out of fear. They keep it because the silence is the last thing they can still hear correctly. The water is the only clock they were built to read, and it is winding down, one long-spaced note at a time, and they are listening to it with everything they have, the way you listen to a voice you love going quiet —

*Tok.*

— waiting, all of them, generation on generation, for the next drop.

And it comes. For now, it still comes.

Listen to how long the silence holds.
