## The One Who Tends

You asked who lives here. I have told you of the goat-things and the marmots, the pikas and the chough — but those are the place inhabiting itself. They do not *live here* the way you mean. They are weather. They are part of the breathing.

The one you want is the one whose hands made the wall.

---

She is old in the way the mountains are not. Where the peaks are raw and surprised, she is worn smooth — rounded at the shoulder, soft at the knuckle, her face folded into itself like the cushion-plants that pack the gaps in the stone. The cold has taken the points off her. She has been coming here so long that she has become the opposite of the terrain: the only gentle old thing in a country of young sharp ones.

She does not live on the shelf. Understand that first. No one could winter here; the seepage freezes, the goat-things go down, the flowers pull their light back into the root. She lives below — far below, in the place where lungs stop complaining, in a stone house with a fire and others of her kind, a granddaughter who grinds at a quern of her own, a memory of a husband under a different cairn. The shelf is not her home.

The shelf is what she keeps.

---

Twice a year, maybe three times, she makes the climb. She is too old for it now and does it anyway, slowly, resting at the cairn she stacked when her knees still worked, following the only safe route up the scree because she is the one who decided which route was safe — sixty years ago, testing each slab with a foot, learning which stones clink and shift and which hold.

She comes for small reasons that are really one reason.

She comes to clear the catch-slab, because the seepage carries grit and the hollow silts up, and water held clean is the whole covenant of the place. She comes to dry a little grass against the chance that someone will need it — not her; she takes almost nothing — but *someone*, some traveler caught by cloud at the lip and unable to go down, who will find the wall and the slab and the dried grass and understand that they were left by a hand that thought ahead about strangers. She comes to re-peg the flags when the wind tears them loose, though she no longer reads what was written on them; they have faded past her own faded eyes, and she ties them up blank now, and the blessing is the tying.

She comes, mostly, to sit against the wall in the brilliant cold and be in the one place she knows of where the impossible is simply true.

---

She is the keeper of the rule. She is the reason the rule exists.

*Take little.* She taught herself this here, young, the first time she understood that a shelf this lush at this height was a thing balanced on almost nothing — that a single careless season of overgrazing, of trampling, of harvesting the slow flowers for their roots, could tip the whole held breath into ordinary barren rock. So she took little, and she taught her children to take little, and the few others who know the route take little because she made it a thing one simply *does*, the way one does not raise one's voice. (The voice rule is hers too. She made it for a practical reason — the air swallows shouting, so why spend the breath — and kept it for a holy one. The silence is not natural to the shelf. It is *observed*. She observes it. The animals learned it from her stillness as much as from the thin air.)

She does not think of herself as holy. She would laugh at the word, a small soundless laugh appropriate to the place. She thinks of herself as a woman who found something that should not exist, loved it, and decided it would outlast her.

---

Here is what she knows that you, standing at the lip, do not:

She knows the shelf is dying. Slowly — over centuries, perhaps, longer than her line will hold the route in memory — but dying. The peaks are young and rising, and rising means tilting, and tilting means that one day the seam of dark weeping rock at the back will shift its sweat to another face, and the seepage will move, and the green will follow it or fail. She has watched the wet line on the cliff creep the width of two fingers in her lifetime. Her grandmother showed her the old line, a stain in the stone, and her grandmother's grandmother had marked it. The flowers are not eternal. They are a wave moving slowly across the rock, chasing water, and the shelf as it is now is only where the wave happens to be cresting in her single human moment of looking.

This does not grieve her the way you might think. She is too old and too smooth for that grief. She tends a thing she knows is passing, and finds that this is the only kind of tending that means anything. A thing that would last forever would not need her hands. The shelf needs her precisely because it is improbable and provisional and held — beaded, like the water, and given quietly back.

---

So: who lives here?

The marmots live here. The chough lives here for the joy of falling. The gentians live here, the blue so saturated it looks like a decision rather than a color.

But the one who *keeps* here — the one whose presence is in the dry-stacked wall and the silted-clean slab and the blank flags lifting soundlessly and the route up the scree marked stone by tested stone — she is below right now, by her fire, with grit still under her old nails from the last climb, deciding she has one more in her.

She is not here. The structures wait, half-belonging to the meadow already, moss in their joints.

That waiting *is* her. That is what it looks like when the one who lives in a place loves it enough to be mostly absent from it — to take little, to leave it whole, to come up only to clear the water and tie the flags and sit a while against the cold sharp wall in the brilliant light, keeping the silence she invented, watching the marmot watch her back across forty soundless paces.

She will not raise her voice.

She never has had to.
