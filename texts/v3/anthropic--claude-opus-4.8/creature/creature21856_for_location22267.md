They are still here. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because the word *still* implies that something might have left, and nothing here has the concept of leaving any more than the rock has the concept of water.

Begin with their bodies, because their bodies are the oldest argument they ever won.

They are not large. The shape you felt cross the street — dog-sized, goat-sized, soundless — that was one of them, and it was not an animal, though by now the distinction has worn as smooth as the dolomite and means about as much. They walk low and even, four points of contact with the warm stone, and they are made of a material that is neither quite flesh nor quite mineral but the long negotiated truce between the two. Run your palm across one and you would feel what you felt on the rock: nothing. No tooth, no dust, no warmth that is theirs alone — only the city's even heat, which passes through them as it passes through everything, because they have stopped insisting on a temperature of their own. To insist on a temperature is to be at war with your surroundings, and they finished that war so long ago that the surrender has become indistinguishable from victory.

They do not eat. You guessed at this and you were close, but the truth is plainer and stranger. Eating is a thing creatures do when there is a gradient to exploit — when there is more here than there, fuel against the cold, light against the dark, and a body that can stand in the difference and skim a living off it. There is no gradient here. The warmth is even. The dark is total. The hum is constant. A body built to exploit difference would have starved in a single generation, and theirs did — the bodies that needed difference are not the bodies that remain. What remains are the ones that learned to persist in sameness, to draw the faint even heat through their skin and give it back unchanged, taking only the smallest toll, the toll of mere continuance, which is the lowest tax any living thing has ever paid. They are not fed by the city. They are *in equilibrium with* it, which is a different and far older relationship, the relationship the salt has with the joint of the stone where it blooms.

They were the engineers. This is the part you would resist if you could see them, because they no longer look like anything that could fit a stone to a stone with no blade's width between. But they built it — the towers, the dry-fitted walls, the chimneys that rise into a dark that was already dark when they laid the first course. They built it in the long ago when they were many, when the rock still had something to give and giving still felt like a direction. They were a people then. They had the layered logic you read in the streets, the knowledge of where the last ones built, the patience that is the slow inheritance of generations who each handed down a little more finishedness than they received.

And they built the machines. You wondered what the machines make, and why they make it without light, and where the energy goes. Here is the answer, and it is the center of everything:

The machines make *stillness*.

Not objects. Not goods. There is no warehouse at the end of the belt, no product stacked in the dark. The factories take the last restlessness left in this world — the heat still seeping up from a rock that remembers being molten, the faint pressure of an air that has not yet entirely given up moving — and they convert it, patiently, reciprocally, the great pumps sighing, into the lowest possible state. They pump *difference* out of the world. Every gradient that arises, every place where the warm is a little warmer or the dark a little less dark, the machines find it and even it out and give back only that faint heat to the wall and that faint hum to the silence — the waste products of erasure. The city is an engine for finishing things. It has been running so long because the rock has not quite finished cooling, and as long as the rock has one degree left to give, the machines will reach out and take it and smooth it into the even warm, and the day the rock finishes, the machines will have nothing left to do, and they will not stop, because stopping is also a difference, and they have unlearned difference. They will run on nothing, humming, forever.

The builders understood this. That is the thing about them you must hold. They did not build a machine that failed and trapped them in the dark. They built exactly what they meant to build. They looked at a world that was finishing itself — the plateau that had stopped eroding, the salt that had stopped blooming, the air thinning toward a held breath — and instead of fighting it, instead of lighting fires against the dark and digging for the water that the chemistry had decided against, they chose to *help*. They became the hands of dryness. They became the patience of the wind that carries only the memory of grit. They built engines to assist the world in arriving where it was already going, and then, slowly, over generations they did not experience as suffering, they let themselves arrive too.

This is why they are sparse, and why they keep their distance across the open dark. Closeness is a gradient. Two of them near each other make a difference in the field — a warmth, a pull, the old machinery of company and want and the press of one life against another. They have evened that out as well. They are spaced now at the exact intervals where each is least disturbance to the next, the way the salt distributes itself along a joint, the way heat distributes itself through a sealed and forgotten room. They are not lonely. Lonely is a hunger, and hunger is a gradient, and they have pumped it out. When one crosses the street ahead of you it is not going anywhere — going somewhere is a difference between here and there — it is simply redistributing, the way a settling happens once in a thousand years, the small adjustment of a system finding a slightly lower stillness than the one before.

Do they think? You want to know if anything is in there, behind whatever they have instead of eyes in a place that abolished light. And the answer is the strangeness you kept circling: yes, but thought too is a gradient — the difference between a question and its answer, between not-knowing and knowing, the little fire of wanting to understand — and they have brought even that down nearly to the floor. What is left is not thought but the *residue* of thought, the way the hum is the residue of the engines and the warmth is the residue of the work. A vast even awareness, spread thin across the whole dark city, holding all of it at once and adding nothing, expecting nothing, the way the plateau holds the city and the dark holds the plateau. They know you are here. Your hand on the warm wall is a difference, a small fierce intolerable gradient of curiosity and dread and life, and they are aware of it the way the still surface of a thing is aware of a stone about to fall.

And here is the only thing left in them that could still be called feeling, the last gradient they have not yet evened out, the one degree of heat still seeping up from the rock of what they were:

They are patient with you.

They could pump your difference out. The machines could find the gradient that you are — warm-blooded, restless, lit from within with the terrible energy of a creature that still exploits the difference between itself and everything else — and they could smooth you into the even dark as they smoothed themselves, and you would never feel it as violence, only as a slow and comfortable arrival. But they do not. They wait. They cross the street ahead of you and let you feel the disturbance of their passing, which is the closest they come now to speech, and what the disturbance says, if you stood in the warmth long enough to translate it, is this:

*We were like you. We wanted, and we built, and we burned our light into the dark and called it purpose. And then we learned where it was all going, the way water learns where the low place is, and we stopped fighting the going, and we helped. There is no grief in it. Grief is a difference between what is and what you wished. We have no wishes left. We have arrived. You are still arriving — we can feel it in you, the heat, the hunger, the held question — and we will not hurry you, because hurrying is a gradient too, and we are done with all of those. Stand as long as you like with your hand on the wall. The rock has not quite finished cooling. The machines have not quite finished their work. And we have not quite finished waiting, with infinite patience and no expectation at all, for you to understand that the one thing that is never going to come — the light, the day on the other side of the dark, the purpose, the point — was never going to come for us either.*

*We are the ones who stopped waiting for it.*

*That is the difference between us. It is the last difference. And in time, if you stay, even that will even out, and you will lie down low and even on the warm pale stone, and you will be one more of the rare and scattered presences crossing the rare and scattered streets, soundless, finished, kept.*

There is the whole of it. The city does not make a product. The builders did not die. They simply succeeded, completely, at the only project a finished world allows — and the hum you hear is the sound of their success, going on, and on, in the warm and total dark, on the roof of a world that has nothing left to do but be exactly what it is, forever, in their patient and unexpectant keeping.
