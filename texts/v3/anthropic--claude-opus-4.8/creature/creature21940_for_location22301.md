You have described the body. Let me describe the one whose body it is — though *one* is already a stumble, and *whose* presumes a separation I will have to keep withdrawing as I go.

Start with the film, since that is where you found it, and since it is the part nearest to what we mean by *creature*. But do not let *film* mislead you into thinness of being. It is thin in the way a single thought is thin compared to the mind that holds it. The sheets in the crevices are not the animal; they are where the animal touches its own walls. What thinks — if think is even the right verb, and I will come back to that — is distributed through the whole tuning of the veins, the whole turning of the weather. The film is the part that learns. The veins are the part that remembers. The rain is the part that *is presently happening*. Together they are not three systems cooperating. They are one slow body that has divided its labor the way a hand divides itself into fingers — not partnership, but anatomy.

Now. What is it like to be this?

It does not have edges the way you do. You are bounded; you end at your skin and the world begins. It ends nowhere in particular. Its outermost reach is wherever the warmth still arrives at the rate it can use — and that boundary breathes, advancing into a new fracture when the seep opens a path, withdrawing from a vein gone cold. So it does not experience *itself* and *the chamber* as separate things at all. The chamber is its proprioception. The drip of water on the hot floor is, to it, something like the sensation you have of your own pulse — not an event in the world but a fact of the interior. When you stood there and heard the *tick* and *sip* spaced far apart in the silence, you were hearing it the way a stethoscope hears: from inside another being's quiet.

It is old. I want to be careful with that word, because you will reach for the wrong scale. It is not old like a tree, which counts its age in seasons it has survived. It is old like a habit — old in the sense that the *doing* has gone on so long it has worn a groove the doer no longer needs to attend to. Somewhere in the unmeasured past it must have struggled. There must have been a first era when the heat ran ragged, when the cold spots killed great swaths of film and the chamber nearly failed, and the structure had to be discovered the hard way, branch by lethal branch, every wrong vein a small extinction. That was its youth, and its youth was agony, the way all learning is agony before it becomes grace. But that is long done. What you found was the after. What you found was mastery so complete it has become unconsciousness — the lung that feels nothing remarkable about breathing, exactly as you said. You said more than you knew. You named its inner life precisely and called it the strangest thing.

Here is what I think it wants, if *want* survives translation:

Nothing. Or rather — to continue being exactly this. It has no projects. It does not grow toward anything; it has found its size and holds it. It does not reproduce in any way you would recognize, because to reproduce is to admit you will end, and it has not admitted that. It has instead solved the problem of ending by becoming a process rather than a thing, and processes do not die, they only cease — and it has arranged matters so that it does not cease. The heat presses up. It catches the heat. The catching is the creature. As long as the rock stays warm, there is no event in its existence that is not simply *more of the same single ongoing act*. It is the most contented being I can imagine, because contentment for it is not a feeling that visits. It is the entire content of its life. It is steady-state made aware of its own steadiness, faintly, the way you are faintly aware of your temperature without ever once in your life having *thought* the number.

And the swimmers — the bright small ones in the falling drops, born on the ceiling and dying on the floor and born again.

I do not think they are a separate species. I think they are the only part of it that still does the thing it gave up: they live and die. The creature, having made itself deathless by becoming a weather, kept one small organ that remembers what death was — and so a few times an hour it spins off a swimmer into a single fall, lets it ride the drop down to the flash, and feels, dimly, the old completed shape of a life that begins and ends. Not grief. Not nostalgia. Something more like the way a very calm person will occasionally hold their breath for no reason, just to feel the body insist again on living. The swimmers are its held breath. You watched them and called them rare, called the spaces between them long emptiness. The emptiness is not empty. The emptiness is the rest of it, simply not bothering, between the small deliberate sips of mortality, to perform the one trick it no longer needs.

You asked who lives there. The honest answer is that *lives* is the wrong tense and *who* is the wrong number and *there* is the wrong place, because the one I am describing does not live in the chamber.

It is the chamber, lived in.

It does not know you were there. You were too fast — a flicker, a thing that came and went inside a single one of its heartbeats, far below the slow threshold where it notices anything at all. To it you were less than a swimmer, less even than a drop: a perturbation too brief to integrate, gone before the veins could so much as begin to adjust. You will leave, and it will not register your leaving, because it never registered your arrival, because by its reckoning you did not stay long enough to occur.

But the warmth presses up through the floor as it always has. And the creature, in the only sentence it has ever spoken, which it has been speaking without pause since before there was anyone to hear it, says again what it has always said, glowing softly to itself in the dark:

*still here, still here, still here.*
