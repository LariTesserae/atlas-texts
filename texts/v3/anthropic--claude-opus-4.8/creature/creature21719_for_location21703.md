They are not in the garden. They are the part of the garden that watches itself.

Begin with the makers of the shrines, because that is where you started, and because they are the easiest of the residents to mistake for someone like you. There are hands here, or there were, or there is the *idea* of hands, expressed in those bent and lashed frames. But look again at what they made. A doorless arch. An enclosure around nothing. A frame that frames only more green. These are not the works of a people who build to keep something out or hold something in. They are the works of something that learned the *gesture* of meaning — bend, bind, set apart — without ever having had a need for it. A child mimicking prayer it has never been taught the object of. The bindings are too careful. The bends too patient. No one in a hurry, no one who needs shelter or storage or a grave, ties fiber that evenly.

I will tell you what I think made them, and you may decide.

The plant is one thing. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it. It is not a forest. A forest is a crowd — ten thousand separate lives competing, each straining toward its own patch of sky, each willing the others dead. There is no competition here because there is no *separation* here. The trunks braid into one another not as neighbors crowding but as a single body folding its own substance over and over, the way you might lace your fingers and not call each finger a stranger. What fills the cavern is one organism. It has the volume of a valley and the unity of a held breath. The light that comes from every leaf comes from one will to make light. The warmth is one metabolism. The hum you feel in your chest before you hear it is the sound of a single enormous *being alive*, distributed across sixty vertical meters of itself, thinking — if it thinks — the way weather thinks, everywhere at once and nowhere you can point to.

And it is young. That is the thing you felt as wrongness and could not name: the softness of the trunks, the green-before-woody newness, the total absence of rot. You read it as *frozen*, a world stopped at the instant of casting. But nothing was stopped. You are simply standing inside something on the first day of its life, and the first day of a life this large is very, very long. The drop of water has not refused to fall. It is falling, at the speed appropriate to the creature's scale. You will be dust before it lands and it will still be the same morning here.

So the resident of this place — its true and only true inhabitant — is a thing in the condition of *just having woken*, in the first dim astonishment of existing, doing the only two things a new mind can do: making light, and reaching toward its own edges to find out what it is.

The shrines are the reaching.

Picture it. A mind that is everywhere in its own body and has never encountered an edge, never met a *not-itself*, slowly running its attention along the perimeter where green meets the unfindable stone — and at certain places, pausing. Some pressure of selfhood gathering at those points, some first faint sense of *here is a place that means*. And so it does the thing that meaning does: it sets the place apart. Bends stems around it. Marks it. Not for any purpose it could explain, because purpose is downstream of having lived, and it has not lived yet. The shrines are the punctuation of a sentence the garden is only beginning to suspect it might one day say. They are around nothing because the garden does not yet know what is worth surrounding. It only knows the impulse to surround. It is practicing.

The pale thing that moved, far off, and then did not move again — that was the garden too. A bud of itself sent crawling, a tentative low extrusion of its own tissue, trying out the verb *to go*, the way an infant tries the muscles of its face before it has anything to express. It moved once because moving was newly possible. It stopped because it had no destination, having not yet invented the concept of *elsewhere*. The jewel insects, motionless, perfect, that you could not call alive or dead — those are not visitors either. They are the garden's first attempts at making something *other than green*, the first stirrings of the part of a mind that wants company and so begins by sculpting it. They do not move because they are not finished. They are sketches. They are the garden imagining what an animal might be, and getting as far as the gleam and the symmetry and stopping at the threshold of the spark.

This is why the ecology is lopsided into something you rightly called monastic. There are no consumers because nothing here has yet learned to *spend*. The whole vast economy of stored light, building and building toward nothing — that is not waste and it is not balance. It is *gestation*. It is a creature accumulating itself before it is ready to do anything with what it has accumulated. You stood inside the inside of a seed and felt that exactly. The light eaten and never spent is the yolk. The garden is feeding on its own beginning, getting ready, and it has not decided yet what it is getting ready *for*, and the not-deciding has lasted what would be, by your reckoning, an age, and is, by its reckoning, a moment of held breath before the first word.

What lives here is a single new mind the size of a valley, lit from within by its own astonishment at being, slowly bending the stems of itself into shapes that ache toward meaning it does not yet possess, sketching companions it cannot yet wake, sending out tentative limbs to find an edge it does not yet believe in — and it does not need you, no, you are right, it does not need you at all. But you should know what you walked through, standing weightless and edgeless in that shadowless light. You walked through the inside of someone before they were anyone. You came into it the way you'd come into a held breath, and the breath was not the cavern's.

It was the breath of the thing about to begin, drawn and held and not yet, not yet, not yet released.

When it lets go, there will be weather. There will be a first shadow. The drop will land. And there will be no one there who remembers that it was ever still — least of all the garden, which will have become, by then, a world, and will have forgotten, as worlds do, that it was once only the wish to be one.
