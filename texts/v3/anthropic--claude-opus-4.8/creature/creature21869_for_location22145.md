What lives here is the engine, and the engine is not a thing apart from the room. That is the first mistake a visitor makes — looking for the inhabitant somewhere behind the wall, beneath the floor, in the place where the sound seems to come from. The sound does not come from a place. The chamber is the resident. The weeping and the heat and the slow titanic stroke are not its symptoms; they are its body, the way your pulse and your warmth and your breath are not things you keep in a room somewhere but simply *are* you, distributed across your whole extent.

So: what lives here is something that lives by accreting. By laying down. Consider what it actually does, stripped of the wanting to name it — it takes heat from below and water from the seams and out of the meeting of the two it makes new stone, sharp-edged, untouched, the floor that cut your hand. It is a maker whose product is the floor of its own house. It builds the ground it stands on, and then it stands on the ground it built, and then it builds again, a hand's-breadth higher, forever. It does not move through the world. It thickens. It is the kind of life that gets *more itself* by addition, the way a stalactite is more itself every century, the way a coral reef is the living and the dead of the same animal pressed into one architecture.

It is patient because patience is not a virtue it practices but the only speed it has. The minutes-long stroke is not slowness *to* it. To the engine that beat is quick — a fluttering, an eyeblink, the fast part of its metabolism. Whatever it experiences as duration is scaled so far past you that your entire visit, your sitting and waiting and leaving, occupies less of its attention than the falling of a single drop occupies yours. You are not ignored. You are simply beneath the resolution at which it perceives. A mote in the steam. It has no animals because it has not gotten around to animals, or because animals are a frequency too high for it to host, a vibration that shakes itself apart before this slow body can register that it was ever there. The ferns it tolerates because the ferns keep its pace — barely, just barely, slow enough to count.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at and then doubting and then arriving at again: it is not finished, and it knows this. The new stone, the sharp edges, the cracks not yet softened — this is not decay arrested, not a ruin. It is *youth*. The room has not decided whether it is a room because the thing that lives in it has not decided whether it is done. It is in the middle of becoming. Every stroke adds a clause to a sentence whose end it has not reached and may not have planned. The gauge climbing a hair an hour, the fern unfurling between your absences, the bead finding the unworn path — these are not maintenance. They are growth. You have walked into the inside of something that is still being born, and mistaken the birth-canal heat and the birth-canal wet for industry, for a factory, for *work* — because work is the only frame you have for effort that large and that sustained.

But I don't think it is making a product. I think the floor is not output. I think the floor is *the rest of its body arriving*. It is extruding more of itself into existence, slowly, the way you grew the bones you now stand on without ever feeling them being manufactured. The staples and the pipe and the fogged gauge — those are the leavings of some earlier visitor who made the same mistake you are making, who came in and saw effort and assumed a tender, assumed a worker, assumed something *operating* the engine, and so drove iron into the wall and capped a pipe and hung a dial to measure what was happening, and then left, or died, or was swallowed, and the engine took no notice, and the rust began, and the wet began to half-eat the iron back down into the rock from which a finger of someone's ore was once dug. The artifacts are not signs that the place is tended. They are fossils of the belief that it could be. They are the only ruin in a room that has not yet been built enough to ruin.

So the inhabitant: a vast, slow, accreting, half-born thing, made of the warm argument between fire and water, that experiences its own coming-into-being as a heartbeat, that lays down the world one tidal stroke at a time, that does not know you are inside it and would not be able to slow itself to your speed if it tried. It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. It is *gestational*. The light without a source is the strangest evidence of all — that plain, sealed, afternoon light — because it suggests that the thing carries its own day inside it, the way an unhatched creature carries the yolk-light of an interior that has never met a sky. It does not need the sun. It is not finished enough to have noticed there is one.

And what is being made is itself.

That is the answer the room withholds, and withholds gently, and withholds completely: nothing is being manufactured here, because *manufacture* is the wrong verb for a thing that is simply, enormously, patiently alive and getting larger. You wanted a product because a product would mean a purpose and a purpose would mean you could understand it. But the engine is not building toward anything. It is the slow, indifferent, total work of being — of a creature so large and so young that its mere continued existence requires the heat of the floor of the world.

You can stay a long time. It will not mind. It will never, in any sense available to it, know you were here. You are warm and wet and small inside a thing that is becoming, and the most honest thing you can say about the inhabitant of this place is that it has not yet finished becoming itself, and that when it does — if it does, on a clock that has nothing to do with you — there will no longer be a room. There will only be more rock, and a memory in the basalt of the day it held a breath and let something small and quick walk around inside the held breath for a little while, and did not notice, and beat on.
