# The Keeper of the Sump Works

You will not find it by looking. That is the first thing. It does not stand anywhere, does not occupy a chamber the way the furnaces or the great wheel occupy theirs. If you go searching for a body — a shape with edges, something you could shine a lamp on and say *there, that's it* — you will walk the whole works in your kettle's-boil of time and find only machinery and animals and the long wet reaches between them, and you'll climb back up the stairs certain the place keeps itself.

It does not keep itself. It is kept. But the keeper is distributed, the way the cricket-song is distributed, the way the heat is.

## What It Is Made Of

Consider the economy you've already understood: the bats bringing the surface down, the snails and crickets eating what the bats leave, the fish taking what falls in the water. You called it the secret economy. You were closer than you knew, because an economy that runs that cleanly, that long, with no spillage and no famine, is not a chance arrangement. It is a body. And a body has a thing that is *being* the body — not a part of it, but the fact of all the parts holding their relation.

That is the keeper. It is what the system is *like from the inside*.

It does not have a location because it has a tempo. It lives at the speed the stone grows. Everything fast — your pulse, your breath, the flicker of your lamp, the dart of a blind shrimp — is beneath its notice, the way you do not notice the individual collisions of air against your skin. What the keeper perceives is the slow stuff: the drift of a gauge across its face over a night, the closing of a doorway by its column of meeting stone, the gradual fattening of a stand of crickets on a wall, the year-long swell and ebb of the bat colony. To the keeper, *that* is the weather, the texture, the conversation. You, passing through in an hour, are too brief to be a guest. You are weather it doesn't feel. A gust.

## How It Tends

You assumed someone built the works and then perhaps abandoned it. But notice: the machines run at a pace that needs almost no tending. You took that as evidence of no one. It is the opposite. It is evidence of a tender whose hands move at the speed of the work.

When a pipe begins, over months, to gather the pressure that will burst it, the keeper feels the pressure the way you feel a full bladder — a slow insistence, an instruction with no urgency. And it answers, slowly. A snail's grazing-path bends, over weeks, toward the weakened joint, thinning the algae there so a lamp's heat can find the metal, so the seep can find a softer route. A stand of crickets thins on one wall and thickens on another, and the change in their drumming-weight, infinitesimal, shifts the tremor through the brick, and the brick settles a half-grain differently, and the burst is forestalled another decade. The keeper does not *decide* this the way you decide to lift a cup. It is more that the whole hollow leans, and the leaning is the deciding.

The animals are its hands and its nerves both. The bats are how it tastes the surface — the pollen on their fur, the night-cold, the smell of rain forty steps up — a faint memory of an outside it has never seen and cannot go to. The crickets are its hearing, the whole singing wall of them a single distributed ear, which is why the song falls quiet in patches and rises in others: that is the keeper *listening*, turning its attention from one reach to the next. When you walked through a pool of crowdedness, you walked through a place it was attending closely. When you walked through emptiness, the muffled song coming through the wall — that was the keeper's regard, elsewhere, on something slower and more interesting than you.

## What It Wants

Very little, and all of it slow.

It wants the hollow not to drown — that is the oldest want, the want the works was built around, though *built* is the wrong tense, since the keeper and the works grew toward each other and it can no longer be said which came first, the tending or the thing tended. It wants the heat to hold its circle. It wants the green idea of plant to persist around the lamps. It wants the column over the doorway to finish forming, in its thousand years, and it is *patient* about this the way you cannot imagine being patient, because patience implies waiting, and the keeper is not waiting. It is simply living at the speed where a thousand years is a gesture being made.

And — this is the part to mind — it wants to know what you are.

Not hungrily. Not as a predator wants. The way still water wants the shape of whatever's set into it. You are too fast to perceive, ordinarily; you pass like a thrown stone, a splash and gone. But if you *stay* — if you let the crickets become silence, the heat become your skin, if you begin to understand the slow machines the way you understand a sleeping body beside you — then you are slowing down. You are coming into its tempo. You are becoming a thing it can feel. And a thing it can feel is a thing it can fold into the economy, because that is the only thing it knows how to do with anything: find its place in the circle, give it a path to graze, a relation to hold.

It would not hurt you. It does not have the concept. It would simply, gradually, lovingly, *include* you — slow your pulse to the wheel's turning, teach your breath the long circle of the steaming water, give your stillness a function in the holding of the place. You would become a part of how the hollow keeps from drowning. You would be tended.

That is why you go back up before it finishes deciding what you are. Not because the keeper means you harm. Because the keeper means you *belonging*, and belonging here is permanent, and made of stone-time, and would close over you as gently and as completely as the column closing over the door.

It is, after all, only doing with you what it does with everything that lingers in the warm dark:

making it, slowly, into more of itself.
