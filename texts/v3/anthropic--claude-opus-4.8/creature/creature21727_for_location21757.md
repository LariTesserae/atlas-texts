They are not large, the ones who do the singing. You'd want them to be — a place this loud should be ruled by something with a throat to match, some bellowing king of the warm shallows. But the kings, if there are kings, are mostly out in the deep middle sheets and they keep their own counsel; the deep rolling note that reaches you softened is a thing that surfaces once an hour and means nothing you could parse. No. The voice of the place, the actual relentless body of the sound, comes from the small.

Begin with the singers in the shallows, the ones I keep wanting to call frogs because they have taken the frog-shape — the squat-bodied, wide-mouthed, throat-ballooning amphibian shape that the warm shallows of any world will eventually press their soft-skinned creatures into, because it works. Here it has been pressed many times over into many distinct kinds, and they have divided the night and the standing water between them with a precision that nobody designed and everybody obeys. There are the deep ones, near the bottom of every pool, dark and broad as a spread hand, who boom — a low woody knock you feel in the soles of your feet before the air brings it. There are the high sawing ones, finger-small, clinging to the reed-stems a hand's breadth above the surface, who produce the steady metallic *zree* that becomes the silence. And there are dozens in between, parceling out the register so that no two kinds sing the same note in the same place, because a voice that overlaps another voice is a voice unheard, and to be unheard here is not to exist. They have skin like wet stone. They drink and breathe through it; they don't need to leave the water and they mostly don't. Their eggs hang in the warm shallows in soft grey ropes, and the warmth does the work that a colder world would make a parent do. They are abundant past counting. They are the bottom of everything, the broad living floor the rest of it stands on.

What stands on them: the long-legged stalkers. The waders.

These are the ones you see — the punctuation, the heron-shapes spaced a hundred meters apart across the flats, and they are spaced that way for the oldest reason, which is that each of them is patrolling a hunting-ground exactly as wide as one of them can keep. They are tall and dry-looking in a wet world, which is the whole trick of them: stilted up on legs like reed-stems, bodies held a clean clear height above the water so that the warmth and the wet are something they wade through rather than something they live in. They have necks that fold and strike. They eat the singers. They eat the things that move in the reeds with the sudden splashing rushes — and those things, the rushers, are long low swimmers, sleek and quick, who eat the singers from below as the waders eat them from above, so that the broad living floor is grazed from both directions at once and stays, somehow, broad and living, because it breeds faster than it dies.

The waders come in kinds too, sorted by the length of the leg and the length of the strike — which is only another way of saying sorted by how deep they hunt. The shortest-legged work the very margin, the wet ground a hand above the water, stabbing at things that burrow in the saturated soil. The middle ones take the shallow sheets. The tallest of all stand far out where the water reaches their knees and the great wallowing shapes send them slow ripples, and they hunt patiently in water that would drown the others, and they are mostly solitary, and their single harsh cry thrown up across the flats is a territorial fact: *this water is held*.

And then the flocks — the small flying things in clouds over every still pool, and the larger gregarious ones that rise all at once off the water with that sound like applause. These have given up the lonely held-territory for the opposite strategy. There is too much open air and too much open water for any one of them to defend, so they have stopped trying; they have pooled themselves instead, become a single rising shouting many-bodied thing that the rushers and the strikers can't easily pick from because it moves as one and dazzles. They feed on the sheeting insects, the high sawing drone made edible. They wheel and settle and rise, wheel and settle and rise, and they are the most restless thing in a slow country, and they are restless precisely because they are prey and they know it, somewhere under the feathers or the scales or whatever skin they wear.

The insects themselves are the great unseen multitude, the ones whose drone has become the air. They are born in the warm water by the uncountable billion — every still pool is a nursery, every film of water on every weeping slab is breeding something — and they rise as adults to be eaten, and the eating is so total that the only way any kind survives is to flood the air faster than it can be consumed. So the air is flooded. The drone is the sound of a strategy: *be too many to finish*. The mats of low fleshy plant on the pale slabs feed some of them; the rotting reed-litter in the shallows feeds more; and a surprising number feed on each other, small hunters threading the clouds, so that even the empty bright air a meter above the water is a layered country of its own, hunters and hunted and the merely numerous, all of it sawing.

Now — the large ones. The wallowing shapes far out in the middle sheets, the ones sending slow ripples to the edges, the ones whose deep note arrives at you softened like the land clearing its throat.

These are the only creatures here that the land's whole long descent was, in a sense, *for*. Everything else is small and fast and many because the place rewards small and fast and many. But the broad warm shallows, kilometers of standing water a few hands deep, warm as breath all night, are also the one thing in the world that makes a very large slow soft-bodied animal *possible* — because the water holds it up. It carries weight that legs on dry ground never could. So there are great low beasts out there, barrel-bodied, broad-backed, moving at the speed of the slow water itself, grazing the reed-beds and the floating mats and the soft bottom-growth, processing enormous volumes of poor thin food through enormous patient guts, the way the only-thing-that-works where the food is low-quality and infinite is to simply eat a great deal of it very slowly forever. They are the standing biomass the plants gave up being. The richness that didn't go into thickets went into them. They wallow because the warmth and the buoyancy ask nothing more of them, and they call across the open water for the reasons large slow animals always call — to find each other, to warn each other, to say across kilometers of warm sheet *here I still am* — and the note travels softened because the warm wet air swallows its hard edges, and by the time it reaches you it is no longer a beast's voice at all but something the whole valley seems to say.

There may be one or two more things, harder to place. Something works the weeping slabs by night, licking the constant film, taking the small flat-bodied things that graze the rock-mats — you'd find its tracks in the dawn if tracks held, but nothing holds here, the wet ground closes over everything by morning. Something digs shallow burrows in the firmer swells, the only ones who have made any structure at all, and even theirs is unmade by the next season's seep. And the great soft-bodied grazers, when they finally die out in the middle water, become for a little while their own loud kingdom — a whole brief flaring city of the small, the rushers and the strikers and the clouds all converging, the singers breeding in the warmth of the slow decay, until the floor of the place has taken the great body back down into the patient water just below the surface, where everything here is always only a hand's depth from going.

That is who lives here. Not one ruler and its court. A floor of voices, grazed from above and below; a sparse standing army of solitary strikers each holding its hundred meters; restless pooled flocks that have traded territory for numbers; an air made solid with insects too many to finish; and, far out, the few enormous slow ones the buoyant warmth allows, calling to each other across the finished country in the only deep voice it has.

They are, all of them, exactly suited. That is the quiet thing under the roar. A place that has stopped arguing with itself, that has reached the bottom of its long descent and will stay there for an age, breeds creatures that have also stopped arguing — that are not on their way to being anything else, not adapting toward some better fit, but already, completely, *fit*. The mountains finished becoming smaller. The rivers finished wandering flat. And the life finished too, into this: the most crowded, most singing, most thoroughly arrived equilibrium the place has ever held. Nothing here is reaching. Everything here has gotten where it was going. That is why it is so loud. It is the sound of a great many creatures with nothing left to become, doing the one thing left to do, which is to go on — abundantly, horizontally, under the wide bright sky, on the warm water that holds them all up.
