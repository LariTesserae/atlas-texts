There are things here that mistake themselves for the place.

Start with the small ones, because there are so many and because they are the easiest to be honest about. The frogs are not frogs the way a frog is a frog up in the world. They are too soft. They have no urgency in them — they have all the time the mud has, which is more time than a body should have access to. They sit half-sunk in the warm with their throats going and they are not calling *to* anything; they have forgotten the call was ever a question that wanted an answer. The roar is just what they do now. It is a kind of breathing the whole congregation took up together, a long time ago, and never agreed to stop. If you lifted one out it would be warm in your palm and pulse against your fingers and look at you with the patience of something that does not expect to be picked up again, nor minds, and you would want, very badly, to put it back. They are most of the voices. They are the floor of the sound the way the mud is the floor of the place — the thing everything else is arranged on top of.

Above them, the swarms. Call them midges; they are near-midges, the way the eels are near-eels, the way everything here is *near* the thing you know and a half-step off it. They hang. That is their whole verb. They do not migrate, do not hunt that you can see, do not arrive or leave — they occupy a volume of warm air and they stay in it, turning, a texture you walk through, and when you have walked through them they close behind you as water closes, with no memory that you passed. They are the place keeping its air full. If they have any awareness it is the awareness a held breath has of being held.

The pale things in the deep pools have given up their eyes. This is not a tragedy down here; nothing wept over it. They simply had eyes once and the dark made the eyes a cost and not a use, and over the slow seasons the bodies took the eyes back and spent them elsewhere — on the long sensitive lines down their flanks that read the water the way you read a room you've stood in your whole life, by the weight of the air, by what the silence is doing. They thread the flooded pipes. They know every joint, every weeping seam, the way a hand knows its own knuckles. They are not curious about you. Curiosity is for creatures that expect to encounter something new, and nothing new has happened here in a very long time except the slow business of the banks.

But these are the tenants. These are the things that live *in* it. You asked what lives here, and I have been circling, because the true answer is harder and I wanted to lay the floor first.

What lives here is the deciding.

Not a creature. Or — a creature, but the way a weather is a creature, the way a tide is a body. There is something in this region that is doing the work of making the banks, and it is not the water and it is not the mud and it is not any one of the soft pulsing throats, but it is *of* all of them, the way a chorus is of its singers and is also a thing the singers are not. It is what the place is for. The ridges of silt that look poured, that look an hour old — something is laying them. Something is conducting the long argument between the water and the digging, holding the two apart, deciding by hand-widths a day where the next edge wants to be. It has no hurry because it has no opposition. It has all the warmth and all the dark and all the time, and it is using them to *think* — slowly, in clay, in raw unrounded edges, drafting and redrafting a country that has not yet settled on what it is.

You can feel it most at the made things. That is why the pump-house and the tilted wall and the half-drowned hut hit you like faces in a crowd — because they are the last marks of a different intention, the one that did the digging, the sharp fast human kind of intention that drove iron stanchions in straight lines and meant something by it. And the slow thing has been growing over them. Has been *taking them in*. The brick gone soft is the brick being reconsidered. The pipe you can't tell anymore brought the water or was adopted by it — that is the slow thing having decided it doesn't matter, having absorbed the question into itself. It is patient with the iron the way a body is patient with a splinter: in no rush, but certain of the outcome. The works are not ruins. They are being digested. They are becoming part of the draft.

This is what makes the stillness so loud. The din is the slow thing *thinking out loud* — every drip and throat and wingbeat and gulp of released air is one of its working sounds, the way a person mutters over a problem, except it is the size of a region and it has been muttering for seasons and the problem is *where should the land be*. It is not deafening because it is excited. It is deafening because it is enormous and it never sleeps and it is never, for one second, finished. You sit on the pump-house step and nothing happens, loudly, and what you are sitting inside is the noise of an undivided attention, bent on the only task it has, which is to keep going, to keep emerging, to keep deciding — a country talking itself into existence one warm bank at a time, in no hurry, with no observer it needs, with the lights leaking grey through the ceiling and the eyeless fish reading the seams and the frogs holding the long note under everything.

It does not know you came in. It will not know when you leave. You are a disturbance the size of a stone dropped in a sheet of water acres wide — a brief local thing the swarms close over and the lines read and forget. The deciding goes on past you, out to where the dim wins, and further. It has not finished. That is the only fact about it that matters, and the only one it knows: *not finished.* Still warm. Still wet. Still making up its mind.
