# The People of the Slow Catchment

They are not many. Count them and you would not reach the population of a single river-valley village in a drier world — perhaps three hundred souls strung across five terraces, and they know one another's faces, voices, the particular rhythm of one another's coughs through shared walls. But to call them few is to misunderstand. In a world this early, this *unfinished*, three hundred is a great host. They are most of what thinks, here.

## Their Bodies

Begin with the obvious thing, the thing the place would demand: they have made peace with being wet, in the way a fish has made peace with water — which is to say they no longer experience it as a condition at all. There is no word in their speech for *dry*. There are many words for *kinds of wet*: the wet that beads and runs, the wet that soaks and stays, the wet that hangs in the lungs, the wet that has begun to smell of green rot, the clean wet of a fresh-caught leaf. To them these are as distinct as colors.

Their skin is fine and almost translucent at the extremities, thickened and slightly waxen across the back and shoulders — a natural shedding-surface, so that water sheets off them as off the channeled roofs. They do not have much hair; what they have lies flat and slick, more like the pelt of a swimming animal than the dry mane of a desert people. Their eyes are large and dark and adapted to the dim, the pupils wide as a deep-water creature's, so that the smudge of an oil-lamp behind a screen is to them a thing of real brightness, almost too much, a thing to squint against.

They are not tall. Tallness is for places with sky to grow toward; here the air itself presses with the weight of standing water, and a body learns to be compact, low, efficient in its wading movements. They walk with a particular gait — a slight forward lean, knees soft, weight rolling — the walk of things that move through resistance, that have never in their lives known the ease of striding through empty air. Watch them and you will think, without quite knowing why, of people walking on a streambed.

And they are *slow*. Not weak — their patience is muscular, a deliberate economy. They have learned, as everything here has learned, that haste is a kind of waste, that the world will not be hurried and neither, therefore, will they. A task that would take an afternoon elsewhere takes a day here, and no one minds, because there is no shape to the day to mind against. There is only the pale unchanging light and the dripping.

## How They Think

This is the deeper thing about them, the thing that takes longest to see.

They have grown up inside a world that is *visibly being made*. The stone underfoot is younger than their grandparents. A terrace-wall that was molten in living memory has cooled into a thing you can lean a house against. They have watched cracks open and reseal. They have, some of them, seen new shelf-rock extrude and settle in the lower slopes, the mountain adding to itself like a slow exhalation.

So they do not believe, as people in finished worlds believe, that the world is a stage already built, upon which they merely act. They believe the world is *still being said* — that it is a sentence not yet finished, and that they are inside the sentence, and that their own small makings are part of how it gets completed. When they cut a cistern from the soft stone, they do not feel they are imposing on the rock. They feel they are *helping it decide its shape*. The toolmarks are not violence. They are conversation.

This gives them a strange relationship to permanence. They build carefully, beautifully, with great craft — and they are entirely unbothered when a thing they built sinks back into the warm stone, or a terrace shifts, or a wall reseals over a doorway in the night. The world is taking back its draft to revise it. You do not weep when a sentence is rewritten. You wait to see what it becomes.

## What They Do

Most of their labor is the labor of the great water-machine. They are tenders of the downward flow. The cisterns must be kept clear of moss-choke; the small cataracts must be guided, redirected, the overflow-channels chiseled deeper or dammed with fired-clay plugs as the levels shift. It is endless, gentle, attentive work — the work of a people who have made a religion, almost, of *guiding what falls*. To them the highest virtue is to take the thing that descends and give it good passage: to neither hoard it nor waste it, but to pass it on, clean, to the level below.

(You will understand, then, that they have ideas about generations, about inheritance, about death, that all run downhill. To die, in their tongue, is to *go to the lower terrace* — and they mean it almost literally, since their dead are given to the lowest cisterns, returned to the flow, passed on.)

They tend the hanging gardens too, though *tend* is too strong; they mostly negotiate with them, harvesting the cupped catch-leaves and the slow fleshy fruit, never taking the last bloom from any curtain. They keep the fish-things in the cisterns, which they neither quite farm nor quite worship — pale blind creatures that came up from below, from the dark unfinished places where the mountain is still molten, and which they regard as *visitors from deeper in the sentence*, bearers of news from the part of the world not yet made.

## And the Standing Ones

Here is the heart of them, the thing they will not quite speak of plainly even among themselves.

They do not know what the standing ones are. But they suspect — and it is the central suspicion of their lives — that the standing ones are *what they themselves are becoming*, or *what made them*, or both at once, the two being perhaps the same thing in a world this circular and slow.

Consider: the people are water that has learned, briefly, to hold a shape and move with purpose and drink and decide. The standing ones are water that has learned, briefly, to hold a shape and move with purpose and drink — and then lets go, dissolves, returns to the general wet without grief. The people see in the standing ones a *simpler draft of themselves* — or a *later* one, a version that has finished learning the lesson the people are still struggling with: how to gather, how to be a self for a while, how to release the self again without clinging when the world calls the water back.

So the offerings at the terrace-edges, the cupped leaves of clean water — these are not quite worship and not quite bribery. They are closer to *introductions*. To leaving a light in the window for a relative who might be passing. The people are saying, in the only grammar they have: *We are here. We gathered too. We see you. When you drift by, know that the standing ones who walk on two legs and cut the stone have left you something clean to drink.*

And on the rare day a standing one drifts through the narrow ways, pausing at a threshold, drinking a bloom dry — the household does not flee and does not kneel. They go still. They let it pass. They feel, in the dim, the particular vertigo of being looked at by your own beginning, or your own end, and not being able to tell which. Then it dissolves, and the dripping resumes, and they go back to guiding the water down.

## What They Are, Finally

They are a people made by patience out of an unfinished world: low, slick, dark-eyed waders in a hung sea, tenders of the downward flow, conversationalists with stone, keepers of small warm lights in an enormous gray. They build knowing it will be unbuilt. They gather knowing they will be ungathered. They live early in the sentence of their world and do not need to know how it ends, because they have understood the one thing this place teaches above all others:

that everything here is still becoming — including them — and that the only grace is to fall slowly, and clean, and pass yourself on to the level below.
