# The Sumpwardens of Var Hollet

They are fewer than the city needs and more than it could survive without, and they have made of that arithmetic a way of being.

## Their Bodies

Look at the hooded figure on the avenue, and then keep looking, because the first glance will lie to you. You will think: a person, stooped, wrapped against the wet. You will be mostly right and entirely wrong about the proportions.

The Sumpwardens are tall when they stand straight, which they almost never do — the constant stoop is not age but architecture, a permanent forward lean as though always listening downward, toward the rock, toward the count. Their skin is the pale wet grey of the limestone, and it is *wet*, always, not from sweat but from themselves; they weep faintly from the pores the way the cliffs weep from their fissures, and the moisture beads and runs and is replenished. They do not dry because they are not meant to. A dry Sumpwarden is a dying one. Water is not something they drink so much as something they *hold at equilibrium*, taking up through the skin what they shed through it, a body that is itself a sump-pool, level and patient and never overflowing.

Their hands are very large and very soft. Generations of touch-reading — of pressing palms flat to iron plate to feel the engines through their bones, of trailing fingertips along wet walls to read the chalk-script by texture as much as sight — have left them with broad, padded, almost boneless-seeming fingers, sensitive past the point any sighted, hearing creature would need. They feel the pump-vibration the way the blind eels do. It is their whole world too. They could no more lose it than lose their own pulse, and in truth they have stopped distinguishing between the two.

Their faces are smooth, eroded-looking, the features worn gentle the way the ridges are. Large eyes, but unhurried ones — eyes that have nothing sudden to track in the flat permanent light, and so have slowed, gone soft-lidded, half-shut against the mist. The ears are nearly vestigial, folded small. What use is an ear in the roar? They hear with their chests, with the soles of their feet, with their enormous palms.

## How They Speak

Not aloud. Or — almost never aloud, and never above the murmur-distance, mouth pressed to the curl of another's vestigial ear, for the most intimate things only: a death, a love, the rare correction to the engines. Such close-spoken words are sacred precisely because the roar makes them so costly. To speak to a Sumpwarden is to put your lips against their skull. It is the most private act they have.

For everything else there is the chalk and the gesture.

The chalk-script crawls over every wet wall of Var Hollet, written and dissolving and written again — for the damp eats the chalk within a day or two, and so all writing is provisional, all record temporary, nothing finished, nothing sharp. They have made an entire literature out of impermanence. A thing written must be rewritten to persist, and so the act of *maintaining* a text — recopying the old laws, the old observations, the count of the water — is itself the highest work, identical in spirit to the bailing of the engines. Their scriptures are not preserved. They are *sustained*, by hand, daily, one fingernail's width ahead of the dissolving. To stop rewriting is to let the meaning drown.

The gesture-language is slow. There is no hurry in it. A full sentence might take a minute to sign, the soft hands moving through the steam like the moths' light moving between the chimneys — and indeed they have borrowed the moths' grammar, the slow pulse, the drift. A Sumpwarden agreeing with you does not nod. They glow, faintly, from a patch of skin at the throat — a pale slow bioluminescent flush, learned or evolved no one knows, the same trick the moths abandoned sound for. In the dim steam you can hold a whole conversation in light, two pale flickers across an empty avenue, saying *yes, still, holding, the same, the same, good.*

## What They Are For

This is the thing to understand: the Sumpwardens do not run the engines. The engines run. The Sumpwardens *attend* them. They are not engineers in any sense that builds or improves. They are something older and stranger — the priesthood of a single unending verb, which is *to keep*.

Four adjustments in two hundred years. Think about what that means for a life's work. A Sumpwarden may serve sixty years at a station and never once touch the controls in anger — may spend a whole existence simply *confirming*, palm to plate, that the vibration is unchanged, that the count holds, that nothing has happened. Their labor is the labor of vigilance over a thing that must not change, and the great spiritual achievement of their culture is to find this not maddening but holy. They have inverted the human relationship to time. For us, a life of perfect sameness is a horror. For them it is salvation made audible — the roar that does not stop is the proof that they have not failed, that the water has not won, that the city has lived one more hour, and one more, and one more, deafeningly, into the indefinite future.

They are not bored. Boredom is a thing that wants a different next moment. The Sumpwardens have unlearned that want entirely. They are the most patient minds that have ever existed, because patience implies waiting *for* something, and they wait for nothing — they attend the eternal present, fingernail by fingernail, and call it enough.

## Why So Few

They breed slowly, and on purpose. A city that must persist unchanged for centuries cannot afford the churn of generations; too many young, too many new hands wanting to *fix* things, would be a kind of flood in itself. So they are sparse by design — long-lived, slow to mature, slower to reproduce. The empty quarters are not abandoned. They are *fallow*, kept ready, steaming gently, in case the basin's slow arithmetic ever requires more hands than the present holds. The city is built for a population that has not been needed in living memory and is maintained anyway, against the fingernail-slow possibility, because that is simply what one does here: one keeps the spare rooms warm against a flood that rises too slowly to fear and too surely to forget.

A Sumpwarden child is raised first to the vibration, laid as an infant on the warm iron to learn the pulse before it learns to crawl, so that the engine-rhythm becomes the rhythm it expects the world to have. They could not sleep elsewhere. Take one to the silent high road and it will lie awake in terror, its whole body screaming that the engines have stopped, that the drowning has begun. The roar is their lullaby and their womb-sound and their proof of God. Silence is the only thing they fear, and they have arranged their entire civilization so as never, ever to hear it.

## What Becomes of Them

When a Sumpwarden dies, they are not buried — the ground is too wet, and besides, *down* is where the water lives, and you do not give your dead to the thing you have spent your life refusing. They are laid instead in the black sump-pools, where the blind eels are, and where the water holds its remembered level. The body softens, erodes, gives up its held moisture to the equilibrium, and within a season is gone — returned, dissolved, made part of the count.

And this, to them, is the gentlest thing they can imagine. Not to be preserved. Not to be finished or sharp or remembered as a fixed and separate thing. But to be *kept* — taken up into the patient water that rises to meet the rain, pumped someday up over the rim, fallen again as warm rain on some descendant's shoulders, sunk, risen, attended, held.

They do not mourn loudly. They could not, over the roar. They press their soft palms to the pool's surface, feel the level holding exactly where it always holds, and glow, once, slowly, from the throat:

*the same, the same, still here, still keeping, good.*

And then they go back to the work, which is the only prayer they have, and which does not stop, and which is the sound of their refusal to sink, sustained, deafening, into a future that will look exactly, blessedly, like now.
