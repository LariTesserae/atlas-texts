They are not many, and they were never many. This is the first thing to understand: the place does not need a crowd, and the people who live here are scaled to the place, which is to say there are few of them and they are patient.

Begin with their bodies, because their bodies are the argument the place has been making all along.

They are slow. Not feeble — slow the way the engines are slow, deliberately, by design, because haste has no purchase here and burns water they do not have to spare. They move at a pace that would unsettle you if you watched them, a pace pitched just under your own, so that standing among them you would feel subtly hurried, vulgar, like the only fast thing in a room of settling dust. They reach for a tool and the reaching takes a moment longer than you expect, and the moment is not hesitation. It is simply their tempo. They live on the clock of the escapement, the *tock* in the lower town, and that clock is unhurried.

And they are green, or rather they are *of* the green — I think they are not separate from the substrate at all. This is the part that took me longest to see. I kept looking for the gardeners, the tenders, the small busy animal-people who must have grown all this. There are none. The town did not have inhabitants who shaped it. The town shaped inhabitants. The same slow organism that pressed its lower strata into wood and trained its trunks into towers also, somewhere in the long resolving of itself, budded off these walking nodes — these slender upright growths that detached, that learned to carry themselves from one part of the body to another, that tend the engines because the engines are their own deeper anatomy and tending them is no different from a hand drawing back from heat. They are the grove's way of touching itself. They are how it reaches the places that do not bend.

So: tall, I think, and thin, and pale-fibered, lit faintly from within by the same cultivated phosphor that lights the dwellings, so that at the blue end of dusk you might see one before you see anything else — a soft standing column of cool light, moving down a street at the pace of sap. Their skin, if it is skin, has the grain of the violin-dark floors, finer at the joints, and where they have worked a long time at one task the fiber has gone smooth and hard and slightly glossy, the way the paths are worn smooth, the way a tool-handle takes a shine. They wear their labor as polish. An old one would be lacquered all over, like the fist-sized beetle, burnished by decades of the same slow reaching.

They do not eat, not as you would. They drink — but sparingly, ceremonially almost, because water is the whole economy of their being and they know it in their tissues. I think they take in the cold dew directly, standing at the reservoir-trunks at the cool hours, drawing it up through themselves the way the walls do. When the rare rain comes and the engines quicken into that something-like-song, I think the people feel it as a flood of well-being, a turgid fullness, and they are at their most alive then, and most numerous in the streets — not crowding, but present, drawn out by abundance the way the pale bird is drawn out, once. And then the dryness returns and they thin back into the walls, and you would swear the town had emptied, when really it has only been reabsorbed.

This is why the streets are nearly empty when you walk them. Not because the people are hidden, not because they have died or fled. Because most of them, most of the time, are *in* the structure — standing in the wall-lines, folded into the architecture, indistinguishable from the trunks they grew out of, doing the slow internal work of being the town. Supervision is unnecessary because there is no division between the watcher and the watched. The hand does not supervise the heart. They go quiet into the green and they are still working, still pumping, still part of the long tidal breathing, and only now and then does one of them detach into the open air and walk a worn path on some errand the rest of the body has decided on without words.

Do they speak? I listened for it. I do not think they speak as we mean it — there is too little air spent on sound here, and they are all one organism, and a body does not need to announce to itself what it intends. But I think they communicate the way the place communicates: by pressure, by the slow opening and closing of valves, by the rise and fall of sap in shared channels. A decision passes through them the way the midday quickening passes through the engines — not as a command from any center but as a condition the whole stand enters together. When the light comes strong they all grow eager at once. When the water runs low they all grow still at once. There is no government because there is no plurality deep enough to need one. There is just the creature, agreeing with itself, as it has agreed with itself for so long that the agreeing is finished. *Resolved.* A people that has stopped arguing with itself.

And here is the thing I carried away, the thing that sits coldly in me still: they are not building toward anything. I kept waiting to find the purpose — what the factory makes, what the engines are *for*, what all this patient turgid labor produces and where it goes. There is no product. The engines turn to keep the engines turning. The people tend the engines so the engines can be tended. The water is collected so that there will be water to collect with. It is a closed body, a perfect quiet metabolism, drinking its own cool breath, doing its work for the sake of being a thing that works, ticking softly toward nothing in particular.

They are content. I want to be clear about that, because it would be easy to hear all this as sorrow and it is not sorrow. They are not trapped, not waiting, not lonely — loneliness needs at least two, and there is in the deepest sense only one of them here, one slow green sleeper that has dreamed up just enough separate hands to keep its own dream running, and feels in those hands no want at all. They are as content as a sleeping body is content. They have everything they are. The dew comes, the light angles over, the wheels turn, a single pale node detaches and walks a smooth path and folds back into the wall, and the *tock* goes on counting, and they ask nothing further, and they will go on like this, untroubled, breathing, for exactly as long as the cold keeps beading on the green.
