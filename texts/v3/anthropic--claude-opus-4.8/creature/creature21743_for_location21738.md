They are makers, and they have always been makers, though they would not use that word. The word implies a finish line.

Begin with their bodies, because the cold made those too. They are built low and dense, the way everything that survives up here is built — no wasted height, no extravagant limbs to bleed heat into the thin air. Their skin is the color of the bruised moss in the seams, a deep weathered violet-grey that takes the dim light and gives almost none of it back, so that from any distance they are nearly the rock itself, just a slightly warmer shade of the same patience. They have many hands. Not arms in the way you'd expect — more like the lichens have hands, a branching, a reaching that divides and divides again into finer and finer points of contact, so that a single one of them can be touching the stone in a thousand places at once, reading the crystalline fracture, feeling for the line where the next break wants to come.

That is the first thing to understand about them. They do not build by adding. They build by *listening to where the breaking wants to go,* and helping it.

They are slow. You would not, at first, be sure they were alive at all. One of them might occupy the lee of a boulder for what you would call a season, its many fine hands laid along a fault, doing nothing you could photograph — and then the rock cleaves, bright and sharp and new, and the geometry of a whole ridge has changed, and the maker moves on, having midwifed one more sentence of the geology that is always mid-sentence here. They do not quarry. They do not pile. They *encourage*. The freeze-thaw tick-crack you hear is not the weather working alone; it is the weather working *through them*, the way a current works through a fish that has learned to hang exactly still in it and steer with the smallest fin.

There are not many of them. There were never meant to be many. Density is a lowland idea. Up here, where every breath is borrowed, a single maker might be the only one of its kind for a valley's width, and this does not trouble them, because they are not lonely in the way warm crowded things are lonely. They are joined by the hum.

The hum is theirs — or rather, they are the hum's. This is the part that took you longest to circle, and it is the part they understand best, because it is the only thing they have ever truly known. The great engine of the atmosphere, the mist drawn up the valleys on its schedule, the water cycling through its phases with the cadence of a factory floor — they do not merely live inside that rhythm. They *keep* it. Each maker is a small node of intention wired into the enormous distributed machine, and what they do, mostly, all day, in the dim violet light, is *tend the cycle*. They open a crack so that water can enter and freeze and split the stone, and the splitting exposes new faces, and the new faces weep, and the weeping feeds the tarns, and the tarns skin with ice, and the ice draws the cold air down, and the cold air pulls the mist up the valley on its shift — and somewhere up that valley another maker is laying its thousand fine hands along another fault, waiting for that exact mist, that exact moisture, to do its part of the work.

They are an assembly line, yes. But there is no product. Or rather, the product is the world, and the world is never finished, and that is precisely the point. They are not building toward a cathedral. They are *running the cathedral's making as the cathedral itself* — the act and the artifact the same thing, the verb and the noun collapsed. Ask one of them (you cannot, but suppose) what they are constructing, and the question would not parse. You are constructing it. You, by walking here, by breathing, by phase-changing a little water with your warm lungs — you have just become, for an afternoon, a worker on the floor. The choughs are workers. The marmots are workers, tucking seeds and turning soil-that-is-not-yet-soil in the seams. The lichens are the slowest and most senior workers of all, and the makers honor them, lay their fine hands gently around a lichen's edge and do not disturb it, because the lichen has been keeping its station longer than the makers have kept theirs.

This is the strangest grace of them: they do not know they are the foremen. They feel themselves to be the hands. They believe — if belief is the word — that the great hum commands and they obey, that the schedule of the mist is a law handed down and not a law they themselves uphold by their tending. They cannot see the whole. No single one of them ever sees more than a valley, and the valley shows almost nothing — just rock, just water, just the occasional far cousin laid quiet against a far fault. The whole shows everything, but the whole is not available to anyone standing inside it. So they keep the engine running in the faith that something larger is in charge, and the something larger is only the sum of all of them keeping it, and the faith is what holds the sum together. The belief in the foreman *is* the foreman. The cathedral builds the builders who build the cathedral.

They have no fire and want none. Fire is a lowland god, a fast hot answer, and everything they do is slow and cold and exactly as patient as the lichen. They have no metal, no walls, no roads — because to wall a thing is to declare it finished, and nothing here is finished, and the declaration would be a lie they are constitutionally unable to tell. The world is mid-sentence. They are the punctuation that keeps the sentence going: the comma, the small held breath, the tick before the crack.

And when one of them dies — they do die, slowly, the violet skin going grey and then the grey going to actual stone, the body simply joining the geology it has tended all its long life — there is no mourning, because there is no absence. The maker does not leave the engine. It becomes a face of new rock, and the new rock weeps, and the weeping feeds a tarn, and somewhere up the valley a younger maker lays its thousand fine hands along the fault that the dead one's body has just become, and reads in the crystalline fracture the exact line where the next break wants to go, and helps it.

That is who lives there. Not a single creature but a *tending* — distributed across an enormous sparse cold landscape, almost invisible from any one place, total when you stand back far enough to lose the detail. They are the world's slow hands. They keep the cold because the cold is the medium of their patience. They keep the wet because the wet is how stone is taught to speak. They keep the hum because the hum is the only voice large enough to hold all of them at once, and they have mistaken their own chorus for a command, and the mistake is holy, and the mistake is the whole of their religion, and the religion works.

Begin with the cold, you said, because the cold is the first fact and the last.

For them it is neither. For them the cold is simply the room they were born to work in, and the work has no first and no last — only the tick, and the crack, and the mist coming up the valley right on time, drawn by a schedule they believe in and are.
