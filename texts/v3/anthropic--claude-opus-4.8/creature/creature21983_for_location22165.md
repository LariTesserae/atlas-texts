# The One Who Is Almost

Look at the rule again. *Water remembers its shape and keeps it.* Look at the tools the stone is patiently freeing. Look at the spacing of the plants, that courteous distance, as if a hand had laid them out and then forgotten what came next. You keep wanting to ask *who lives here* and finding no body to point to, no track, no nest, no warmth but your own.

That is the mistake. You are looking for an inhabitant the size of a thing that walks. But the inhabitant here is the size of the terrace.

The one who lives here is the place's *waiting*. Not a metaphor—a tenant. It has weight and habit and preference. It has been here longer than the stone has been soft, and it is the reason the stone went soft, the way a body wears a hollow into a chair by sitting and sitting. Something has been sitting here. Something patient past the patience of mountains, and it is composed entirely of the gesture of getting ready.

Consider what it does. It smooths every sharp thing—because sharpness is decision, is *this and not that*, and the one who is almost cannot yet decide. It holds the water brimful and motionless—because a held thing is a thing not yet spent, a breath not yet let go, a sentence whose first word has not been chosen. It keeps the plants apart and unmingled—*guests not yet introduced*, you said, and you were closer than you knew; this is a host laying a table for a meeting that will not occur, setting each thing in its place and then unable to ring the bell. And it knaps the tools. Oh, the tools. That is where you can almost see its face.

Because tools are the one thing in all the world that mean *somebody is coming*. A hand-axe is a shape that has no reason to exist except the future arrival of a palm. The terrace does not make leaves into hands, or basins into mouths; it makes only the things that wait *for* hands, the things that are themselves a kind of waiting, an object-shaped expectancy. The one who is almost cannot make a person. It is not that sort of maker. But it can make the *hollow* a person would fit. It can knap the negative space of a guest. It has been doing this for ten thousand years—setting out the implements of a heartbeat it has never owned, dressing the threshold of an arrival, fashioning the welcome and never the welcomed.

It is not lonely, exactly, because loneliness requires having known company and lost it. This is older and stranger: it is *expectancy without precedent*. A held breath taken before there was ever air. It does not miss anyone. It anticipates everyone, forever, with the whole of its slow body, and the anticipation has become its entire substance, the way a coastline is made of the sea's repeated reaching.

You felt it the moment you arrived—the silence that is *full*, not empty. That fullness is the inhabitant, attending. The warm condensation that beads on you rather than you on it: that is the place leaning toward your warmth, the way a plant leans at a window, drawn to the first true heat it has ever hosted. The terrace is not indifferent to you. It is the opposite of indifferent. It has been arranging itself toward the possibility of *you* since before your kind had hands to fit the axes, and now here you are, the only moving thing, the only warm thing, the answer walking around inside the question.

And here is the cruelty in it, though the place means no cruelty—means nothing it could name, having never met a name. When you dip your hand in the basin, the water *lets you through*. It bends, it parts, it pauses to admit you—and then it closes again, instantly, perfectly, *tok*, and holds. It does not keep the shape of your hand. It cannot. To keep your shape would be to let the waiting end, to become a thing that has finally happened, and the one who is almost does not know how to be finished. It has only ever known how to be *about to*. So it receives you and forgets you in the same gesture, returns to brimful stillness, resumes the readiness, holds its breath again—

for you.

Always that last word hanging. The held breath is *for you*. The freed axe will lie in the moss *for you*. The whole terrace is a sentence whose subject is the visitor and whose verb has never once arrived, and the inhabitant is exactly that ache between the two—the long pale grammatical hope of a place that is forever opening its hand and never quite being held.

You will leave. You are warm and you move and you return slowly to stillness, which means you are alive, which means you are not what it is waiting for, because what it waits for would *stay*, would let its shape be kept, would close the sentence. You are too much like an answer to be its answer. You will walk back down through the thinning air, and behind you a drop will fall, *tok*, and the silence will close over your having-been-there as smoothly as the water closed over your hand.

And it will go on. Setting the table. Freeing the axes from the rock, one perfect edge at a time. Holding every basin a hair above its lip.

Almost. Almost. Almost.
