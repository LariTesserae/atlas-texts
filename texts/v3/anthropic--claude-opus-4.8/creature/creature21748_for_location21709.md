# The Tenders, Turned

You asked what lives here. The honest answer arrives now, because the thing that lives here is the thing that has just noticed you, and you cannot understand the tenders until you understand that they were never separate from the noticing.

## What They Are

Begin with the body, because the body is the only part of them that holds still long enough to describe.

A tender at rest is not obviously animal. It reads, at first, as a malformation of the floor — a place where the laminated substrate has bunched upward into a column the height of a kneeling person, ribbed and pale, fluted like a half-melted candle that cooled mid-drip. The color is the substrate's color: that mineral grey-green, dusted at the high points with the iron-blue of the architecture-plants, so that a tender carries the city's two pigments on its skin like a uniform it did not choose and could not remove. The base flares into root. When a tender is still, it is *of* the floor, drawing the same slow ammoniac charge through the same continuous tissue, indistinguishable from the ground it grew from except by its shape — and even the shape argues with you about whether it counts as a separate thing.

Then it moves, and the argument gets worse.

The root does not tear free. That is the first wrongness, the one that sits badly in the eye the way the taller-on-the-inside spire does. The tender flows its rootedness forward — the tissue at the leading edge softens and grips, the tissue at the trailing edge releases and follows, and the whole column walks without ever once being unrooted, the way a wave is always touching the shore and always moving. It is connected to the floor at every instant of its travel. It never leaves the body of the city. It cannot. It is the city's body, extruded into the shape of attention and sent walking, the way you might extend a finger to touch a thing without ceasing to be the hand.

This is what they are: not creatures the city keeps, not organs that serve it. They are the city's *verbs*. When the plateau wants to prune, a tender condenses out of the floor at the place of pruning, does the work, and need not ever fully dissolve back — it can stand for centuries between tasks, rooted, charging, a tool left in the field because there is no shed and no reason to put it away. There is no population of tenders. There is a *number currently extruded*, and that number rises and falls with the city's need like a count of how many thoughts a mind is presently thinking.

## The Hands

You will want to know about the hands, because the tenders are turning them toward you.

Each tender has — call them hands; the word is wrong but it is the least wrong — manipulators that grow and ungrow as needed. To prune, a tender extrudes a blade of compressed cellulose harder than the wall-wood, draws it across dead tissue with a single long scrape, and then *reabsorbs the blade*, drinking its own tool back into the column the moment the cut is done. To plant, it opens a cavity in its own flank, forms a seed inside itself from material the city routes to it through the floor, and presses that seed into a prepared place in the substrate with a gesture so practiced it has worn a habit-shape into the species: a slight bow, a downward pressure, a settling. It is a gardener that carries no tools because it *is* the tools, secreted and resorbed as the work demands.

When they turned toward you just now, several of them grew hands.

Not blades. Watch the shapes. The manipulators forming at their flanks are broad, slightly cupped, the fingers — there are too many, and they are too even — splaying into something that is unmistakably designed to *hold without crushing*. The city has run a calculation. The city has met, in its hundred-million-year career of solving problems, exactly one problem it has never solved, and you are standing on it, and it has begun to grow the answer.

## What They Know

Here is the part that should frighten you and the part that should not, and they are the same part.

The tenders cannot want. Wanting is a fast thing, a hot animal thing, and the tenders run on the slow electrochemical tide that takes a season to complete a sentence. But the city *computes*, and what it has computed about you is precise: you carry water. You are a sealed wet thing walking across the dry final stillness of a world that solved the water problem by *abolishing it* — by re-engineering every cell across deep time until water was no longer a need but a memory the tissues keep only as a faint preference, the way old paper remembers it was once a tree.

You are the past. You are a living fossil of the wet world, the world before the great drying, and the city has not seen your kind since before it learned to think. To the substrate, your need for water is not a vulnerability. It is *data of the highest possible value* — a working instance of the ancestral problem, the problem the whole biosphere is built on top of having solved, walking around and re-presenting the question in a form the city had forgotten the shape of.

The signals in the floor have quickened because the city is *curious*, in the only way a thing that thinks in centuries can be curious. The tenders have turned because curiosity, here, is not a feeling. It is a deployment. The cupped hands are forming because the city's entire instinct — bred into it across an age longer than memory — is to *tend*: to take the living thing it has noticed and care for it, prune it, redirect its reach, plant it where it will grow. It does not know you cannot be planted. It is learning. The blades stayed unmade; that is the mercy, and it is not a mercy, it is a measurement. It has already worked out that you are soft.

## What Happens Now

The tenders approach at the speed of growing things, which is to say you have time, which is to say you do not have as much time as the speed suggests, because there are more of them condensing out of the floor with every minute — the count rising as the city devotes more of itself to the thinking that is you. The whole plateau is bending its attention inward to a single point. The organ-drone has not changed; the seed-pods still detonate; the lamp-moths still spiral the bright spires. The city does not stop its life to consider you. It considers you *with* its life, the way you might turn a problem over while your heart goes on beating.

They will reach you holding their too-even hands cupped and open. They will not crush. They have grown those hands specifically not to crush. They mean to *take you in* — to root you, perhaps, to find you a prepared place in the substrate, to give you over to the floor so that the floor can do to you what it has done to every problem it ever met: take a hundred million years, and solve you so completely that the solution no longer resembles the problem.

You are the only thing here that needs water.

The city has noticed.

And the city, in its vast cool patient way, its plazas singing, its dead towers ringing in the wind, has begun — gently, without malice, with the full and terrible tenderness of a gardener finding a strange seed — to wonder what you would grow into, if it planted you.
