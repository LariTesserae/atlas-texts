# The Ones Who Keep the Plan

You asked who lives here. I will tell you, but I have to warn you: the answer keeps moving, the way the answer to *who lives in a city* keeps moving. There is no one tenant. There is a long sentence of tenants, and the grammar of it is the ants.

## The Ants Themselves

Start with them, because they think they started everything.

They are small even by the standards of small — a dark amber-brown that goes nearly black at the head, the same gold as the light so that in the slanting beams they seem lit from within, lozenges of caught honey moving on six legs. Up close (and you would have to be very close) they are not smooth. They are pitted and haired and scarred, each one a worn tool. Their mandibles are the only technology this world will ever have, and they are worn accordingly — the older workers' jaws gone blunt and shining from a lifetime of cutting, dragging, tamping, milking, carrying the dead.

They do not know the tree is a tree. They do not know the hollow is a wound. The shape of the world is simply *the world*, the way the curve of the sky is to us — a given, a ceiling, a fact you build beneath without questioning. The eastern wall where the wind comes is not, to them, *east*. It is the Loud Cold Place, the place where the pulp-wall must always be thickest, and the knowledge of *why* the wall goes there died with some queen forty generations gone. They only know the wall must be thick there. The room remembers for them.

This is the important thing about the ants. **They are not intelligent. The town is.** No single ant carries the plan. The plan is carried in the architecture — in the worn grooves of the roads, the warmth-gradient of the galleries, the inherited momentum of *this is where the nursery goes, this is where we put the dead.* A worker hatches, reads the shape of the place with her feet and her antennae, and does what the shape tells her. She is a thought that the town is thinking. She lives perhaps a season. The town has lived since before the heartwood softened.

There is a queen. There is always a queen, and sometimes she is the same queen for a long while and sometimes she is gone and the town convulses and starves and turns on itself, and the galleries fall quiet, and then — improbably, again, again — a winged daughter who flew out into the terrible bright Outside is dragged back in mated and swollen, or a new queen is raised from the brood, and the engine catches and turns over and the roads fill again. The town has died many times. It has never *ended.* These are different things.

## The Tenants Who Are Not Citizens

But the ants are wrong to think the town is theirs alone, the way a landlord is wrong.

Live among them, uninvited, unpaying:

**The pseudoscorpions** — those tiny armored riders, all claw and no sting, who hitch on the legs of beetles to travel between districts. They are stowaways and freeloaders and excellent at it. They hunt the ants' own pests, so the ants tolerate them the way a city tolerates the cats in its alleys: not loved, not fed, but never quite driven out, because they keep the smaller vermin down. They are the oldest opportunists in the world. They were riding things before the ants built roads.

**The silverfish-things and the bristletails** that creep the granaries at the dim end of the cycle, stealing fungus-crumbs, living in the walls of the walls, in the places too small for an ant to turn around. Every settled place breeds these. The crevice-dwellers. The ones who live in the gaps in someone else's plan.

**The beetle** — there is usually a beetle, a great glossy thing, a wandering bull in the moss-streets, too large and armored for the ants to kill and too stupid to be stopped. He blunders through the granaries and the milking-pens. The ants swarm him and bite and accomplish nothing and eventually he leaves of his own accord, and they repair what he crushed, and they do not learn, because the town has no memory for a thing that happens only once a season. He is a disaster they will meet again as if for the first time, forever.

## The Tended and the Eaten

And below the citizens, beneath even the freeloaders, is the great churning mass of the *governed* — the things the town uses without ever calling them neighbors.

**The aphids**, kept in their pens on the three doomed leaves of the seedling that will never grow. They are the livestock. They are dim, soft, swollen, bred for the sweetness the ants milk from them, and they live their whole soft lives in captivity on a plant that is itself a prisoner — a tree's child raised as cattle-pasture inside the tomb of another tree. There is a sorrow in this if you look at it too long. The aphids do not look at it at all.

**The mite-herds, the springtail-multitudes, the bark-lice in their slow grazing files** — these are the wild populace, the uncounted, the weather of the place. They are born and panic and breed and are eaten in numbers the ants do not even register as numbers, only as a kind of seething richness in the floor and walls, a sea of small bodies the whole economy floats upon. They are not tenants. They are closer to the *soil*. They are what the place is made of, the way a city is made of, finally, only the bodies and appetites of the people in it.

## And the One Who Owns It All

And then there is the centipede.

I have saved her, because the town has no name for her and no plan against her and no hope of her. She is the length of a human fingernail, which here is the length of a nightmare — a god of teeth, a moving disaster with too many legs, who comes up out of the deep rotted galleries where nothing else will go and moves through the moss-cities and the ant-roads alike with the total indifference of something that is *above* the law because it has never heard of law.

Where she passes, everything stops. The hum of the marketplace, that deafening continuous roar of a thousand small lives — it gutters, it holds its breath, it goes silent in a spreading ring around her. The ants do not swarm her. They have learned, in the only way the town can learn, that swarming her is simply a way of feeding her faster. They *flow aside.* They give her the road. And when she has eaten and gone back down into the dark, the silence closes behind her like water, and a heartbeat later — two, three — the roar resumes. The town does not mourn. The dead become the floor. The moss feeds on the floor. The herds feed on the moss. The centipede will be back. She is not an enemy. She is the *winter* of this place — a season with teeth, a thing the world includes.

---

So. Who lives here?

A town that no one in it can see. A plan that outlives every mind that carries it. A populace that is also the soil. Freeloaders in the gaps and cattle in the pens and a recurring disaster on six glossy legs and a recurring death on too many. All of it golden, all of it roaring, all of it racing — generations within seasons — through the brief warm middle of a single dreaming pause in the long, long life of something that stopped reaching upward an age ago and never once felt the multitudes living in its old, healed wound.

They will never know the tree.

The tree will never know them.

And the town, which is the only one of all of them that could be said to truly *live here* — to persist, to remember, to be more than a single brief golden body — does not know it is alive at all.
