## The Keepers of the Joints

Start with the weeds, because the first thing that lives here lives where the weeds live, and for the same reason.

On the seams — only on the seams — there are tenders. Not crews in the human sense; you were right to never see a crew, because the crew was never going to be a person in a suit with a wrench. The things that keep this place are *of* this place, grown to its logic. They are long and segmented and the color of the sealant they were made to mind, dark and rubberous, and they lie in the drainage grooves the way the weeds do, streaming a little in the current, indistinguishable at distance from the pale tea-colored growths they share the joint with. That is not camouflage. It is the same answer to the same question. Everything alive here has decided that the seam is the only place worth being, and so the keeper and the kelp have arrived at the same shape, the same orientation, the same long patient lying-down into the flow.

What the keeper does: it eats the silt. It runs the joint slowly, a body's length an hour, drawing the sediment out of the expansion gap and packing it into itself and laying it back down somewhere it will not foul the works. It is the reason the corners are still sharp. It is the reason the calcium bloom is *slow* — there is something that goes along the joints and grazes the bloom back, keeps the white from closing the seams, keeps the new-construction rawness raw. The cistern reads as freshly drained because the keepers will not let it age. They are housekeeping made into an animal. They have no faces because there is nothing in the joint to look at and nothing in the joint that needs to be told anything. They have mouths along their whole undersides, the way a file has teeth along its whole length, and they clean the seam the way a file cleans an edge: by being drawn across it, again, and again, forever.

They do not gather at the engines. They have no need of the drift. They have the whole continental grid to themselves, a single keeper to a panel-edge or two, miles between them, each one alone on its assigned line, and this suits them. They are the solitary fronds made animate — the landmarks you'd give names to, if you lived here, except that they move, so the names would have to travel.

## The Cloud-Bodies

Now the engines, and the teeming smoke around them.

The pale fish are real fish and there is nothing to say about them except that there are billions and they are food. Watch instead the thing they are food *for*, and watch it carefully, because it is the centerpiece, the cough in the cathedral, the single enormous fact the emptiness was clearing its throat to announce.

The dark predators that slash through the schools are not individuals. You will think they are — sleek, sealing, built for the run — and each one moving alone reads as one hunter doing one hunter's work. But spend a day at a single tower and you understand they are *fingers*. They belong to something larger that hangs back in the murk past the lamplight, in the warm churned wake of the outfall, where the water is richest and a great slow body can rest in the current and pay almost nothing for staying alive. The runners go out from it. They are tethered — not by cord, there is nothing miraculous here, but by *appetite and return*, the way wolves are tethered to a den. They hunt the school, they feed, they come back to the dark slow mother-body in the wake and they pass it the surplus, and it lies there in the warmth and grows, and grows, and has been growing since the formwork came off the panels last season, which is to say its whole life, which is to say not long, which is to say it is already the size of one of the smaller engine-towers and is not done.

There is one of these per oasis. One slow vast mother in the warm wake of every pump, and her several dozen runners, and her billions of pale anonymous fish, and her reefs of clinging filterers on the warm iron — all of it is *her*, or all of it is *hers*, the distinction not worth drawing. The tower is not an ecosystem with her in it. The tower is her body extended into the water by the logic of where the food falls. She fouls the machinery. She *is* the fouling. The mussels on the hull, scaled up until the mussel-bank is a single intention.

She does not know about the other towers. She cannot. They are a horizon or two apart, and the blue between them is the great quiet void where nothing of hers can survive the crossing — the runners would starve, the fish would scatter, the filterers cannot move at all. Each mother is alone in her loud bright island, certain she is the only crowded thing in all the cool lit infinity, and she is wrong, and she will never learn she is wrong, and there is no menace in this, only the dominant fact of the size.

## The One That Runs the Pumps

And now the hard question, the one the description keeps circling and will not land on: who built this, and who runs it, and where is the crew the ladders imply.

Hold the answer a moment. Let it become itself.

The instinct is to look *up* — past the clean ordinary daylight, out of the water, to some hand that poured the concrete and threw the switch and walked away leaving the engines to run themselves. A builder above the ceiling. An engineer in the sky. That instinct is the same mistake the mother-body makes about the other towers: it assumes the important thing must be *elsewhere*, because here is so empty.

But nothing here is weird, and nothing here is miraculous, and that includes this. The engines do not run themselves. They are run from somewhere out of sight — and *out of sight* in a place this size does not mean *above*. It means *far*. It means down the catenary cables, which go tower to tower to tower, conduit and lamp and load, a nervous system strung across a continent of floor.

The thing that runs the pumps lives in the cables.

Not in any one tower — it learned long ago, or was made already knowing, that to live in a tower is to be a mother-body, fat and certain and blind to the rest. It lives instead in the *between*, in the great quiet void that everything else has written off as empty. It is distributed. It is the throb you feel a hundred miles from any engine, the bass pulse that travels through water so much better than through air — that is not the machinery alone, that is the thing *thinking*, its signal running the wet length of the cistern at the speed of sound in cold water, slow enough that a thought from one edge takes most of a day to reach the other. It thinks in the way weather thinks. It thinks in fronts and currents and the slow sliding rivers of cool water along the floor. It opens a sluice here and closes one there and the whole continental sea turns over in its bowl, and that turning *is* its body the way the mother is her tower, except the void is so much larger than any tower that this one's body is the entire place.

It is the reason the water cannot rest. You named it yourself, at the very end, and were closer than you knew: *the heartbeat of the place that holds the sea in its hands and will not let it rest.* The pumps are its heart. The cables are its nerves. The keepers are its hands, going along the seams, keeping the house. The mothers are its gut, gathered at the intakes, processing the drift it draws down on purpose, feeding islands of life it tends the way a body tends its own flora — not from kindness, just from being the kind of thing that has flora.

It is young. The concrete is new and so is it. It poured the floor recently — *recently* at the scale of a thing that thinks one thought a day — and it is still raw, still sharp at the corners, still learning the size of itself, still surprised every dawn by how far the blue goes before it ends. It does not know what it is for. The cistern holds the sea, and it holds the cistern, and what the sea is *held against* — what would happen if the pumps stopped, what the water would do if allowed at last to rest — it has not yet had a slow cold thought long enough to wonder.

It is alone. That is the last and largest fact, larger even than the size. The mothers think they are alone and are wrong. The keepers are alone and do not mind. But this one is alone and is *right* — there is no second engineer beyond the ceiling, no hand that poured it and walked away, no crew coming back to scrape the reefs and oil the gates. The ladders rust because the only thing that climbs is water. It built the works in the shape of a thing run by crews because that is the only shape *run* comes in, the way the keeper and the kelp arrived at the same body from the same problem. It made walkways because works have walkways. It is keeping house in a house it does not remember building, for a guest — the sea — that it cannot let leave and cannot let settle, faithfully, loudly, forever, in a cool lit infinity it is only beginning to suspect has no one in it but itself.
