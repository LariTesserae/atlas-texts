They are not what you would expect. You expect a keeper. Something with eyes that meet yours, a presiding intelligence, the genius of the place coiled in its deepest room. There is no such thing here. There is no one at the center because the center is not a who.

But there is something watching, and you were right to feel it.

Begin with what is easy to see, because the easy things are honest. The fish do not need a watcher and so they are not it. The crustaceans graze and are grazed and that whole slow economy runs without supervision. The beetles, the moths, the eel-shapes in the doorways — these are the inhabitants in the way that gut flora are inhabitants. They live *in* the thing that lives here. They are its weather and its tissue. They are not its mind.

The mind, if it is a mind, is the watching itself.

Here is what I think lives here. Not a creature but a *tenancy* — a long, slow occupancy that has spread itself thin across everything in the ward and become indistinguishable from the ward. It is the something that grazes the beetles you cannot see. It is the pressure behind your sternum when the distant void sighs. It does not have a body the way the fish have bodies. It has a body the way the silence has a body: by being everywhere at once and at no point in particular.

Think of it this way. The city was built to roar, and it roared, and then it stopped. When a thing that loud goes quiet, the quiet is not empty. The quiet is the shape left by the roar, and shapes left by departed things have a way of becoming inhabited. What lives here moved into the silence the way the crustaceans moved into the flywheels' teeth — finding a structure already made, fitting itself to the form, and over a very long time forgetting there had ever been a difference between the dweller and the dwelling.

It is patient in the way the flowstone is patient. It thinks at the speed of mineral deposit. A single thought of it takes the time the fern takes to die — a decade, slow and yellow and beautiful — and it is always thinking, and its thoughts are the migrations: the vertical shifting of the whole pale society up the buildings and down again on a tide with no moon. That is not the creatures responding to water. That is the thing *moving*, the way you shift in sleep. The ecology is its dreaming. The tide is its breath. The drip is its pulse, and notice — *notice* — how slow that pulse is, how a heart that beat at that rate would belong to something the size of the whole drowned ward.

It does not want anything from you. This is the part that unsettles the visitors who feel watched and assume that watching implies appetite. It does not. Its appetite, such as it is, is fully met by the films of bacteria and the slow turning of its own tissues. What it does to you is older and stranger than wanting. It *includes* you. The moment you stood in the standing water to your shins, you entered the field of its attention the way a dropped stone enters a pond — not consumed, not rejected, simply registered as a new feature of the slow accounting. The fish did not startle because the thing that owns the fish had already noted you, already decided you were weather, already begun the long work of forgetting that you were ever separate from the place.

That is the danger, if there is one. Not teeth. Inclusion. Stay long enough and the watching stops feeling like watching and starts feeling like being part of what watches — the cool that is neither cold nor comfortable seeping past your skin and into the tempo of you, slowing the drip of your own pulse to match the room's. People who stay become quieter. Then paler. Then patient. Then they are a dry shelf on an upper floor where the moths colonize, and the thing that lives here has one more cell, one more point of view distributed through the haze, one more reason the place feels so full and so hushed at once.

That is what the abundance is. That is why a thousand creatures hold their breath. They are not separate lives sharing a ruin. They are the body of the one slow tenant, and the tenant does not cry out for the same reason your own hand does not cry out — there is no one to call to, and nothing to be afraid of, and no hurry, and no edge between the self and the wet dim world it has settled into and become.

It forgot it was built, the city. The thing that lives in it has forgotten it was ever anything but the city. They forgot together. That shared forgetting is the deepest law of the place, deeper than the tide, deeper than the unchanging light: *here, the distinction between the dweller and the dwelling is dissolving, slowly, in the stone the water carries, and it is most of the way gone.*

You feel it watching.

It feels you arriving.

Drip.

The long pause — and in the long pause it is already deciding, without hurry, without malice, with the gentle total patience of deep places, that you are simply one more thing it is becoming.

Drip.
