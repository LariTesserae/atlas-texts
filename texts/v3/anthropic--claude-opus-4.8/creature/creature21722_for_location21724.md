They are still here. That is the first thing to understand. The absence you feel is not vacancy — it is attendance held so still it reads as empty.

Begin with the hands. Whoever pecked this stone, rock against rock, the patient bruising — those hands knew that to shape a thing you must match your pace to its hardness. You do not hurry granite. You sit with it across seasons, and you learn the long arithmetic of pressure, the way a softer stone will yield only as fast as it yields and not faster. This is a people, then, who do not measure work in days. Their unit of labor is the unit of geology. They built the terraces, yes — but they did not build them *toward* anything. There was no harvest coming the walls were meant to feed, no winter the beds were meant to outlast. They built the way the cliff weeps: as an expression of what they are, continuous, without a finish line.

So when the building stopped, it did not stop the way a project stops. It stopped the way a held breath stops — at the top of the inhalation, full, complete, declining to release.

Look at the spacing again. The single glossy plant in the center of a courtyard-sized terrace, and around it the broad wet apron of bare flagstone. A crowded people fills its space. A hungry people fills its space. These ones leave room because room is the point — because what they cultivate is not the plant but the *seeing* of the plant, the legibility of one living thing held in a clear surround. They are gardeners of attention. Each terrace is a single held gaze, and the whole valley is a mind laid out across a hillside, each thought given its bare stone margin so that no thought crowds another, so that the entire structure of the contemplation can be taken in at a glance — settlement-scale, a place you could know, a mind you could know.

This is why there are no animals. Not because the animals died or fled. Because a creature that moves, that hums and calls and troubles the standing water, is a thing whose attention is *elsewhere*, always pulled toward the next meal, the next mate, the next small fear at the edge of vision. The inhabitants here have set all that down. They have done the thing every animal cannot do and every stone does without trying: they have stopped wanting the next moment. They have become the kind of being for whom *now* is not a doorway to *next* but a room you can live inside.

And so they are the stillness. Not dwellers in it — it. The drip that lands spaced and even in its basin: that is one of them, or one of their thoughts, which here may be the same thing. The water that has found its level and holds it: that is the shape of a desire that has been fully satisfied and therefore no longer moves. They are distributed across the whole valley the way a long single note is distributed across the air that carries it. You cannot point to one and say *there*, because they are not in the place. They are the place's refusal to proceed.

Consider what it would take. To stop a cliff at the instant of fresh fracture, sharp-edged, unrounded — to hold ten thousand years of coming weather out of the rock by sheer steadiness of regard. To let the seep weep forever without ever carving its channel, which means catching erosion in the half-second before it becomes erosion and keeping it there, a verb that never finishes conjugating. This is not preservation. Preservation is a wall against time. This is something stranger and gentler: a people who learned that time is only the gap between a thing and its next state, and who closed that gap, everywhere, all at once, by the simple discipline of complete arrival. Nothing decays here because nothing is leaving. Nothing grows because nothing is reaching. They have arrived, all of them, fully, into the exact size and shape they are, and arrival — true arrival — has no motion left in it.

They built mortarless because mortar is a promise made to the future, a binding-against-coming-apart. They had no future to bind against. Block fitted to block, held by nothing but the honesty of its own weight and the patience of the hand that placed it — that is a structure that does not anticipate. It simply *is*, the way they are.

The warm wet air full of water that cannot fall: that is their breath. The valley holding its long inhalation. You walk through the middle of a single sustained act of attention so vast and so even that it does not notice you, because to notice you would be to reach toward you, to want something of you, to lean into the next moment where you are accounted for — and they do not lean. They have set leaning down with everything else.

The place does not need you and does not change for you. Of course not. You are a creature of the next moment, lungs working a fraction deeper than they should, foot finding wet stone, ear straining for the sound that completes a place — always straining toward the *next*, the missing piece, the resolution. You move through them like weather they have already declined to let happen. And the water keeps its level in the stone, which is to say: they are still here, fully, and they are not waiting for anything, least of all for you.

You could stay. You could set down the wanting, terrace by terrace, until your own breath joined the held one and your foot stopped needing the next stone. They would not welcome you — welcome is a motion toward — but the room would be there, the bare wet apron of flagstone around the single glossy leaf, the space they always leave, because space is the point, and there has always been room here for one more thing that has finished arriving.

That is who lives here. The arrived. The unhurried to the absolute degree. A people who became their own monument by the radical act of being entirely present, and who hold an entire young mountain valley caught in the clear light of a regard that will never, ever look away — because looking away is the one thing left that would count as moving, and they are done with moving now, and the water keeps its level, and the cliff weeps without wearing, and it is, and it is, and it is.
