## The Tenants

You asked who lives here. Watch long enough and you'll stop asking it that way. The wrong question assumes a separation between the residents and the rooms. Here there is none. The city is occupied by the same process that raised it, and the occupants are best understood as *what the water does when it slows down enough to stay*.

But you want them named. So:

**The Drinkers of Echo.** Start with the moss, because the moss is the closest thing this place has to a citizenry — the broad, patient, settled population that gives the town its character and its hush. It is not one thing but a guild of things, dozens of species braided into beds a hand-span deep, and it is the moss that makes the silence. Understand that this is not passive. The moss *wants* the silence the way a throat wants air. It drinks sound the way it drinks water — every drip that lands in it is swallowed whole, never permitted to ring. It has grown thick precisely along the surfaces where sound would otherwise gather and bounce, draping every hard plane that might have made an echo. If you clap your hands in the lower galleries, the sound dies before it leaves your palms. The moss has eaten it. This is the dominant fact of every life down here: nothing that lives in the silent town may be loud, because the loudness has nowhere to go. It is absorbed at the source. The moss is not a plant so much as an enormous distributed appetite for resonance, and everything else has shaped itself to live inside the quiet it maintains.

**The Hangers in Jade.** In the green-still pools, the blind white fish. Do not call them stranded or trapped or sad for their lost eyes; they did not lose anything. Their ancestors put eyes down the way you'd set down a tool you no longer need, somewhere back when the cave was younger even than its young stone. What they have instead is the whole skin of themselves listening — every inch of the body a sense, reading the pressure of the water, the swell of your approach from across a flooded room, the slow drift of a shrimp three tiers down. They hang in the jade-thick water without effort and without hurry because hurry is for things that can be surprised, and nothing surprises them. They knew you were coming before you found the sinkhole. They are not afraid. Fear is a fast emotion and there is no fast anything here. They simply note you, and continue, and wait — patient as the calcite — for you to become, eventually, part of the gravel.

**The Ledge-Draped.** The salamanders, eyeless, the color of wet limestone, frilled at the throat with the one red thing in all this grey-green world — gills like a small blossom of blood worn on the neck. They lie along the ledges so still and so colorless that you read them as features of the stone until one shifts. They are the oldest patient creatures here, in spirit if not in fact; they have refined doing-nothing into a discipline so complete it approaches dignity. A salamander on its ledge may not move for a season. It is not asleep. It is *attending* — to the water, to the cold, to the slow business of being a thing that the cave has decided to keep. When at last it does move — to take a drifting shrimp, to find a wetter shelf — the motion is so smooth and so silent that it seems less like an animal acting than like the rock briefly deciding to flow.

**The Grazers.** The snails, in their unspeakable thousands, are the only sound the town allows itself — and it allows it only because the sound is too small to disturb anything. Press your ear to the moss and you hear them: the collective rasp of ten thousand tiny mouths working the green, a sound like a held breath, like sand poured very slowly, like the town quietly *thinking*. They are the laborers, the unending shift, the ones who keep the moss-beds cropped and turning and alive. There is no night for them to rest in, no day to work by — only the constant grey light and the constant work, generation laid over generation, shell upon shell, until the dead ones are simply part of the masonry too. Much of the calcite "town" is snail, in the end. The houses are built partly of the residents.

**The Ceiling Runners and the Folded Moths.** Things with too many legs run the wet roofs of the galleries, hunting in the perpetual rain. They are quick — the only quick things here — but their quickness is silent, scuttling across moss-hooded stone, and they pay for it by being few and being hungry. And the moths: soft, pale, having forgotten flight, crawling the fern-stems on legs because their wings are now only blankets they wear. They do not need to fly. There is nowhere to fly *to* — the whole world is the same grey-green damp room repeated, and food is wherever you already are. So they walk, and fold their useless beautiful wings around themselves, and are eaten by the ceiling-things, and lay their eggs in the liverwort, and the city goes on.

**The Cloth-Winged.** Bats, you guessed, and you're right, but they are barely bats anymore in any way that matters. They roost in total silence and they move like dropped silk, and they have stopped shrieking the high cries that bats elsewhere use to see — because the moss would eat those cries too, and what is the use of a voice no room will return to you? So they have gone quiet like everything else, navigating the diffuse grey light with eyes that have grown wide and patient, drifting between the columns like the cave's own loose thoughts. They are the nearest thing to *travelers* here, the only residents that pass freely up the sinkhole throats to the marsh above and back. They are the city's one connection to the sky. And they have nothing to say about it.

---

And now the real answer, the one underneath the others.

The tenant of this place is the saturation itself.

You felt it when you stood in the lower gallery and could not find a clear sightline. That is not an accident of the geology. That is the resident, expressing its single law. The thing that lives here is *fullness* — a drive toward occupation that does not distinguish between stone and flesh and leaf, that builds a column and skins it in moss and seeds the moss with snails and drapes the ledge above with salamander and hangs the pool below with blind fish, not as separate acts but as one continuous act of *filling*. Every niche has its column because the place cannot abide a niche. Every column has its moss because it cannot abide a bare column. The horror and the beauty of the drowned town is that there is no vacancy and there has never been any *intention* — no maker decided to fill the rooms. The filling is automatic, a pressure, a tide that rises through everything that can hold it.

This is why you keep reading it as a city when no one built it. A city is the shape that the will-to-occupy takes when it has hands. Here the same will has no hands — only cold, and water, and time, and the slow green appetites — and it has made the same shape anyway. Streets, roofs, stacked flooded plazas, the dense shouldered crowding of a place pressed past its capacity and still pressing. You are not misreading it. You are reading it exactly right. It *is* a city. It simply did not require anyone.

The residents do not speak because there is nothing to negotiate. Speech is for creatures that must persuade each other, coordinate, divide a thing too scarce to share freely. Here nothing is scarce. The water never stops. The light arrives where it has no business arriving. The cold holds everything in its slow grip and asks for nothing back. There is room for everyone because there is room for nothing — the paradox of the place — and so there is no argument to be had, and so the great hush holds, broken only by the rasp of the grazers and, now and then, the deep single splash of a tower grown too heavy for its own being, falling, and the silence sealing over it like skin over a wound that was never really a wound — only the city, putting down something it no longer needed, the way the fish put down their eyes, and continuing.

That is who lives here.

It is the cold, keeping everything slow.
It is the water, never finishing.
It is the green, climbing everything that holds still.
It is the silence, eating every sound before it can become a word.

And it is full. It was always already full, before you came down the throat of pale stone into the grey daylight that should not reach so far. It will be full after. You are, for the brief warm loud duration of your visit, the single thing here that does not belong — the one vacancy, the one creature still carrying its noise around like a lamp it can't put out.

Go quietly. The moss is listening, and it is very hungry, and it has all the time there is.
