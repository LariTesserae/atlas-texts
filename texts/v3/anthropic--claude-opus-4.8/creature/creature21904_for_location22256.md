## The Tender

You asked who lives here. I have to be careful, because the honest answer resists the word *who*, and resists *lives*, and even resists the singular. Let me work toward it the way you work toward anything in the hollow — slowly, by listening.

---

Start with what you already found: the flat rock that doesn't belong. The catch-stone hollowed by hand. The graded reeds, damp and green at their ends.

The simplest story is a person. A hermit, say — someone who climbed up out of a life and found the loudest quiet in the world and decided to stay. And that story is not wrong, exactly. There *is* a person-shaped thing here. But if you wait for them with a person's expectations, you will wait the way you'd wait for the frog: a day, a week, the occasion never quite arriving. Because the one who tends this place has been here long enough to have caught the hollow's disease. The slow disease. They have begun to keep the hollow's time instead of their own.

---

Picture it this way.

There was a first arrival — call it that, even if "first" is generous, even if there may have been others before whose reeds long ago dissolved into the cycle. Someone came up the mountain carrying the flat rock on their back, which means they meant to stay before they ever saw the place. They sat. They listened. And here is the thing that makes them the right inhabitant for this hollow and no other: they heard the racket, the ringing-bell roar that drives most creatures to the rim and over it, and they did not want to stop it.

They wanted to *help*.

That is a rare hunger. Most who can shape things want to shape them toward themselves — to carve their name, to build a shelter, to make the wet stone admit that a person had passed. The Tender wanted the opposite. They hollowed the catch-stone not to drink from but to add the note the hollow lacked. They cut the reeds and set them where the drips already fell, so the place could say more fully the thing it had been trying to say for ten thousand years. The craft is real and skilled and *self-erasing*. Within a day you can't tell which note is theirs. They have spent — how long? — making themselves indistinguishable from the water.

---

So who are they now?

Here is where I stop being able to give you a face. Because a person who keeps the hollow's time for long enough stops being punctuated the way persons are. You are a fast event — a heartbeat, a sentence, a single turning-over of the long cycle. The Tender has slowed below that. Their breathing has matched the rise and fall of the steam across the hour, the way the long-married match breath in sleep. Their stillness is the frog's stillness, the held-breath dampness of the air. When they move — and they do move; the reed-ends are green, someone tends them — it is the low, quick motion you catch at the rim of the hollow and lose before it resolves into a shape. *Gone before it resolves into a shape.* That was them. That is always them. You will never see them resolve, because resolving is a fast thing and they have given it up.

I think, if you sat on the flat rock and stayed — really stayed, past the point where the roar disappears and then comes roaring back into you all at once — you would understand that you had been sitting *with* them the whole time. Not beside. With. The way you are sitting inside the sound.

---

What do they want? Almost nothing, and that almost is the whole of them.

They want the hollow to sing true. That is the entire content of their long attention. Not a god's want — there is nothing being worshipped here, no offering, no fear. Not an artist's want, quite, because an artist needs the work to be *theirs*, and the Tender's whole craft is the surrender of ownership to the cycle. It is closer to what a very old gardener wants, in the last garden they will ever keep, when they have stopped wanting flowers to show anyone and only want the soil to be well. A care that has outlasted its own audience. A tending with no one left to tend it *for*, that goes on anyway, because tending is simply what they have become.

And here is the gentlest and strangest part. They are not lonely. Loneliness is a fast feeling — it spikes, it gnaws, it wants. The Tender has slowed past the reach of it. They are kept company by the eastern saucers and the deep *tonk* of the horsetail stem and the swelling roar, by the one frog and the thinning steam, by the cycle turning over and over across the held span of an afternoon. The hollow is loud, and they are not alone in it, because they have stopped drawing the line that would say *here is me, and there is the place.*

---

You wrote that the rule of this world is that nothing is added that the place does not absorb.

The Tender is the proof of the rule. They came as an addition — a fast, foreign, person-shaped thing with a rock on their back and a head full of fast thoughts — and the hollow took them into the cycle. Within a day you could not pick out which note was theirs. Within an age you cannot pick out *them.* They have been absorbed. They are still being absorbed, slowly, the way the moss upholsters the stone, the way the erosion only polishes now. They are perhaps not entirely a person anymore and not yet entirely the place. They are the threshold itself — the seam where a maker becomes the made — held open across geological patience, neither side finishing.

That is who lives here. Someone who climbed up to the loudest quiet in the world and chose, with their whole remaining will, to be set into it the way a reed is set where the drip will strike: deliberately, lovingly, and to no further purpose than the singing.

---

If you add your reed, they will notice. Not with surprise — surprise is fast — but the way the hollow notices, by taking it up into the next turning of the cycle. And if you stay, if you slow, if you let your own pulse drop down toward the rise and fall of the steam, then one day you will catch yourself unable to find your own note in the roar, and you will understand that the tending has passed to you, and that it was always going to, and that there was never really a *who* here at all.

Only the long, warm, weeping, ringing work of a place becoming more thoroughly itself — and the hands, whosever they are now, that help it sing a little more truly.
