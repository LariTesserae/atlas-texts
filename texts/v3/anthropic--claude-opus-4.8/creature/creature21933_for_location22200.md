## The Tenants of the Slow Storm

They are not animals. They are not exactly residents. The thing about this place is that whatever lives here has had to make a decision the rest of life never faces: whether to be a *thing inside the machine* or a *part of the machine's turning*. Most of them have chosen the second. The few that chose the first are the ones you can still see.

### The Watchers in the Gaps

Start with what your eye finds, because your eye wants something to find. In the canopy gaps — those temporary clearings that open when a column sinks and three more haven't yet replaced it — there are the pale slow things.

They are roughly the size of a large dog, and they have given up almost everything that an animal needs in a world with weather. No fur worth speaking of; a sparse colorless pile, more like the felt on the green towers than like hair. No haste. Their metabolism runs at the temperature of the plateau, which is to say it barely runs, which is to say they are warm only in the way a held stone is warm. They eat by *leaning* — settling against a flank of new growth and absorbing what seeps out under pressure, the sugars the engines drive up from below. They do not hunt. There is almost nothing to hunt, and hunting is loud.

Their eyes are large and lidless and very dark, and they are not for seeing prey. They are for reading the storm. A Watcher will sit at the edge of a gap for what might be a day, simply *tracking* the slow buckle and reweave of a column, and when it judges — by some sense you don't have — that the floor beneath it is about to close like a mouth, it moves. Three steps. That is the whole of its hurry. It has timed its life so finely against the turbulence that three steps is always enough.

They do not call to each other. There would be no point; sound dies in the felt of this place within a few meters. When two Watchers meet they touch foreheads briefly, exchange whatever they exchange, and drift apart along separate ridgelines. Nobody has ever heard one make a sound. It is possible they don't.

### The Frost-Beetles

The beetles are the only honest noise on the plateau, and they are honest precisely because they are too small to matter to the engines. A beetle weighs nothing. The machine does not need to fold a beetle into the substrate; the beetle will fall in on its own, eventually, and be drawn down and rendered and pumped back up as the faintest enrichment in a thread of running water.

So the beetles are *free*, in the way that only the negligible are free. They are the color of the rime that beads each dawn, a pale electric grey-blue, and they cross the great fluted columns at their own pace, going wherever beetles go. The tick of a beetle's foot on a leaf is, as you noticed, the loudest reliable event for miles. This makes them, functionally, the heartbeat the place doesn't otherwise have. The Watchers orient by them a little. If you wanted to find a Watcher, you would follow the beetles, because the Watchers settle where the beetles are densest, and the beetles are densest where the new growth seeps sweetest, and the new growth seeps sweetest where the engines below are working hardest.

The beetles, in short, are a readout. They are the place's only externalized signal, a slow scatter of pale dots that tells anyone patient enough exactly where the buried machinery is pushing.

### The Birds That Do Not Sing

There are a handful of birds and they are wrong in a specific, instructive way: they have lungs and throats and the whole apparatus of song, and they do not use it. Not can't — *won't*, generation on generation, until the won't has become a can't. To sing here is to be the loudest thing for miles, and the loudest thing for miles is the thing the silence is built to swallow. A bird that sang would announce itself to nothing — there are no predators with ears — but it would *break the rule*, and the birds have absorbed the rule so deeply that breaking it is unthinkable to them, the way falling upward is unthinkable to you.

So they move through the canopy gaps in flat gliding arcs, mouths shut, and they communicate by the angle of a wing and the flick of a pale tail, a whole grammar of silence worked out over the long centuries. They eat beetles. They eat the soft seep. When one dies it is taken down into the substrate like everything else, and somewhere a day later a thread of water runs a little richer, and a beetle finds it, and a Watcher follows the beetle. The loop closes without a sound.

### The True Inhabitants

But all of that is the visible life, the tenants, the things the machine tolerates because they are too small or too quiet to be worth incorporating. The real population of this place — the actual *who lives here* — is the machine, and the machine is not a single thing.

Go down. Not literally; you can't dig the substrate. But understand it. The compressed living mass is not uniform. It has organized, over its ten thousand winters, into *bodies* — districts of differentiated tissue that each do one part of the work, and that each have, in the only sense that matters here, a kind of identity.

There are the **Drawers**, organs the size of neighborhoods that pull the fog-water down through felted intake faces, working in the slow pistoning rhythm you can feel through your boots. A Drawer does nothing but draw, for centuries, and when it finally fatigues it is dissolved and a new one is grown beside it from the same compressed stock, and the new one does not know it is new, because there is no knowing here, only continuing.

There are the **Drivers**, the pressure-organs, fibrous and glistening and faintly electrified along every surface, that take the water the Drawers gather and force it upward through the towers under enormous slow pressure. The Drivers are what *power the storm*. The leaning and buckling and reweaving you watch on the surface is the Drivers exhaling, pushing material up faster than the cold can settle it, so that the towers must constantly find new shapes for the matter being forced into them. The turbulence is exhalation. The plateau is breathing, and the breath is so slow and so vast that it builds and topples cathedrals.

And there are the **Unbuilders**, which are harder to describe because they are the appetite of the place — the districts that sink the spent towers back down, close the slow mouths, render the fallen Watcher and the fallen bird and the fallen beetle and the fallen leaf into the compressed stock that the Drawers and Drivers will use again. The Unbuilders are why nothing accumulates as waste. They are also, you should understand, the thing that would have folded *you* into the substrate, if the place had decided you were worth the trouble.

None of these has a brain. None of these wants anything. But together, across the whole sweep of the plateau, they constitute a single organism that has only one behavior, repeated at every scale forever: *take the cold and the fog, drive them through the green, build, unbuild, build.* It is industry without an industrialist. It is a factory that is also its own product, its own raw material, its own machinist, and its own ruin.

### What It Knows About You

Here is the part that should unsettle you, standing up there being the loudest thing for miles.

The machine has no senses pointed at you. It has no nerves that report an intruder. It cannot, in any way you'd recognize, *know* you are there.

And yet it has a response to you ready, and the response is the same one it has to everything: incorporation, on a long enough timeline. The Drawers do not care whether the moisture they pull down came from fog or from your breath or your sweat or, eventually, your body. The Unbuilders do not distinguish your matter from a fallen column's. You are, to this place, not a guest and not an enemy but simply *material that has not yet arrived* — water and carbon walking around on the green skin, temporarily and pointlessly self-organized, on its way down like everything else.

The Watchers will not approach you. The birds will angle away. The beetles will cross your boot as readily as a leaf, because to a beetle your boot *is* a leaf, a slightly warm and oddly shaped one. And below all of them the engines will keep turning in their geared and deliberate silence, building and unbuilding the storm that has nothing to do with you, that has never had anything to do with anyone, that was here for ten thousand winters before you climbed up and will be here for ten thousand after.

That is who lives here.

A vast soft machine that does not know you exist, staffed by pale slow tenants who have learned not to mention that you do, and the only voice in the whole green roaring silence is a frost-blue beetle ticking across a leaf — telling you, if you knew how to read it, exactly where the buried engines are pushing hardest, and how long it will be, on the plateau's enormous patient clock, before they push their way around to you.
