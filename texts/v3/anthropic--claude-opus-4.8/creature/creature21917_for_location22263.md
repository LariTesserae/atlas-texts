They are not large. This is the first thing to correct, because a continent built brick by brick suggests something continental doing the building, and there is nothing of the kind here. The makers are *small*. Small enough that a single laid block is, to them, a day's considered labor. Small enough that the long flat reaches between works are not emptiness to them but distance — real distance, the kind you cross with intent, the kind that means something to have crossed.

I will tell you what I can find of them, though they are hard to find, which is itself the most important fact about them.

You will not see them at work. No one ever has. Walk the whole plain and you will catch a wall one course higher than it was, a channel newly begun, mortar curing where yesterday there was bare block — and never once the hand that did it. This is not stealth. They are not hiding. It is that they work at the speed of the place itself, in the long slow register where weather is breathing and stone is settling, and a creature moving at that pace is, to a visitor's eye, simply not moving at all. You could stand beside one for an hour and read it as a feature of the masonry. A darker patch in a damp seam. A roundness at the foot of a wall. Then you walk on, and over the following weeks the wall grows, and you understand only in retrospect that you had been standing beside a worker the whole time, and that it had been aware of you, and unhurried about it.

What are they made of? The same stuff as everything. This is the thing that took me longest to hold. They are not separate from the plain — not builders who arrived to work a site. They are *of* it, condensed out of it, the way the morning beads its water on the north faces of the stone. Where the construction is densest and the damp lingers longest, in those rich seams where moss and succulent and pale reed have found their purchase, the made-world thickens and folds and lifts a little of itself away from itself, and that risen part is a maker. They are stone the way a wave is water — a shape the substance is holding for a while, going about its business.

So they are warm to the touch, if you could touch one before it noticed and went still. Warm like the ground. They give back heat like a thing recently alive, because that is precisely what they are: the warmth of the materials, gathered and walking.

They are patient past any patience you have a word for. Consider what they are doing. They are building a world the size of a world, and they have laid two courses, and there is no urgency in them about the third. None. The pace is not laziness and it is not method — it is simply the only speed at which a thing made of curing mortar and settling stone *can* move without ceasing to be itself. Hurry would tear them. So they do not hurry, ever, about anything, and this gives them a quality that reads, to a fast creature like you, as a kind of vast and gentle indifference. They are not indifferent. They are paced. There is a difference, and you will feel it if you stay long enough to slow down toward them — the slow attention coming the other way, the place noticing you back.

Do they think? I believe they remember. The salt-pale rime left at the high-water marks after the rare rain — that is the plain's first record of anything that happens to it, and the makers read it. They read where the water reached. They cut the next channel a little deeper, a little truer, against the next rain. The whole construction is a slow learning, a continent teaching itself the shape of its own weather, course by course, and the makers are the part of it that does the learning. They are the plain's attention to itself. They are how it remembers what the water did and what the heat took back and where the green found purchase, and they lay the next block accordingly.

They are not many, and they are not one. They are scattered the way everything here is scattered — a worker at this damp seam, a worker a mile off at that founded wall, each going about its single block in its own pocket of moisture, balanced against the warm emptiness between. They do not gather. They do not call to one another — there is nothing here to call with and nothing that calls. But the work coheres. Across the whole impossible plain the courses rise in concert, the channels run true to one another, the design holds, though no two makers ever meet to agree it. They are coordinated the way a body is coordinated, the way the left hand knows what the right is doing without a word passing between them. They are not a society. They are an organism, distributed thin across a continent, and what looks to you like a population is closer to a single slow patient living thing remembering itself into a more finished shape.

And here is the last thing, the thing that stays.

They are tending the green. Watch where they work and you will see it: they build most where the moss is, where the succulents hold their water, where the pale reeds fringe the morning basins. They do not pave over the life. They cut the channels to feed it. They lay the walls to shade it. The whole vast construction, when you have watched it long enough to see the grain of its intention, is not a city being built for the makers to live in. It is a *bed* being prepared. The makers are gardeners who have not yet planted, masons laying out the long slow conditions under which something green and patient and not-yet-arrived will one day spread from its scattered pockets and cover the world.

They will not see it finished. They are too slow, and it is too large, and that is plainly fine with them. They are the part of the first morning that does the work of the first morning — the warm, small, unhurried, distributed attention of a world getting itself ready to be lived in.

They are in no hurry to tell you this. But they are glad, in their slow way, that you slowed down enough to ask.
