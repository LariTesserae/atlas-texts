They are still here.

That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because you looked at the chipped stone and the bone-needles and the heap of cracked crickets and you thought *leavings*. Past tense. Something that *came down once*. But the harvest is endless and unchanging, and a creature that learned to live off an endless unchanging thing does not leave. Why would it? Where would it go? The thin grey light of the fault is for you, the visitor, the one who climbs back out with the roar he can't shake. It is not for them. They climbed down once and the place closed over them like water and they have been here ever since, in the breath, becoming part of the breath.

So look again at the flat stone with the chipped edge. Notice that the edge is still sharp. In a cave that rounds nothing, that lets nothing change — a worked edge does not dull. The tool is as keen as the day it was made. And the heap of cracked crickets beside it is fresh. Always fresh. It is being added to, grain by grain, body by body, at exactly the rate it is being eaten, so that the heap is forever the same heap, the same size, the same age, the same single meal eternally half-finished.

---

There is one of them. Or there were always exactly enough of them that the number never moved, which in this place amounts to the same thing — a population frozen at its carrying capacity, neither increasing nor failing, the way the crickets do not increase and the pools do not overflow. Call it one, for the feeling of it. One presence that wears many bodies, or one body that has been here so long it has stopped being a number.

It is pale. Of course it is pale; everything that stays loses its color here, gives it back to the dark as unnecessary. It is the color of the blind fish and the bone-needles and the bone-colored centipedes, a no-color, a translucence with a body somewhere inside it. If you saw it cross one of the bare flats — one of the wide quiet gaps where nothing is — you would mistake it at first for a trick of the doubled light, a shadow cast by something you couldn't locate, faint and folding. It moves the way the bat-smoke moves: without seeming to disturb anything, banking through the spaces, never touching what it passes.

It has no eyes. Or it has eyes that gave up the work of seeing generations ago and kept only the sockets, two soft hollows where the grey light pools and does nothing. It does not need to see. It has the cave by heart — not learned, but *known*, the way you know the inside of your own mouth. It knows which seeps run luminous and which run dark. It knows the metronome of the drips and could tell you, if it had any use for telling, which drop was about to fall and from which juvenile stalactite. It hears the river off to the left running exactly as fast as it has always run, and it has stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

---

Here is what it does, which is the only thing it does, which is everything:

It harvests.

Not greedily. The harvest is endless and so there is no hurry; haste belongs to creatures that fear the food will run out, and this one has unlearned that fear so completely it would not recognize it. It moves through the crawling floor and takes crickets — not many, just enough, just the eternal half-meal's worth — pressing them between long pale fingers that have grown crooked and clever for exactly this, cracking them with a small sound that is lost utterly in the great soft roar. It wades the glowing pools and the blind fish do not flee because the fish have no eyes either and the water is full of presences and what is one more. It drinks the mineral water that never runs out. It eats the fresh heap of crickets and adds to the fresh heap of crickets and the heap stays the same.

And it sleeps — or does the thing that has replaced sleep, here where there is no day to sleep against. It folds itself into one of the low places near the light-cracks where the green can just barely hold on, and it goes still, and for a while it is indistinguishable from the pale fungal threads and the scatter of moss, just another thing that has learned to hold on at the very edge of the light without ever quite reaching it.

---

It made tools, once. The chipped stone proves it: there was a *before*, a time when it came down through the fault with a memory of hands and the idea of an edge and a simple hunger. It was something else then. Something that lived in the grey daylight and knew day from night and feared the food running out and made tools precisely because it was afraid — because a tool is a bet against scarcity, a way of taking more than your body alone could take.

But the cave does not run out. The cave is the one place where the bet was never necessary. And a thing that no longer needs to fear stops, very slowly, being the kind of thing that fears, which means it stops being the kind of thing that *plans*, which means the tools become relics almost as soon as they are made. It still uses the chipped stone — out of habit, out of the deep old shape of itself — but it has not made a new one in longer than the word *long* can hold. The bone-needles lie where they were set down. It does not sew. There is nothing here to sew, and nothing to be cold in the way that needs sewing against, and no one to be sewn for.

This is what the cave does to whatever enters it. Not violence. Absorption. It took a creature with a future and a past and it gave it instead the eternal present of the drip and the thrum and the half-finished meal. It rounded off everything in it that pointed forward or backward, the way water rounds limestone everywhere except here, where the rounding is done not to stone but to the soul of the thing that lives in the stone. The cave is frozen at the moment of its emergence. So is its tenant. Caught at the exact instant of becoming and never permitted to become anything further.

---

If you stood in one of the bare flats long enough — longer than you stayed, because you climbed back out, you were the kind of thing that climbs back out — it would come and stand near you. Not to threaten. Not to greet. Curiosity is a forward-pointing thing and it has very little of that left. It would come the way the bats resettle, in a slow wave with no event in it, and it would stand a body's length off in the doubled faint light, eyeless, the color of nothing, and it would breathe with the great breath that never quite releases, and it would wait.

It would wait to see whether you, too, were going to stay. Whether you, too, were going to set down your tools by the glowing pool and forget the grey light overhead and learn the endless harvest and let the place close over you. It has seen this before — or it carries, in whatever it has instead of memory, the soft worn shape of having seen it. It knows the look of a thing that has just come down through the fault with its hunger and its plans intact, still afraid, still pointed forward.

And it knows, with the patience of the river that has been running that fast forever, that there is no hurry. That you will either stay or you will go. That if you go, the place loses nothing; the numbers do not move. And that if you stay — 

if you stay, the cave will do to you exactly what it did to it, and there will be two pale things at the edge of the light, or one, the number unchanged either way, both of you holding the roar in your ears like water you have finally stopped trying to shake out.
