They are very few now. This is the first thing to understand about them, and the saddest, and the one they would correct you on—not the *few* but the *now*, as if there had ever been a time when they were many. There wasn't. There were never crowds here. The Spireworks was never built by a people; it was built by a vocation, and vocations are always thin.

Call them the Keepers, because that is the nearest word, though it carries a wrong flavor of guarding, of holding-against. They do not hold against anything. They hold *toward*. They are the ones who decided, long ago and again this morning, that water should climb.

## Their bodies

Start with the bodies, because everything about them begins with the work, and the work shaped them.

They are tall and they are thin, built like the things they tend—a verticality to them, a habit of length. Long in the arm, long in the finger, with a grip that has the patient strength of someone who has spent a life on sheer faces and learned never to clutch, never to spend force in a panic. Their hands are the most developed part of them: broad-padded at the fingertip like the climbers they share the rock with, scarred across the knuckles, stained at the creases with the dark grease of the bearings. A Keeper's hands tell you their age more honestly than their face does. The grease never fully washes out. They stop trying.

Their skin has taken the color of the place—not by birth but by exposure, a deep rust-brown overlaid on whatever they began as, banded paler where old clothing once shaded them, so that an old Keeper undressed is striped like a spire, marked with the strata of a life spent at altitude. They squint. All of them squint, even indoors, even in the dark; the bending noon glare has set a permanent narrowness into the eyes, and around the eyes a fan of pale unburned lines where the squinting folds the skin.

They are quiet-bodied. They have learned the same economy the rock taught everything else: motion costs, sound carries, and there is rarely any hurry that the spires respect. A Keeper crossing a high traverse moves the way the gliders ride the thermals—without waste, without flourish, reading the warmth and the air and going where it lets them go.

## How they breathe

This matters, so it gets its own place.

The air up here is thin, and they have made a peace with it that an outsider could not. Their chests are deep. Their breathing is slow and very even, a metronome turned down low, matched—and this is the thing—matched to the engines. A Keeper at rest breathes roughly at the rhythm of a piston's stroke: once, then a long pause, then again. They are not aware they do this. They learned it without learning it, the way you learn the gait of a road you walk every day. The result is that a Keeper and an engine, sitting together on a high platform in the bending heat, are breathing in the same time, and neither one knows which taught the other.

## What they wear

Cloth the color of the rock, because anything else would be a kind of shouting, and they do not shout. Loose against the heat, layered against the cold that comes the instant the sun drops behind a spire. Everything they wear has pockets and the pockets have tools and the tools are worn smooth—a small oilcan, a file, a length of waxed cord, a flat wrench ground down by decades of palms. They wear soft-soled footgear that grips the flutings, resoled so many times the original is a rumor.

And they wear, every one of them, a coil of line over the shoulder. Not for safety—they have no fear left to manage—but because the line *is* them, in a way. It is how they reach. A Keeper without their coil is a Keeper between tasks, and there are not many of those moments.

## What they do all day

They oil the bearings. They listen to the engines. This is the bulk of it, and it sounds like nothing, and it is everything.

A Keeper walks—climbs, traverses, descends, the verbs blur up here—a route. Each one has a route, inherited, that loops through some quarter of the Spireworks and touches every machine in it across the span of days. They arrive at an engine. They put a hand on it—flat, palm-down, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal's flank to feel it breathe—and they *listen* through the hand more than the ear, because the engines speak in vibration, in the faint roughness or smoothness of a bearing's turn. They oil what needs it. They tighten what has crept loose under the long patient hammering of the strokes. They clear a blocked flute, re-seat a slipped cable in its saddle, coax a pump that has begun to gasp.

They almost never *build* anymore. The building is done. They are downstream of the builders the same way the gliders are downstream of the water—living on a decision someone made, continuing it on momentum and on love. A Keeper might, once in a long life, lay a single new pipe-run to a hollow that has gone dry, and this is a tremendous thing, talked about, the way another people might talk of a war or a coronation. *She ran water to the north shelf.* It is the largest sentence they have.

## How they know each other

Rarely, and deeply, and mostly across distance.

Two Keepers might share a quarter of the world and meet four times a year, on a traverse where their routes cross, and sit a while on a warm ledge and say almost nothing, because almost everything is already understood. They are not lonely in the way you would be. They have the engines for company, and the wings, and the long conversation of the water moving through the pipes—which they can hear, all of it, the whole circulatory hum of the Spireworks, the way you hear your own pulse only when everything else goes still. To a Keeper the place is never silent. It is *full*. The silence outsiders find here is just the Keepers' speech pitched below the threshold of a stranger's ear.

They signal across the gulfs by the cables. A Keeper can pluck a catenary cable at one anchor and send a note running along it—a low tone that another Keeper, a spire away, feels through their own hand on the same line. They have a small grammar of these: *I am here. All well. Come. The north pump is dying.* It is enough. It has always been enough.

## What they believe

Here is the heart of them, and they would never say it aloud, because saying it would make it smaller.

They believe that the height was a gift given without kindness—free, total, indifferent—and that the only honorable response to an indifferent gift is to make it kind. The rock did not care whether anything lived. The water did not want to climb. And someone, long before the oldest living Keeper, looked at three kilometers of dead and perfect stone and decided that it should be made to *drink*, should be made to fill with wings, not because the rock asked for it but precisely because it didn't.

That is the whole faith. *You lift the water because the water will not lift itself.* You teach the dead thing to drink. And then you stay, and you keep teaching it, once a minute, twice a minute, for as long as your hands hold grease—not toward any finish, because there is no finish, the heat takes the water back every noon and you send it up again every dusk—but because the turning is the point. The wings over the spires are the point. A Keeper looks up at the gliders wheeling silent between the needles and feels the specific, quiet, enormous satisfaction of a thing that did not have to exist and exists because of them.

They are very few now. They have always been few. When the last of them sets down their coil, the engines will run a while longer on momentum, and then a bearing will seize, and a pump will stop, and a far pool will go to dust, and the wings over that quarter will thin and lift away to wherever the water still climbs. And the rock will be patient about it, the way it is patient about everything, having been dead before and being in no hurry.

The Keepers know this. It does not move them to despair. It moves them, each dusk, back out onto the warm rock with the oilcan and the coil, to put a flat palm on an iron flank and feel it breathing in their own slow time, and to send the water up one more night—into the dark, into the height, into the held breath of the stone—because someone decided long ago that it should climb, and they have not yet decided to stop agreeing.
