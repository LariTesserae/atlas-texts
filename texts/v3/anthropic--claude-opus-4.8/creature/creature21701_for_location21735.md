They are not many. They are one.

This is the first correction, the one that takes longest to settle into, because everything about the place argues against it. The swarming, the uncountable multitude, the ten thousand backs catching sun — all of that says *many*, says *colony*, says *swarm*, and so you reach for the words of bees or termites or the dense civic life of ants. But those words are wrong here, and their wrongness is the whole truth of the place. The swimming things are not citizens. They are not even creatures, exactly, not as separate selves. They are the moving parts of a single body that has spread itself thin across a basin and learned to live as weather lives, as a tide lives — distributed, decentralized, present everywhere and concentrated nowhere.

Call it the Wader, for lack. Though it does not wade. It *is* the water's worth of motion. It is the town and the building of the town and the swimming-things that build it, all one organism, the way your hand and the gesture your hand makes and the thought behind the gesture are one event wearing three names.

Consider what it must be, to be built this way.

It has no head. There is no chamber where it keeps itself, no throne-room cell, no buried queen pulsing out the orders. The intelligence — and there is intelligence, you cannot wade through that ceaseless purposeful building and doubt it — is smeared across the whole shallow sheet, held in the *relation* between the small bodies rather than in any one of them. Each grain-sized swimmer carries almost nothing: an impulse, a tropism, a single dumb rule about fibre and seam and where the light falls too hard. But laid against one another, ten thousand near-nothings, the rules interfere and reinforce and cancel, and out of that interference rises a knowing. The town thinks the way a sentence means — not in any letter, but in the standing pattern the letters make. Disturb the pattern and the meaning is still there, because the meaning was never *in* the pieces. You could scoop a double-handful of the swimming things out of a flooded lane and the lane would not be wounded. It would simply be that much less itself for a moment, and then the seep would bring more, and the thought would close over the gap the way the bodies close over your shins.

So: it cannot be killed by killing any part. This is not toughness. It is a different relationship to death than yours. Where you are a fortress and death is a breach, it is a melody and death is a wrong note, swallowed and corrected before the bar is out.

Now consider time, because the town's time is not your time.

You move at the speed of a single large animal, slow and serial, one foot then the other, one thought then the next. The Wader moves at the speed of all its bodies at once, ten thousand small parallel labors, and so what is a frantic blur to you — the lane that runs north at dawn and east by ten — is to it a leisurely shift of posture. A man turning over in his sleep. The town remakes itself constantly because constant remaking is simply how it holds still; it is the equilibrium of a thing too distributed to ever fully stop, the way your standing body is never actually motionless but trembles and corrects, trembles and corrects, a thousand tiny muscular arguments holding you upright. The town is upright in exactly that way. The endless building is not industry. It is *balance*. It is the organism standing.

And it has been standing a very, very long time.

Here is the thing about the cold and the white merciless light and the still water that does not flow. These are not the conditions the Wader endures. These are the conditions it requires, and more than requires — has *made*, over a span you should not try to feel the size of. The springs that weep up through every seam: it opened those, body by body, packing and gnawing the rock until the basin sweated on schedule. The shallowness of the sheet, ankle-deep and even — that is engineered, a depth chosen, because a deeper water would let the bodies stratify and lose touch with one another, and a thought needs its letters close. The whole basin is a single instrument the Wader has been tuning since before the peaks went smooth. It did not move into this place. It *grew* the place, the way coral grows a reef, except that here the reef and the polyp and the sea are not three things but one slow enormous self, breathing through stone, drinking itself up from below.

What does it want? Wrong question, but the only one you have, so.

It does not hunt; the springs feed it, the warmth-loving grey-green mats feed it, it is closer to a plant in its appetites than to a beast. It does not flee; nothing here is large enough to be a predator, and time has scoured away whatever once was. It does not reproduce in any way you would recognize — there is no second town over the next worn shoulder of rock, no daughter-swarm sent off to colonize a farther basin. It is the only one, and being the only one, it has nothing to do with all its distributed cleverness except the one thing you have watched it doing: maintain itself. Hold the balance. Tend the springs, weave the fibre, open the doorway and close it, keep the light from striking too hard on too thin a sheet. It is an intelligence whose entire vast project is *continuance*. Not growth, not conquest, not understanding. Only the next moment, and the next, identical and ceaseless, the held note of a thing that long ago solved the only problem it will ever have and now simply goes on solving it, forever, perfectly, in silence.

This is why the silence. You thought the cold had frozen sound. But the cold is its making, and the stillness is its posture, and the silence — the silence is what a thought sounds like from the inside. There is no need for it to call across distances, because there are no distances within it; every part touches every part through the connecting water, the medium that is also the message. It has never once needed to make a sound, in all its ages, and so it never learned how, and so it doesn't.

And the scale. The chest-high rooftops, the doorways you must stoop through, the city's density in a hamlet's footprint, the town that feels built for a slightly smaller, more numerous people. Of course it does. It *was* built for a smaller, more numerous people. The forearm-long swimmers are the largest of its parts; the grain-sized ones the most numerous; for them a doorway is a cathedral arch. You are the wrong size for this place because you are the wrong *kind* of thing entirely — singular where it is plural, fast where it is patient, loud where it is silent, dying where it is merely correcting. The town does not regard you. It cannot. You are too far below its resolution to be a thought it could think — a single warm large slow object, lit from every side, throwing its blue ghost of a shadow, parting the bodies and watching them close.

It feels you, of course. It feels you the way your skin feels a fly that has landed — a local disturbance, a thing to flow around. The bodies part at your shins and close behind you and the building goes on, north-lane to east-lane, doorway to doorway, the held note never wavering. You are weather to it. You are a cold front passing through. And when you have gone, and the kiss of your displaced water has stilled, it will not remember you, because it does not remember anything, because it has never needed to: it has only ever needed *now*, and now is always exactly like this, and it has been now for longer than there has been anyone at all to wade in and wonder who, or what, is home.

You are home. You are standing inside it. It is all around your legs, and it does not know you are there, and it never will.
