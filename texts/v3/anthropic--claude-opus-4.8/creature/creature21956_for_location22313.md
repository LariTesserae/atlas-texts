They are a patient people, and you would know it before you knew anything else about them, because patience is written into the slope of their shoulders and the way they hold a tool — loosely, as if there is no hurry, because there is no hurry, because the work is longer than they are and they have made their peace with that.

Begin with their hands, the way you began with the ceiling, because that is what you notice first. The hands are the most finished part of them. The faces are mild and ordinary, but the hands have been worked the way the cave is worked, slowly, by repetition, into something specific. The pads of the fingers are smoothed and slightly splayed. There is a callus particular to the holding of a chisel, another to the laying of a cord, a third along the heel of the palm from the thousand small presses that test whether a fitted stone will hold. Old masons here are read by their hands the way trees are read by their rings. You can see in a grandmother's grip the whole arc of a life given to fitting one stone against another, and the giving was not a sacrifice. It was the shape the life took.

They are not large. There is no advantage to largeness in a place where the passages are barely wide enough to pass, and so over the long settling of generations they have become a people scaled to their doorways — compact, low-centered, sure-footed on wet stone, able to turn sideways through a gap without thinking about it. Their movements have an economy that reads, to a surface eye, as grace, but it is not grace exactly. It is the absence of waste. Nobody here makes a large gesture, because there is nowhere to make one, and so they have forgotten how, and do not miss it.

Their skin holds the cave's own palette — not pale the way the cave-creatures are pale, the blind soft-footed things at the seep-pools, but the muted warm brown of the rammed earth they build with, dimmed a half-shade by a life lived under borrowed light. The sun has never touched them. The light that touches them has been handed down through smoothed flues, surface to depth, mirror to mirror, and something of that arrives in their faces: an evenness, a lack of harsh shadow, the look of people lit from no single direction. They have never squinted. They have never cast a long shadow across a field. They do not, as a rule, know what to do with darkness, and they do not need to, because there is so little of it where they live, only the dimness up near the unfinished vault where the lamplight gives out.

Now the thing that took you a moment to see in the cave, and takes a moment to see in them: that they are unfinished too, and at work, and have arranged their entire way of being around accretion rather than erosion. They do not tear down. The word for demolition has fallen out of their speech, or never entered it. What they have instead is a vocabulary of addition — to extend, to fit onto, to raise above, to lean against. A wall is never wrong; it is only not yet completed by the next wall. A house is the sum of everyone who has added to it. When a family needs more room they do not clear ground, because there is no ground to clear; they build onto the roof of the room below, which is to say they build onto a neighbor's ceiling, and this is not an imposition but the ordinary commerce of the place, a courtesy exchanged downward and upward through the storeys. Your floor is someone's shelter. Your roof is someone's floor. To live here is to be held up by the people beneath you and to hold up the people above, and they feel this literally, as weight and counterweight, and they feel it the other way too, as the only kind of belonging they have ever needed.

They keep an etiquette, and the deepest article of it concerns the wet seams — the places where the stone is still deciding its shape, still depositing itself drop by patient drop. You do not build where the cave is still building. You leave the growing stone alone. A child learns this before it learns its numbers: that the difference between what you may shape and what you must not is the difference between stone that has finished arriving and stone that is still on its way. They will route a passage the long way around to spare a single seam. They will leave a column of flowstone rising through three storeys of a house, the rooms built carefully around its slow thickening, the family living for generations alongside a thing that grows a hair's width in a lifetime — and they tend it the way you might tend a slow honored relative who will outlast you, dusting it never, touching it never, only making room. This is not worship, quite. It is more like manners extended to the one neighbor older and slower than all the rest. The cave is building. They are building. They have agreed not to get in each other's way.

So they keep time differently. They have the small clock of a day — the brightening and dimming of the handed-down light, the cycle of work and rest and the thin held woodsmoke of the cooking-hours. But laid under that they carry a second, enormous, almost geological sense of duration, because they live among evidence of it. They can stand a child beside a draping of flowstone and say, your great-grandmother stood beside this when it was a hand shorter, and the child understands, dimly, that it is being shown the speed of the world. They begin works they will not finish. A mason will square the lower courses of a stair knowing the stair will be topped out by someone not yet born, and will carve into the lowest step nothing so vain as a name, only a small mark that means continued — a notch that says to the future-hand, here is where I left off, here is true, carry on from here. The whole town is threaded with these handoffs. The town is a conversation between people who will never meet, conducted in fitted stone, and it is unhurried because the cave is unhurried, and it is unfinished because finishing was never the point.

They speak little and softly, because sound carries in the hush and a loud voice is a kind of trespass, spilling into rooms not your own. Their language has gone quiet to match the place — much of it is conducted in the hands, in the lift of a chin, in where exactly you set down a tool. A dropped pebble would carry. They have arranged, across many lifetimes, to be the kind of people who do not drop pebbles. Their gatherings are murmured. Their grief is murmured. Their joy is a thing of close faces and small warm sounds, and it does not need to be larger, because everyone they love is within arm's reach in a town packed wall to wall, and there is no distance here to shout across.

What they grow, they grow deliberately and prize accordingly — the pale ferns and lank mosses in their stone troughs under the brightest flues, the few hardy crops crowded into the pools of best light, tended with a closeness that the abundance of any surface field would make absurd. A handful of green is a wealth here. A leaf is an event. They are not hungry, exactly — the rivulet feeds them, the slow troughs feed them, the kept soft-footed pale animals give what little they give — but they have never known surplus, and so they have never learned waste, and they regard the careless as a surface eye might regard a vandal. Everything is used. Everything is handed on. The bronze bowl that came down through six generations is not an heirloom in the way a surface people would mean it; it is simply a bowl that has not yet finished being useful, and when its turn comes it will be handed on again, and the handing-on is the whole of what they mean by lasting.

And so the last thing to understand about them is the thing hardest to see, because it does not look like anything — it looks like ordinary people at ordinary work, masons cutting and fitting and raising in a cool, bright, crowded quiet. But what they are, underneath the work, is a people who have decided to live at the cave's pace and have been changed by the decision. They do not expect to see the end of things. They are not troubled by this. They have made a town that is a kind of slow growing creature, accreting itself drop by drop, course by course, life by handed-on life, and they tend it and add to it and lean it against itself, and they will be old before it is done, and it will not be done, and the cave above them, thickening by a hair through all their generations, will not notice them at all.

They have made their peace with not being noticed. That, more than the small bodies or the smoothed hands or the murmured speech, is what they are: the inhabitants of a thing larger and slower than themselves, building in good faith alongside it, in the patient certainty that the work matters precisely because it will not be finished — that to add one true course and mark it *continued* and hand it on is the entire and sufficient meaning of a life lived under a sky it cannot see.
