They are called, among themselves, by a word that is not spoken but made — a flat clap of two wet palms, because no voice could survive here. You would render it as **the Quiet People**, and the joke of that name is the first thing they would teach you, if they taught you anything.

## What They Are

They are not many. A place this loud cannot feed many, and they know it, and they have known it for longer than they can count, which is why their number does not change. There are never more of them than the flock can spare, and never fewer than the weirs require. They keep this balance the way the stone keeps its flatness — without effort, because every other shape has already been worn away.

They are small and pale, the women and the men alike, the color of the weeping rock, as if the country had decided to fold a little of itself upright and send it walking. Their skin is always wet; they have stopped distinguishing between the inside of the body and the outside of the air. Their feet are wide and webbed at the root of the toes, splayed for the standing water, hardened beneath into a sole that reads the bars by feel — they walk the submerged stone in the dim and never stumble, because the soles of their feet have memorized the whole geography that their eyes cannot find in the doubled light.

They are deaf. Not born so — *made* so, every one of them, by the wall of sound that surrounds them from the first breath. The roar took their hearing in childhood, gently, completely, and they do not mourn it because they have never known its absence to be a loss. They live inside the scream the way a fish lives inside the river, and they feel it the way you felt it: in the bones of the face, in the teeth, in the palm pressed to weeping rock. They read the flock's roar as pressure and rhythm. They know the hour by the texture of the noise. They know a storm is coming because the wall of sound goes thin and strange before the weather turns. The deafness is not silence. The deafness is a deeper, fuller listening, conducted through the skull.

## How They Speak

With hands, of course — but not the way you imagine. Their hands are too busy for delicate signs; their hands are always working, gutting, stacking, mending. So the language migrated. They speak with the whole body, with the **set of the shoulders**, the **angle of the spine**, the long slow language of *posture*, which can be read across a flat from a great distance through mist that would swallow any smaller gesture. To stand a certain way is to say a certain thing. A child learns to read a parent's stance before it learns to walk the bars. Whole conversations pass between two figures a hundred paces apart, who never come closer, who simply lean and turn and hold, like reeds, like the stakes of the old weirs taking the current.

And they touch. When they are near, they press — forehead to forehead, palm to sternum — and speak through the bone-conduction of contact, vibrating words into one another by humming in throats they cannot hear. They are a people of enormous physical intimacy and almost no privacy, because the only true secret here is the thought you keep from touch.

## What They Believe

They do not believe the water will win. They believe the water *has already won*, long ago, and that everything since — the birds, the swimmers, themselves — is the afterlife of a country that already died. This does not frighten them. It is restful. They are the ghosts of the mountains, walking the smoothed-down grave of stone, and a ghost has nothing left to fear. The fresh stone on the cairn is not a marker of life persisting. It is a courtesy paid to the dead by the dead — a way of saying, *we are still here, in the manner of the still-here, which is to say: continuing.*

The cairns are not graves and not shrines. They are **counters**. Each is a tally kept by one person across a lifetime — a stone added at each turning of the flock, each thinning, each return. To read a cairn is to read a life in the only writing the country permits: stacked, patient, wet on the underside. When a person dies, their cairn is not knocked down. It is simply not added to. The flats are dotted with the finished cairns of the finished dead, weathering at exactly the rate of the living stone, until you cannot tell whose tally is still being kept and whose has stopped. This is the point. The dead and the living are stacked from the same rock and worn by the same water and there is, finally, no difference worth marking.

## How They Live

They come and go with the flock. When the screaming birds are thick on the bars, the Quiet People withdraw to the hut-platforms at the flats' margins, because there is no room and no need; the flock feeds itself, and to harvest then is greed, and greed unbalances the count. They wait. They mend cord. They press their foreheads together and hum the long postural epics of who stood where, and what the water did, across more turnings than your kind has had cities.

When the flock thins — and it thins on a rhythm older than memory, the swimmers ebbing, the birds dispersing to wherever birds go — *then* the Quiet People walk out onto the open stone. They take the swimmers in soft nets of fiber they grow from the drifting paper-mats, the only crop this country offers. They take the eggs the flock has abandoned. They take only into the gap the flock has left, never into its abundance, because the abundance is not theirs and the gap is the country's gift.

And they go barefoot and bare-handed and almost without tools, because the wet eats metal and the country has taught them that to own much is to spend your whole life keeping it from dissolving. Better to own little. Better to own a cairn, which the water cannot steal because the water gave it. Better to own the stance of your mother, held in your own spine. Better to own the rhythm of the roar against the bones of your face.

## What You Would See

If you came at the right hour — the flock thinning, the pewter light at its lowest — you might see one of them far out on a submerged bar, standing still in water to the ankle, alone, a small pale upright shape where you swore there was nothing. You would not hear them, of course. No one hears anyone here. But you might see the posture: shoulders squared, head tipped a precise few degrees, weight settled into the soles that read the stone.

And if one of *them* could read *you* — could see, across the mist, the way you stand with your hands over your ears, flinching at a roar they have never once heard as noise — they would understand at once what you are. Not a stranger. Not a threat. Just a creature that has not yet been worn smooth. A thing still loud inside, still arguing, still mistaking the screaming for something other than the sound the living make while the stone lies down.

They would add a stone to a cairn, perhaps, to mark that they had seen you.

And then they would go back to the swimmers, in the gap the flock had left, in the dim, in the wet, in the place that finished becoming itself a long time ago and asks nothing further of anyone — least of all to be afraid.
