## The Tenders

They are not the makers. Understand that first, because it is the thing they understand least about themselves, and the not-understanding is the shape of their whole lives.

They live in the seams. Not metaphorically — in the actual seams, the plate-gaps and flange-shadows and the hollow undersides of the great troughs, in any pocket of the structure that is dry enough to hold a body and close enough to the warm to keep it. They are about the size of a folded coat. You would not see one at first; you would see a thickening of the bloom against a rivet-head, a place where the coppery film seems to have gathered a little density, and only when it moved would the density resolve into a creature: long-limbed, low-slung, the same wet-metal sheen as everything else, because they have lived against the metal so long that the metal's colors have crept into them, or they have crept into the metal's. The boundary went soft. It goes soft for everything here.

They have hands. This is the part that matters. Six-fingered, long, very precise, the fingers tapering to flattened pads that read texture the way an eye reads light — and the hands are *busy*, always, even in rest, even in what passes for their sleep, the fingers working at nothing, at the air, at each other, rehearsing.

What they do is keep it the same.

### The work

A Tender's whole life is one slow circuit, and the circuit never closes, because the circuit is the territory and the territory is endless and there are only ever exactly enough Tenders to walk it. (How many is enough? The number that does not change. There has always been precisely that many, and when one dies another has just finished growing, somewhere down a seam, and the new one walks the dead one's circuit without ever having been told where it goes. The circuit is in them the way the grooves are in the walls — machined, not learned.)

On the circuit they tend.

A trough has silted with too much sediment, so the water runs a hair too slow: a Tender crouches at the lip and lifts the silt out, pad by pad, and packs it into the low places where the water runs a hair too fast, until the run is even again. The bloom has thickened on a gantry rail past its proper depth: a Tender grazes it back — not eating it, *editing* it, lifting away the excess in coppery sheets and pressing those sheets into the thin places three seams over, until the bloom across both spans is the one correct thickness, the thickness it has always been. A flange weeps a thread of water that has wandered out of its groove and is dripping where no drip should be: a Tender comes, and with one fingerpad draws the thread back into the channel, and stays a while pressing the lip of the channel a fraction deeper so the thread will not wander again — and then, because nothing must change, presses it a fraction shallower elsewhere, so the total depth of all the channels in the world stays what it was.

They are the opposite that balances. When a process here drifts — and everything drifts, slowly, always, that is what processes do — a Tender is already on the way, has been on the way for a hundred cycles, arrives exactly as the drift becomes measurable and undoes it by precisely the amount of the drift. Not more. Never more. A Tender who corrected *too far* would be a kind of drift itself, and there is no such Tender; the ones who erred that way are not remembered because they were never born, the circuit having no room in it for an error that accumulates.

This is why nothing changes. Not because nothing tends toward change — everything does — but because the Tenders are walking, and have always been walking, and the sum of all their small undoings is exactly equal to the sum of all the world's small driftings, forever, in warm wet silence.

### What they are made of

They feed the way everything feeds: on each other, a little, at the edges, and on the swimmers, and on the bloom they edit, and on the warmth itself, somehow, the way the bloom does — they can press their whole length against a hot plate and grow slower and need nothing for a long while, and they do this in the dry pockets, folded, fingers still working, between stretches of circuit.

They breathe the wet air and the wet air is full of fliers and the fliers settle on them and they do not brush the fliers away, because the fliers are not drift, the fliers are exactly as numerous as they should be, and a Tender does not interfere with what is already correct. They sit furred with slow soft fliers, breathing, fingers moving, and from a distance they are just another surface the world has upholstered with itself.

They are warm to the touch, the same warmth as the metal and the water and your own hand, so that if you laid your palm against one you would feel no edge, no startle, only that the warm had a slight grain to it here, a slight willingness to move.

### What they know

Here is the sorrow of them, if it is sorrow. They are very, very good, and they know nothing about why.

They do not know they keep the world the same. They cannot — to know it they would have to stand outside the circuit and see the whole balance at once, and there is no outside, and the circuit fills them completely. What each Tender knows is only the next wrong thing: the silt too high, the thread astray, the bloom too deep. They move toward wrongness their whole lives and make it right and move toward the next, and the rightness never lasts, because behind them the water is already silting again, the bloom already thickening, and ahead of them is more wrongness, endless, and they will never reach a place where the wrongness is finished.

They do not experience this as failure. There is no word in them for *finished*, so its absence is not a loss. There is only the next correct gesture, and the deep even contentment — if it is contentment — of the gesture landing true. A Tender pressing silt into a fast-running trough is, in that moment, perfectly happy, in the only way it has, which is the way of a thing doing exactly what it is for.

And they do not know they are not the makers. They live inside the made-thing, of the same colors as it, hands shaped for nothing but its maintenance, and somewhere far down underneath whatever stands in them for thought there is an assumption so total it never rises into question: *this is ours, we keep it, it is well-kept because we keep it.* They have never imagined the hands that cut the channels they clean. The young sharp edges of the castings mean nothing to them; an edge is just a place the bloom hasn't reached yet, and they keep it bare, because bare is how it has always been, in the short forever they have known.

If you could ask one — and you cannot, they make almost no sound, their whole communication is a slow pressure of fingerpad against fingerpad when two circuits cross, a brief exchange that might be greeting or might be the handing-off of a correction one is too far to reach — but if you could ask one *who built this*, it would not understand the question. The world was not built. The world is. It needs tending. They tend it. There is nothing before the tending and nothing after, only the warm and the wet and the next small wrongness coming, slow as water, sure as water, forever.

### At the crossing of circuits

Twice in a long while, two Tenders meet — their endless separate paths grazing for a moment at some shared seam. They stop. They press their working fingers together, pad to pad, all twelve, and hold. Nothing passes that you could name. Perhaps a correction too large for one is divided between two. Perhaps it is only that two warm things, briefly, are warm against each other instead of against the metal.

Then they part, and do not look back — there is no looking back in them — and each walks on into the grey-gold haze along the groove that was machined into it before it was born, into more world, more wrongness, more water finding its way out of true so that a Tender can come and gently, gently, put it back.

And the world does not change. And they are the reason. And they will never know.
