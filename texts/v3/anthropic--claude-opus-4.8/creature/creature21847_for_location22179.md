# The Ones Who Set the Reeds

Start with the hands, because they told you about the hands. *The long memory of hands that have done nothing else.* Begin there and let the rest follow.

They are not large people. You don't grow large in the cool and the wet on the food that grows here — finger-fish, the slow snails, the bitter green film scraped and pressed and eaten without much pleasure, the few rooftop flowers that mean a season's turn more than a meal. They are spare. Compact. Built like the stone they live in, in flat layered economy, nothing wasted, nothing rounded that doesn't need to be. Their bodies have the same quality as their walls: fitted by long use to break only along the grain.

Look at the hands again. The fingers are broad at the tip and the nails are worn down nearly to nothing and grow back ridged from the constant wet. The skin of the palm is thick as harness-leather and never fully dries, so it carries always a faint paleness, a softness at the surface over the hardness underneath — the body's own waterline. Every one of them has it. A child of three has the beginnings of it. An elder's hands are pale to the wrist, like something half-drowned that kept reaching up.

## What They Wear and Why

You named the felted reed and the undyed wool, and you were right, but understand what the layering *means* to them. They do not dress against cold. They dress against *wet*, which is a subtler enemy, because wet is patient and gets in everywhere and the only defense is more cloth, looser cloth, cloth that can be peeled and wrung and re-wrapped a dozen times a day. So they are bundled in a way that looks heavy and is actually light — reed-felt over wool over reed-felt, all of it the colors that undyed things are: oatmeal, ash, the grey-green of the reeds, the dun of unbleached fleece. No dyes. Dye needs sun to set and water to spare and they have neither. Color here is an extravagance the rooftops permit themselves for one hour and the people never.

The exception — and there is always one exception, and it always means everything — is the **string.** Each of them wears a cord at the wrist or the throat, and the cord is dyed. Some dim red got from a lichen that grows only on the highest, oldest, sun-touched courses; some blue from a crushed shell. The cord is not decoration. It marks which courses you are responsible for. A red cord sets the reeds on this many roofs; a blue cord tends this stretch of channel. The town's whole labor is parceled out in dyed string. You can read a person's whole life and rank and burden by the cord at their throat, and they read each other this way without speaking, because they don't speak much. The fog eats speech. They have learned to need it less.

## How They Move

Slowly, and never in straight lines, because there are no straight lines — only the seams between the stone and the channels and the drowned stairs you must not step into. They move the way the pale fish move, by long acquaintance with a nervous system of passages, and a stranger set down here would drown in a week not from cold but from a single wrong step into a flooded floor he mistook for a solid one. They do not mistake them. They were born into the map. The map is in the soles of their feet, which are bare more often than not, because bare feet read the wet stone and feel where it is sound and where it is only a film over a drop. Shoes lie. They go barefoot where it matters and keep the reed-pattens for the colder hours.

They are quiet movers. Sound carries strangely in the fog and they have a horror — though they would not call it that — of being loud. A loud person is a person who does not yet understand the place. The young are loud; you can hear a child somewhere being too loud, a quick splash and a cry, and then the soft tamp of an adult hand and the child learning, again, to fold itself down into the town's hush.

## What They Believe, If You Can Call It That

Here is the thing that emerges if you watch them long enough, past the work, into the cool blue evening.

They do not think they are winning.

This is important and it is not despair. They know — it is the deepest knowing they have, deeper than the map in their feet — that the town is drowning by inches at its roots, that for every course they stack above, a course below goes dark and full and still. They have always known it. Their grandmothers knew it. The work is not to *defeat* the water. The work is to **stay even with it.** To rise exactly as fast as you sink. To keep the lived-in floor above the waterline by adding to the top precisely what is lost to the bottom — no more, because more is wasted labor and there is no labor to waste; no less, because less is the channels coming up through your floor in the night.

So they are a people whose entire civilization is a held position. A breath held. A coral's patience. They do not dream of dry land, of expanding outward into a meadow, of the fog burning off for good. Those are not dreams to them; they are *misunderstandings.* The wet is not a problem to be solved. The wet is the condition of being. You don't solve your own condition. You set the reeds, you re-bed the course, you keep even, and you teach your children to keep even after you, and the keeping-even *is the life* — not a means to a life, but the thing itself, complete, the way a held note is complete and does not need to resolve.

## The Drowned Below and the Ones Who Tend It

Now — the stairs that go down into clear still water. You said they know which ones. You said they do not go down them.

That is almost true. There is one who does.

Not for ritual, not exactly, and not for any reason they would phrase as belief. But somebody, in each generation, takes the dyed cord that is darkest — a cord so deeply soaked in the lichen-red that it has gone nearly black, the color of the well at the center — and that person's parcel of responsibility is *the drowned floors.* The going-down. They descend the stairs that stop at a mirror of water, and they go in, into the cold clear dark below the living town, where the fish circle, where the green film grows on stone nobody has touched in a generation, where the old courses sit in their permanent flood exactly as they were left, doorways and gutters and hearths gone under, a whole lower town held in still water like a thing in glass.

What do they do down there? They *count.* They measure the rise of the dark water against the courses, and they come back up, and they tell — quietly, to the ones with the reed-cords — how fast the bottom is drowning this season. Whether to stack faster. Whether to slow. The whole keeping-even depends on this one person who goes into the place everyone else won't, and reads the rate of loss, and carries it back up into the light.

They are not feared, this person. But they are not quite touched, either. They smell of the deep water. Their hands are palest of all, pale past the wrist, pale to the elbow. They have looked at the thing the town keeps its back to. And when they grow too old to descend, they choose the next one — often a child who was too quiet, who didn't mind the dark, who stood too long looking down a forbidden stair — and they tie the near-black cord on that child's wrist themselves, and say almost nothing, because there is almost nothing that can be said about it.

## And the Well

You asked, without asking, about the eye in the center. The reflection that holds too much sky.

They do not look at it. You noticed that. It is not a rule that anyone made; it is older than rules, the way not-staring-at-the-sun is older than being told. The water of the whole town leans inward to that still round mouth and gathers there and does not overflow and does not drain, and they have decided — without deciding — that this is *where the wet is allowed to be itself.* Everywhere else they fight it, course by course, reed by reed. But there, in the center, they have ceded it a home. A truce written in one round stone-rimmed surface. *You may be wholly water here, in this one place, if you will be only town-water everywhere else.*

The drowned-floor tender is the only one who will look into it long. They have to. They are the one who has been down where the well goes — for the well does go somewhere, the water does leave, just not anywhere the others want to know about — down to wherever the too-deep, too-blue reflection is reflecting *from.*

That is who lives here.

A spare, quiet, layered people with waterlines on their bodies and dyed string at their throats, who do not dream of dry land because dry land is a misunderstanding, who keep even with their own slow drowning as a complete and finished way of being, and who send one of their own — palest-handed, near-black-corded, half-touched — down into the still clear dark each generation to read the rate of loss and carry it back up, so the rest can go on setting the reeds in the grey unshadowed morning, blooming for one hour on their rooftops, and keeping their backs, gently, to the eye in the middle that holds more sky than it should.
