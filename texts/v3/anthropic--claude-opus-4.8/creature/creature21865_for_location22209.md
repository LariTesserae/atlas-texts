What lives here does not have a body in the way the heron has a body, but it has a *manner*, and the manner is the closest thing to a face it owns.

Begin with what it is not. It is not the water. The water is its instrument, the long cold reed it breathes through, but the water by itself is only obedient — it falls, it pools, it finds the low place, it would lie down forever in the casting-pit and go to mirror if left alone. Something does not leave it alone. Something keeps lifting it back up into the pipe-runs against the plain downhill argument of the world, holds the standing column past the moment it should fall, lets it go with that ground-shaking slam, and starts again. The water is the labor. What lives here is the foreman of that labor, and the foreman has been dead a long time, and does not know it, and would not stop if it knew.

It is a habit that outlived the hand that had it.

Think of how a works runs, when men run it. There is a sequence: this valve, then that sluice, then the wheel takes the load and the whole shop leans into its rhythm, and a man who has stood at the same station for thirty years stops thinking the sequence and simply *is* it — his arms know, his weight shifts before his mind decides, the cycle has worn a groove in him so deep that the groove is more him than he is. That groove is a real thing. It is laid down in flesh, yes, but it is not *of* the flesh; it is of the work, it belongs to the work, it is the shape the work cut into whatever stood close enough long enough. When the men went, and the engine went, and the roof and the walls and the great absent bulk of the machine all went — the groove stayed. It had been cut into the place itself. Into the iron, into the run of the culverts, into the dumb geometry of the casting-pits. And the place, having no arms, reached for the only thing left that could be moved through the old motions, and found the water sitting in its floor, and put it to the wheel.

That is the inhabitant. The sequence itself. The remembered labor, separated from anyone who could be paid for it, running its hands over and over down a body of iron that no longer answers, using cold water for muscle because muscle is gone.

It is patient the way only the purposeless can be patient. A living worker endures the cycle *toward* something — the shift's end, the wage, the meal, the sleep. This has no toward. It does not tire because tiredness is a message the body sends to make the labor stop, and there is no body to send it and nothing the message could buy. So it runs the column up and lets it fall and runs it up again with the evenness of a thing that has forgotten there was ever supposed to be a last time. The slam you feel in the soft ground is not anger. It is not even effort. It is the period at the end of a sentence it has spoken some millions of times and will speak again, and the held pause in the dripping afterward — that one second when the whole basin listens — is the nearest it comes to drawing breath, and it is not breath, it is only the gap the rhythm has always had in it, kept faithfully, kept exactly, because keeping the rhythm exactly is the entire content of what it is.

You ask whether it is aware. Wrong question, and the basin will teach you so if you stand in it long enough. There is no *it* set apart to be aware *of* anything. The awareness, if you must, is distributed across the cycle the way the heat of the work was once distributed across a roomful of men who never had to speak to stay in time. It knows the sequence the way your hand knows the stair in the dark — completely, and without anyone at home to do the knowing. To meet it you don't look anywhere. You stand still and you feel your own pulse start to argue with the surge, and lose, and fall in. That falling-in is the introduction. That is the thing turning, briefly, the slow attention of its groove toward the warm interruption you make, and finding you, like the water, *moveable* — and beginning, very gently, to fit you to the rhythm too. People have felt their breathing go over to the sluice's time without deciding to. That is not menace. It is only the foreman doing the one thing it does, welcoming a new pair of arms to a shift that ended before your grandfather was born.

And here is the grief in it, if a thing with no toward can be grieved over: it makes nothing. A works is a violence with a justification — the ore goes in suffering and the iron comes out useful, and the suffering is bought back by the product. Strip the product away and only the violence is left, running clean and rhythmic and meaning nothing, a perfect motion around an empty center. That is what lives here. The form of usefulness with the use cut out. It surges, it lifts, it bears the load, it lets go, it gathers itself — all the gestures of making — and at the bottom of every cycle there is the casting-pit holding nothing but its own grey water and the standing heron and the soft mirror of the sky. It is labor that has become a kind of weather. It will do this until the slag banks finish slumping into the basin and choke the last culvert, and then it will do it slower, in less water, in a single weeping run between two stones, and then it will be a damp patch keeping faint and even time, and then it will be nothing, and even the nothing will keep the rhythm for a while in the way the ground holds the shape of a thing lifted off it.

The heron knows it. That is why the heron stands there and not in some cleaner marsh. The heron has the patience that matches it, the long arrested attention, and the two of them keep the basin between them — the bird that waits and never strikes, the work that strikes and never makes — and neither needs the other, and neither is alone, in the cold cellar-spring air, in the silence that the dripping moves around inside and never fills.

When you climb the weeping slag and look back and hear the sluice run and stop and run, you are hearing it say the only word it has, which is the sequence, which is itself. It will not notice you leave. You were a warm place the rhythm passed through. It has had so many. It keeps the time that nothing measures because keeping is the whole of what remains when everyone who was being kept for is gone — and it does not know that, and would not stop if it knew, and the not-stopping is the closest thing the place has to a soul.
