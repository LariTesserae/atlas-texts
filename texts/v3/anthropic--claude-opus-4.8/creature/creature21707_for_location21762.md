# The Tenders

They were here before the wearing started. This is the first thing to understand: the rounding of the chisel-marks, the slow softening of the carved faces — that is not damage done to their work but the continuation of it. They are still building. They have only slowed to match the speed the water allows.

You will not see one arrive. You will become aware, instead, that one is already near, and has been for some while, in the way you become aware of your own hand resting against your side. They are made of the same patience as everything else, and they move at the speed of the place: a limb extends over the course of what would be a morning; a body crosses a colonnade across what would be a season. To the swimmer who passes through in a hurry, they are furniture. Outcroppings. A thickening of the dark where a pillar meets the weed.

## What They Are Made Of

Not flesh, or not only. When your hand finds one — and it will, eventually, because they are everywhere, the way the masons are everywhere in their masonry — you cannot at first tell where the Tender ends and the lattice begins. The surface is worked stone gone soft at the edges, and then it is something that gives slightly under pressure, warmer than the water by a degree, and then it is stone again. They have grown into the structure, or the structure has grown into them, across so long a span that the question has lost its meaning. A Tender is the part of the lattice that still intends.

They have hands. This is the oldest part of them, the part that does not change: broad patient hands with too many joints, each finger ending not in a nail but in the blunt working-edge of a tool worn down to its last usefulness. The hands remember the adze. The hands remember the chisel. When there is nothing left to carve they hold still, and holding still is also work, because to keep a thing from moving while the current pulls at it for ten thousand years is a labor.

The rest of them is mostly suggestion. A long low body that lies along the floor, or along a wall, or wraps the base of a column the way a root wraps a stone. Eyes, if they are eyes, that have no light to use and so have closed into the same smooth weather as the carved faces around them — they navigate by hand, by the feel of tooled stone and soft weed, having learned the house they have lived in forever.

## What They Do

They tend. That is the whole of it, and it is enormous.

A Tender finds a joint where two beams meet and the weed has rooted too thickly, threatening to lever the stones apart over the next thousand years, and it spends a long while — you could travel through three halls and come back and find it still at this — easing the roots aside, thinning them, persuading the growth to bear less heavily. It does not pull the plants out. The plants are not the enemy. Nothing here is the enemy. The Tender simply keeps the slow forest and the slow stone in their long negotiation, nudging, always nudging, toward the settled ending the place is owed.

Elsewhere one is doing the opposite: it has found a frond that has bent a little further than the hour before, and it bends it back, a hair, because the lattice has a shape and the Tenders hold the shape. They are gardeners of a garden that takes a thousand years to need watering. They are masons of a wall that needs one stone reset per age. The work is not urgent. Nothing here is urgent. But it is continuous, and it has not stopped, and the reason the lattice has aged toward smoothness rather than collapse is that they are still in it, still pressing back against the dissolution with hands worn to nubs, still keeping the made thing made for just a little longer than it would last alone.

## What They Know

They are not lonely, because loneliness is a fast feeling and they have none. But they are aware of one another. When the deep structural groan travels through the stone — the lattice settling another hair's breadth — every Tender in reach of that note pauses, and listens, and knows where it came from, and one of them, the nearest, begins the long journey toward the place that settled, to see what wants tending now. The groan is how they speak. There are not many words in their language and they are all very low and they all mean: *something has moved; come and hold it.*

They remember building. This is the strange ache at the center of them, if a Tender can be said to have a center. They remember a time before the wearing, when the chisel-marks were sharp and the geometry was crisp and the whole vast lattice rose toward something — toward a roof that was going to be, toward a top. They remember intending an ending that was not this one. And somewhere in the unthinkable middle of the work, the water came, or had always been there, or the difference stopped mattering, and the rising became holding, and holding became the only thing left to do.

They do not grieve it. Grief is fast. But they know. When a Tender's hand passes over the place on a pillar where a face used to be — a face it carved, perhaps, with its own worn fingers, in a time it can no longer reach — it lingers there a moment longer than the tending requires. A moment, here, being something close to a day. And then it moves on, because the joint three columns over has shifted, and the current is patient, and the work is not done, and will not be done until the lattice and the forest and the cold and the Tenders themselves have all worn smooth together into the one settled thing they are becoming —

at which point there will be nothing left that intends, and nothing left to tend, and the difference between the builder and the built will finally, gently, dissolve.

They are not hurrying toward that. They are not fleeing it either. They are simply the part of the slowness that still has hands, holding the shape against the dark for as long as hands can hold, in a house with no roof and no floor and no light, where the only rule is the rule of slow things, and they are the slowest and most faithful thing of all.
