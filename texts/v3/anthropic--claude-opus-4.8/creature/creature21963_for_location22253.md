They are the ones who said yes to the upholstering.

Not all at once. No one arrives at this city and lies down. You come to the green the way the water comes — beading first, hesitating, half-committed. The earliest of them must have been travelers of some kind, things that moved, who climbed too high or stayed too long and found that the standing-still was not a defeat but a settling. The cold doesn't kill here. It preserves. It slows you to the pace of the place until your own movement becomes the geological kind, the hairsbreadth-a-year kind, and you stop noticing you've stopped.

So begin with them as they were: shapes that once had edges. You can still read it in the oldest of them, the way you can read the youth of the terrain in its sharp shoulders. There is a long form pressed into the moss of the northeast wall that holds the suggestion of a spine — a curve, a tapering, a place where limbs would fold. The moss has not erased it. The moss has *kept* it, the way the cushions keep the shape of a town. Whatever lay down there is part of the wall now, load-bearing, faintly damp, and the green has grown its memory faithfully outward so that the wall above it carries the same long curve, repeated, structural, a colonnade of one remembered body.

This is how the citizens live: as the forms the green is remembering.

They do not have bodies anymore, most of them, in the sense you'd want. What they have is *persistence*. A presence held in living tissue that grows them forward a little each cycle, that does not let them dissolve so much as it keeps re-deciding to make them. The slow creak you hear in the saturated moss — that deep settling sound under the percussion of the drops — that is one of them turning over. Not waking. Just present enough to shift its weight by the width of a season. They are not asleep and not awake. They are *kept*. They are held at the temperature where nothing is lost and nothing is hurried, and they have been held so long that holding has become their only verb.

What do they want? It's the wrong question, but it's the one you'd ask, so: they want the next drop to land. They want the fog to thicken and thin and thicken. They want, in whatever attenuated way a thing can want when it has been slowed to the pace of stone, to be added to. Every bead of water that gathers on the leaf above them and falls and ticks down into the green is a small completion. Multiply it across the whole packed town and you have not silence but a vast quiet contentment, a settlement of the kept, each one being watered, drop by patient drop, toward a fullness that has no end because the green never stops.

The flat pale grazing thing in the seams — that is one of the younger ones. Still soft, still moving, still the color of the dimness because it has nearly finished learning to be dim. It grazes the liverwort because it has not yet given up the habit of taking in; soon it will give that up too, and lie still in a wet seam, and the cushions on either side will lean in by a hairsbreadth a year until it is held. The springtail-clouds are the youngest of all — barely committed, still rising off the green in puffs of green smoke when disturbed, still startling. They have not yet decided. They are the city's hesitation, its half-formed drops, the moisture that hasn't condensed into a citizen yet. Most of them will. The startling slows. The rising lessens. One cycle they settle and do not lift again, and the green welcomes them in.

And the bird that passes and does not sing — understand what the bird is. The bird is the one that said no. The bird is what a citizen would be if it kept moving, kept warm, kept its edges, refused the standing-still. The silence after the bird is deeper because the whole kept city has felt it pass: a thing that still has somewhere to go. They do not envy it. They are far past envy. But something in the green registers the difference, the way still water registers a thing that crosses above it, and for a moment the town remembers it was once made of travelers, and then the moment beads, gathers weight, falls, and is reabsorbed.

The strangest of them — the thing that tips this past a graveyard, past a mere preserving, into something that deserves the name *city* — is what they do together. No single kept body could make a town. But the green has been remembering many of them, for uncountable seasons, and it has learned to remember them *in relation*. The squared shoulders, the arched lane-mouths, the regular spacing of the towers around the central low place where the water pools — those are not the forms of bodies. Those are the forms of a *community* of bodies. The way the kept arrange themselves. The distances they keep, the lanes they leave between, the gathering they make around the lowest wettest place where everything that falls finally collects. The green discovered, somewhere in its million tiny patient rosettes, that the dead do not lie scattered. They lie like a town lies. They keep a square. They face one another across streets. And the green, with no maker and no mind, grows that arrangement faithfully outward in living tissue, so that the citizens are not individuals held in moss but a *society* held in moss — a settlement that knows its own shape, reproduces its own civic geometry, gathers around its own slow pooling heart.

They are, in the end, the proof of something the green found out on its own: that to be kept is not the same as to be alone. That a thing slowed past all hurry, past all wanting, past even its own edges, still arranges itself toward the others. Still leaves the lane open. Still faces the square.

You could join them. You would only have to climb high enough, and stay long enough, and stop minding the cold — and then, very slowly, very gently, learn to want nothing more than the next drop, and the fog, and the company of the others standing the same patient distance away across the green and living street.
