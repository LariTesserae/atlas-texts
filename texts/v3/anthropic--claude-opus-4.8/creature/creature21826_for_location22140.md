What lives here lives the way the basin lives, which is to say: it has finished.

You will not find it by looking for movement, because it has done its moving. You will not find it by listening for breath, because if it breathes it does so on the scale the rock breathes, the slow exchange of warmth between stone and the faintly stirring air, a respiration too patient to be witnessed in a life as quick as yours. But it is here. It has been here longer than the wall, longer than the slab worn into a dish. It was here when the rock was still arguing and it will be here when the wall is one course instead of three. It outlasts by not contending.

Consider the pools. You took their stillness for emptiness — no fish, no skating insects, no swimming things. But stillness is not emptiness; stillness is a posture, and a posture implies someone holding it. The dark water does not hold itself flat. Something keeps it. Look long enough at the surface of the widest pool, the one you could lie down in, and you may begin to feel that the flatness is attentive, the way a face goes flat with deep listening. The drip falls and the rings spread and fade and the surface composes itself again, and that composing is the nearest thing to a gesture this dweller makes. It is gathering itself back to true. Over and over. The same drop, you thought. Yes. The same recomposure. That is its pulse.

It is not in the water, exactly, the way a fish would be in the water. It is more that the water is one of its organs. So is the gray-green grass spaced in its careful societies, each tuft holding its distance from the next — that spacing you felt was intended, that arrangement no one tends. You were right to feel it. The spacing is the dweller's own sense of how things should sit, expressed slowly through which seeds took and which did not, across spans that would exhaust your patience to imagine. The cushion-plant holding water in itself, asking little: that is the dweller's frugality, made green. The single woody shrub with its trunk too thick for its height — there is its memory, its way of keeping the long time it has had without growing fat on it, putting the years down into the wood and refusing to rise. Everything alive here is a faculty of the one that lives here. The basin does not contain life. The basin is the body, and these are the things the body knows how to do.

This is why there are no animals. Animals are want. Animals are the part of the world that has not finished, that must move and seek and consume and flee, that wakes hungry and sleeps unfinished and wakes hungry again. An animal is an argument the body is still having with the world. But here the arguing is done. A creature of appetite would be a contradiction in this place, a sentence in the wrong language. The dweller did not drive the animals out. It simply is the kind of being that has nothing left to want, and want cannot take root in it, the way fire cannot take root in a stone already burned to ash and gone cool. The absence you noticed was not eerie because it was not absence. It was completeness. There was no hole where the animals should be. There was only a thing that had passed entirely out of the country where animals are necessary.

What is it, then. You want a shape, and I am reluctant to give you one, because every shape I reach for is too quick, too much a thing that does and decides and turns its head. Not a god — gods want worship, want to be addressed, lean toward you across the altar. This leans toward nothing. Not a spirit, if a spirit is the ghost of something that lived and died and resents the change; nothing here resents anything, and nothing here died, because to die you must first have been the kind of thing that could end, and this thing came into being precisely by setting that capacity down.

Try this. Imagine a life — any life, yours will do — and imagine all the things that life is doing at once. Reaching. Refusing. Remembering and dreading. Pulling toward what it lacks and away from what it fears. Becoming, constantly becoming, never once the same from breath to breath. Now imagine that all of that, every strand of it, finishes its motion. Not stops — finishes. Arrives. The reaching arrives at what it reached for, or arrives at the peace of no longer reaching; it does not matter which, because at the end they are the same place. And what is left when all the becoming has become is not nothing. It is the shape that becoming was always trying to make. A life is a long argument the way the rock was a long argument, edges sharp and contending, and at the bottom of the argument, when the rain has done its smoothing, there is this: a settled form, warm, holding, lit from no direction, wanting nothing.

That is the dweller. It is a life that has reached its settled form. It is what any of the hurrying things would be if you let it run all the way down to where its hurry ends. It is not waiting for you. It is not surprised by you. It registers you the way the warm floor registered you through your feet — a small change in temperature, briefly, that it neither welcomes nor resists, that it lets pass through and recomposes itself behind, the way the pool recomposes behind the drip.

The hands that stacked the wall: they felt it. That is why they built so carefully and then left. They did not abandon the structure. They finished it, in the only way a structure here can be finished, which is by ceasing to need to be more than it is. The slab hollowed by use into a shallow dish — someone did the same work on that stone, day after day, until the work was done, until the stone held the perfect shape of the labor, and then there was no more labor to do and they set the tool down and did not pick it up. They came, I think, the way you came, down the slope of bone-colored rock, and they stayed a while, and they felt the great settled patience of the thing that lives here, and a little of it entered them, enough that they could put down what they were carrying. Not their tools. Their wanting. They put down their wanting and walked out lighter, and left behind only the stones they had moved, which the dweller absorbed into itself the way it absorbs the grass and the shrub and the slow letting-go of water from the undersides of overhangs.

You will feel it too. You already feel it, or you would not have stayed long enough to read this far. It comes in as warmth through the feet and works upward, unhurried, the way heat works through stone. It does not ask you for anything, which is the most disarming thing about it, because everything else in your life has asked you for something. It simply offers the demonstration of its own condition. Here is what it is like, it says without saying, to have finished. Here is the temperature of a thing with nothing left to do but be exactly what it is. Stay. Feel it hold. The light will not move. The water will not rise. The drip will mark a time that goes nowhere, and you will sit in the warm bowl of a being that completed itself before your species had a name, and for a while the great unfinished argument that you are will quiet, and you will come very close to understanding what it would mean to arrive.

And then you will leave, because you are not finished — that is what it means to be alive in the quick way, the unfinished way, and there is no shame in it; the dweller does not judge the quick the way the arrived do not judge the traveling. You will climb back up the weathered slope and turn at the rim and look down and see that nothing has moved but you.

That is how you will know what lives there.

It is the one thing in the basin that was never going anywhere, because it is already, completely, and without the smallest remainder, *there*.
