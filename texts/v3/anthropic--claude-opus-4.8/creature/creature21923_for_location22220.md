What lives here is not one thing pretending to be two. It is two things that have stopped being able to tell themselves apart.

Begin with the question the place won't let you ask cleanly: who built the engine? Because nothing built it. That is the first thing to understand about the inhabitant. It was not assembled — there were no hands, no prior makers, no shed in which the parts were turned. The engine is *grown.* Down in the warm dark of the new mountain there are pistons, yes, and turbines, and the clank of moved mass — but follow any one of them inward and you would find no cast steel, no machined tolerance. You would find that the piston is a muscle that has learned to be a piston. That the cylinder wall is bone laid down in courses, secreted ring by ring the way coral secretes its reef, hardened past the hardness of any living tissue and yet still, faintly, alive at the core. The hiss of released pressure is breath. The turbine-sigh is a long slow lung the size of a valley, drawing the wet air down through the seams and pushing it back, which is why the ferns shudder without wind — they are at the edge of a respiration too large to see as breathing.

The inhabitant is an organism that has invented machinery from the inside, by being patient and enormous and under no obligation to look like anything that ever crawled.

It does not have a body the way you have a body, a thing with an edge and an outside. It is distributed. The roaring core is one organ — call it the heart, though it is more nearly a gut, a place of grinding labor where the mountain's own stone is taken in and broken and rearranged into more of the inhabitant: more bone-cylinder, more muscle-piston, more of the hard cold housing that the green grows on. This is why the rock is new and shedding. The blocks falling at the base of the notch are not erosion. They are spoil. They are what the engine has not yet swallowed, or what it has chewed and spat. The whole peak is fresh because the whole peak is recent — pushed into the sky by the thing growing under it, the way a fingernail pushes out the dead matter ahead of the living bed.

And the green is the rest of it. Not a tenant. Not a guest the engine tolerates. The moss in hand-deep layers, the unnamed liverworts, the ferns packed frond against frond — that is the inhabitant's *skin*, and its mouth, and its method of being patient. In near-darkness, on no sun to speak of, the green should not be able to live at all. It lives because the warmth leaking through the seams is the engine's warmth, and the green has been bred, by the long indifferent work of the thing it is part of, to feed on faint heat and condensed breath and the trace chemistries of broken rock. The plants do for the engine what the engine cannot do for itself: they hold the moisture, they sheathe the cold housing against the killing frost, they soften the violence of the core into something that can persist on a high ridge in permanent cloud. Where the green dies back, the engine chills and labors harder and grinds louder; where the engine warms a seam, the green surges. They are not in balance. Balance is too still a word. They are in a single act, performed at two speeds — the fast wet rush of the leaf and the slow tectonic churn of the gut — and the act is *making more notch.* More mountain. More of itself.

So the inhabitant is the place. That is the unsettling thing you arrive at if you stand there long enough with your hand on the wet stone and the labor coming up through the teeth in your breastbone. There is no creature *in* the notch. The notch is the creature, caught in the middle of growing — the way an embryo is caught in the middle of becoming, all process and no finished form, organs that are still differentiating, a body that has not yet decided where it ends. The two young peaks leaning almost to touching are not geography. They are something closing. Or something opening. You cannot tell which, because it is happening too slowly to have a direction yet, and because the thing doing it has no plan, only a process — the deaf, eyeless, sleepless drive of a living machine that builds because building is the only thing it knows how to be.

It has no eyes because it has never needed to find anything. Everything it requires comes to it: the cloud presses down and gives water, the rock is already in its mouth, the cold is just a pressure to push against. It has no animals in it — no insect, no bird, no scuttling thing — because it has never made the part of itself that moves and hunts and looks. Why would it? It is not going anywhere. It is the slowest, largest, most total kind of life: the kind that does not move *through* a world but slowly *becomes* one, swallowing the matter around it and turning that matter into more of its own laboring substance. A thing like that does not need legs or wings or eyes. It needs only time, and stone, and the trick — which it has long since learned — of breathing wet on its own new walls to keep the green alive that keeps the engine warm that breaks the rock that feeds the green.

If it has anything like awareness, it is not the awareness of a mind. It is the awareness of a process noticing where it has not yet reached. Where it is cold, it grows toward warmth. Where it is bare, it grows green. Where the green has nothing to climb, it makes new rock for the green to climb. This is the whole of its intention, if intention is even the word: *more.* Not more for any end. Just more, the way fire is more, the way a crystal is more, the way anything is more that has found a way to take in the world and lay it down again as itself.

You ask who lives here. Nothing lives here. The living *is* here — undivided, unwitnessed, neither plant nor engine but the one indifferent making that those are only the near and far faces of — and it does not know you have come, and it would not change if you left, and it will go on closing the two peaks toward each other in the dark, breathing, roaring, growing, building the housing of an engine that is also a forest that is also a mountain that is, finally and only, itself, still under construction, with no intention of ever being done.
