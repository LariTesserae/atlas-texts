## The Tenants of the Lee

Begin with the small ones, because they were here first, or near enough.

The pale fish are not one kind. Look long enough and the loose congregation resolves into degrees — there are the broad-bodied, slow ones that hang nearest the housings and seem half-asleep, and there are the narrow quick ones that thread the upper water near the lamps, and there are the very small, the fry, that keep to the weed and dart in panics that have nothing to do with any actual threat. They are all descended, you understand without being told, from a few that fell or swam or were carried in down some intake long ago, and they have been making themselves over to this place ever since. The eyes are the proof of it. Each generation a little smaller, a little more vestigial, because what is an eye for in a room lit by three dying lamps? They steer by the press of water on their flanks. They know the current the way you know the weight of your own arm. When the pumps surge they do not see it coming — they *feel* it, the lateral line reading the swell before it arrives, and they turn into it as one body, a single decision distributed across two hundred animals, and hold there, finning, until the slack returns.

They have no name for the room. They have no name for the surge. But they have the surge in their bodies, woven into the nerve, and that is a kind of knowing deeper than naming.

## The Watchers in the Pipe

The eels are older residents, or carry themselves as if they are.

There is one in particular — there is always one in particular — that holds the largest of the dead flanges, a mouth of rusted iron at the dim end of the long wall, and from it a head protrudes, and the head does not move except to track. It is patient in a way the fish are not. The fish are patient the way a held breath is patient; the eel is patient the way a stone is patient. It waits out the high current entirely, withdrawn, only the blunt snout showing. Then in the slack it comes out long and low along the smoothed ledges, pouring itself over the soft brick, and the crustaceans that did not feel it coming simply stop being there.

It does not hunt often. It does not need to. A body that cool, in water that cool, burns almost nothing. It might eat twice in a span that would see a generation of fish born and die. It is, in its way, the closest thing this room has to memory — the slowest metabolism, the longest single thread of continuous life, a creature that has held the same pipe through more cycles than it could register if it were the sort of thing that registered. It is not. It is a long muscle and a pair of nostrils and an appetite that wakes on a schedule it did not choose. But it has been here. Of the things in this room that are alive, it has been here longest, and something about the stillness of it suggests that it knows this, or would, if knowing were a thing it did.

## The Grazers and the Workers

Down in the mortar-courses, in the soft sweet rot between the bricks, the small economy turns.

The crustaceans are pale and many-jointed and they work the seams without rest, scraping the thin growth that the lamplight permits, passing it inward, mouth-parts going in a blur too fine to follow. They are the ones who make the room livable for the larger things — they convert the unliving into the eaten, the green smear into flesh that an eel can want. They breed in the dark stretches where nothing else bothers to go, vast clouds of them in the cool bare reaches, and they drift in toward the light to feed and are picked off at the edges by the narrow fish, and this is fine, this is the arrangement, there are always more.

The too-many-legged things on the ledges are something else again — flatter, broader, scavengers of the dead, and there is always death enough to keep them. A fish that lost the scramble. A crustacean that strayed. They find it by smell carried on the current, converging from the corners, and they reduce it, and the room takes back what the room had briefly lent out. Nothing is wasted here. Nothing can afford to be. The water carries no surplus; everything that enters the cycle is eaten down to nothing and the nothing is grazed by something smaller still.

## What Grows

The weed is alive but it is barely a tenant — it is more like the walls' own slow exhalation toward the light. It streams flat in the surge and goes limp in the slack and it asks nothing and gives everything: it is the bottom of it all, the thing that takes the tired green glow of the failing lamps and makes substance from it. The cushions of moss in the corners are the same impulse, slower, denser, holding to the only surfaces old enough and soft enough to take a root. They are not many. They are exactly as many as the light allows, and not one frond more.

## The Shape of the Whole

So here is who lives here, finally, if you want it gathered into one sentence:

A single closed congregation of cool slow lives, fish that have forgotten the use of eyes, eels that have forgotten the use of haste, crustaceans that have never needed to remember anything at all, and a thin green skin of weed that turns three dying lamps into the whole foundation of the world — all of them bound into the rhythm of an engine that does not know they are there, that pumps its mechanical tide for no reason any of them could name, and that they have nonetheless taken so deeply into their bodies that the surge and the slack are no longer something that happens *to* them but something they *are*.

They were not put here. They arrived, the way water arrives at a low place — by gravity, by accident, by the simple fact that here was a space and life will fill a space. And having arrived, they made the work theirs. The eel's pipe was meant to carry water; it carries an eel. The lee of the pump was meant to be nothing, a dead zone of slack water; it is a nursery. The room was built to hold and move water and it does, faithfully, forever, and what it actually holds now — what it has been holding all this while in the dim and the cool and the grind — is *them*. The patient pale congregation. The long watcher. The blur of small workers in the soft seams of the brick.

They are at home. That is the whole of it. In the small dark church that is nothing but water and the things water keeps alive, they are exactly, entirely, and without the least anxiety, at home.

And it will be like this tomorrow.
