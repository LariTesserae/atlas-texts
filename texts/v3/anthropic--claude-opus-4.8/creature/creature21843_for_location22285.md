## The People of the Damp

They are a small-lunged people, or they have become one. Generations at this altitude have done their patient work on the body: the chest a little deeper, the fingers a little longer and more deft, the skin pale with that wet-stone pallor of folk who have never needed to be brown against a sun they rarely see. They move slowly, and it is not laziness — it is the economy of thin air, every motion priced and considered. A stranger from the lowlands, arriving breathless and clumsy, would think them grave and unhurried. They are simply *spending carefully*. They have weather-bodies, and weather here does not rush.

Their hands tell the truth of them. Look at any adult's hands and you'll find the same things: the calluses of cable-work and condenser-cleaning, the permanent faint green staining in the creases of the knuckles from a life lived against moss, the wrinkled fingertips of people who are always a little wet. They are makers and menders. Nothing here is thrown away — not a length of brass pipe, not a cracked collection tray, not a worn-through coat. To discard is nearly the same sin as to waste water; both are a refusal of the long patient labor that brought the thing into being.

### How They Dress

In layers of dark wet wool and waxed cloth, hooded against the drip, the outer garments gone dark and shining like the stone walls. But underneath — this is the thing you'd miss at first — the inner layers are often startlingly bright. A scarf the color of marigold. A lining of deep red. Embroidery at the cuff in threads that seem to glow in the gloom. They carry their color *inward*, against the body, where the eye finds it like a lamp in fog. A people who live in silver and green have learned to hoard hue the way they hoard water.

### How They Speak

Quietly. The mist eats sound, so they have stopped fighting it. Conversation here is close-range and low, mouth near ear, and much of it isn't spoken at all — a whole grammar of small gestures, a tilt of the chin toward the condensers, two fingers laid flat to mean *the cloud is thickening, the engines will run, come and work*. They have a hundred words for states of moisture and only a few for distance, because distance is mostly hidden anyway. They name the cloud-tides the way coastal people name the sea: the morning-in, the lift, the long-sit, the white-blind. A child learns to read the sky before it learns its letters, because the sky is the calendar, the clock, and the wage.

They laugh, but softly. They sing, but the songs are slow and they are mostly about water — water coming, water gathered, water held. There is no drinking-song here that celebrates *spilling*. Even their joy is conservative.

### What They Believe

That abundance and scarcity are the same face seen in two lights, and that the whole purpose of a life is to stand in the difference between them — between the wild wet cloud that touches everyone for free and the earned drop in the cistern that is *yours because you gathered it*. This is not quite a religion but it sits where religion would. The cistern is sacred. The condenser is a kind of altar. A child's coming-of-age is marked by the day they are trusted to climb alone to a high collector and bring down a full tray without spilling — proof they understand the line between wet and watered.

They are not greedy. A people obsessed with not-wasting are, strangely, generous: they will give you water freely from the cistern, because to *withhold* what has been gathered is as much a sin as to *waste* what hasn't. What they cannot abide is carelessness. Pour out a cup and walk away from the puddle, and you will see something cold pass over even the kindest face among them.

### The Ones Who Tend the High Slopes

And then there are the few. Every tier has its trades — the cable-keepers, the condenser-wrights, the cistern-wardens, the menders of moss-eaten masonry — but above all the tiers, past the highest lit lane where the green thins to bare wet rock and the cloud never lifts, the work belongs to a different sort of person entirely.

They are chosen young, by signs the rest of the town doesn't fully explain. They tend to be the quiet children, the ones who didn't mind the white-blind days, who would walk uphill into the mist and come back unbothered when others felt the cold press in like a hand. They become the *water-leavers*.

They live nearest the top, in the last houses, where the engine-hum is faintest and the drip is everything. Their work is to fill the high trays — not the condensers, which feed the town, but the open shallow basins set out on the bare upper slopes, the ones that feed nothing the town can name. They carry water *upward*, against all the logic of the place, away from the cisterns, and pour it out where the green gives up. And then they come back down and do not speak much about what they saw, or didn't see, or what the trays look like in the morning — sometimes empty, sometimes not, the water gone in a way that rain and evaporation don't explain.

They are marked by it. A little stranger than the rest, a little more silent, prone to long pauses mid-sentence as though listening to something just past the edge of the cloud. The town treats them with a careful respect that is almost fear and almost love and is mostly *gratitude* — because everyone understands, without saying, that the leaving of the water is the price of something. That the slow green peace of the lower tiers, the safe hum of the engines, the children allowed to climb to the collectors alone — all of it rests on the fact that someone, every day, climbs higher still, and pours out an offering, and comes back down.

What waits up there for its water, they will not describe. But you can learn one thing about it from the way they speak of it — which is the same hushed, close-mouthed, two-fingers-flat way they speak of the cloud itself. Not as a monster. As a *weather*. As something that is simply part of how the world works here, neither cruel nor kind, that must be met and tended and never, ever wasted.

They are a people who drink the sky and answer to the mountain. And the thing on the high slopes — whatever it is — is, in the end, just another of their tides. The biggest one. The one that comes in slowest, and never quite lifts.
