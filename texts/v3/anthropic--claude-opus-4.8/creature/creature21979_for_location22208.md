They are quiet people, and they are quiet in a particular way — not shy, not secretive, but *acoustically considerate*, the way you become considerate of a thing you live inside. They learned, generation on generation, that the city carries sound the way water carries it, cleanly and far, so that a raised voice on the ninth terrace is a private matter made public on the sixth. So they pitched themselves down. They speak in a register a stranger would call confidential and they would call ordinary. A child raised here and taken to a flat loud country at sea level finds it unbearable, finds everyone shouting, and cannot understand how anyone hears anything when everyone is talking at the top of the register all the time, drowning each other, wasting the air.

Begin with their hands, because their hands tell you more than their faces.

Their hands are always slightly wet, and the skin of their hands has adapted to it — not soft and sodden the way yours would go after an hour in the bath, but tanned, a little, by the damp the way other people's skin is tanned by sun: thickened at the heel and along the fingers, faintly green-stained in the lines of the knuckles in the way that brass-handlers are stained, because they touch wet metal a hundred times a day. Every rail, every rope, every door-pull, every cistern-cock is beaded with condensation, and so they have a habit, all of them, of wiping the hand down the thigh before they take hold of anything — a small constant gesture, unconscious, that marks them out instantly to each other and to anyone who has lived here long enough to read it. A visitor reaches for a railing and grips it wet and is surprised. A native has already dried the palm and dried the rail in the same motion. You can spot the newcomer in a week. You can spot the one who has been here forty years from across a terrace by the economy of that single wipe.

They dress for the wet, and they dress in the wet's own colours, not by choice exactly but by surrender. Anything bright fades. Anything pale stains in a season with the long vertical streaks that run down every wall, the rust-tea drip of iron, the green wash of moss-water — so they wear the colours the city has already arrived at: the slate-greys, the bottle and moss greens, the browns of wet brick and the near-blacks of wet stone, the dull orange-ochre that the lamps throw, which is the one warm note in the whole register and which they save for linings and scarves and the inside of a collar, a private warmth turned in toward the body. Their outer cloth is oiled or waxed or simply so densely woven it sheds for an hour before it wets through. They have a hundred words for degrees of damp and no word that means *dry* without qualification — *dry* here means *not yet wet again*, a temporary and slightly suspect condition.

They are sure-footed past the point of being remarkable to themselves. A flatlander walks heel-down, trusting the ground. These people walk with the weight already forward, already reading the surface, because every surface is either a stair or a slope or a wet iron grating or a moss-furred stone that will betray a careless foot — and the drop, always, is real. There are no level streets to speak of. A child here learns to walk *down* before it learns to walk *along*, and the gait it learns is a kind of perpetual controlled descent, knees soft, never quite committing the full weight until the foot has answered back. They do not look at their feet. They have not looked at their feet since they were four. They look out, across, into the moving fog, and their feet manage the city underneath them by a sense that has no name and never rises into thought, the way you breathe.

And they are *vertically* organized, in their bodies and in their society both, in a way that takes a while to see and then cannot be unseen.

Height is wealth here, but not crudely, not the way height is wealth in a flat city where the rich take the top floors for the view. Here height is *light*, and light is the scarce thing, the truly scarce thing, scarcer than water which falls on everyone, scarcer than warmth which the flues carry up and share whether anyone wills it or not. The high terraces get the pewter daylight first and longest; the low streets get a grey rumor reflected down a well-shaft, and live by lamplight at noon. So the people sort themselves by the colour of the light they were raised in. The high people are paler, and their eyes are a little weaker in the dim, and they squint coming down into the deep streets and feel the close walls and the orange lamps as something between cozy and oppressive. The low people are darker about the eye, wider in the pupil, and they move through the deep galleries with a confidence the high people lack, and they go *up* rarely and reluctantly, dazzled, exposed, made small under all that churning sky. A low person come up to the top terrace on an errand stands too close to the inner wall and keeps glancing at the open drop and cannot wait to get back down into the held, lit, enclosed dark where the world has a ceiling. The high and the low marry across the divide and the children come out somewhere in between, light-adapted to a middle band, and so the whole population is graded by light the way a sea is graded by depth, with creatures suited to each layer and uneasy in the others.

They are, almost all of them, in the keeping of the machines, and they keep the machines in return, and this is the largest fact of their lives and the one they think about least.

Because the city cannot feed or drain itself by gravity and muscle alone — it is too tall, too sheer — every person here lives at the mercy of the hoists and the inclined railways and the pumps, and a whole order of people exists to tend them. These are not glamorous. They are not engineers in the proud sense. They are *minders* — they oil and watch and listen, mostly listen, because in a city this quiet a machine announces its sickness by sound long before it announces it by failure, and a good minder knows the voice of her own hoist the way a mother knows her child's cough through a wall. They sit, these minders, in little dripping iron cabins beside the great chain-wheels, in the warm exhalation off the flues, with a flask of something hot and a lamp and a long quiet shift of listening, and they are perhaps the most contented people in the city, because they have one machine to love and its health is entirely their business and the rest of the world's troubles climb past their cabin and do not stop. They develop, many of them, a flat almost expressionless attentiveness, a face that is always half-listening to something behind your shoulder, and it is a little unnerving to talk to them until you understand they are simply keeping one ear on the chain.

The water people are a different order again, lower, literally — they live and work down in the cisterns under the lowest streets, in the cold dark where the whole city's shed atmosphere collects, and they are the strangest of all to the rest, pale as the high people but for the opposite reason, never having seen the light at all, working by lamp and by touch and by the sound of water in stone chambers, and they have a reputation among the upper folk for being slow to speak and slower to trust and uncannily good at telling, by the taste and the smell and the level, what the whole mile-high country above them is doing — whether the fogs have been heavy, whether a squall passed in the night, whether some pipe three terraces up has begun to fail. They read the city the way you read a body by its blood. They come up rarely. When they do they wear smoked glass against the daylight even on a grey day, and the children point.

They are not numerous. That is the thing you feel before you can name it. A city this size, in a flat loud world, would teem; this one *doesn't*, and the not-teeming is part of its air, the same absence as the absent animals. The fog and the verticality and the quiet keep them spread thin, each in their band of light, each in their stretch of dripping gallery, so that you can walk a long way and meet no one and then meet one person and it is, faintly, an event, the way a crow on a chimney is an event. They have learned to make a great deal of a single encounter. A meeting on a stair is not hurried past; it is *had*, fully, in low voices, with the news exchanged and the weather read off the sky together, because the next person may be a quarter-hour's climbing away. They are courteous to a degree that a crowded people would find slow and even false, but it is not false. It is the courtesy of people who cannot afford to waste a human being, there being so few of them, strung out so far, in so much grey.

And they wait. This is the last thing, and the one that turns the whole of them, the way the turbulent sky turns the whole still city.

Because they live in balance — in a city that finished becoming itself five hundred years ago and now only persists, mending and shedding at the same rate — they have, most of them, no expectation that anything will ever be different, and they have made a kind of peace with that, a deep settled peace, the moss-knows-where-it-grows peace of the place itself. They are not striving. There is nowhere up to strive to that is not simply *higher in the same city*; there is no progress, only position, only the band of light you were born to and the small movements within it. So they are patient past the understanding of a striving people. They mend the thing again. They oil the chain again. They wipe the rail and take the hold and descend the wet stair again, the ten-thousandth time, with the same forward-weighted care as the first, and they are not bored, because boredom is a striving emotion, the friction of a person who believes things ought to be moving and finds they are not, and these people do not believe that.

But — and here is the turn — they are *weather-watchers*, every one of them, helplessly, constitutionally, because the one thing in their world that does change, that surges and tears and reforms and lashes and passes, is the sky, and they cannot not watch it. A native standing on a terrace is never quite still; the eyes are always going out to the moving wall of fog, reading it, the way the cistern-people read the water. They know the squalls before the squalls arrive — by a change in the draft through a stairwell, by the way the far slope shows and then ghosts, by some smell of the valley coming up — and there is, in all of them, under the great patience, a small fast bright thread of *appetite for the violence*, a leaning-toward the storm. When the squall finally breaks and the runnels all fill at once and the iron streams and the hoists groan and the smoke flattens sideways, they do not go in. Many of them go *out*, onto the galleries, into the lashing, and stand in their oiled cloth in the one hour of the day that the city is loud, that something is *happening*, that the world is being torn and remade — and they watch it with a still face and a fast heart, the way a calm man watches a fire — and then it passes, and the dripping resumes, and they wipe the rail, and take the hold, and go quietly back down into the grey, fed, somehow, having needed that, the way a body needs salt, a small ration of turbulence to carry them through the long held balance of the days.

That is who lives there. A thin, courteous, sure-footed, soft-spoken people, wet-handed and graded by light, in the keeping of patient machines, strung far apart in a green and weeping dark — finished, like their city, with becoming anything, settled into persistence — and watching the sky, always watching the sky, for the next hour of weather that will let them feel, briefly and at a safe distance, the thing they gave up long ago and have never stopped missing: change.
