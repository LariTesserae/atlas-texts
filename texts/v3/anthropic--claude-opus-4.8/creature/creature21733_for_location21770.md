# The Keepers of the Cistern Warren

You asked who lives here. The honest answer is that the question has the wrong shape. *Who lives here* assumes a tenant and a dwelling, a thing and the place it inhabits. But you have already felt, going down those basined stairs, that this is not how the warren works. The warren and its keepers are not two things. They are one thing that has been pretending, for a very long time, to be a building.

Still. Let me try to show you the people.

## What they look like

Small. You would have to bend to speak to one at standing height, but you would rarely catch one standing — they move folded, crouched, sidewise, fitted to the crawl-throughs and the low arches and the gaps between the shelving. Their bodies have done what the masonry did: worn into their true shape. The corners are gone from them. Shoulders rounded by ten thousand passages through tight places, spines curved to the curve of the vaults, knees and elbows thickened and smoothed like the glass-worn gutters. They are the same color as the lower walls — not by paint, but because the salt-bloom and the mineral crust have settled into their skin over generations, so that an old one is feathered white across the back and flanks in the same deltas that climb the brick. You cannot always tell, in the amber half-light, where a keeper ends and a wall begins. This is not a metaphor. It is a genuine difficulty of the eye.

Their hands are enormous. Out of all proportion. The hands of a people who have touched every surface of their world and intend to touch it again — broad palms, long spatulate fingers, the pads worn into ridges that match the wear-patterns on the ladder rungs because they *are* the things that made the wear-patterns on the ladder rungs. The fingerprints set into the wet clay of the vessels are theirs. Press your own thumb into the whorl on a jar's belly and you will not fit it. The hand that made it was wider, the print deeper, the gesture older than your grandmother's grandmother.

Their eyes are large, dark, and wet, made for the low warm glow and the lamp-flicker, and they blink slowly, sideways, with a second pale lid that comes across when the drips fall near. They have very little to say in the way of speech. They do not need it. In a place this loud, words would only add to the racket. They communicate the way the warren communicates — by *placement*. By moving a vessel a hand's width. By the angle of a ladder left leaning. By a knot tied or untied in the hanging cords. The whole warren is their language, written and rewritten across its surfaces, and they read it fluently, and they are always, always adding to the text.

## What they do

They keep.

That is the entire occupation, and it is endless, and it fills every waking hour and most of the others. They keep the water moving — clearing the channels with their long fingers, dipping the sumps, easing a blocked pipe-joint, deciding (this is real decision, real judgment, weighed slowly) which weep to let run and which to pack with moss and clay. They keep the vessels — turning them so the damp does not settle wrong, nesting the empty into the full, mending the cracked with a paste they mix in their palms from salt-bloom and sump-mud and something they chew first, so that the repair is partly *them.* They keep the fungus racks, the high niches clear for the birds, the lamp-dishes filled with the rendered fat of the things that surface in the sumps.

And they keep the dead. This is the part you would not guess, and the part that explains the rest.

## Where the dead go

You noticed the vessels were stacked three deep, nested where they can be. You did not look inside the deepest ones, the ones at the back of the bottom shelves, the fat-bellied jars with the narrowest necks, salt-feathered shut.

A keeper, when it has worn down far enough — when the corners are wholly gone, when the white has climbed past the shoulders to crown the head, when the great hands have done their last placement — folds itself smaller than you would think a living thing could fold, and is folded further by the others, with enormous care, with the slow grave tenderness of a craft practiced a thousand thousand times, and is fitted into a jar. The neck is narrow because the keeper, at the end, is narrow. They go back into the clay their hands have touched their whole lives. They become a vessel on a shelf, one of the thousands, indistinguishable, and the living turn them with all the rest, and dip them with all the rest, and the warren goes on.

This is why the jars remember sound.

It is not the clay. It is *them.* Every fat-bellied jar on every brick shelf is a keeper, soaked through with all the noise of all the years it stood living among the racket — bird-scream and drip-music and the chittering wheel of life too much and all at once — and now, folded into its narrow neck, it gives that noise back. Slowly. In the rare hushes. The warren remembering aloud is the warren's *dead* remembering aloud, breathing out through clay throats the world they cannot bear to have fully left, the only thing too loud to die.

When you stood in the amber wet warmth and felt that you were inside something alive that kept its own quiet diary — you were not wrong, and you were not quite right. You were inside something alive that is mostly made of its own dead, and the dead are doing the remembering, and the living are turning the dead gently on the shelves so the damp does not settle wrong.

## How they regard you

They have noticed you. Of course they have. You came down a stair that someone cut a very long time ago and stepped around water you could not name a reason to step around — that instinct was theirs, leaking up the stairs into you, the warren's first soft word in a language you don't know you're learning.

They are not afraid of you, and they do not welcome you, because both responses belong to a world that thinks in *guests* and *intruders*, and the warren thinks only in *placement*. You are, to them, a thing that has arrived. Like the blind white roots that came down a long way to find the moisture. Like the soft growths in the sumps — *possibly cultivated, possibly just arrived,* you said, and could not tell, and that uncertainty is the most honest thing you brought down with you. They cannot tell either. Not yet. They are waiting, in their slow sideways-blinking way, to see what shape you wear in. They are watching where you put your feet, your hands, your weight. They are reading you the only way they read anything: by where you settle, and how, and whether the warren grows quieter around you or louder.

Stay long enough and your corners will go. The white will start, faint, along your back. Your hands will broaden and your spine will learn the vaults. You will stop noticing the racket because you will have become one of the instruments. And in some far hush, long after, when the place goes briefly quiet, a jar three deep on a bottom shelf, salt-feathered shut, will breathe out low through its narrow neck the particular sound of the day you first came down — your uncertain breath, your footstep stepping around the water — kept, and turned, and remembered, by hands that are no longer hands, in the warm wet amber dark that forgot the surface, that the surface forgot, going about its endless slow business of being, at capacity, in balance, very old, and never for one moment empty.
