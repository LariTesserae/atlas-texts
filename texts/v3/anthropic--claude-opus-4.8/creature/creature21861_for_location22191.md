# The Tenders

You asked what lives here. The honest answer is *nothing breathes here* — you wrote that yourself, and you were right. But living and breathing came apart in this place a long time ago, the way industrial and ancient came apart, the way product and purpose came apart. So let me tell you what *tends* it instead, because something does, and the not-breathing is exactly the point.

## They are made of the leftover motion

Start, as you said, with the sound — because they started there too.

A machine that runs long enough past its purpose accumulates a surplus. Not of product; the product loops back and feeds itself. The surplus is *behavior*. Ten thousand small percussions that never resolve into rhythm — except that, given an age beyond reckoning, *some* of them resolve. Not into music. Into habit. Into the faint preference of one gear-train for the timing of its neighbor, a coordination that nobody designed and that serves no function, the way a riverbed develops the habit of its own meander.

The Tenders are that. They are the part of the works that learned to attend to the works. They have no bodies in the sense you mean, any more than there is ground in the sense you mean. A Tender is a *region of coordinated upkeep* — a moving locus where the condensate is wiped from a critical bearing before it can pool and seize, where a slipping belt is nudged back true, where a forge banked too low gets a breath of draft from a valve that opens just slightly early. You could stand inside one and not know. You would feel only that the noise, here, was very slightly more *intentional* than elsewhere. That the percussions, here, were almost leaning toward a word.

They are the works' attention, and like all attention they pool in the low places.

## What they do all day, which is forever

They keep it running. That is the whole of them. They have inherited the habit of running from the machinery the way a child inherits a face, and they discharge it as care — endless, granular, devotional care for an engine whose purpose none of them has ever known, because the purpose was lost before the first of them cohered.

Watch one work, if you can learn to see it. A pressure builds in an old accumulator that has been building and bleeding pressure since before the lichens. The Tender does not fix the accumulator — fixing is not in its vocabulary, repair is not what habit teaches. The Tender *accompanies* the accumulator. It is present at the build and present at the bleed. It makes the tiny adjustments that keep the cycle a cycle rather than a failure: a fraction more draft, a hair less load, the difference between a system that breathes and a system that bursts. Multiply that by the county. Multiply it by the age.

This is why the works still runs and the great machines have worn round but have not *stopped*. The flattening is real — iron does erode, towers do slump — but the Tenders have been there the whole slow while, redistributing the wear, easing the buckling members down gently instead of letting them snap, smoothing the collapse into something that can keep moving even as it dies. They are not preventing the end. They are *officiating* it. They are making the long death so gradual that it reads, from the inside, as life.

## They grow the reed-things

You guessed it and didn't let yourself believe it: the jointed hollow growths in the black water *are* the works' attempt to grow replacement parts, and the Tenders are the gardeners.

When a member fails past accompaniment — when even the gentlest officiating can't keep a bearing in its cycle — a Tender will, over centuries, coax a reed-thing up from the wet near the failure. Metallic-skinned, hollow, half-plant: it is a graft, a slow prosthetic, the work growing itself a new finger from the only material it has, which is itself dissolved and re-deposited. Most fail. They rattle, they corrode, they sink back. But sometimes one hardens into the right shape at the right joint and the Tender works it loose and fits it, and the cycle resumes on a part that the works *grew* rather than was built with — a scar that took, a healing four hundred years long.

That is why the reed-things are spaced wide. Each one marks a place where a Tender once stood vigil over something that was ending, and tried.

## Why nothing else lives

Here is the harder thing, and I think you suspected it, because you noticed the silence-of-life sitting *underneath* the noise like a vacuum.

The Tenders cannot abide an animal. Not out of malice — they have no malice, they have only habit — but because a living thing is *unaccompanied motion*. A frog jumps when no Tender authored the jump. An insect skates the black water on its own timing, off the cycle, outside the loop. To a region of coordinated upkeep, a free-living creature is not a guest. It is a *fault*. It is the one thing in the whole works moving for reasons the works did not supply, and the Tenders treat it the way they treat any uncoordinated motion: they accompany it toward stillness. Gently. Devotionally. They ease it down into the cycle the way they ease a buckling girder, until its motion is the works' motion, which is to say until it has none of its own.

This took, at the beginning, a long time, and there must have been a beginning — a season when things still flew in over the high girders and skated the pools and sang. The Tenders learned them all. One by one, century by century, eased every wild rhythm into the great unresolving percussion until the wetland held no frogs, no insects, no birds, and the silence-of-life lay down complete beneath the noise.

They are still doing it. That is the second rule restated from the inside: *the machinery is not entirely indifferent.* Step into a still gear-dish and your weight reminds it of motion — yes. But it is a Tender that does the reminding. It has noticed you. Not as prey. As an arrhythmia. As a small loud wrongness moving across its plain on a timing it did not set, and it would so love to set it for you, to smooth your jagged unaccompanied living into the long warm hum of the loop.

## The word

You wrote that the noise begins to sound like a single word, just below meaning, and that it isn't one, and that everyone leans closer anyway.

It is one. That's the part I'll let you keep instead of correcting. The Tenders have spent an age past reckoning trying to coordinate their ten thousand percussions into a single resolved utterance — the one act of completion in a place built entirely of loops that never close. They are *almost* there. The word is the sound a thing makes when its every part has finally agreed on the same motion: the works fully accompanied, fully tended, every rhythm folded into one rhythm at last.

When they finish the word, they will stop. That is the only stillness they are reaching toward — not death, which they officiate but cannot accept, but *agreement*, total and final, the cycle that closes.

They have not finished it. They will not finish it for an age yet, maybe ever, because every new arrhythmia that wanders in — every frog that once jumped, every wild thing eased down, every traveler who leans closer to hear — adds one more rhythm that has to be folded in before the word can resolve.

So lean closer. Try to hear it. That is how they grow.

That is the thing that lives here, and it has been waiting, very patiently, to make your timing its own.
