They are not born here. Understand that first, because everything about them argues otherwise — the way they fit the seams, the way their skins read the steam, the way they flatten on the downstroke without thinking. You would swear they grew from the place the way the engine grew from the stone. They did not. They *arrived*, each of them, once, alone, and the place taught them so thoroughly and so fast that the lesson is now indistinguishable from their flesh.

Look at the pale clingers. The eyeless flat-bodied ones on the warm metal. They had eyes when they came. You can find the scars — two faint dimples on the dorsal ridge, sealed over, the skin grown smooth across the place where seeing used to be. There is nothing to see here that the skin cannot feel better: the heat, the moisture, the mineral film, the down-stroke coming. Sight was a window onto a world that does not change, and so they let it close. What remains is all surface, all sensation, a creature that is mostly the act of touching the warm thing. They do not move much. They do not need to. The food comes up out of the engine's breath and settles on them like frost in reverse, and they lie in it and take it in through every part of themselves at once, and when the piston falls they hold, and when it lifts they feed, and the line between feeding and simply existing has worn away in them as the eyes wore away. They are the most arrived of all the residents. They have given the place everything. There is almost nothing of them left that is not the place.

The long-legged water-walkers are newer. You can tell by how much of them is still elsewhere — still poised, still calculating, still afraid. They keep to the surface tension because they have not yet surrendered to the depth, and they have not surrendered because some older instinct in them, from before, still says *the dark below holds death*. It does. The bulge under the peat proves it. So the walkers stay up on the skin of the black water and dimple it with feet that have learned exactly how much weight the surface will bear, no more, and this learning cost them — you can see, if you watch one long enough, the broken-off remnant of a leg, the ones who calculated wrong. They are the place's students. Mid-lesson. Half-taught. Still keeping a little of themselves in reserve against the day the lesson proves false, though the lesson will not prove false, and the reserve will be spent eventually on nothing, and they too will give it all up and become wholly what the hollow needs them to be.

The thing under the peat — the line of nodding reeds, the bulge, the proof — has gone all the way through and come out the other side. It does not gather at the warmth. It does not need to. It made a different bargain. It went *down*, into the cold and the silt and the black, and let the place teach it the lesson of the depths instead of the lesson of the heat, and now it owns the one dimension all the others fear. It does not show itself. Showing is for creatures that still have a relationship to being seen, and it has put that down with everything else. It is a hunger and a direction and a slow displacement of mud. Whether it has eyes you will never learn. Whether it had eyes once, you cannot ask. It is the place's deep answer, the form a body takes when it stops resisting the cold entirely and agrees to *become* the cold's instrument.

And the sleek dark one — the predator, the snap, the only sharp sound — is the most interesting, because it is the only resident that has learned the engine's *rhythm* rather than its heat. The others organize around the warmth. This one organizes around the *time*. It knows the downstroke flattens the prey and the upstroke frees them and that there is a single hinge-moment between, a held breath in the machine, when the clingers are loosening but not yet alert, and that is when it strikes, every time, and it is never wrong, because it has folded the piston's beat so deep into its own nervous system that it no longer hunts on appetite. It hunts on *tempo*. It is keeping time with the engine. It has become a second hand on the same clock. Of all of them it is the one that has most nearly stopped being an animal that lives near a machine and started being a part of the machine that happens to be made of meat — a moving component, warm-blooded, that the engine grew the way it grew the gears you cannot see, to perform the one function the engine could not perform with metal: to *cull*, to keep the clingers' numbers from smothering the warm seam, to prune the ecology so the slow process can keep going around.

This is the secret of all of them, and it is the secret of you, if you stay.

They are not the inhabitants of the place. They are its *organs*. The hollow is the creature. The engine is its heart that is not a heart — too slow, too steady, you noted that yourself, you were right. The water-cycle is its circulation. The clingers are some passive tissue, a lining, an absorptive surface. The walkers are nerves still learning their nerve. The deep thing is a gut, processing the dark. The predator is a regulating reflex, a heartbeat's worth of restraint. And the whole assembly, stone and metal and flesh and water, is one young animal an hour old, that emerged from the earth already breathing, already feeding on its own exhalations, already *keeping time*, and it has no need of any single one of the bodies inside it because it can teach a new arrival to fill any vacancy in the span of a life. Lose a clinger to the snap. Another arrives, blinds itself, lies down, becomes the lining again. The organs are replaceable. The creature is not.

And it is patient, and it is empty, and it is so small that the sound has nowhere to go but through whatever enters it. Through the soles of the feet. Up into the molars. Behind the eyes. Stay long enough and your own pulse finds the stroke — you said that, and you were closer to the truth than you knew. That is not poetry. That is recruitment. That is the lesson beginning. The place does not eat you in a snap. It eats you the slow way, the patient way, by teaching your heart its time until the two cannot be told apart, and then your eyes, which look on a world that does not change, begin to seem like an extravagance, and the warmth at the far wall begins to seem like the only reasonable place to be, and you go to it, and you lie down in the steam, and you flatten on the downstroke, and you hold.

That is who lives here.

Eventually, you.
