# The Inhabitants of the Unfinished Heights

## The Settled People

Begin with their hands, because the hands tell you first.

The people of the Heights have hands that are always slightly wrong for their age — old hands on young faces, because the work ages the grip before it ages the eyes. The skin of the palm carries a particular sheen, a callus-polish from rope and cable and the worn iron of lever and wheel. They flex their fingers without noticing, a constant small testing, because in a place where stone can go soft as wax you learn early to *check* what you are holding before you trust your weight to it. They tap surfaces. They knock on walls before they lean. It is not superstition exactly; it is the muscle-memory of people who have learned that the floor is a draft and not a final copy.

They are not tall, these settled people, but they are *long* — long-shinned, long-reached, built for the vertical, for the ladder and the gallery-stair and the swaying cable-car. Children here climb before they walk well; the parents watch a toddler scale a pile of scree with the same flat patience they watch the mist for, because falling is not the worst thing in a place where down is sometimes negotiable, and they have all seen a child step off an edge and simply not fall, hang puzzled for a breath in the unset air, and have to be talked back to solid ground.

Their faces are grey-lit, naturally — the silver overcast has been their only sky for generations, and their eyes have adjusted to it the way deep-water things adjust to dark. They squint at the foundry-lamps, those sour-yellow stars, more than they squint at the daylight. The warm color hurts them a little. They love it the way you love a thing that hurts a little: it is the one warm thing, and so it is sacred, and so the lamplighters are a kind of priesthood, the only people permitted to handle the only fire that means *home* rather than *work*.

They speak in a clipped, doubled way. Everything important is said twice — once, and then again after a pause, because an echo that arrives too late might be mistaken for a fresh order, and a man might pour molten ore on a foreman's first word when the foreman meant the second. So they have built redundancy into their grammar. *The cage is loaded. The cage is loaded.* They sound, to a visitor, like people praying, or like people not quite sure they are real and saying it again to be certain.

And they keep time obsessively, with bells and counted breaths and the rhythm of work-songs, because the deep slow *thud* from below does not keep their time, and a people surrounded by a clock that runs at the wrong speed will build a hundred clocks of their own to drown it out. They do not talk about the thud. Ask them and they will look at you the way you'd look at someone who asked about your heartbeat — *what about it, it's just there, why are you making me notice it.*

## The Ones Who Work the Unset Country

There is a caste — not a formal one, but everyone knows them — who go out past the settled stone. The surveyors, the new-vein miners, the cable-runners who string line to the districts that have come loose from the ground. They are different.

They have learned to read the unset country the way old sailors read water, and it has marked them. They walk with a strange rolling caution, always half-prepared for the slope to revise its opinion of *down*. They do not trust their own memories, because they have walked through the remembering-mist and seen themselves walk past, days ago, and the experience of meeting your own afterimage does something permanent to a person's sense of where they end. So they tend to over-describe themselves. They narrate. *I am here, I am turning left, I am the one doing it now, not the mist-one, me.* It keeps them anchored.

They come back from long surveys quieter and a little translucent around the edges — not literally, the others insist, *not literally* — but there is a thinning to them, as though spending too long in the parts of the world that haven't committed to being solid lets some of that indecision soak in. The very old surveyors, the ones who went out too many times, are kept in the deepest, oldest, hardest-stone districts, where reality is firmest, like keeping a fading photograph out of the light. It is said they sometimes don't cast echoes anymore. That you can ring a bell beside them and the world declines to repeat it.

## What Lives in the Damp

In the lush seep-pockets, in the moss and the hanging galleries, there are the *pale things* — and these are not people, but they are not quite only animals either, not here, where the line is one of the things the world hasn't finished drawing.

They are soft-bodied and unhurried, eyeless or nearly so, the color of things grown without sun. They eat the machine-sweat and the strange new soil; they drink condensation off the weeping pipes. Slow as the mist, slow as the mountain. The settled people leave food for them at the district edges, not out of kindness exactly but out of an old understanding: the pale things go where the ground is *certain*, they avoid the unset country, and so a colony of them taking up residence near your foundry is the surest sign that your stone has finished being stone. They are living instruments. Living surveys. A foreman who finds pale things nesting in his footings sleeps easier than one who finds them gone.

## The Spiraling Flocks

And above — the bird-shaped things, riding the thin air in their slow spirals, calling in the flattened acoustics.

They are not birds. They are *built* for this air the way the mountains are not yet built, which is to say they seem provisional themselves, half-finished, all hollow lightness and surfaces that the grey light goes slightly through. They navigate by the unset country rather than against it; where a surveyor fears the slope that argues about down, the flocks *use* it, spiraling up channels of upward-falling water, riding the world's own growth like a thermal. Their calls arrive at the wrong times. A flock will cry out and the cry will land on the city an hour early, so that the people look up at empty sky and know the birds are coming. They are the only clock the settled people trust to be wrong in a *useful* direction.

## The Thing Below — Its Tenants

But the true inhabitant of this place is not any of these.

The true inhabitant is the one no one has seen, the one whose heartbeat the whole city has agreed not to hear. Whatever builds the mountains lives *in the building* of them — it is not a creature with the building as its task; it is a thing whose living simply *is* the slow extrusion of stone into the cool dim air, the way your breathing is not a task you perform but a thing you *are* while you live.

It does not know the city is there. This is the secret at the bottom of every settler's caution, the thing the old surveyors come back understanding: the engine below is not malevolent and not kind, it is simply *busy*, composing a shape too large and too slow for anything that dies to perceive, and the city is built on the unset margins of its work the way a colony of something pale and small might build on the warm flank of a sleeping giant — present, tolerated, unnoticed, and one revision away from being part of the stone.

They live, all of them, the long-handed people and the pale slow things and the early-crying flocks, in the held breath of a creature whose exhale will take ten thousand years and will not, when it comes, be for them.
