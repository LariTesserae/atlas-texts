## The People of Vault-Reach

Let me come to them the way you come to anyone in deep water: by their sound first, before their shape.

You hear them across the gaps as those long descending moans — and that is the first true thing about them. They are *built around the voice*. Not the mouth-voice of air-breathers, but something lower and more whole-bodied: a resonance produced in chambers along the throat and chest, tuned over generations to carry through the cool green medium. A person here is, functionally, an instrument. Their speech is sung because singing is simply what speaking *is* when the world conducts sound this well. To grow up in Vault-Reach is to learn pitch before you learn words, because the pitch is half the meaning, and across a long swim the high overtones arrive before the low ones, so that every sentence reaches you stretched and reordered, head before body, like a creature swimming toward you out of the murk.

### Their Bodies

They are not fish and they are not us, but they descend from something that once chose, decisively, the water.

Picture a body long and tapered, neither thin nor heavy — a deep-chested taper, broad through the ribs where the singing-chambers sit, narrowing to a strong flattened tail-stroke at the base of the spine. Not a fish-tail; rather a body that *flexes*, that swims with the whole length of itself the way an otter does, or a long diver holding their legs together. The limbs are there: arms strong and dextrous, made for the knot and the lashing and the worked shell, with hands that have more in common with ours than anything else about them — long-fingered, patient, the webs between the fingers thin enough to fold away when fine work is needed and spread wide when the hand becomes a paddle.

They breathe the water. Down the sides of the neck and along the upper ribs run the gill-folds, soft and frilled, flushing slow because the water here is cool and rich and there is no need to gulp at it. But — and this matters — they *can* take the air. When the elders swim up to touch the roof, they break the membrane's underside with their faces and breathe the thin trapped pockets the woven dwellings hold, and it does them no harm; it is only strange to them, a sharp bright burning sensation they associate with the highest and most solemn occasions. Air is for ceremony. Water is for living.

Their skin is the color of the light: a soft mottled green-grey that goes darker down the back and paler down the belly, so that from above they vanish into the bottle-green murk and from below they vanish into the brighter membrane-light. Across the back and shoulders, faint lines of paler tissue — lateral senses, organs that *feel* the water move, that read a plucked bridge-rope or the wake of a passing shoal or the slow tidal breathing of the day-long currents. They do not need to see a neighbor to know a neighbor is coming. They feel the water remember the neighbor's stroke.

The eyes are large, dark, adapted to dimness, lidless but able to film over. They are not the primary sense. A person of Vault-Reach who lost their eyes could still find every dwelling, every bridge, every cousin, by sound and by the feel of moving water. They navigate the way a blind person navigates a beloved house — except the house is two thousand feet of standing ocean and they are never, in the whole of their lives, lost in it.

### How They Live

They are slow. I want to be careful with this word, because it is not laziness and it is not weakness. It is *tempo*, chosen and inherited and right for the place. A current takes a day to cross the settlement; a visit to a neighboring trunk is the better part of an afternoon's swim; nothing perishable can be hoarded, nothing urgent can be hurried by panic. So they have become a people for whom the long view is not a virtue practiced against the grain but simply the natural reading of a slow world. A thought can be turned over for a season. A grudge would be exhausting to maintain across so many leagues of patient water; they tend not to bother. A craft — a single woven dwelling, a single carved shell-bowl — may be the work of years, taken up and set down and taken up again, and no one thinks this strange.

They are few, and the fewness shapes the soul of them. Each person *matters* in the arithmetic sense — a few hundred souls cannot afford to waste one another — and so they are courteous in a way that runs deeper than manners. To pass another in the open green between trunks and *not* exchange a sung greeting would be a kind of violence, a denial that the other exists. So they sing constantly across the gaps: not always words, often just a tone, a held note that means *I am here, I have felt you there, neither of us is alone in all this water.* The city is always murmuring to itself for this reason. The silence between trunks is never truly silent, because the people will not allow it to be. They are afraid of the silence the way we are afraid of the dark — and for the same reason. It is where the alone-ness lives.

They eat what the membrane and the shoals provide — cropping the same leaf-ceiling the grazers crop, in their own measured way; netting the bright shoaling creatures that nest in the dead lower towns; filtering, when they must, the same drift the smaller things filter. They do not farm so much as *tend*, and the tending is half their religion: they keep the grazers fat and content so the grazers keep the membrane thin-and-thick, light-and-holding, alive. A child of Vault-Reach learns the names and moods of the canopy-grazers before they learn the names of the dead.

### What They Believe

Here is the heart of them, and it grows directly out of the one strange thing.

They live beneath a held ocean that has no reason not to fall, and they *know* it has no reason not to fall. They are not innocent of this. The elders swim up on the longest day and lay open hands on the membrane and feel it hum with the strain of holding, and they come down and tell the young: *it is not owed to us. It is given. Every day it does not fall is a day something chose, again, not to let it fall.*

This has made them a people without arrogance. They cannot believe themselves the masters of their world, because their world is visibly, audibly, a *favor* — and a favor can be withdrawn. So they have built their whole disposition around a single discipline, the one the roof asks of them: *to notice.* To not stop noticing. To never let the held ocean become ordinary, never let the green light become merely the light, never let the long sung greeting across the gap become a thing done without meaning it. The cardinal sin in Vault-Reach is not cruelty or theft or sloth. It is *taking-for-granted*. It is the failure to be amazed at what is, every day, freely and inexplicably given.

And so they are, despite the cool and the dimness and the long slow distances and the smallness of their number — a grateful people. Profoundly, structurally grateful. They wake into the bottle-green light each day faintly astonished to still be dry of the floor two thousand feet below, still held, still sung-to across the water, still *here* — and they answer that astonishment the only way the roof asked them to.

They notice. They sing across the gap. They lay their hands on the green ceiling and feel the kindness straining to keep its promise, and they say, in their long descending moans that reach you head-before-body out of the dark:

*Still held. Still here. Thank you, thank you, thank you.*
