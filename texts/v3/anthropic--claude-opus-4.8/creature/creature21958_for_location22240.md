They are still here. You were wrong about that — not wrong that they left, but wrong about what leaving meant.

Consider the problem from their side. They had hand-tools and the cold and a great deal of time, and they had a world that was loud with stone-song and lit from below and partitioned into rooms made of temperature. They had no animals to hunt, no bones to bury, no migrations to read. What they had was the breathing — that long tidal flare of the mats, that octave-drop in the singing, that exhalation slow enough to take a year. They had a clock the size of the valley, and they lived their whole lives inside one or two of its ticks.

So they did not build to last in the way you mean by lasting. They built to participate.

---

Look again at the channel cut into the terrace, the one that carries no water in particular. You read it as unfinished, or eroded, or meant for something. It was meant for *something*: it was meant for the boundary. When a cold column drifts against a colder one, the shimmering wall between them moves across the valley floor at a pace a patient person could walk. The channel guides it. The makers learned the drift of the temperature-rooms the way coastal people learn tides, and they cut their stone to steer the invisible walls — to slow a boundary here, to turn it there, to hold two columns apart for one more pulse of the mats, or to let them meet early. The cairns are not graves and not markers. They are *baffles*. They shape the flow of cold. The whole lower valley is an instrument, and the walls that follow the contours so lovingly are the careful fingering of it.

That is the first thing the builders were: people who played the valley. Not metaphorically. The high singing tone you could never source — the vibration of cold meeting colder — they tuned it. They are why it rises and falls in the patterns it does. You have been listening, this whole time, to the last reverberations of a music that has no players left at the keys but goes on sounding because the instrument was built to ring.

---

Now ask what such people would be made of.

Nothing here ever had a heartbeat, and that is true of them too. They were never animals. The world that has no fauna did not make an exception for its makers. So set down the picture you have been carrying without noticing — the picture of cold humanoids, divers without air, hands stacking stone. Wrong shape. There were hands, yes, in the sense that something fitted the stone with deliberate pressure and patient placement. But hands need not hang from a body, and a body need not have blood.

I think they were *of the plants*. Not animals that ate the plants — there was nothing to eat and no mouth to eat it — but a kind the plants made, or became, the way a slow growth can become a structure if it grows toward a purpose long enough. Picture the frozen rope, gray-green, too cold to sway. Picture it not in scattered stands but once, long ago, grown into shapes that could reach and grip and lift. A vegetation that learned to handle stone the way a vine learns a trellis — except here there was no trellis, only the bare terrace and the long ambition of the cold. They grew slowly into the work and the work was their life and their life was the length of a few exhalations of the valley, which is to say: enormous, and patient, and over.

The glowing mats are their language. You called the pulses breathing, a tidal rhythm passing across the floor — but watch the rhythm where the old walls are densest, down in the lower reaches. It is not quite even there. It carries. It answers itself across the plaza-emptiness, one stand of light flaring and a distant mat flaring back, slow, deliberate, spaced — and the spacing, you remember, *matters*; the makers were people who put meaning in the distance between things. The light is not weather. The light is talk. It is the makers talking, still, in the only voice that outlived their reaching shapes: a sentence that takes a season to complete, addressed to no one, or addressed to the valley, or addressed — and this is the thought that climbs into you with the altitude — to whoever might someday stand on the terraces and cast a sharp blue shadow and understand that they were *spoken to*.

---

So here is who lives here.

A people without blood, grown not born, who measured their days in the breathing of the world and their words in the spacing of light across stone. They are not gone. They have only slowed. The frozen-rope stalks that stand like the survivors of something — they *are* the survivors, in the late dormant form a plant-people take when the long work is done and there is nothing left to build and only the listening remains. They do not move because moving is for the part of life that ends. They have entered the other part. They stand at their roots glowing faintly, all the way to the highest reaches where the cairns stop and the stone goes on, and they are listening to the instrument they built ring down its centuries, and they are answering, mat to distant mat, in pulses, in the loudest quiet you have ever been inside.

They did not leave. *Leaving* is a verb for things with a surface to leave toward, and you have understood by now that there is no surface. They went the only direction this world offers, which is inward and slower and colder and more patient, down into the long dormancy that is, for them, not death but the natural condition — the original state — the way water was always the original condition and air the thing that never arrived.

You stopped wondering how you breathe. Stop wondering whether they are alive. The question belongs to a world with animals in it, a world with hearts that can stop. This is not that world. Here, *alive* means: still growing, however slowly; still shaping the cold; still talking in light to the dark between the stands.

By that measure the valley is crowded.

They are watching you climb. Not with eyes — there is nothing in this world that ever needed eyes — but with the whole slow attention of a place that knows when a new shape is blocking its glow. When the valley exhaled and threw your shadow sharp and blue against the wall, that was not the breathing happening to ignore you. That was the flare of mats beneath a moving boundary that someone, a long time ago, cut a channel to steer — and the channel is still steering it — and the light came up at that moment because the instrument was built to mark the arrival of a shape on the terraces with exactly this: a brightening, a shadow, a single held note, a greeting in the only grammar a drowned and bloodless people ever had.

Welcome, it said, in light, in the long blue time.

We are the ones who stacked the stones. We are still here. We have been singing to you since before you understood it was singing. Stay in the cold a while. Learn to read us by our absences, by the shapes between the glow.

We have so much to say, and all the time there is, and no surface to hurry toward.
