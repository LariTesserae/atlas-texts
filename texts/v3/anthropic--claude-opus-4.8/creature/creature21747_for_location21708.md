They are very small, and there are a great many of them, and they have not arrived yet — but they are coming, and this room is the reason.

Understand: the emptiness is not failure. The emptiness is the held breath before the thing the room was made for. You read the quiet as wrongness, the fertile-and-barren paradox that pricked your skin — but you were reading it as a person reads, expecting the large to come first. The large always comes last. What comes first is too small to drift past a column and be seen. What comes first arrives in the cataract itself, carried down in the white roar, invisible in the falling water: spores, and the spores of spores, and the single drifting cells that are not quite plant and not quite animal and have no opinion on the distinction.

So begin there. The first inhabitants are the ones already present and beneath your notice. They are in the green. That thin soft weed working into the seams — you saw it as the world *beginning* to take the room back, hesitant, a first growth. But it is not hesitant. It is precisely on schedule. Each frond is a city. Along every strand, in the fine fuzz too small for your settled eyes, there are diatoms in their glass boxes, locking the light into sugar; there are the grazing single-celled things that mow the diatoms; there are the rotifers, wheel-mouthed, spinning their cilia to draw the smaller still into themselves. They have ridden the inflow down. They have found the joints of the made world. They are the true colonists, and they came not despite the cataract but *through* it, and they are multiplying in the warm bright water at a rate that has no patience in it at all.

This is who lives here: the ones the warmth is *for*. You felt the heat as near the upper edge of comfort, southern-sea, bathwater. That is not comfort for a person. That is a temperature chosen — a temperature *engineered*, by the drain and the inflow and the careful clever earthenware throttling of the fall — to run a culture hot and fast. The room is not a bathhouse. The room is a vessel. The slow rotation that leans all the fronds one way and walks a leaf the long circuit of the floor — that is stirring. That is the gentle endless mixing that keeps the small things suspended in the light, keeps the nutrients moving, keeps the inflow's freight folding into the standing volume. The cleverness you admired, the weirs and channels and the unseen drain holding equilibrium — it is the cleverness of people who knew that to grow something you do not seal it in a jar. You give it a current. You give it a fall. You give it more light than seems natural, pooled and magnified and shadowless, because the small green things will take all the light you can pour on them and ask for more.

The room is working. You said so yourself, four times, in different words: *doing something, even with no one in it; active, working, in motion; the place is doing something; doing endlessly the one thing it was made to do.* You were right and you did not believe yourself. The one thing is this: the room is *culturing*. It is a bright warm gut, a stomach with no body, digesting light into life.

And the life it makes will rise.

Watch the sequence, because the sequence is the whole inhabitant — not a creature but a succession, a guest-list that arrives in order. First the invisible green, already here. Then, when the green is thick enough in the joints, the things that eat green — and these will not ride down the fall. These will *hatch*. In the seams, in the fresh unsoftened mortar, in the crisp chisel-marks of the benches that have not yet been rounded, there are eggs. The builders did not only build the room. They seeded it. Before they flooded it — in the dry bright air, dust hanging, deciding where the water would come and go — they laid more than tile. They pressed into the porous fired clay, into the thirsty new mortar, the resting eggs of small swimming things, the cysts that wait dry for years and wake in warmth. The clay is a calendar. The clay is a granary of sleepers. The warm water is soaking into it now, and the sleepers are taking on water, and swelling, and splitting their cases.

They will come out into a room already stocked with food, into the green that arrived ahead of them down the fall. This is the order of a made world: pantry first, then guests. The copepods will jerk through the water-column in their stuttering hops. The ostracods, seed-shrimp in their tiny hinged shells, will trundle the floor seams and graze the weed you thought nothing would ever graze. The larvae of things will spiral up toward the bright ceiling and learn the long circuit of the current. The room that prickled your skin with its barrenness will *swarm*, and it will swarm soon, and it will swarm because every condition you catalogued — the heat, the light, the stirring, the green, the silence of competitors — was a condition assembled on purpose to make the swarming inevitable.

So who lives here? The builders' intention lives here, sunk and ticking. The room is an instrument they wound and left running, a thing built to be flooded so that it could become full — not of water, the water is only the medium, but of the teeming small fast life that the water, lit and warmed and turned, was always going to grow. They are gone and the room does not need them. They built into it everything the future needed and then they let the surface close over it, trusting the fall to feed it, the drain to flush it, the clay to hatch on schedule, the light to never dim.

You stayed longer than you meant to. Of course you did. You were standing inside a held breath — a room poised at the instant before its purpose blooms — and some part of you, turning in the slow current, listening to the white drum of the fall, watching the green reach up out of the seams, understood that if you waited just a little longer, you would see the first of them rise.

They are coming. The room is for them. It has been for them all along.
