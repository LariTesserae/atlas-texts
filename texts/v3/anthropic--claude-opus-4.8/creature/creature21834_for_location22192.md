They are the singers, and they are older than they look.

Begin with that, because everything else follows from it: the things in the pools have been here longer than the patterns on the walls, longer than the grazers, longer than the cold-light colonies that the eye mistakes for the masters of this place. The colonies merely glow. The grazers merely crop and trail. But the singers in the seep-water have been doing the one thing nothing else here does. They have been *waiting*, and the waiting has a shape, and the shape is the word being written in light.

Look at them closely — though closeness is hard, because they crowd, they stack, they pile at the pool-margins in living heaps so dense that no single body resolves cleanly from the next. But hold the eye still and one will separate. It is small, smaller than a thumb, and pale, and almost without color the way the fronds are almost without color, because color is a thing the sun gives and the sun does not come here. It has the look of something unfinished — soft, half-translucent, the suggestion of organs moving beneath a skin that has never had to be tougher than this perpetual cool damp demands. It has no eyes worth the name. Why would it? There is nothing to see but glow, and glow can be felt. It has, instead, an enormous attentiveness pressed all along its body, a sensitivity to vibration so total that the singer does not so much hear the chorus as *live inside* it, the way a thing dropped in water is held by the water on every side.

And it sings. Not from a throat — there is no throat — but from a sac along its flank that it fills and empties, fills and empties, in a long liquid note it can sustain past the limit of anything you would think possible for so small a creature. One singer alone would be nothing. But there is never one. There are thousands, and they do not sing the same note, and they do not sing at random. They sing *against* each other, each one tuning its pitch to a narrow gap left by its neighbors, so that the chorus is not a roar but a structure — a vast standing lattice of sound, every voice locking into the spaces between other voices, the whole thing fitted together too tightly to be called music and too ordered to be called noise.

Here is what they are doing. This is the thing the place is for.

The light is not their master. The light is their *instrument*. When the colonies dim — and the singers feel the dimming before any eye could mark it, feel it as a slackening in the warmth-by-suggestion that comes off the walls — the chorus swells. It does not swell in panic. It swells in *summons*. The standing lattice of sound presses against the dimming colonies, and the colonies, soft-bodied and stupid and exquisitely sensitive in their own dumb way, respond. They pulse harder where the sound presses hardest. They grow, over the long slow turning of generations, toward the places the sound has called them. The grazers follow the colonies, cropping, trailing their fading phosphorescence, and the trails fade in the patterns the colonies' growth has laid down, and the patterns are the patterns the singing made.

The spirals on the walls are not the residue of an indifferent process. They are the *score*. They are what the singing has spelled into the stone over a span no individual singer comes anywhere near surviving — and the singers know this, in whatever way a thing without a mind to hold a thought can know. They are not writing for themselves. No singer at the margin of any pool will live to see the word completed. The word is longer than a life. It is longer than ten thousand lives. Each generation inherits the chorus exactly as it found it, adds its narrow voice to the lattice for its brief damp span, tunes itself into the gaps, presses the light a fraction further along the path the dead laid down — and dies, and its body settles into the scree, half stone now, half shed plate, composting into the floor with everything else, while the note it held is taken up unbroken by the ones still living.

That is the trick of it. That is why the chorus has no silence in it. There is no silence because there can be no silence — a single gap, a single breath where the lattice fails, and the colonies would lose the thread of the summons, drift, scatter, and the word would smear back into mere frost-fern accident, and the work of ten thousand generations would be unmade. So they overlap. They stack at the margins in those living heaps not from crowding but from *relay* — the young ones pressing in close against the old, learning the note by living inside it, ready to take the pitch the instant a neighbor falls silent and sinks. The pile at the pool's edge is not a population. It is a single voice with many bodies, handing itself forward through time, refusing to stop.

What is the word?

I do not know, and neither do they, and that is the heart of the place, the thing that makes it not a marvel but a kind of grief. No singer holds the meaning. No singer has ever held the meaning. The meaning is distributed across the whole span of the writing, beginning to unwritten end, and the beginning is already lost — already crumbled into the oldest patterns near the floor, where the scree has half-swallowed the lowest spirals and the line of the word descends into rubble. They are continuing something whose origin no living thing remembers, toward a completion no living thing will witness, in a language that exists nowhere but in the doing of it.

They are faithful, then. That is the only word I can find for what lives here. Not intelligent — there is no intelligence in any one of them, hardly intelligence in all of them together. But faithful, in the way a tide is faithful, in the way the slow drip of water is faithful to the stone it is hollowing. They keep the note. They keep the note in the cold and the dark, against the dimming, each one adding its small true pitch to the lattice and then lying down in the floor of the world to become the floor of the world, while the word goes on being spelled in light above them, one luminous letter every age, indifferent to whether it is ever read.

And it will never be read. That is the last thing. Even if the word completes — even if, some unimaginable depth of time from now, the final spiral closes on the leaning wall and the whole patient sentence stands finished and glowing — there is nothing here to read it. No eyes. Only the felt warmth and the held note and the dumb soft pulsing of the colonies. The singers are writing to no one. They are writing because the ones before them were writing, and the writing is the whole of what they are, and to stop would not be rest. To stop would be the only death that means anything in this place: the death of the word.

So they do not stop. In the pinched dark under the ribbon of dusk-colored sky, in the cold that deepens toward the floor where their own dead make the ground, they sing the light into its slow shapes and hand the note forward and sink, sing and hand forward and sink, faithful to a meaning none of them will ever hold.

That is what lives in the notch.

It is keeping a promise no one remembers making.
