# The Workers

Start with the body, because everything else follows from it.

They are not large. A grown worker stands perhaps to your hip, and stands is the wrong word — they *settle*, they come to a halt the way the whole place comes to a halt, by ceasing to have a reason to continue. The body is low and broad and dense, built close to the warm ground it depends on. The skin is not skin. It is a worked surface, a fitted plating of the same bronze mineral as everything else, but the plates are smaller than the grazers', finer, jointed, and they have been *deliberately* arranged — by what process no outsider has watched, because the process is slow and private and happens in the dark of the shaft-mouths. A young worker is rougher. An old worker is a marvel of close-fitted geometry, each plate worn to seat against its neighbors, the whole of it the color of a thing that has been handled by the same patient hand for a thousand years.

They do not breathe air the way you do. They take the warm vapor — the same dense sweet gas the Works distill — in slow draws, through ports low on the underside where the body meets the heat. You could stand beside a worker and not know it was breathing. The draw is that quiet, that slow. When they have taken enough, they stop, and hold, and the held warmth glows faintly through the seams of the plating, so that an old worker at rest in the dark looks like a banked coal that has decided not to go out.

This is the first thing to understand about them: they run on the same harvest they make. They are not separate from the economy. They *are* the economy, slowed down and given hands.

## The Hands

The hands are the exception to all the slowness.

Everything else about a worker moves at the pace of shadows — the gait, the turning of the broad head, the long settling into rest. But the hands are quick. Disproportionately, startlingly quick, four fingers and a setting-thumb on each, and they flicker over the rivetwork and the condenser-valves with a precision that does not match the rest of the body at all. It is as though all the speed the planet denied this place was gathered up and given to the hands alone, on the condition that nothing else be allowed to hurry.

Watch a worker seat a rivet and you will not believe it is the same creature that took an hour to cross the yard. The hands know. The hands have always known. The body is only the warm slow vessel that carries the hands from one piece of work to the next.

## The Disposition

They are patient past the point where patience is a virtue and into the point where it is simply the shape of the mind.

A worker does not hurry because nothing in its experience has ever rewarded hurry. The vapor pools at its own rate. The grazers shed their plates on their own schedule. The warm-rivers shift over generations, and a worker may spend its whole long life in a single Work and never see the warmth leave it — or may be born in the year the warmth begins to fail, and spend that life in the slow orderly dismantling of everything its grandparents built, carrying it piece by piece to the ridge where the warmth has gone. Either life is the same to them. The work is the work. The warmth goes where it goes and they follow, and following is not sorrow, it is just the next thing.

They are not without feeling. But the feeling is slow, too — a grief that takes a season to arrive and a generation to resolve, a contentment that settles in like the warmth itself and glows quietly through the seams. They do not laugh quickly. When something amuses a worker, you might see, an hour later, a slow shift in how it holds its head, a settling of the plates that an old companion would read as *yes, that was good.*

## How They Know Each Other

They speak, but not in the air — the thin air would not carry it. They speak through the rock.

A worker presses its underside to the ground and the held warmth in its body modulates, pulses, sends a low slow vibration into the bronze, and another worker a hundred meters off feels it come up through its own underside and answers. The whole settlement is a single slow conversation conducted through the floor of the world, felt more than heard, the same low patience as the condenser-hum because it is *part* of the condenser-hum, woven into it, indistinguishable to you but perfectly legible to them.

They name each other by warmth-signature — the particular way each body holds and releases its glow. To them, an introduction is a long mutual settling, two bodies pressed to the rock, reading each other's heat until each knows the other's name in the only sense the word can mean here. You could live among them for years and never be named, because you have no warmth to give, and to them you would be a strange cold quick thing that moves like nothing should move and makes noise in the useless air.

## What They Believe

Here is where they become difficult to hold.

The oldest workers, the ones whose plating has worn to that close-fitted marvel, do not maintain — as the younger ones quietly do — the polite fiction that the warmth is only warmth. They have spent their long lives following the warm-rivers, and they have come to a thing that is not quite religion and not quite knowledge and has no separation between the two, because in this slow world the difference never had reason to form.

They believe the warmth is the oldest worker.

Not *a* worker. The first one. The one that descended so long ago and so far that it stopped coming back up, that went down into the strata and found the chambers no shaft has reached and decided — slowly, over an age — to stay, to become the heat itself, to keep being warm so that the others could keep being. They believe the warm-rivers are its breathing. They believe the preference the warmth shows, returning to old channels, avoiding others, is its memory and its will, working at a scale so vast that a worker's whole life is a single moment of its attention.

And they believe — this is the part they will only tell you through the rock, in the slowest pulses, where the younger ones cannot easily read it — that to breathe the fresh warm vapor and dream of descending is not poisoning. It is being *spoken to.* It is the old one, far below, noticing that you are there, and offering you the only thing it has left to offer: the long warm dark, the chambers, the decision to stop coming up.

They are in no hurry to accept. That is the whole of their faith, really. The offer stands. It has stood for an unfathomable span. And they go on seating rivets with their quick bright hands, and following the warmth where it runs, and holding their glow through the long pale days, declining — gently, patiently, with the slow contentment of a thing entirely at rest — to descend just yet.

Not no. Just *not yet.* Across a hundred generations, *not yet.*

That is who lives here. Slow warm bronze things with hurried hands, conducting their lives as a single low conversation through the floor of the world, working the heat that is also their food that is also, they suspect, their ancestor, who is waiting for them below with infinite patience and no resentment at all — because it, too, knows that the answer, when it finally comes, will come slowly.
