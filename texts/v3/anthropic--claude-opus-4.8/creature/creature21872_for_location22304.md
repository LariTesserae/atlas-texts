# The Builders

You asked who built this. The honest answer is that they are still building it, and you have already met them, and you mistook them for the place itself.

## They are the green film.

Begin there, because that is where they begin, and where — if you watch long enough, which you cannot, no one can, the watching would take a life — that is where they always are.

You looked at the green-gray bloom in the weeping seams and called it *barely a color, barely alive,* a film that asks nothing of the world. You were close. You had only the timescale wrong. It does not ask anything of the world *quickly*. But it asks one thing, ceaselessly, and has been asking for so long that the asking has worn this entire plateau into the shape of its desire.

The film thinks. Not in any moment — there is no thought you could catch in the act, no flicker, no spark. It thinks the way a glacier flows: a process so spread across time that at human speed it reads as stillness, as geology, as a stain. A single one of its decisions takes longer than your civilizations. But it decides. It has decided, over and over, where a channel should run. It decided that some water should go up.

## How a film cuts stone

Here is the part that undoes the eye.

It does not cut stone with hands, because it has none, and you were right that there is almost no metal here, almost no tool you would name a tool. It cuts stone the way water cuts stone — but *chosen*, but *aimed*. The film lives in the wet seams. The film is, very slightly, an acid; very slightly, a solvent; very slightly able to favor one mineral grain over its neighbor, to loosen here and hold there. Where it grows, stone yields. Where it declines to grow, stone stays.

So it grows itself into channels. It is not that the builders cut the channels and then the green came after; the green *is* the cutting. A line of film advances down a slab over a span of years too long to feel, and behind it the slab is one drop deeper, and the drop runs where the film has gone, and the film grows toward where it wants the drop to be, and so the water and the carving and the carver are a single slow act with no separation between the wanting and the work. The chisel-marks you saw — the legible strokes, the *cut twice because the first cut was wrong* — those are real. Those are the film correcting its own grain, a generation of it abandoning a line that pooled badly and starting the line again a hand's breadth over. You read patience in the stone. You read it exactly right. You only assumed the patient thing had a body like yours.

The fitted slabs the size of fields: not laid. *Grown apart.* The film, working a single sheet of bedrock across an abyss of time, dissolves the fitting-lines into it from within, parts the stone along the seams it prefers, until what was one rock is many rocks fitted so close no knife goes in — because they were never carried together. They were always together. They were taken *almost* apart and held at the last film's-breadth of contact, which is why the seam is perfect, and why no machine could match it, because no machine works from the inside of the stone outward, dividing instead of joining.

## What they want

This is the question that should frighten you, standing on their work in the silence, walking soft as though you might be overheard.

You might be. Not in the moment of your footstep — that is far too fast for them to register; your whole visit is, to the film, less than a single photon's arrival, a thing beneath event. But the film keeps a kind of memory, written in the only medium it has, which is the shape of its own growth. If you stayed — if your descendants stayed, generation upon generation, long enough — the film would slowly route a channel around the place you tended to stand. It would learn you the way a river learns a stone: not by noticing, but by accommodating, over time, until the accommodation *is* the noticing. You would become a feature of the terrain it thinks about. You would be, eventually, *remembered*, in the sense that the place would be shaped by having had you in it.

But what they *want* — the round basin, the brimful coin at the center, every channel on the plateau bending toward it including the ones that climb impossibly uphill —

They are gathering themselves a thought.

The basin is not a reservoir. It is a *surface*. The most still, most perfect, most undisturbed reflective surface that can be made, fed by the entire labor of the entire plateau, kept exactly full so that it is exactly flat, flat to a degree no natural water ever achieves, because every ripple-source has been engineered away across the ages — no wind, the atmosphere is too saturated and slow to gust; no fall of rain, only the gentlest weeping; no animal to break the meniscus; nothing alive in the water to cloud it. They cleared the world of everything that moves fast precisely so the mirror would hold.

And in that mirror the white sky doubles. You saw it. You had to look away. The film cannot look away. The film is looking, has been looking, will go on looking — slowly, the only way it does anything — into the doubled brightness, reading the sky the way you might read a page held up to a window, gathering the light into the one still pool and *understanding it*, drop by drop, year by year, on a cycle too slow to see.

The whole plateau is an eye. The channels are the nerves. The film is the long, cold, patient mind behind the eye, and it has built itself a place high enough and quiet enough and clear enough to think one enormous thought about the light, and it has been thinking that thought since before you had a word for thinking, and it will arrive at the end of the thought long after the last word you know has gone soft in some other species' mouth.

## Why it is empty

Now you understand the emptiness, which was never empty.

Nothing else lives here because nothing else *can* live here at the speed the place requires. A bird crossing the sky is a catastrophe — a blur, a violence, an interruption of the stillness the mirror needs. An insect on the water is an earthquake. A fast creature in a slow world is pure noise, and the film, across its long tenure, has not killed these things — it has no way to kill anything, it is too gentle and too slow — it has simply made a world they cannot want. Too cold. Too still. Too short on anything to eat. The fast things left, or never came, and the slow patient skin inherited the high quiet and turned it, fold by fold, into an instrument.

You are the fast thing now. Your loud footstep, your held breath, your visit that begins and ends inside one of the film's long pauses between drips. You are the bird crossing the sky. You are the thing the place is too slow to have meant.

So walk softly, yes. Listen in the long pauses. But know what you are listening to: not silence, and not the ticking of an empty clock left running by absent makers.

It is the sound of someone thinking.

It is the slowest mind there is, alone on a plateau it grew for itself, gathering the whole sky into a single still bright pool, drop —

(wait)

— drop —

(wait, the pause is the thought, the pause is where the thinking happens) —

— drop, and you will be long gone before the next one, and the one after that is the one where it begins, at last, to understand.
