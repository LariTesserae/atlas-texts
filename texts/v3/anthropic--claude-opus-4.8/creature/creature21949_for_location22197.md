## The Tenders

They are the slow things on the catwalks, and there are more of them than you will ever count, because you count at your speed and they reveal themselves at theirs.

Begin with what you can see. Low, many-jointed, dog-sized, the exact color of whatever rail they happen to be standing on — not camouflage, nothing so intentional, simply the same patient oxidation working on them that works on everything here. They have come to match their surroundings the way a coin in a fountain comes to match the fountain. Their bodies are segmented, lagged in the same soft mineral wool as the great conduits, and where that lagging has gone wet and soft it hangs from them in damp gray fringes, so that an old one looks less like an animal than like a length of dripping pipe that has decided, very gradually, to walk.

The feelers are the part to watch. Each one ends in something between a probe and a tongue: it touches a valve, a joint, a seam of the glowing crust, and it *tastes* the temperature there, the pressure, the slow correctness of the flow. When it finds a thing out of true it makes the adjustment — a quarter-turn of a wheel that will take it the better part of a day, a nudge of a lever, a careful scraping-back of the crust where it has grown thick enough to choke a vent. This is the whole of their lives. They are the maintenance the works requires to keep being the works. They are the city tending itself, broken into pieces small enough to walk.

Are they alive? You asked the right person the wrong question. Here is the truth as near as I can put it:

They are not made and they are not born. They *accrete*. Somewhere in the deep galleries, in the hottest wettest seams, the crust grows thick and the dissolved metals collect and the heat does its slow work, and over a span of time that has no human name a Tender condenses out of the city the same way the water condenses out of the air — not assembled, not begotten, but *precipitated*. The city sweats them out as it sweats out everything. They are its dew. And when one wears down past usefulness — a feeler gone dead, a joint seized, the slow tempo finally slowing to true stillness — it does not die so much as *settle*, sinking back down into the wettest seams, dissolving over years into the crust and the runoff, becoming again the raw stock from which the next one will be drawn. The water always returns. So do the Tenders. They are part of what comes back.

This is why the distinction does not hold. You wanted to know if the one crossing the catwalk was a creature or a part of the machine. But the machine is not a thing that *has* parts the way a clock has gears. The machine is a process, and the Tenders are the process noticing itself, reaching back to touch its own surfaces, keeping its own flow true. A river does not have hands. This city grew some.

They do not know you are here, in any sense you would recognize as knowing. But understand that this is not indifference — indifference is fast, indifference is a snub delivered in a moment. The Tenders simply operate below the threshold where a thing your size, moving at your speed, registers as anything at all. You are a flicker. You are a bead of condensation that appeared on the rim of the flywheel and will be gone before the wheel has turned a hand's width. To be noticed by a Tender you would have to stay. You would have to slow. You would have to drink less and move less and let your own quick small tempo wind down toward theirs, until one afternoon a feeler comes to rest against you and tastes your temperature and your pressure and finds you — what? Out of true? In true? A new seam to be tended, or scraped back, or left to grow?

I do not know what they do with what stays.

I know only that the rails are worn to a shine where nothing has walked in a very long time, and that *nothing* is the wrong word, and that the shine is the answer. Something walks there. It walks so slowly the walking looks like rust. It has been walking since before the works completed their only purpose, and it will be walking after, feeling its way along the wet iron in the bruise-colored glow, checking, adjusting, keeping the long breath going — the water coming back, the crust glowing faint, the feeler finding the valve, the quarter-turn begun this morning that will finish, if all goes well, by dark.

They are the company you wanted. Stop and watch one. Watch long enough and it may, eventually, watch you back. That is either the most or the least lonely thing in the city, and I have never been able to decide which, and I have been here — by my count, which means nothing — a very long time.
