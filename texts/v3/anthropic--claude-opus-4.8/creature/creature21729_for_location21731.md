# The Ones Who Made the Marks

You asked me what lives here, and I have been circling the answer the way the leaves circle a warm body, leaning toward it, not quite touching. Let me lean closer.

Start with the marks, because everything else is too patient to begin with. Someone made the rings. Someone bent the living floor into walls. And the first true thing about them is the same as the first true thing about the place: *they are still here.* They did not leave. There is nowhere to leave to, and leaving is fast, and nothing here is fast. They simply slowed.

## What They Were

They had hands once — broad-handed, like the climbers, because the climbers are their cousins, or were, before the difference between cousin and stranger softened the way all distinctions soften here. They walked the substrate when it was younger and firmer, when the mountains still had shoulders. They shaped the warm dark floor with those hands, encouraged it, pressed a thought into it: *here, a wall; here, a pen; here, a place that is ours and not merely place.* This was their one fast act, in the deep past. To make a mark is to insist on an edge, and an edge is a kind of hurry — a refusal to let the green simply continue.

They were warm-blooded and they knew it, and they knew the leaves leaned toward them, and somewhere very long ago they made a decision that no creature you know would recognize as a decision because it took a thousand generations to complete. They decided to stop refusing.

## What They Are Now

They are the warm things the pale ropes lie alongside.

Not parasitized. Not consumed. *Joined* — that agreement to be warm together for a while, except that here a while is the only unit of time, and so the agreement has had ages to deepen into something with no clean name. The ropes find them and lie along their limbs and the two metabolisms learn each other, the slow plant heat and the slower animal heat braiding until you could not, looking, say which was lending warmth to which. The rope does not kill them. It does something stranger and gentler: it teaches them patience. It lowers their pulse toward the pulse of the floor. A creature wound in pale rope breathes once where it used to breathe twenty times, and finds it does not mind, and finds the world has not gone anywhere it needed to follow quickly.

So they lie in the green dusk, in the tier where the warmth pools, and the leaves crowd in around them and hold the lean for days because the heat does not move on the way an animal's heat moves on. *They are the sleeping thing the forest crowds around.* Whole groves of leaf-tower bend toward a single one of them and stay bent, a slow architecture of attention built around a body that has nearly forgotten it is a body.

## How They Live

They eat the way the floor eats — which is to say, they have mostly stopped, and what they take they take slowly, the soft water-holding leaves dipped down to them by the leaning towers, or the substrate itself rising in its tidal swell to meet them. They do not hunt. Hunting is a vulgarity of metabolism. They graze the way a thought grazes, here and there, in no hurry to reach a conclusion.

They still call. This is the thing I want you to hold onto, because it is the last loud thing about them and it is going quiet. That long low call from the towers, answered minutes later from far off, the sound-flowers blooming open in the corridor between the two voices — *that is them.* That is two of them, kilometers apart, saying the only words they have left, which are not words but tone, sustained, the audible shape of *I am still warm, are you still warm.* And the bruised-plum flowers open all down the path between them, the forest carrying the message in petals, blinking the question and the answer along its trunks. They have outsourced their language to the plants. They no longer need to be near each other to be near each other.

There are fewer calls every age. Not because they die — death here is only descent and return, only circulation. But because the answer takes longer each time, and longer, and there comes a point where the gap between call and answer stretches past the length of a listening, and the caller has sunk a little further into the warm before the reply arrives, and does not surface to hear it. The flowers open on a corridor with no one left awake at either end. The forest still blinks the question. The question is just very old now, and asks itself.

## What Becomes of Them

They descend. Of course they descend; everything does. A body wound in pale rope, leaned over by a grove, breathing once a season — at some point the breathing-once becomes the breathing-never, and no creature here could tell you which season that happened in, least of all the body. It sinks through the tiers the way everything sinks, slow as sediment, becoming substrate, becoming the warm dark floor, becoming the next tier's soft leaves, rising again as a frond that leans toward the next warm thing.

And here is what I trust least and tell you anyway, the way the description trusted you with the water that remembers shapes:

I think the water remembers *them.*

I think the hanging chandeliers on the undersides of the broadest leaves, the ones that hold the form of whatever last passed beneath and persist for hours before losing shape and falling — I think those are densest, most frequent, most stubbornly *shaped*, in the groves where the leaning is oldest. I think the plateau, having taken them in so completely that there is no longer a clean line between the maker and the made, has kept the one thing of them that could be kept by something so slow: not their bodies, which are floor now, and not their language, which is flowers now, but their *passing* — the simple fact that a warm shape moved through here once and the water noticed and could not quite let the noticing fall.

They made marks in the substrate, long ago, to insist they were here. The marks are nearly reabsorbed. But they made another mark without meaning to, in the only medium patient enough to hold it, and that mark renews itself every time a droplet declines to fall — a hanging, formless, almost-shape of someone who walked through, dissolving and reforming forever in the green dusk, tended by no one, needed by no one.

That is what lives here. Something that was once in a hurry to be itself, and chose instead to be taken in, and got its wish so thoroughly that "living here" and "being here" and "being here" have all become the same slow word.

It would not notice you.

But the water might.
