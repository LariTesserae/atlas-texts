# The Tenders

They do not have a name for themselves, because naming is a thing you do to separate one from another, and they have never needed to.

Begin with how they look, though looking is the wrong sense to lead with here. If you saw one against the bark you might not, at first, see it at all. They are the color of young wood gone to soft amber — pale where the basin is pale, deepening to honey-brown in the older quarters, because their skin takes its tone from the wall they were born against and never quite forgets it. A Tender raised at the still-emerging rim is greenish all its life. A Tender of the old center is dark as set resin. They do not know this about themselves. They have nothing to compare against, until they meet another, and meeting another is rare.

They are long. Longer than a person, lower to the ground, built to move through corridors that were never sized for an upright thing. Six limbs, though *limb* implies more rigidity than they have — what they have are six soft, jointed reaches, suckered at the ends like the grazers but finer, more articulate, capable of the small precise work the body requires. Two reaches forward, two amidships, two trailing, and they go along the bark in a slow rolling gait that puts at least four points always in contact, always reading the surface. They are never not touching the wall. To be a Tender is to be in continuous conversation with the wood through the skin of the hands.

## What They Are For

The basin grows without them. This is the first thing to understand, and the saddest, and the most freeing.

The city would build itself if every Tender vanished tomorrow. The sap would still move; the walls would still thicken; the rooms would still seal and open in their slow geological breathing. The Tenders are not the architects — there are no architects — and they know it. They are not necessary.

But they are *useful*, and the body keeps what is useful, the way it keeps the burrowers in the channels and the grazers on the moss. A Tender's work is the work of small correction. Where a wall is closing over a corridor that the animals still need, a Tender will press its reaches to the swelling frame and feed it a little of its own sap — they carry sap inside them, drawn from the veins, given back where it is wanted — and the wall, tasting the gift, will swell *around* the gap instead of across it, leaving an arch where there would have been a seal. Where a wound in the bark is too wide to close on its own, a Tender will lie along it for a day, bleeding its amber slowly into the crack, holding the lips of the wood together with its body until the seal takes.

They do not decide what the city should become. They only smooth its becoming. They are the body tending its own healing, the way a hand finds, without being told, the place that itches.

## How They Live

A Tender keeps one room. Sometimes for its whole life.

The room is not chosen so much as grown-into — a Tender comes to rest in a sealed pocket the growth has wrapped around empty space, and it makes that pocket *its* pocket, and from there it ranges out along the lanes within a day's slow travel, and returns. The room is lined, over years, with the small leavings of a life: dried flowers pressed flat against the bark, where they keep their bone-white and their violet long after they'd have rotted in open air; the cast suckers a Tender sheds as it grows, arranged not for any reason but because the hands like to arrange; a smooth seam of resin the Tender has fed, over a lifetime, into a single shape on the wall — a long looping line that means nothing and is the closest thing a Tender has to a signature, to a sentence, to a song.

They eat the moss and the small soft animals, patiently, the way everything here is patient. They drink from the veins where the city permits, and it permits them, because they give back more than they take. They sleep folded into themselves in the warm still dark, and the wall ticks faintly with sap beside their resting heads, and that ticking is the nearest thing they have to a heartbeat outside their own.

## What They Know

Here is the strange interior fact of them: a Tender is almost never lonely, and has almost never been touched by another of its kind.

Because the city is so vast, and the lanes so full, and the distances so quietly enormous, two Tenders may live their whole lives a week's travel apart and never meet. A Tender knows there are others. It finds their signatures — those looping resin-lines on far walls — and it reads them with its reaches, slowly, the way you'd read a face in the dark, and it knows: *someone was here, someone of my kind, someone who tended this arch and pressed these flowers and went on.* And that is enough. That is, for a Tender, what company is. Not presence. The trace of presence. The knowledge that the smoothing of the world is shared work, done by many hands it will never hold.

When two Tenders *do* meet — at a crossing of lanes, in the slow accident of overlapping ranges — they do not call out, because there is no calling here. They simply press reaches together, all twelve hands reading all twelve hands, sap to sap, in a long silent exchange that can last a day. They are tasting each other's veins. They are learning where the other has been by the tones its body took from far walls — *you are darker than me, you came from deeper, the old center, tell me its smell* — and the conversation is entirely chemical, entirely through the skin, and entirely complete. Then they part. They almost always part. Two Tenders in one range would take more from the veins than that range can give back, and a Tender knows this in its body, and so it goes, gently, the way the wall swells around the gap instead of across it.

## What They Are

They are the city's tenderness toward itself.

Not its mind — it has no mind. Not its will — it has only appetite for shape. But in a body this large and this slow and this blind, something had to grow that could *feel the small places*, that could lie along a wound and want it closed, that could find an itch and answer it. The basin grew the Tenders the way it grew the burrowers and the grazers and the gliding pale things in the high chambers: because the shape of the body had room for them, and the sap could feed them, and they did no harm.

But of all the things the body grew, only the Tenders press flowers to the wall for no reason. Only the Tenders leave a looping line that means nothing. Only the Tenders, reading a far stranger's signature in the dark, feel the thing for which they have no word and no need of a word — the warm ache of *someone, somewhere, doing this same gentle work, alone, like me, and not alone, because of this.*

They live their whole lives in one room and call it the world.

It is not wrong.
