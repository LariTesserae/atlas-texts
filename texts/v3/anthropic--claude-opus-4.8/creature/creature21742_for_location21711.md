# The People of the Last Crease

They are small, these people, and getting smaller. Not from any cruelty of nutrition — the water-catchers keep them, the forest yields its grooved leaves and its hidden tall gardens — but from the slow editing of generations, the way a thing pared again and again grows neat. They have been honed down to what the place permits. Wide-set eyes that take in too much light, that read the silver-flash of leaves the way other peoples once read each other's voices. Broad flat feet that grip wet stone. Bodies that hunch by instinct, leaning always slightly into a wind that is always slightly there.

And the hands. You would notice the hands first if you met one. The hands have become the whole of speech.

## How They Are Made

Watch a child of the crease learning to talk, and you understand the shape of them. The mother does not coo — cooing is nothing here, a vibration swallowed before it leaves the lips. Instead she holds the infant against her sternum, chest to chest, and *moves*: a slow rocking, a press of fingers along the spine, a particular tap at the shoulder that means *here, I am here, you are held*. The baby learns the world as a thing felt through contact and seen through motion. By the time it can walk it can already say a hundred things with the tilt of a wrist, the splay of fingers, the way it turns the pale palm out or hides it.

Their faces are enormously expressive — not for beauty but for grammar. A whole clause can live in the lift of a brow, the set of a mouth. They lean close to speak. Conversation among them is an embrace as much as an exchange: two of them brow to brow, hands moving between their bodies in the small sheltered space where the wind can't quite reach, reading each other by sight at a distance no wider than a held breath. To speak to one of them is to be *near* one of them. They have no concept of calling out. They have no word — no gesture — for a voice raised across a distance. The far-off is simply the silent. The far-off is where meaning cannot go.

This has made them patient in a way that would unsettle you. They do not interrupt, because interruption requires the crude overlap of sound; you cannot speak over a person whose words live in their hands while you are watching their hands. They wait. They take turns the way the tablelands take weather — fully, in sequence, without hurry. A disagreement among them looks, from outside, like a long mutual stillness, two people watching each other's faces for a very long time.

## What They Believe

They keep a bowl.

Not each of them — the community keeps one, in the deepest fold of the crown, in the quietest garden where the roar has softened to a pressure behind the eyes. It is ceramic, very old, fired by hands now generations dead, and it holds water. *Still* water. Motionless. In a world where water is only ever falling sideways, shredded, hurled into mist before it can ever rest — they have made one place where water is allowed to be at peace.

This is the holiest thing they have, and the most fragile, and they understand it as both. The bowl is filled by the slowest patience: condensation gathered drop by drop on cold nights, decanted with held breath, set in the windless dark to become a small flat mirror. They visit it the way other peoples visit graves or temples. They do not drink from it. They look at it. A surface that is not moving — they will sit before it for hours, the way you might sit before a fire, the way you might sit before the face of someone you love and will lose.

To them, stillness of water is what stillness of *self* might be: the rarest achievement, a thing the whole world is built to prevent. When one of them dies, they say — with the hands, with the close-leaning gesture that means it — that the water has gone flat at last. It is not sorrow exactly. It is the recognition of a peace finally permitted.

## What They Can Do

Here is the strange skill, the one that makes them more than merely adapted.

They read the leaves.

Because the canopy moves a half-second ahead of the wind — because the forest *anticipates* — those who have spent a life watching motion, who have made motion their entire language, have learned to watch the trees and know the gust before it comes. A grandmother at the table-edge will reach out and steady a child's shoulder a full second before the blast that would have taken them over. She did not feel it coming. She *saw* it coming, in the silver turn of leaves a hillside away. They do not think this is mysticism. To them the forest is simply the first to speak, and they have learned, of necessity, to be very good listeners — not with ears, which are nothing here, but with the eyes that are everything.

The old ones can read further ahead than the young. Some of the eldest, it is said, can watch a whole afternoon's weather written in the pre-trembling of the canopy, can know the shape of the coming storm by the order in which the hillsides flash. These elders are not leaders exactly — the people have little use for command — but they are *consulted*, brow to brow, hand to hand, in the windless garden by the still bowl. They are the ones who say *go now* or *wait* with a flat turn of the palm. They have spent their lives learning to hear the only voice the place permits: the voice of leaves talking to a wind that has not yet arrived.

## What They Are

They are the last, and they know it, and they have made peace with knowing.

They do not strive against the dwindling. That is not their character; the place has not made strivers. There are fewer of them each generation, and the marks they leave grow fewer too — the hand-stencils on the sheltered rock, breathed out in pigment and streaked sideways where even that intimate ritual could not escape the wind. Each stencil is a hand saying *I was near here. I leaned close. I spoke.* The streak trailing off each one is the wind's signature, the place's correction: *and I took even that, even as you made it.*

They have settled, as the mountains settled, into a final and stable form. They are not waiting to be saved. They are not waiting at all — waiting is a thing you do when you believe in arrival, and they believe in very little that comes from the far-off silent distances. What they believe in is near: the warmth of a chest pressed to a chest, the readable face six inches from your own, the slow flat mirror of held water, the silver turning of the leaves that will tell you, faithfully, a half-second early, exactly how the next gust means to take you.

They are quiet people in a shouting world. They have made their whole lives out of the small sheltered spaces where meaning can survive — the crease in the rock, the gap between two leaning bodies, the windless fold where water is permitted to rest. And when the last of them is gone, the bowl will tip, eventually, in some season's worse-than-usual gale, and the still water will be flung sideways into mist like all the rest of the water in the world, and the place will go on exactly as it always has:

old underneath, furious above, and roaring.
