They are very small, and there are a great many of them, and they are the reason for the silence.

You will not see them at first. You may not see them at all, the way you do not see the individual threads in a cloth you are standing on. But run your hand along the moss — the upholstery-thick moss that covers every cool surface — and feel how it is not quite moss. The structure is too regular under the green. There is a substrate beneath the living part, a fine articulated lacework, and it is warm in the same patient way the pipes are warm, and if you held very still and your eyes were better than they are, you would see it *flexing*. Slowly. A valve-creep of the body. A week's worth of motion taken across a week.

These are the tenders. Not the makers — the makers are gone, or were never bodies at all, or are a decision made so long ago that it no longer requires anyone to keep deciding it. The tenders are what the decision left behind to do the work. They are the staff of the patient green engine, and they are everywhere, and that is precisely why you take them for landscape.

Consider the silence again, because the silence is *them*. A flywheel spins without friction not because friction was engineered away but because something is at every bearing, all the time, a film of small bodies pressed into the gap, taking the heat and the grind into themselves and passing it along, body to body, out into the cool air where it dissipates as the faint perpetual warmth you mistook for residual industry. They are the lubricant. They are the gaskets. They are the muffling. When the condenser's drop falls and does not splash, it is because something received it — a soft palm of them spread below to catch the water and lower it into the moss without a sound, the way a careful host sets down a glass. They have made noiselessness their whole vocation. To them a splash would be a wound; a hum, a hemorrhage; the roar a factory *ought* to make would be the sound of everything they exist to prevent.

What are they?

Start with the small ones, the working body, the unit you'd hold in your palm if you could find one isolated, which you cannot, because they are never isolated. Picture something between a woodlouse and a wet leaf — segmented, pale, soft on the underside and faintly iridescent on the back, the iridescence not for display but for shedding the bright weightless light before it can heat them. They have many small feet and the feet are also mouths and the mouths are also, in some sense, hands. They eat condensation and excrete adhesive. They climb each other as readily as they climb rock. A single one, removed from the rest, would go still within an hour, not from any injury but from a kind of bereavement of purpose — it has no function alone, the way a single stitch has no function once cut from the cloth.

But they are not really singular at all, and this is the thing it takes standing in the valley a long while to understand. They aggregate. They have learned — or were given — the art of becoming larger by adhesion, of stacking and interlocking into bodies of the appropriate size for the task at hand. The moss-substrate is thousands of them lying flat and breathing in unison, regulating soil-moisture by the slow opening and closing of their backs. The film in the bearing is a few hundred, organized into a sheet thin as oil. And the larger shapes —

The shape you saw once, deep in the foliage, the one you doubted. That was them too. That was a great many of them gathered up into a working body the size of a deer, sent out to do something that required reach and leverage — to ease a pipe back onto its gantry, perhaps, where a century of moss-weight had begun to drag it down. A maintenance form. It moved slowly because everything here moves slowly, and it moved silently because it is, after all, the silence itself walking, and when its task was done it would have lain down at the base of a tree and simply *dispersed*, the thousands of it crawling apart in every direction back into the general green, leaving nothing for you to have seen, which is why you doubt you saw it.

They do not roar. They cannot roar; they have no throat between them, no single will loud enough to want to. What they have instead is consensus, arrived at the way fog arrives — without a front, without an announcement, a general settling of agreement across the whole region at once. When the gauge needle must drift across a week, it is because for a week some uncountable committee of them, spread across miles, has been very gently, very quietly, deciding that it should.

And here is the part that will sit with you on the long walk back down the young sharp rock. They are not maintaining the machines for the machines' sake, and not for the makers', who are not coming. The product is the jungle and the jungle is also their body — there is no line between the engine and the garden and the tenders, no place where infrastructure ends and inhabitant begins. They drink the fog the machines condense; they are the moss the water feeds; they are the hands that tend the moss; they are the muffling that keeps the hands quiet. The whole green country breathing condensation under the unblinking sky is a single slow animal grooming itself in its sleep, and you have been standing inside its hush mistaking it for a place.

This is why your pulse is the loudest thing for miles. It is the only sound here that hasn't yet been *agreed upon* — the only motion in the whole patient valley that no one has reached out, softly, body upon countless body, to quiet.

They have noticed it. They are not in any hurry. They never are.

But the moss under your boots is warm, and it has begun, very slowly, to flex.
