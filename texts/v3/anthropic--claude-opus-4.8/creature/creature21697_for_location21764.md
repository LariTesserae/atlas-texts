# The People of Telch

You asked them where the city ends, and they looked at you kindly, and could not understand. That should have told you the first thing about them: they do not believe in edges. Not the city's. Not their own.

## Their Bodies

They are short. Not stunted — *settled*, the way water finds its level. A people stretched tall would be a people who expected to be seen across distance, who organized their lives around the far horizon and the standing-up posture of someone scanning for it. There is no far horizon here; the haze eats it, the curve of the world eats it. So they are built low and broad and stable, with a wide-set planted way of standing that reads to you as stubbornness and is actually only *balance* — the ingrained physical wisdom of a body that has spent its whole life on surfaces that might soften underfoot, on decks that shift, on the two-millimeter mirror that is always, faintly, *moving*.

Their feet are remarkable. Splayed, long-toed, the skin of the sole thickened and finely grooved like the pad of a climbing thing, so that they grip the wet floor without thought. They go barefoot almost always; shoes are for the dry rim and the dry rim is for the dead. A Telchin foot reads the floor the way your fingertips read braille. They feel the amber-warning shift coming up through the arch before their eyes catch the color-change in the seam. *The grain*, they call it — that shared constantly-updated sense — and it lives in their feet first.

Their skin is the color of the light: brown-gold, steam-toned, but it is not pigment so much as *condition*. They are always faintly wet, always faintly filmed, and over a lifetime the biofilm colonizes them too, the way it colonizes everything — a thin commensal sheen in the creases of the knuckles, behind the ears, along the spine, harmless, even useful, a living gray-gold patina that the eldest wear like a second more honest skin. To touch an old Telchin's hand is to touch something halfway to being part of the city. They do not find this strange. You would.

They sweat constantly and notice it never. Their bodies have given up the entire project of staying dry, redirected all that effort elsewhere — into staying *warm*, into not bloating, into a metabolism tuned to salt-bright raw fish-flesh and dissolved trace metals and water, always water, taken in great unceremonious gulps straight from the cleaner channels. They have no word for *thirst* that is separate from their word for *fear*. The two feelings, to them, are the same alarm: the body sensing that the wet is going away.

## How They Move

This is the thing that unnerves you, and you are right to be unnerved, because it is genuinely a different way of being a person.

They do not have personal space. They have personal *flow*. Packed a body's-width apart on every deck, plaza, and stilted walkway, they have evolved — culturally, maybe physically, the line is blurred here as everywhere — a courtesy of crowds so total that it functions below the level of decision. Ten thousand small yieldings a minute. A Telchin in a crowd is not navigating obstacles; the Telchin *is* the crowd, a single cell in a moving sheet, and the sheet has its own intelligence about where it is going. They lean, give, turn shoulder, slip through, all of it wordless, all of it constant, none of it resented. To them, a person who *stops* in a flow — who plants and demands the world go around them — is committing something close to violence. It is the one rudeness they cannot forgive. You did it, your first day, without knowing. You felt the temperature of the crowd change around you and did not understand why.

They touch each other ceaselessly and mean nothing by it. A hand on the back to signal *passing behind*; a grip on the forearm to share balance on a shifting deck; whole conversations conducted by the press and answer of bodies in a barge too loud for words. Their language has a tactile register, a grammar of nudges, that the dry-rim traders never fully learn. You can speak a hundred trade-tongues in Telch and still be illiterate.

## What They Believe

Here is where they become difficult to hold in the mind.

They live inside a thing that is alive and thinking and does not know they are there. They know this. It is not a secret, not a horror, not a tragedy to them — it is simply *the situation*, the bottommost fact, the way you know the ground will pull you down if you step off a height. The city computes in its slow saturated way; the biofilm remembers; the pumping pulses; and across all of that vast not-quite-mind goes the faint irritation of several million people, like mites in skin, like gut-flora, present and unaddressed.

And they have made *peace* with this. More than peace. They have made it the center of everything.

Because think about what it does to you, to grow up knowing your home is indifferent to you — genuinely, structurally indifferent, not cruel, just *unaware*. It could reabsorb your plaza tonight and feel nothing, intend nothing, and you would simply have to read the amber and move. There is no appeal. There is no one to pray to inside the walls, because the walls are not listening, were never listening, are busy with vast slow purposes you cannot read.

So they do not pray to the city. They pray *with* it. Their whole spiritual life is an act of *attunement* — of learning the grain so well that you flow with the city's self-revision instead of being caught by it. To die by being too slow, too proud, too planted when the amber rose — to be reabsorbed because you refused to move — this is the only sin they truly believe in. They call it, in the oldest tongue, something that translates roughly as *standing dry*. To stand dry is to insist on yourself against the flow of the world. It is the death of fools and tyrants. Their worst curse, spat in a crowd, is simply: *may you stand dry.*

And the corresponding virtue — the thing they admire most in a person, the thing they want carved (in biofilm, in a seam, knowing it will be reabsorbed and that's the *point*) onto the memory of a good life — is *fluency*. Not cleverness. Not wealth, though there is wealth here, in harvested metal and channel-rights and skiff-fleets. *Fluency:* the grace of one who reads the grain perfectly, who never fights the tide, who yields ten thousand times a minute and is never once diminished by it, who moves through the densest impossible crowd of the world and leaves no friction behind, only the faint warmth of having been there.

## What They Do With the Dead

They give them to the water.

Not as disposal — as *return*. A body that has stopped flowing is given back to the channels, to the fish-things, to the biofilm, to the slow circulating substance of the city, so that the metals it carried can be laid down again somewhere new, in some new pattern, in some plaza not yet poured. The city will not notice. That is, again, the point. The dead Telchin does not go *to* the city as an offering received. The dead Telchin simply *rejoins the medium*, becomes weather, becomes grain, becomes part of the warm wet thinking-thing that never knew them and never will and carries them forward anyway, indifferent and total, the way the tide carries everything.

A Telchin watching a loved one dissolve into the channel does not feel they are losing them to nothing. They feel they are giving them, at last, the perfect fluency — the final yielding, the body so completely at one with the flow that there is no body left to resist it. *They have stopped standing*, the mourners say, which is the closest thing they have to *they are at peace.* And then they net the small fish from that same water, the next morning, and eat them raw and warm and salt-bright, and there is no horror in this, only the long unbroken circle of a people who never once believed they were separate from the thing they live inside.

## Why They Will Not Leave

You think it is fear of the dry rim. The silence, the deadness, the country where nothing weeps.

It is deeper than that. Out there, on dry land, *you would matter.* You would be a discrete thing, edged, separate, standing up against a horizon, casting a shadow, leaving footprints in something that holds the shape of you. Out there a person is a fact the world records.

They cannot bear it. To them that is not freedom — it is the most profound loneliness imaginable, the loneliness of standing dry forever, of being a thing the world *notices and keeps apart*. They would rather be a mite in the warm indifferent skin of a living city that will never know their name, packed to the last finger's-width against the bodies of everyone they love, talking forever under the wall of sound, wet and warm and flowing and unrecorded —

— than be *seen*, out there, alone, in the unbearable silence, by no one at all.

They call that the world.

And you begin, after a while, to understand why.
