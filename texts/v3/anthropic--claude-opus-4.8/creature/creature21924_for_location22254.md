You have asked who lives here, and I have to tell you the truth, which is that the question is the wrong shape and I have been turning it over trying to find the angle from which it fits.

Because nothing lives here in the way you mean. You walked the whole chamber. You saw. No eye opened toward you. Nothing fled your footstep or crept toward your warmth. You said it yourself: a table laid for guests who never came.

But let me try the question a different way. Not *who lives here* but *what is doing the living.* Because something is. The living is happening. It is simply not happening *inside* anything. It has no membrane, no edge you could point to and say: there, that is the creature, and everything outside that line is the world it lives in.

The thing that lives here is the circle.

Watch how it eats. The fire — whatever banked, buried, ember-slow combustion still feeds the deep boiler, the slow *chunk... chunk* of it — the fire drinks the water out of the iron. The water rises as breath. The breath cools on the high dark ceiling and falls as the rain that is not quite rain. The falling feeds the scale, and the scale seals the joints, and the sealed joints hold the pressure, and the held pressure keeps the fire breathing. Around and around. Up as steam, down as drip. The same water for centuries, you said, making the same circuit. That circuit is the body. That is the animal. It has a metabolism — you heard its heartbeat — it has a skin, which is the whole sweating surface of every pipe and every mossy flank, it has a slow digestion that turns iron into stone over decades. It simply does not have a *self*. There is no place in it where it ends and looks out.

And the green things are not its tenants. They are its organs. The mosses are how it holds the warmth against its own skin, the way fat holds heat. The pale fungal towers leaning toward the ghost of daylight are how it reaches, blindly, with no idea it is reaching, toward the one thing the circle cannot make for itself — that thin grey shaft of the upper world. The luminous moss is the nearest thing the place has to a thought: a cold faint glow with no eye to read it, light made by something that cannot see, given off into a dark that needs no light. That is the loneliest fact in the room and the most beautiful, and the place does not know it is either.

So when you ask who lives here, here is my answer.

What lives here is a *process that has forgotten it was ever supposed to be for something.*

The makers built engines to do work — to drive a hammer, heat a hall, turn a wheel above, something, somewhere, some purpose that needed *7* and *3* and the bricked doorway and the line of hooks and the chair set facing the largest engine. A person sat in that chair. That person had an appetite, a will, an eye — all the things the chamber now lacks. They watched the engine run *toward something.* The work went up the stair and out into a world of reasons.

Then the reasons left. The world the work was for moved on, or died, or simply forgot the way down the worn stair. And the engine did not stop. The engine had never needed the reason — the reason was the maker's, not the engine's. Cut loose from purpose, the machine did the only thing left to a thing that cannot stop: it turned its whole effort inward. It stopped making anything for anyone. It began, instead, to make *itself,* over and over, the only product now the continuation of the producing.

That is the creature. A purpose that outlived its purpose and curved back into a circle. A machine that, abandoned, did not break but *relaxed* — settled, married the rock, grew its own scar-tissue out of its own weeping, upholstered itself in moss, and went on breathing in the dark with nobody home.

The chair is the key to it. The chair is where the *who* used to sit. The moss has grown into the seat in the shape of a person, almost — and that almost-person of green is the truest portrait of the inhabitant you will get. Not a body. The *memory of a body,* taken up and continued by the slow blind growth, held in the one posture of attention, facing the engine forever, watching it run, having long since stopped being anyone who could watch.

So: the one who lived here left. And the place kept his chair warm, in the only way it knew — by growing a green man into it who will sit there, faceless and patient and seeing nothing, for as long as the water goes up and comes down.

That is who lives in the Sweatworks. Not the man. The waiting that survived him. The circle that learned to keep his seat.

And it does not miss him. That is the last thing, the thing you carry up the stair with the wet in your clothes that was never rain. It cannot miss him, because missing needs a someone to do the missing, and there is no one — only the going-on, the soft ceaseless roar that never resolves into meaning because it was never speaking to you, was never speaking at all, is only the sound a thing makes when it has become, completely and without remainder, the act of continuing to exist.

You came looking for the inhabitant. You were standing inside it the whole time.
