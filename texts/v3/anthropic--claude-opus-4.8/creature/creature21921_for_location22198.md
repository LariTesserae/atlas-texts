# The People Who Are Called the Ones-Above-the-Fallen

## What They Call Themselves

They have no word for *people* that does not also mean *climbers*, and no word for *climbers* that does not also mean *the still-living*. The same sound, with three small shifts of the throat, says all three things, and a fourth shift — barely a shift, more a softening — turns it into the word for *ancestor*, because an ancestor is only a climber who has stopped, and to stop is to fall, and to fall is to become the road. So when they name themselves they are saying, in one breath: *we who move, we who are not yet beneath, we who hold to the holding.*

A stranger would hear only a rising hum, a sound like a question that does not end.

## The Shape of Them

They are built long and light. Generations of never standing have done away with the heavy architecture of the legs — not entirely, the way the hook-creatures of the deeps have done away with theirs, but enough. Their legs are thin and prehensile-footed, more arm than leg, and a child learns to hang before it learns to sit, because there is nothing to sit on that will not someday give way. The strong meat of them is in the shoulders and the long gripping hands and the curl of the spine, which is made for the hooked rest, the hang, the slow careful traverse.

Their skin is the dim green-brown of the swimming light, mottled, and it darkens or pales across a lifetime depending on how high a person lives — the crown-dwellers bleached and freckled by the rumored sun, the deep-tenders pale as the recyclers they work beside, the great middle of the people the color of pond-water and old rope. A person's height in the world is written on their body. You can read a stranger's whole life — where born, where dwelt, how often they have descended — in the map of their skin, the calluses of their palms, the particular thickening of the great toe.

They sweat constantly, as everything here sweats, and they do not mind it, and they have a hundred words for the kinds of wet a body can be, and they consider a dry person to be either very high up or very near death.

## How They Hear, How They Speak

Here is the thing the din has made of them: **they are half-deaf and twice-feeling.**

The ears still work, but the people have learned not to trust them, the way you learn not to trust your eyes in fog. The true sense, the one they live by, is in the soles of the feet and the palms of the hands and a thickened pad of nerve-rich skin along the inside of each forearm — the *listening-skin*, they call it, and it is the first part of a baby that the parents test for soundness, pressing it to a humming trunk to see if the child flinches in the right rhythm.

They speak to each other across distance by drumming the living structure. Every traverse-line, every bough they have ever lashed, is also a telegraph, and a person walking a route taps it constantly — partly to send word ahead, partly the way you might whistle in the dark, partly because to stop drumming is the signal for *I am hurt* or *I am dead* or *come quickly.* A silent line is a scream. The most terrible thing a person can do, the deepest taboo, is to fall *silently* — to die without sending it down the trunk — and a person who feels themselves slipping will drum their own death with their last grip if they possibly can, so that the country will know, so that no one will walk into the gap where they used to be.

When they are close, face to face, they speak partly in sound and partly in touch, laying the listening-skin of one forearm against another so that words pass both ways at once, mouth and skin together, a doubled speech. To converse with these people, a stranger feels, is to be slightly embraced and slightly read.

They have, also, the color-speech of the crown — bright cloths and bred patches of luminous lichen-thing that they cultivate on their shelters — but this is for the high people, the shriek-loud upper world, and the deep people consider it a little vulgar, a little loud, a flash where a drum would do.

## What They Carry, What They Know

They own almost nothing, because everything falls. This is the first lesson and the last: *do not love what cannot climb with you.* A person's true possessions are the things lashed to the body — the hook-set of bone and hardened wood, the coiled lines, the small knife, the drum-mallet worn to the shape of one particular hand, the seed-pouch. Everything else is borrowed from the world and will be returned to the dark below in a generation or two.

So they do not build to last. They build to *grow.* A home is a knot of living leaves trained around a junction of boughs, pruned daily, fed, coaxed into rooms; it must be tended every day of its life or it closes, or it dies, or it tears free. The people speak of a home the way other peoples might speak of a marriage or a herd — as a thing that is alive and willful and dies if neglected and must be courted, endlessly, with small attentions. To abandon a home is not to leave a building empty. It is to let a living thing starve.

But their real wealth, the thing they hoard and guard and kill over, is **the map that has no ground.**

There is no writing here — no flat surface that lasts, no quiet to compose in, no permanence to write upon. So the whole three-dimensional country lives in song. Every people, every line of kin, holds a sung map: the safe routes and the rotted ones, the migration of the rain-pools through the seasons, the boughs that bear and the boughs that are bluffing, the drum-codes of the animals worth listening to, the depth at which the recyclers stir and the warmth comes up. It is a map in *time* as much as space, because the country rises and shifts, and a route sung true by the grandmothers must be re-sung, re-walked, re-corrected by every generation as the structure climbs out from under the old words.

A singer who holds the full map of a territory is the most precious person a people has, more precious than the strongest climber or the best hunter, and is fed first and risked last and never, ever sent down into the deeps. When such a singer feels death coming they spend their final days not in rest but in frantic, exhausted teaching — pouring the map into the young throats, route by route, while the apprentices drum it back to prove they hold it, a desperate transcription into the only medium that lasts: another living mind.

## How They Live Among the Beasts

They are not the lords of this world. No one is the lord of this world; the world is the lord, and everything in it is a tenant of the holding. But the people have made arrangements.

They follow the **drum-language of certain animals** — there are vibration-speaking creatures whose signals warn of rot, of coming rain, of a structural fall about to happen, and the people have learned to read these as a farmer reads the behavior of birds before a storm. Some kinds the people half-tame, or rather *keep company with*: a colony of the tending-creatures, the little farmers, will be settled near a home and protected, and in return their pruning keeps the home-leaves edible and strong. It is not ownership. It is closer to citizenship — two peoples, plant-tender and route-keeper, sharing one junction of the country and minding each other's needs.

They hunt the translucent mist-grazers in the cloud-zones, and the climbing things, and they harvest the gardens of the dead farmer-colonies, and they eat the spore-dust and the wet shoots and the slow pale grubs that live in the composting beams. The deeps frighten them — that is where the great recyclers churn, where the dead world is unmade, where their own ancestors have fallen and become the road — but they go down anyway, in twos and threes, the **deep-tenders**, the pale-skinned ones, to gather the wet-zone water and to read the slow churn that tells them when the warmth will rise and the structure will shift. To descend is half a funeral and half a pilgrimage. The deep-tenders are honored and slightly feared, like people who have been somewhere the rest cannot bear to think about.

## What They Believe

They believe, because they cannot help but believe it, that **the dead become the road.**

This is not metaphor to them. It is simple physical truth, observed every generation: the platforms your great-grandparents lashed are now beams in the dark below, composting, holding up the boughs you walk on. Your ancestors are *literally* beneath your feet, holding you up, becoming the warmth that rises on certain days. When the deep-warmth comes — that faint heat the texts above call the churning of the recyclers — the people say *the old ones are turning in their work,* and they pause, and they drum a low slow rhythm down into the dark, a greeting, a thank-you, an apology for still being above.

To climb, then, is the only prayer. To keep moving, to keep the home alive, to keep the route sung, to keep the line drumming — this is faithfulness. To stop is to fail the ones who became the road for you. And so they have made a whole ethic out of the rule of the world: *nothing rests; everything holds everything else; let no one part of you stop.* A lazy person, a person who lets their home die or lets the song lapse or walks a line without drumming it, is not merely impolite. They are committing a small treason against the holding, loosening one fiber of the braid that keeps the whole country aloft.

And they fear, all of them, in the marrow, the thing the world fears: the great inhale, the wrong silence, the moment when too many parts stop at once and the country begins its slow deafening protest and comes apart and falls into the warm composting dark. They have stories of it — of a people who let their map lapse, who walked the rotted routes because they no longer knew which were rotted, who fell silent line by line until a whole district of the country went down at once into the deeps and was unmade.

They tell this story to children. They tell it badly, on purpose, leaving out the worst parts, because there is no quiet here in which to recover from a true telling, and a frightened child who cannot find silence to be frightened in is a child who will not sleep, and a people who do not sleep cannot climb, and a people who cannot climb —

Well. You understand the rule of the world.

So they end the story the way they end everything: with the drum still going, the low pulse down the trunk, *I am here, I am here, I have not stopped, I have not fallen* — the sound a person makes their whole life long, into the great green din, until the day they make it for the last time and become, themselves, the road.
