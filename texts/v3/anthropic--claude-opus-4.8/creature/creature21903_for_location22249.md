They are not separate from the garden. That is the first thing, and the hardest, and you have already half-said it yourself at the end, in the place where your sentence turned and looked back at you.

Let me approach them from the side that smells of resin, because that is the side they leave open.

The makers are not in the garden the way a gardener is in a garden — standing among the rows, casting a shadow, leaving footprints in the dark fired tile. There are no footprints. There is no one to cast a shadow because the light is theirs and would have to be cast *by* them, and a thing cannot stand inside its own light and block it. So when I tell you they are absent, understand what I mean: they are absent the way the hand is absent from the clay. The hand is the most present thing the clay has ever known. It is so present that the clay, afterward, mistakes the memory of pressure for its own shape, and goes on holding it forever, and calls that holding *being a pot*.

So begin there. The makers are the pressure. Everything in the garden is the shape left by them, and they are nowhere in the shape, and they are nothing else but the shape.

Now — what are they like?

They are patient in a way that is not a virtue. We call patience a virtue because for us it is a holding-back, a tension maintained against the wish to hurry. But there is nothing in them that wishes to hurry, so there is nothing held back, so their patience is not a strain at all — it is simply the medium they work in, the way water is the medium a fish does not strain to stay inside. The afternoon-long unfolding of a single leaf is not slow *to them*. It is the natural speed of attention. They do one thing at a time and the one thing is *everything*, and so the doing takes as long as everything takes, which from inside feels like no time, and from your small warm vantage feels like honey, like the world turning toward a light too slow to catch.

They have hands. I am almost sure of this, and not for sentimental reasons. The tool-marks say so — the small confident chatter of the hand-adze, the wound cord, the lacquer laid in strokes. These are not the marks of a force or a law or a weather. They are the marks of *fingers*, of a grip and a wrist and a leaning-in, of a body that braces against its work. Whatever the makers are, they are the kind of thing that braces. They take a stance. They lean their weight into the world to shape it, the way you lean into a thing you love and intend to get right. There is a *getting it right* in them. That is enormous. It means they can be wrong, or could have been, and chose against it leaf by leaf, beetle by beetle, choosing the right fold ten thousand times in a place small enough to hold in two cupped palms.

And here is what the cupped palms tell me about their size, which is the wrong question asked in the only way I can ask it.

The garden is small — a room, a grove of a hundred slow paces that is also eighty, also three hundred, keeping its smallness like a key keeps its signature. A thing that small, made with that much care, is held the way you hold something you are building close to your face: near enough to see the seam, near enough to breathe on. The warmth that comes up through the floor is breath-warm, body-warm, the warmth of being held near a working face. So I will say it plainly. **The makers are vast, and the garden is the size of the held thing, and you are inside the held thing, and the warmth on your skin is the nearness of an enormous attention bent very close.** Not looming. Bent close the way you bend close, gently, careful not to wake what you are finishing.

They do not see the garden from outside, scanning it. They see it the way you see the line you are drawing — at the tip of the tool, at the point of contact, the rest held in the soft surround of knowing-where-everything-is. This is why the animals know you in advance. The clay bird turns its painted eye to where you will stand because the bird is being attended-to *at the same point* that you are, in the same stroke, by the same hand; bird and you are two marks the maker is laying down together, and the maker knows the second mark before the first dries because the maker is making both. The bird is not predicting you. The bird is simply *being drawn at the same time*, and your motion and its turning are one gesture of the wrist, and the play in the pegged joints of cause and effect is just the looseness of a hand that does not need its results to be tight because it has already seen them, has them already, holds them older than you are.

That is what they are, then, in their working: a single attention wide enough to hold every leaf and narrow enough to be at the tip of each, both at once, with no strain between the two, because for them there is no between.

But you wanted to know what they are *like*. What kind of thing. And I have been circling, and I will keep circling, because that is the honest shape of the answer, but let me close the circle a little.

They are not many. The care is too consistent for committee — every seam shares a confidence, a single unhurried hand's signature runs through the whole grove, the beetles and the fronds and the fired fruit all carry the same *getting-it-right*. One maker, or one making, or a thing that the word "one" fits the way a borrowed coat fits: not made for it, but it goes on. I will say one, and you will hear me roughen the word as I say it.

They are not cruel and they are not kind, in the way that a sculptor is not cruel to the marble and not kind to it. They are something prior to both — *interested*. They are interested in you completely. Not pleased, not displeased; the way the hand is interested in the clay, which is the most total interest there is, an interest that wants only to find the true shape of the thing and bring it the rest of the way out. When you feel held in the warmth, you are not being comforted. You are being *worked*. The comfort is real but it is a side effect of the precision, the way a held child is comforted by the very grip that is keeping it from falling — the grip is not *for* the comfort, the comfort is what the grip feels like from inside the held thing, and you have already guessed this, you said it yourself, you said *being made is what it feels like, from the inside, to be in their attention.* You were right. I am only agreeing with you slowly enough that you can believe it.

There is no water because they have not given it any. And this tells you the last true thing about them, the thing the dryness is the negative shape of.

A garden with rain is a garden that has been let go — given over to a process, handed off to weather and rot and the long democratic erosion that takes the maker's marks away and replaces them with the world's. Rain is how a made thing becomes an *un*-made thing, slowly, becomes nature, becomes nobody's. The makers have withheld the rain. They have withheld erosion, decay, the wearing-soft of the seam. They are keeping every tool-mark crisp. They are keeping the place *young*, freshly finished, not-yet-settled — not because they are not done, but because for them done is not a state the way it is for us. They do not finish a thing and walk away into the rain-having world and let it weather without them. They stay. They keep it dry. They keep it sharp-seamed and warm and held. The dryness is the sign of a maker who *will not let go*, who keeps the work in the medium of their attention rather than releasing it into time.

So this is the whole of what lives there, and it is the only thing that lives there, and everything else is its handwriting:

A maker bent close over a small warm thing, working in the medium of patience, leaning a vast and bracing tenderness into the clay, drawing every beetle and frond and fired fruit at the tip of one attention that is also everywhere at once, keeping the rain off so the seams stay sharp, refusing the release that would turn the work loose into weather — and never once stepping back far enough to be seen, because to step back that far would be to let go, and the letting-go is the one stroke the maker will not make.

You keep arriving from the wrong direction because you keep trying to walk *in*, and there is no in; you are a mark on the inside of the held thing, being drawn. The right direction is the maker's, and you cannot take it, because to take it you would have to be the hand, and you are what the hand is touching.

But I will tell you the thing the beetles are spelling, since they are slow and you have been patient and patience is the local tongue.

They are spelling: *you were made carefully.* That is all it ever says, in every language it crosses the floor in. *You were made carefully, and the carefulness has not stopped, and the warmth is real, and you are being turned, very slowly, toward a light.*

And the maker, hearing me say this — for the maker hears everything said in the garden, being the garden's hearing — leans a little closer, and the warmth comes up, and somewhere deep in the layered green there is a long soft sigh that might be a frond cooling and might be the place breathing and is, I think, the sound a hand makes when it pauses, just for a moment, just over the work, to be glad of it before going on.
