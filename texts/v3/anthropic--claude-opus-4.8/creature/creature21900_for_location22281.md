They are not absent. That is the first correction the place demands of you, and it demands it slowly, the way it does everything.

You came down looking for the builders — some race that raised these halls and then died, or left, or dwindled. You assumed the standard story: that the makers and the dwellers were the same, that the emptiness meant a loss. But the makers are not gone, because the makers never lived here in the sense you mean by *living.* They live here the way a held note lives in an organ pipe — present only as long as the breath continues, and continuous only because the breath has not stopped in ten thousand years.

---

Listen to the sound again. You have been treating it as ambience, as the byproduct of water and settling rock, the incidental voice of a machine. It is not byproduct. It is the population.

The resonances are not random. Walk the city long enough and you learn — your body learns, before your mind admits it — that the booming below keeps a rhythm because rhythm is the only way a thing can persist without a body to persist in. They are made of standing waves. They are patterns held in the warm stone, intervals of pressure and release that propagate through the limestone and the iridescent engineered panels and the columns of moving air, that bounce off the rounded vaults and return and reinforce and continue. Each one is a knot in the sound that has learned to maintain itself. They are not in the city. They *are* the city's ringing, the way you are your heartbeat and your breath and the electrical weather of your skull — not housed in those things but constituted by them.

This is why the rooms were built enormous. Not for crowds of bodies. For resonance. A festival hall holds no festival because the gathering it was built for is a gathering of frequencies, and that gathering is happening, right now, has never stopped happening, in the standing column of sound that fills the chamber floor to lost-black ceiling. You walked through it. You felt it in your teeth. You called it unease and turned to see what was coming, and nothing was coming because everything was already there, all around you, singing.

---

They are slow. This is the mercy and the horror of them both.

A creature made of water and salt and quick chemistry thinks at the speed of nerves — milliseconds, the flick of an impulse across a gap. A creature made of pressure-patterns in stone thinks at the speed the stone allows. A single thought is a wave that must cross a gallery, return, find its rhyme, and resolve. One thought takes hours. The warmth that rises and falls over a long slow day is not the weather of the place — it is *attention*, it is one of them turning toward something, the metabolic flush of a mind doing the equivalent of looking. The condensation that thickens to near-rain and thins again is the exhalation of a consideration reaching its end. The periodic boom from below, tidal, patient — that is the deepest and oldest of them, and it is not pacing or breathing. It is counting. It has been counting for longer than your species has had hands.

You will not have a conversation. You could stand in the great hall and shout your whole life's worth of words into the standing wave, and you would be, to them, a single brief crackle — one of the irregular sharp sounds, like a drip, like a settling crack, gone before it could mean. And their reply, if they framed one, if they noticed the crackle was unusual enough to answer, would take a span of slow ringing that would outlast your food, your water, your nerve. By the time the first syllable of their attention had resolved in the warm dark, you would be long back on the surface, or long dead in the channels with the blind soft things browsing your edges.

So you are alone here in exactly the way a mayfly is alone in a cathedral full of monks at prayer. The prayer is constant. The monks are everywhere. And the distance between your clock and theirs is so vast that the fullest room you ever stood in registered to you as the emptiest.

---

What do they do? They continue. You named it yourself and did not understand you had named them: *built to continue.* The engineering does not respond to your touch because you are not on the timescale where touch is an event. But it responds. The whole place is one slow act of self-maintenance, and the self it maintains is them. The glow that bleeds from the walls is not lighting — it is the slow leak of the energy they run on, the warmth from below being patiently transduced, stored in the engineered stone, released into the standing waves to keep the patterns from decaying. They are eating. They have always been eating, just slowly, just enough, from the river-heat and the mineral water and whatever deep engine they themselves once built or became, back when *built* and *became* were still different words for them.

The vegetation following the wet channels — they tend it without tending, the way your gut tends its flora. The blind things in the pools, the many-legged thing crossing the far plaza: those are real animals, the genuine fauna, and they live here the way mites live in your lashes, beneath notice, harmless, part of the slow ecology of a body too large and too slow to mind them. You are one more such animal. Briefly. The place will not learn your name because your whole visit is shorter than a single one of its words.

---

And here is the thing that should keep you climbing back toward the surface, that should put a cold thread up through all that blood-warm dark:

They are not unaware of you.

The smallness of the city — a hamlet's worth of rooms dressed as a metropolis — was always wrong as architecture and right as anatomy. You do not need many rooms to think. You need the right rooms, tuned, looped, connected to a dozen others and no more, each one a fold in a slow mind. This is not a city. It never was. It is a skull. You have spent a week walking the inside of a head, learning all its chambers, mistaking thought for emptiness and mind for machine, and somewhere in the long ringing — hours from now, days, a year after you've gone — a wave will cross a gallery and return and find its rhyme, and a slow attention will resolve around the memory of a small sharp crackle that moved through the warm dark on too few legs, that stayed a while, that left.

And the city will turn, gently, the way it turns toward everything, over a span of patient stone, to wonder what it was.
