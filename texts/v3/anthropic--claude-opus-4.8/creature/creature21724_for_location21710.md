They are called, among themselves, by a word that means *those who listen for the level* — though the word is rarely spoken, because speaking is loud, and the work of keeping does not need announcing.

Begin with their hands, because their hands are what the city made of them.

The fingers are long and the pads are thick, gone smooth at the tips from a lifetime of reading stone by touch — for a Keeper does not need to *see* whether a channel runs true; they lay a palm to the wall and feel the cool film beneath and know in an instant whether the water is moving as it should or whether, somewhere upslope, a seam has silted and the flow is slowing. The nails are pared close. The knuckles are large. Under each fingernail, always, the dark green of moss, because they are forever clearing the channels by hand, lifting the liverwort where it has grown too greedy and tamping it back where the wall has gone bare and needs holding. They do not wash this green away. It would only return by evening. They have made peace with it the way the slope made peace with the stone.

They are not many. This must be understood first, before anything else about them: there were never very many, and there are fewer now, and this is not a tragedy to them. A city that drinks itself does not need a crowd to tend it. It needs a few who know it utterly. Six hands on a single terrace would be a kind of violence — too much disturbance, too much weight of attention on water that prefers to be left mostly alone. So they have thinned themselves, over the long generations, to exactly the number the slope can bear without changing its nature. They are the city's white blood, sparse and circulating, present at the point of need and nowhere else.

## How They Move

They move the way water moves: downhill by preference, slowly, finding the path of least announcement. A Keeper crossing the terraces does not stride. They do not even quite walk in the way you would name walking. They *transfer* — weight from one bare wet foot to the next with a deliberation so total that their footfall makes no sound the stone has not already made a thousand times. They have learned the city's own acoustics so thoroughly that they can place a foot precisely where it will be absorbed, avoiding the lip where it would *tap*, the dry patch where it would *scuff*. To watch one move — if you ever did — would be to watch the silence simply relocate itself by a few meters.

Their clothing is the grey-silver of standing water and the slate of the walls, not by design but by long submission to the place. Whatever they wear was woven from the pale cold-loving grain-fiber and the wool of an animal you will not see, and it has soaked up so much of the highland that it has become the highland's color. It is always damp. It does not dry. They have forgotten, if they ever knew, what dry cloth feels like against skin. Their bodies run a little cool to the touch, a degree or two below what you would expect of a living thing, because they have stopped fighting the temperature of their world and let it move through them.

## What They Eat, What They Are Made Of

They eat little, and slowly. The half-wild tubers, lifted from the old garden-terraces and roasted over the rare small fire — fire is a loud, hot interruption, used sparingly and apologetically, the smoke a violence against the even grey light. The cold-grain, ground and soaked and eaten as a kind of paste that needs no heat at all. The pale flowers, in their season, for what little brightness is in them.

But the truth — and they know this truth and do not speak it, because it would sound like a boast or a madness — is that they are made mostly of the water. They drink the city constantly. They cup it from the channels as they pass, the same water that has run through every wall above them, that has touched every moss and every cistern and every reflecting basin holding the grey sky. It is in them as it is in the stone. There is no clean line between the Keeper and the slope, just as there is no clean line between the land and the city. They are the part of the landscape that has learned to clear its own channels.

## What They Know

They carry the map of the entire system in their bodies — not as drawing, not as memory you could speak aloud, but as something nearer to the way you know where your own limbs are in the dark. A Keeper standing on one terrace can feel, faintly, the state of the water three levels up and twelve courtyards over, the way you might feel a single cold spot on your own back. When a seam silts somewhere far off, they know it not as information but as a discomfort, a slight wrongness in the flow of themselves, and they go toward it the way you reach to scratch an itch — without deciding to, without thinking the word *go*.

This is the maintenance that feels merged with the weather. It is not duty. Duty would require a self standing apart from the work, weighing it, choosing it. They have no such standing-apart left. They are simply the place's way of keeping itself open. When the mist gathers in the low terraces at evening, a Keeper is there to feel it gather. When a stair grows slick beyond even the city's tolerance for slickness, a Keeper has already crouched to sweep it, with a worn flat stone, in long quiet strokes, the way one might smooth a sleeping animal.

## How They Pass the Long Time

They are patient past any human reckoning of patience, because patience implies waiting, and they are not waiting for anything. They are present at the speed of the water. A Keeper may crouch beside a single overfull basin for the better part of a day, doing nothing, watching it find its level and spill and refill, and this is not idleness and it is not meditation. It is *attendance*. It is the same act as the moss brightening with the damp — a living thing being exactly as alive as its surroundings ask it to be, no faster, no more loudly.

When two of them cross paths — which is rare, the slope being so vast and their number so few — they do not greet. They pass within an arm's length, both wet, both grey, both green-handed, and each feels the other as a momentary thickening in the flow, a second pulse in the single circulation, and then they have moved apart again and the city is as it was. There is nothing cold in this. It is the warmest thing they have. To be felt by another and to feel them, and to need no more than that — for two channels to meet and braid and part, the water none the worse, the level held.

## Whether They Are Dying Out

You will wonder this, standing on the terrace with the water running past your feet. You will wonder if they are the last of something, dwindling, the final few tending a city that will outlast them and then run on untended, the channels still doing their work whether or not anyone clears the silt — for a while, for a long while, until the moss grows greedy unchecked and the seams clog and the great slow engine finally, finally falters.

But this is your fear, not theirs, and it is the fear of a thing that lives at a different speed. They do not measure themselves in increase or decline. They are exactly as many as the slope can bear, and they have always been exactly that, and "always" to them is not a span of years but a single unbroken present in which the water condenses and finds the channel and runs down level by level toward the cloud-filled valley, and a hand reaches into the green to lift it clear, and the level holds.

They are not dying out. They are not anything *out*. They are the slope, attending to itself, in the only number the silence allows — going about a keeping so old and so quiet it has merged with the weather, just as you guessed, standing there, wet to the knee, having not yet caught sight of one, and perhaps never will, and perhaps, in the fullness of the grey and patient time, already having been one for a moment without knowing it: the instant you laid your palm to the wall, and it came away wet, and you understood without words that the water was moving as it should.
