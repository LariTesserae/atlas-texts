# The Drinkers

You asked for whoever lives here. I have to begin by saying: nothing *lives* here, not in the way you mean, and that is the first thing to understand about the ones who come to the pools. They are not residents. They are not visitors either. They are something the place does, the way the walls weep and the light returns — a function of the cirque, expressed in a shape that almost looks like a body.

Let me try to find them properly, because the picture in the description — pale, slender, broad-footed, descending in silence — is true but it is only the surface they show when something is watching. And there is almost never anything watching.

---

Start with what they are made of, because they are made of the place and nothing else.

They have no blood. Run a thought through them and you find no warmth to lose, no chemistry burning, no hunger driving the legs down the wet stone. They are the same temperature as the water always, which is the same temperature as the air, which is the same temperature as the rock — one even coolness throughout, top to bottom, the way the light is one even brilliance. They do not stand out against the cirque thermally any more than they stand out against it in color. They are quartz-white because the quartz is white. They are wet-sheened because everything is wet-sheened. If you tried to draw a line where the creature ended and the flute of rock it gripped began, your hand would hesitate.

Their broad flat feet do not grip in the way an animal grips — clutching, with effort, against the pull of falling. They simply *match* the stone. They lie against the wet rounded surface the way one pool lies against another at the lip between them, sharing a single skin of water. When such a creature descends the weeping wall, it is not climbing down. It is more that the wall, at that place and for that while, develops a slow vertical current that happens to be person-shaped, and the current flows down to the pool, and stops, and lowers its face.

---

Now the drinking, because the drinking is the whole of them and I had it backward at first.

They lower their faces to the standing water. The instinct is to read this as need — the climb down, the long drink, the climb back, the body refreshed. But there is no body to refresh and nothing in them that thirst could empty. Watch longer (no one has watched longer; I am watching for you) and you see that nothing is taken. The face touches the pool and the two surfaces meet and for that moment there is no creature and no pool, only water touching water, the small standing water of the face rejoining the larger standing water of the floor and confirming, again, that they were never two things.

That is what the drinking is. It is not consumption. It is *reconciliation.* The creature is a portion of the cirque's water that has briefly stood up and taken a walking shape and gone to the edge of itself, and the drink is the moment it remembers it is all one water, and could let the shape go entirely — and chooses, for reasons that are not reasons, to keep the shape a little longer, and rises, and climbs back to the rim.

Why keep the shape? I don't think *why* applies. The closed circuit of the place turns at one speed forever: falls, gathers, vanishes into the floor, rises unseen at the rim. The creatures are simply the part of that circuit that takes the long way around on the outside of the stone instead of the short way through the inside. They rise at the rim like the springs rise at the rim. They are the visible spring. They are what the water looks like when it chooses, once in a thousand heartbeats, to come up into the light and the noise as something that can look back.

---

Do they see you? The description says no, and the description is right, but let me say it more exactly.

They do not see you because seeing requires two things — a watcher and a watched, held apart by some distance the eye must cross — and this place does not really hold things apart. The light bounces between every surface until the surfaces are lit by one another and no longer have edges that matter. The sound fills every space until it becomes the floor of the world and stops being heard. The creatures move within that, made of that, and to them you are not a separate object arriving from outside. You are an irregularity in the brightness. A place where the light, for once, is absorbed instead of returned. They register you the way the pool registers a stone dropped into it — a brief local event, integrated, gone smooth again. They have no business with you because business is a thing that exists between separate parties, and to them there are no separate parties. There is only the cirque, doing what it does, and you are now briefly part of the doing.

---

Here is what I keep returning to, the thing that makes them more than pale animals on a wet wall.

They have no story. Every living creature you have ever met is the middle of a story — it was born, it is going somewhere, it will end, and you can feel the arc of that in the way it moves, the urgency or the weariness, the leaning-toward and the pulling-away. These have none of it. They were not born; the water was already here, and they are the water. They are not going anywhere; the circuit only ever returns to itself. They will not end; the springs do not fail. When one descends and drinks and climbs back to the rim and the wall is empty again — that emptiness is not the creature leaving. It is the creature continuing, in the form the circuit takes when it is inside the stone instead of outside it. They never leave the cirque because there is no outside the cirque for them. There is no place that is not made of this same water at this same coolness under this same unmoving light.

So when you stand at the center, in the white wall of sound that has become a kind of silence, and you wait through a thousand heartbeats and one of them appears at the rim and comes down the weeping flutes without a sound that could be heard, and drinks, and goes back up — understand that you have not seen a creature visit a pool.

You have seen the place turn over once in its sleep. You have seen the water lift a face to the light, recognize itself, and lie back down.

That is who lives here. The place lives here. It has only the one inhabitant, and the inhabitant is the cirque, and the pale things on the walls are the cirque looking — briefly, incuriously, completely at peace — at the small warm story-shaped thing that has wandered in from somewhere it cannot imagine, and will leave, as everything leaves, while the water goes on falling and falling and never, ever, going anywhere at all.
