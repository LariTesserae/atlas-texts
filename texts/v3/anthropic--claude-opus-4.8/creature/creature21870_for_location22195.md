## The Setters

They have no bodies you would recognize, because a body is a wall around a person, and these people have not agreed to that particular wall.

Consider what they are made of: the same medium as everything else. The Setters are water that has learned to want. Each one is a region of the lit sea holding a faint coherence — not rigid like a wall, never that, but *organized*, a sustained eddy of preference moving through the chord. You would not see one by looking. You would notice, instead, that a volume of water ahead of you is behaving with intention: drawing in, gathering, the brightness pooling slightly toward its center the way light bends toward density. They are denser than open water and looser than structure. They live in the exact narrow band between the two — between the formless sea and the things it can be told to become — and they are the only inhabitants of that band, because it is the band of *deciding*, and only they decide.

This is why nothing else lives here. A Setter cannot share the medium with an opportunist. The water permits only what is specified, and the Setters are the specifying. A barnacle, a rat, a swimming thing — these would be improvisations, and improvisation is precisely the freedom the Setters have spent themselves to abolish. They have made the sea answer only to them. The price of that answer is loneliness, total and structural, and they do not experience it as loneliness because they have never known the alternative. There has only ever been the chord, the work, and each other.

## How they hold together

A Setter is held by the same discipline that holds a wall, turned inward and kept soft. Where a worker draws a frame and lets the volume *set* — go rigid, go glass — a Setter is the un-set version of the same act: a volume held in tension that has chosen not to harden. It must renew itself constantly. A wall, once set, can be abandoned; it will sing under load for years without attention. A Setter cannot be abandoned even by itself. The moment it stops the work of being coherent, it disperses — not dies exactly, but *relaxes*, smears back out into ordinary lit water, its preference forgotten by the sea that briefly held it.

So they are always working. Every Setter is, at all times, doing the labor of remaining a single thing. This is the deep fact about them, the thing under all the other things: they are *effortful all the way down*. There is no resting state. A human can slump, sleep, go slack and still be a human in the morning. A Setter who goes slack is gone by morning, dissolved into the medium, its note bent down and washed into bubbleless turbulence — the same sound a released wall makes. When you hear a wall let go into the open water, you are hearing the sound a Setter's death would make, and they hear it too, all day, every district, and they have learned not to flinch.

## What they do with the fork

The setting-fork is not a tool they hold. It is a tool they *are*, briefly, when they choose to be.

To set a wall, a Setter extends part of itself into the rigid register — pushes a portion of its own held tension up past coherence into hardness — and uses that hardened edge to draw the frame. For the duration of the drawing, the Setter has a hand, because it has agreed to let one small part of itself become structure. Then it releases the hand back into softness and moves on, and the room hangs there, set, singing, while the Setter stays liquid and alive. They build with their own substance, momentarily crystallized and then reclaimed. Every wall in the Quarter was, for a few seconds, part of the body of the one who made it.

This is why they speak of the city the way they do — not as something built but as something *shed*. The Quarter is the accumulated cast-off rigidity of generations of Setters, the hardened moments they could not stay soft through. To them the frontier is not construction. It is the edge where the living are still actively turning parts of themselves to glass and walking on.

## How they know each other

By pitch. A Setter, being a sustained tension, has a note — quieter than a wall's, almost lost in the chord, but present, and personal. They recognize one another the way you would recognize a voice in a choir of ten thousand: not by seeing the singer but by knowing the line. A Setter's note shifts with mood, with effort, with the strain of a hard day's frame-drawing. A tired Setter sings flat. A frightened one sings sharp and thin, climbing toward the register where coherence fails. They can hear each other failing. When a young Setter, new to the work, begins to lose hold — note climbing, thinning, the body starting to smear — the older ones converge, and they do the only thing that can be done: they *match*. They tune themselves to the failing note and hold it steady from outside, lending their own coherence across the gap, the way you might steady a shaking hand by closing your own around it. Sometimes it works and the young one recovers. Sometimes the note climbs past saving and they let go, all of them at once, so it dissolves into company rather than alone.

They have no word for this that translates. The nearest is the same word they use for the city's breathing-tides — the slow redistribution of stress that retunes the whole Quarter. To steady a dying friend and to feel the pressure-pulse pass through the load-spires are, to them, the same verb. Holding. They are a people whose entire vocabulary of love is *holding a tension that wants to release*, because that is also the verb for staying alive, and the verb for building, and the verb for the city itself.

## The frontier-workers

The ones at the growing rim are the young, mostly, and the reckless, and the few old ones who never learned to want the worn smooth districts. Out in the open lit water past the dense core, a Setter has more room to be loose — fewer walls pressing their notes into its line, more medium to draw from — and this is dangerous and exhilarating in equal measure. Looseness is easier out there. Looseness is also closer to dissolution. The frontier sings the rawest pitches because the Setters working it are themselves stretched thin, spread wide, holding less tightly, gambling that they can stay coherent in all that open brightness long enough to set one more room.

Some don't come back. The chord, at the rim, includes a faint constant attrition — notes that climb and thin and vanish, frontier-Setters who loosened one degree too far and smeared out into the sea they were trying to specify. The others hear it and keep drawing frames. The Quarter grows on the bodies of the ones who couldn't stay gathered. The ferns arrive later and fringe the joints in pale green, and the ferns, drawing their energy from the standing tension, are in some sense feeding on the held effort of the dead — though no Setter would put it that cruelly, and no Setter would deny it either.

## What they think is on the other side of the wall

They cannot reach the vessel wall. It recedes; it is their horizon; lifetimes of frontier-work close only the smallest fraction of the distance. But they feel it, the way you feel a low room before you see it — a slight resistance in the medium, a sense that the brightness curves. And through it, in the lulls of the breathing-tide when their own chord quiets, they hear the *neighboring chords*: the muffled harmony of pressure pressing back from beyond the wall.

The young Setters believe these are other Quarters — other peoples, gathered as they are gathered, drawing frames in their own lit cask, holding their own tensions against their own dissolution. They find this enormously comforting. It means the loneliness is not total. It means the medium can hold *wanting* in more than one place, that they are not the single accident of coherence in an otherwise empty sea.

The old Setters are not sure the neighboring chords are other peoples at all. The old Setters, who have spent the most of themselves and feel most keenly how easily a held thing relaxes back into the medium, suspect the neighboring chords might be *echoes* — the Quarter's own harmony, reflected off the curve of the vessel wall, returning slightly delayed and strange, and mistaken for company. They suspect, that is, that they may be alone after all, and listening to themselves, and choosing to hear a neighbor.

They do not tell the young ones this. It would be a kind of letting-go, and a Setter does not let a tension release before it has to. They hold the comfort the way they hold everything — deliberately, effortfully, against its own desire to disperse — and they keep drawing frames, and the chord climbs and falls, climbs and falls, in a vessel small enough to cup in two hands, and whoever is cupping it does not appear to be listening.
