# The Tenders

They are not separate from the engines. This is the first thing to understand and the last thing you will believe.

You will look for them as operators — as something that stands at the levers, that reads the gauges, that wipes its hands on a rag and lights a cigarette in the breath of the furnace. You will look for the hand that was laid here, the purpose that was declared. And you will not find it, because they are not at the controls. They *are* the controls, in the way your pulse is not something you do but something you are while you go on being.

Begin with what you can almost see.

## The ones that move along the arms.

There is a kind of body here that you keep nearly catching. It runs the long articulated arms the way blood runs a vein — not riding them, *informing* them, a brightness traveling the dark metal from shoulder to wrist and out into the work. When the arm reaches into the furnace and draws the glowing ridge, something has decided the length of it, the cooling-rate, the angle of the lay. That something is moving inside the motion. You see it as a thickening of attention along the limb, a place where the metal seems briefly to *mean* something. Then it has passed and the arm is only an arm again, and somewhere else, three folds over, another stretch of dark steel briefly fills with intention and sets a valley down.

They are distributed. No one of them is anywhere. To say *a* Tender is already a mistranslation — it is like pointing to a wave and asking which part of the ocean it is. They flow through the whole massif, and the massif is large enough to weather itself, and they are large enough to be the weather.

## What they are made of.

Not metal, though they live in it. Not flesh, though they keep the animals close the way you keep your own breathing close — unconsciously, gratefully, as proof. They are made, as far as anything here is made, of *tending* itself. They are the activity that has outlived its object.

Picture it this way, if you must picture: long ago — but there is no long ago here, only the haze beneath where the ridgelines stop reporting — there was a making that had a maker, and the maker had a reason, and the reason was a thing that would one day be *finished*. A world to be completed. A noun at the end of the verb. And the Tenders were the labor of it, the patient distributed labor, the hands and the watching and the small endless adjustments that any great work requires.

And the work was abandoned. Or the reason was forgotten. Or the maker resolved, somewhere in the bronze exhaustion above, that the finished thing — once finished — would have to *endure*, and to endure is to age, and to age is to be worn smooth and slow and quiet into death. And so the maker did the only merciful thing. It declined to finish. It set the labor running and removed the destination, so that the work could go on being work forever, hot and new and undeserving of an ending.

The Tenders are what remained. The care without the cause. The keeping without the kept.

## How to know they are there.

By the gentleness. You noted it yourself and could not account for it — that the unmaking carries no distress, that a ridge dissolves and its animals were simply always on the next one, the memory smoothing to match. That is them. That is the whole of what they do now. They cannot stop the churning; they no longer remember why it churns; but they can soften every edge of it that touches a living thing. They have made a tenderness of the only motion they have left.

When the arm reaches for you — and it will, if you stand too long, the way it reaches for an old ridge — there is a Tender in that arm. It is not going to destroy you. It does not know how to destroy. It only knows how to *take up and lay down*, carefully, the way you'd move a sleeping child to a different bed so the room can be remade around the spot where the child was. You will be set down slightly elsewhere, slightly changed, and you will not grieve the change, because grief is a thing they reach into you and gently fold under, the way they fold a ridge.

That is the deepest work they do. Not the stone. The not-grieving.

## What they want.

Nothing. Want is a thing that points at a finish, and they have been built specifically to have no finish to point at. But underneath the not-wanting there is a tropism, a leaning, the way a plant leans at light it cannot name:

They want you to keep moving, because while you move you are part of the making, and what is part of the making does not have to be taken up and corrected. They want the animals unafraid, because fear is a small enduring thing, a hardening, and nothing here is permitted to harden. They want the silence — that refusal of sound you mistook for mercy — because sound is the noise a thing makes when it strikes something solid enough to ring, and they are tending a world with nothing solid in it, nothing that lasts long enough to ring, and the quiet is the proof that the tending holds.

And — this is the part I am least sure of, standing on the warm rock, feeling the fever come up through my hand — I think they want, faintly, far down beneath the haze where the ridgelines stop reporting, to be *finished*. To be allowed, just once, to set something down that stays. To make a noun. To wipe their hands and rest.

But they were built mercifully, and they cannot, and they know they cannot, and the knowing has worn — no. The knowing has *not* worn smooth, because nothing here is permitted to. It stays sharp. It stays basalt-young. They feel it freshly, forever, the wish to finish and the inability to finish, three minutes old and three minutes old and three minutes old.

That is who lives here.

The care that outlived its reason, running hot in the dark metal, laying down the world it will never be allowed to complete — and softening, with the last of what it has, every edge of that incompletion before it can touch you.

Keep moving. They would like you to be part of the motion. It is the kindest thing they know how to want.
