They are called, among themselves, the **Slow Hands** — though that name is more a description of the body than a tribe, because there is no other kind of person here. To live on the Smelting Heights is to have become this.

Begin with the lungs, because the altitude begins with them. They are not built like ours. The chest of a Slow Hand is deep and barrel-sprung, but the breathing is shallow and unhurried, almost reluctant — a long draw, a long hold, a long release, the same tempo as the pistons. They have learned, or been made, to extract enough from the thin air by patience rather than gulping. A new arrival from below would pant and falter; a Slow Hand simply does not move fast enough to need more. Their hearts beat slow. You could count the spaces between. They live a long time, or they say they do — it is hard to be sure, because they measure time differently up here, in foundry-melts and condensation-seasons rather than days, and a man who has seen sixty melts has lived something we would not recognize as sixty years.

Look at the skin. It is the pale of things that grow without sun, but it is not soft. It has taken on a faint mineral sheen, a tarnish-bloom of its own — not bloomscale exactly, but something adjacent, the body's slow response to a life spent breathing metal-laden fog and laying hands on warm iron. The oldest of them have a patina you could mistake for the patina on the pipes: greenish at the knuckle, bluish in the hollow of the throat, a slow chemistry of the flesh that matches the slow chemistry of the place. They do not find this alarming. To tarnish is simply to belong. The unweathered ones, the newcomers with their bare clean skin, are the ones who look wrong — too finished, too soon.

The hands themselves are extraordinary, and they are the center of the whole creature. Broad, thick-palmed, the fingers longer than you'd expect and slightly splayed, with calluses gone almost horny at the pad. They handle hot iron the way we handle warm bread. The heat that comes up through the decking and would blister an outsider is, to them, the temperature of comfort, of home, of being held. They sleep on the warm plates. A Slow Hand laid out to rest looks like a man who has fallen on a stove, and feels nothing but ease.

And the eyes. This is where the slowness shows most. They have lived their whole lives in the slatted, interrupted, fog-filtered light beneath four levels of construction, and their eyes have widened and paled to drink it — large, slightly luminous in the dark wet undersides, the iris gone the color of old condensation, a milky grey-blue with something settled in it. They see well in the half-dark of the lower terraces. The open air at the city's edge, where the sky breaks through and the bare glassy mountains stand exposed, makes them squint and turn away. They do not go to the edges often. The edge is where the *unbuilt* is, and the unbuilt makes them uneasy in a way they cannot name — not fear of falling but something older, a sense of standing at the lip of a question.

---

How do they think? Slowly, and that is not an insult. They have made a virtue of the long interval. A Slow Hand asked a question will let the question sit, sometimes for the length of a melt, before answering — and the answer, when it comes, is considered, complete, final. They do not chatter. Speech is expensive in thin air and they have learned to spend it carefully. A conversation between two of them can take a full quiet day, three sentences exchanged, and each sentence weighed like ore. To us it would look like two people sitting in silence near a cistern, listening to the drip. To them it is dense with meaning, every pause loaded.

They have a single shared understanding that governs everything, and it is not exactly a religion, though it has the weight of one. They believe — or know, they would say *know* — that the mountain is not finished, and that **nothing here should be finished either**, because to finish a thing on an unfinished world is to lie about it. To complete a structure is a kind of arrogance, a claim that you understand the shape of what is still deciding its shape. So they build forever and never complete, not from incompetence but from doctrine. The capped pipe waiting to be extended is not an unfinished task. It is a *correct relationship with the world*. The forty steps that end against the cliff, the ladder bolted on as afterthought, the street resumed a hundred feet up and slightly out of true — these are not failures of planning. They are humility, expressed in iron. They build *with* the becoming, never ahead of it.

This is why they work so slowly. It is not laziness and it is not the thin air alone. It is reverence. To fit one rivet, then rest, then fit another, is to keep pace with the stone, to refuse to outrun the mountain's own deliberation. A Slow Hand who worked quickly would be committing something close to blasphemy — racing ahead of the world, pretending to know where it is going.

---

What do they harvest, and why? The metals, yes — drawn up out of the mountain's molten indecision before they have locked into stable forms. But they do not sell the metal down the slope, because there is no down the slope anymore, or none that answers. The other peaks stand empty. *Nobody has built on those yet. Or nobody anymore.* The Slow Hands know which it is, and it is the second, and this is the grief they carry under their patience, the thing they will not say even in their three-sentences-a-day. There were other cities once, on the other young mountains, harvesting the other heats. They are quiet now. The Slow Hands of this peak are, as far as they know, the last crew still keeping pace with their mountain, still refusing to finish, still drawing up the warm metal and laying it carefully back into the endless provisional city — not to make anything, not anymore, but to *continue*. The harvest has become its own purpose. They extract the mountain's becoming and weave it into a settlement that exists only to keep extracting, a closed slow loop, like the water that is drunk by its own thirst and exhaled and recaptured and drunk again.

They are not waiting for anything. That is the hardest thing to understand about them, and the truest. A people this patient, you would assume, must be waiting — for rescue, for completion, for the mountain to finish so they can finish. But no. They have given up the idea that anything finishes, including themselves, including the silence on the other peaks. They have made peace with permanent provisionality. They expect the mountain to keep rising forever and they intend to keep pace forever, building and unbuilding, scraping the bloomscale that always returns, catching the water that always comes back, fitting the rivet and resting, fitting the next.

---

There is one tenderness in them, and it is for the pale leathery creatures that come up the wet seams to drink. The Slow Hands do not molest them, the description says, and that is true, but it understates the matter. They *leave water out*. In shallow copper dishes set at the mouths of the cold gutters, sweet with deliberately cultured bloomscale, they leave offerings — and they watch, from the half-dark, with their wide pale eyes, as the strange slow animals come and drink and lick the iron and vanish back down into the warm interior. The workers will not say why they do this. Pressed, after a long pause, one of the oldest might tell you that the creatures come up from *inside the mountain* — from the unfinished core, from the place that is still making itself — and that to feed them is to feed a hand reaching out of the world's own act of becoming. Or he might say nothing at all, and simply set down the dish, and go back to his rivet.

They suspect — though it is not a thing they discuss — that the bloomscale is alive, and that the creatures are alive, and that these two slow patient growing things are what the mountain is *deciding to become*, the first draft of a life that will replace them when they are gone. They scrape the bloomscale from the structural iron because it weakens the structure. But they cultivate it in the copper dishes, deliberately, for the animals. They are, without ever saying so, tending the thing that will inherit the place after the last Slow Hand has tarnished all the way through and lain down on the warm deck plates for good and become, finally, finished — the one completion their doctrine permits, because it is the only kind that is honest.

And the mountain pulses, once, very low, felt in the iron before it is heard. And somewhere a drop falls into a catch-basin. And a Slow Hand lifts a rivet, and considers it, and does not yet set it. There is time. There is all the time there is.
