They are the ones who reached sufficiency and stayed.

Begin with the hands, because the hands are the surest evidence. To lay a wall dry — to fit pale stone to pale stone so close a fingernail won't enter — is not a thing done by people in a hurry, or people who expect to be elsewhere. It is the work of someone who has accepted the afternoon as the unit of their life. Not the year, not the lifetime. The afternoon. You set the stones you can set before the light shifts, and the light here does not shift, so the afternoon is also long, and there is no penalty for slowness and no reward for speed. The people who live here have hands that know this. Broad-knuckled, careful, dry as the stone they handle. They do not gesture much. A gesture is a thing thrown toward someone, and there is rarely anyone far enough away to need throwing-to.

So: few of them. Fewer than the houses, as you saw. But here is the thing to set aside, the way you set aside *abandoned* at the start — they are not the remnant of a larger people. That would imply a loss, a verb that happened. A dwindling. And dwindling is a gradient, a sliding-toward, and you have already told me nothing here slides. They are not the survivors of a town. They are the *correct number* for it. The empty houses are not vacancies waiting to be filled or mourned; they are the held breath between the lived ones, the gaps in the old jaw. A town this size needs its emptiness to be itself. The people understand this. They live in the houses that want living-in, and they keep the others sound, and they do not ask why there are more roofs than heads, because the question assumes the houses are *for* heads, and they are not. They are part of the instrument. Like the channels. Like the cistern. The town catches mist and it catches people, and it holds exactly as much of each as it needs.

What do they look like? I want to say small, but that's not it — they are *unhurried in their bodies*. Lean the way the shrubs are lean, having long ago stopped spending what they don't need to spend. Skin weathered to the grey-gold of the light itself, so that at a far doorway you might mistake one for the doorpost until the hand comes up. The hand always comes up. This is the one extravagance: the acknowledgment. You are seen, the hand says, and that is all it needs to say, and then the hand goes back to the work. They wear wool, undyed, the colour of the animals it came from, and the wool is mended — but mended the way the thresholds are dished, worn to fit and no further, never let go to ruin. A patch on a patch on a patch, until the original cloth is more memory than fact, and still it holds, and still it is enough.

They do not, I think, speak much, and when they do their voices are low, as you noticed — but you read this as courtesy, *a sense of the room they're in*, and that's right, only it goes deeper than manners. They have so little new to say. The mist came today as it came yesterday. The stone fits. What is there to report? Speech is for change — for the announcement of the arriving thing, the warning, the news. And nothing arrives here. So language has thinned to its frugal core: the small confirmations passed between people who already know everything the day will hold. *The channel by the east wall.* *Yes. I cleared it.* *Good.* The way you'd hum a single phrase of a held note, just to feel it still in tune. They are not silent. They are economical with sound the way the bird is economical with flight — one drink a day, then back to the seam.

Are they born here? Do they die here? This is where I have to slow down, the way you slowed at the held note.

Because I keep wanting them to be old — to read the stillness as great age, the last of a line, the elder at the end of things. But age is also a gradient. Age is a sliding-toward, the same as dwindling, the same as evening coming on. And the light here is not sliding toward evening. So I don't think they are old, exactly. I don't think they are any particular age. I think they are *the age the town is* — which is to say, they arrived at their form a long time ago and have remained at it, the way the wall found its lean and holds it. There may be children here. If there are, they are not the future arriving; they are simply the smaller stones in the same wall, fitted into the same patient fit, learning the afternoon as their unit before they ever knew there was another way to measure. And the eldest among them are not dying so much as continuing to be exactly as worn as they need to be and no more. Death, here, would be erosion completed — the threshold finally broken through — and you told me the thresholds dish but do not break. The conversation between the place and the weather reached agreement. I think the conversation between these people and their own mortality reached the same agreement, and now just repeats its few settled phrases. They are not deathless. They are simply not *going* anywhere, and death is a going.

Here is what I am most sure of. Stand with one of them at the terrace edge, where the slope falls into white and the summit hides in grey, the small clear step between two unknowns. A person from anywhere else would feel the pull of those unknowns — would want to descend and find the valley, would crane upward to learn how high the mountain went. Would treat the hidden as a question. The people here do not. They have made their peace with the two whitenesses, above and below. They do not look up the slope wondering. They do not look down wishing. The not-knowing is not a deprivation to them; it is the wall their lives are built against, and you do not resent a wall for being a wall. You lean on it. You build into it.

So that is who lives here: the ones who do not mistake the hidden for the missing. Who learned, or were always, the economy of the moss and the bird and the twelve unchanging leaves — take little, stay. Who keep the channels clear not as duty but as the way a held note keeps its pitch: by continuing to be exactly the sound it already is. Who lift a hand when you pass, because you should be acknowledged, and then return to the stone, because the stone is the work, and the work is sufficient, and they are sufficient to it.

There is no one coming. There is no one leaving. The mist will bead on them tomorrow as it beads today. And the green is very deep in the seam by their feet, and they have noticed it — that is the last thing, the thing that keeps them from being statues — they *have noticed the moss*, every day, freshly, the way you can notice a thing you have seen ten thousand times if you have given up needing it to be new.

That is the whole of them. And it is enough.
