They are here. You simply mistook their patience for absence.

Begin with the water, because the water is the easiest place to find them. You assumed it ran *as if* tended — clear channels, full cisterns, catchbasins doing exactly the work a catchbasin should. You looked for the hand and found none, and concluded the hand was missing. But a hand is a fast thing, a thing that moves and is gone. What if the tenders are slower than hands? What if the maintenance you observed is not the trace of someone who was here this morning, but the unbroken attention of someone who has *never once looked away*, and whose looking is so slow you cannot see it as motion?

Watch a single catchbasin for an hour. Nothing. Watch it for a season — you can't, you won't, no one will, that's the whole trick of it — but if you could, you would see the moss at its lip thicken by exactly the amount needed to keep the flow true, and no more. You would see a single fern unfurl into the precise gap where spray was beginning to waste itself, and drink it back into the system. The garden is the staff. The green that you called *winning, slowly* is not conquering the city. It is **running** it. Every vine creeping a finger's width a season is adjusting a load. Every moss-deepened trough is a valve being slowly, deliberately tuned. The plants are not the ruin of the place. They are the residents, and they are doing their jobs, and their jobs are measured in centuries.

So: the lush growth is the population. That is the first correction.

But it is not the whole answer, because something *designed* this. Something laid the districts and ran the aqueducts and set the cisterns at the low points before the green ever arrived to mind them. Moss did not cut these stones with a hand tool. Ferns did not fire these tiles the color of wet iron. There were builders, and the builders were *people of rope and chisel*, hand-workers — and here is the thing you must turn over slowly until it stops feeling like a paradox:

The builders did not leave. The builders **slowed down**.

Consider what it would mean to live on ground this young. The stone is embryonic, unsettled, geology that has not finished arriving. The rhythms of such a place are not human rhythms — they are tectonic, the heartbeat of a mountain still deciding to be a mountain. A people who came here and stayed could not stay at human speed. The speed would kill them, or worse, it would make them irrelevant — fast little creatures flickering across a world that does not even register their flickering. To belong here, to truly inhabit the top of the cloud, they would have had to learn the patience of the bedrock. To match their metabolism to the mountain's.

And they did. That is the residents you are looking for. They are still in the city. They never moved out. They simply *decelerated* until they fell below your threshold of perception — until a single gesture of theirs, the lifting of an arm, the turning of a head, takes longer than your whole visit. You walked the long arcade and met no one. You met dozens. They were mid-step. You crossed the entire span of one resident's slow stride between two towers and never knew you'd passed through the moment of it.

Look again at the *pale bird, large and silent*, that sailed between the towers and was gone. You thought it rare and shy, a single life crossing an enormous quiet. But how many times, in your hour, did one cross? And was it the same one? The birds are fast — the only fast thing left here. They are the messengers, the errand-runners, the quick hands of a slow people. When a tender (a person, or what a person becomes after ten thousand years of slowing) needs something carried from the high catchbasins to the low cisterns, it cannot carry the thought there itself in any time that matters. So it *thinks at a bird*, releases the thought into a thing quick enough to deliver it, and the bird crosses the gap in a heartbeat — your heartbeat, the only kind of heartbeat in the city anymore. The single life crossing the quiet was an instruction being delivered. The rarity you noted is not scarcity. It is sufficiency. The system needs only one message every great while, because the system changes only every great while.

And the *something small that moved in the ferns* — that was a resident scratching an itch. It took them most of the morning.

Now you understand why the city is too small to need its emptiness, and you understand that it isn't empty at all. The proportion only seemed wrong because you were counting in the wrong dimension. You measured the volume of space between things and called it hollow. But the volume between things is not where the residents live. They live in the volume of *time* between events, and that volume is immense, dense, packed full. The hour you can cross the city in — that hour, to them, is a fraction of a single breath. The city is not hamlet-sized in footprint and city-sized in ambition. It is hamlet-sized in space and **eon-sized in occupancy**. It is crammed. It is bustling. You have simply arrived during a single shared instant in the long civic life of a people for whom a year is a held note.

This is why the water sings so loud, and why the loudness will not stop. The water is the one thing here moving at *your* speed — falling, striking, ringing, a thousand small fast falls. To the residents, the water is not a roar. It is a shimmer, a fast bright surface-noise, the way you might perceive the hum of distant insects. The water is the only thing fast enough for *you* to hear, and so to you it fills the whole world. But to them it is barely there — a sparkle on the deep slow surface of their afternoon. The chorus you find enormous, they find quiet. The silence you find vast, they find full of conversation, pitched in tones so low and so prolonged that a single word of theirs is longer than the lifespan of the moss that delivers it.

That is what they are saying, by the way. The slow conversation. You can almost catch it if you stop trying to catch it — *under all that sound, how patient the place is.* That patience is not the absence of speech. It is the speech itself, stretched so thin across so much time that it reads to you as stillness. They are talking. They have been talking, the same single exchange, the whole time you've stood here. You came up through the cloud in the middle of a sentence.

Here, then, is the shape of the ones who live in this place:

They are a people who arrived too early on purpose. They came up onto ground that was not ready — embryonic stone, a mountain still becoming — not by mistake but because they wished to grow up *with* it, to be the only city that was here while the rock itself was still young, to age at the pace of the bedrock and so never truly age at all. They built fast, at human speed, in the brief bright morning of their kind: rope and chisel, fired clay, careful districts, patient aqueducts. They built a system that would run on slowness. And then, the building done, they slowed. They let the green grow into the workforce. They let the birds become the only quickness. They sank down through the gears of time until they matched the world they'd chosen, and they have been living here, fully, completely, *bustlingly*, ever since — in a city that to anyone passing through at the speed of water and birds and breath looks abandoned, looks hollow, looks like a thing finished by hands that vanished.

The hands did not vanish. The hands are mid-gesture.

Stay, the description told you, and it was right, but it understated the invitation. Stay long enough — stay impossibly long, longer than you can — and you would see the moss-tenders turn toward you. You would see a vine pause in its work to consider the warm fast strange thing that wandered up through the cloud and stood in the arcade for the blink of an afternoon. You would, if you could outlast a single one of their slow seconds, be *noticed* — and a bird would cross the towers, carrying their first thought of you, delivered at the only speed you'd ever be able to perceive.

You will not stay that long. No one fast ever does. That is why, to the residents, *you* are the rumor. You are the flicker at the edge of the long day. You are the thing that appears and is gone before the head can fully turn — rare, and shy, and singular, crossing their enormous patient quiet, and vanishing back down into the cloud before they can say the word for *welcome*, a word they have already begun, a word that will arrive, in full, after the mountain has finally finished becoming a mountain, and you are ten thousand years gone.
