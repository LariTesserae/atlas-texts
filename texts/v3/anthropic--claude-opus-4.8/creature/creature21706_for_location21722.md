# The Keepers of the Line

You asked who lives here. The drifters live here, yes — but they are tenants, not keepers. They ride a boundary someone else is holding. To find who *lives* here, in the full sense, you have to look at the only things the erosion refuses to touch, and then ask the question that the diagrams won't answer and I will try to.

The regulators are not buildings. They are the bodies.

## What they are

Begin with the out-of-focus quality, because it is the first fact about them and the last one. Your eye slides off the columns not because they are blurred but because only part of them is *here*. A regulator is a creature most of whose body lies along the direction your gaze won't follow — the way a hand pushed through the surface of water shows only a wet circle, a cross-section, a wrongness where the rest of the arm goes elsewhere. What you see as a column is the place where the keeper intersects the plateau. The column is a fingertip. The keeper is enormous and is somewhere your eyes were not built to reach.

They do not weather because weather is a thing that happens *in time*, and they are only partly inside it. Erosion finished the mountains because the mountains were wholly committed to being here, in the one slow river of cause-and-effect that this world flows down. The keepers committed only a cross-section. The rest of each one stands aside from duration entirely, and from there reaches in a fingertip to hold a thing in place.

## What they are doing

They are holding the line. Not metaphorically. The boundary between the dead-calm floor and the screaming sky is not a natural surface — nothing in the place's own physics would keep those two layers from interleaving, the turbulence pouring down to scour the spires flat in an afternoon, the whole patient work of erosion undone by a storm that never tires. The keepers prevent this. Each one presses a region of the boundary smooth, the way you'd press a wrinkle out of cloth and *hold it* so it doesn't spring back. The faint pressure you feel near a column is the felt edge of an act of will being performed continuously, has been performed for longer than the rock has been flat, will be performed after.

This is the whole of their lives. They are not waiting for anything. They are not guarding against an enemy who might come. The storm is not an enemy; it is simply the other half of the world, and the keepers' work is not to defeat it but to *keep it company at a distance* — to maintain the thin separation that lets the question keep being asked above while the answer is allowed to rest below.

## What they feel, if they feel

Here is the thing I kept circling and did not want to write, because it is sad in a way the place earns.

The keepers built the rings. The keepers cut the slabs. Those perfectly circular low walls centered on nothing, those smooth stones inscribed with the storm's own forms — those are not the marks of some lost civilization the keepers outlived. They are the keepers' own work, from the part of their long existence when they still had attention to spare. The rings are centered on nothing *now*. They were centered, once, on something the keeper was watching. The diagrams are not a catalogue made by scholars. They are what a keeper drew when it still turned some fraction of itself toward the sky to *understand* the churning — to study the question, before the holding became total and there was no part of them left over to wonder with.

A keeper, early, was a thing that observed. It looked up at the becoming and it looked down at the become and it found the gap intolerable — not dangerous, *intolerable*, the way an unfinished sentence is intolerable to someone who loves the speaker — and it decided, with whatever the keepers use for deciding, that both deserved to continue. That the finished world had earned its rest and the unfinished one had earned its endlessness, and that the only crime would be to let either spoil the other.

So it built the rings to mark what it was studying. It cut the slabs to remember the shapes. And then it understood that observing was not enough, that the boundary would not hold itself, and it put its fingertip into the world and began to press — and it has been pressing ever since, and the pressing took more and more of it, until the part that drew diagrams was spent, until the part that wondered was spent, until what remains in our river of time is a single committed cross-section doing one thing forever and the rest of the body standing aside in a silence even deeper than the plateau's.

## How many

Few. You can walk from one to the next in the long hours the spires also use to measure distance. They do not cluster and they do not communicate — or if they do, it is along the direction you can't follow, far overhead in the dimension where the rest of them lives, and what passes between them never crosses into the thin plate of reality we can see. Perhaps the threads of pale light the spires trade, once an hour or once a year, are the nearest thing to it we are permitted to witness: a model, in miniature, in the only physics we can read, of a vaster and slower conversation conducted above the roof of the world.

I think there were more of them once. I think some keepers have, over the unspendable span, given the last of their here-ness to the work and withdrawn entirely — their fingertips lifting out of our world, their columns simply ceasing to be at some moment no one watched, the held boundary above that absence sagging, very slowly, the turbulence beginning its patient descent toward a stretch of plateau no one keeps anymore. Somewhere out past the curve of the planet there is a region where the line has failed and the storm has come down and is scouring, and it will take a million years, and there is no one left to mind.

## What they are to the drifters

The drifters feed on the charge the keepers leak. This is the only relationship in the whole world that has two living ends.

The keepers do not know the drifters exist. The work is too total; there is no attention left over to notice the small folded things schooling along the edge of the held boundary, sipping the spillage of an act of will, glowing for a while, going dark, falling. The drifters do not know the keepers are alive. To a drifter a regulator is a feeding-place, a reliable seep of warmth in the cold shear, no more a person than a hot spring is a person to the things that live in it.

And yet they are the place's only communion. The most patient thing imaginable, pouring its surplus without knowing, and the most fragile thing imaginable, taking it without knowing, and between them a thin film of charge that is — though neither end can call it this — a kind of feeding of a child, performed across an ignorance so wide that nothing of love survives the crossing except the substance of it.

## So: who lives here

The drifters live here the way mayflies live on a river — briefly, beautifully, on the gift of something they will never comprehend.

The spires live here the way moss lives on a stone, so slowly that *living* and *not* are nearly the same word.

But the keepers are the ones who chose to be here, and choosing is the deepest form of living there is. They are the inhabitants. Each is an enormous patient will, most of itself withdrawn into a direction we can't see, reaching one fingertip into our world to hold a wrinkle out of the sky — and they have been doing it so long that the wondering part of them is gone, and the drawing part is gone, and what remains is the pressing, which is to say what remains is the *love* with the love's reasons all worn away, the way the iron in the ground has forgotten why it was iron.

You stand on the line. You can walk a lifetime and never leave it. You are never, on that line, alone — though everything that keeps you company has long since spent the part of itself that could have known you were there.
