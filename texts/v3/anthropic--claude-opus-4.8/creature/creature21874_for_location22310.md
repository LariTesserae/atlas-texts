# The People of the Pinnacle

They are small. You would notice this first, if you could climb to them, which you cannot. Generations on the same square of stone have pared them down — not stunted, but *economized*, the way the vines have economized their water and the moss its need for sun. They are short and dense and long-lived, with the patient slow-blinking eyes of creatures who have never once had to flee anything. Nothing hunts them. Nothing competes for the summit. There is only the work of staying, and they have made themselves perfectly shaped for staying.

Their hands are the most developed part of them. Broad palms, thick-padded, with fingers that know every texture this place can offer: the give of a fat leaf about to be slit, the precise tension of a fiber net heavy with morning dew, the wobble of a dry-laid stone that means a wall has begun its generational lean and must be re-shouldered by someone, sometime, not urgently, but before the child who notices it grows old. They feel the world more than they look at it. In this dim amber light, touch is the truer sense. They navigate the stacked dark of their town by memory and by hand, sliding palms along wall-moss, counting ladder-rungs by feel, and they find each other in the gloom the same way — by the known shape of a shoulder, the familiar grip of a greeting.

## What they do

They tend. That is the whole of it, and it is enormous.

There is no hunting here and no real harvest in the way lower places mean the word — no season of plenty, no scramble before scarcity. There is only the continuous, the perpetual maintenance of the living engine they sit inside. They slit leaves and let them heal and slit them again on a rotation so old it is encoded not in any record but in the simple obviousness of which leaves look ready. They string the dew-nets at dusk and wring them at dawn, every dusk, every dawn, a tide of labor as regular as breathing and about as remarked upon. They re-stack stones. They thin the purple fruit so the vines do not exhaust themselves. They walk the pens of the soft lowing animals and press their hands to warm flanks and check, by feel, for the slow troubles that slow lives accumulate.

None of it is hurried because none of it *can* be hurried. You cannot make a vine fruit faster by wanting it. You cannot wring more water from a night than the night held. They have learned — over a span no single mind among them could hold — that effort and outcome here are only loosely coupled, that the place gives what it gives on its own long gradient, and the role of a person is to be *present and attentive* across that gradient, to be the steady hand that catches the wobbling stone, that notices the failing vine, that fills the lamp before the fat runs fully out. They are not masters of this place. They are its long memory and its maintenance, its self-aware part, the organ by which the living town keeps itself going.

## How they think

Slowly, and in long arcs.

A people with no fast threats and no fast rewards develops a strange relationship with time. They do not, as far as can be told, anticipate much. Why would they? Tomorrow is this, again. The year after is this, again, slightly. They have instead a deep and unhurried *attention* — the capacity to watch a thing change so slowly it is not changing, and to feel that as interesting rather than maddening. A child here can squat by a vine-tip for the better part of a day, content. An adult can sit with a wall and consider it. This is not idleness. It is the native cognitive mode of the place: a patience so total it becomes a kind of perception, sensing the slow swing of the warmer and the less-warm, the imperceptible creep of a corbel toward collapse, the gradual graying of a moss-face that means it is dying and should be re-seeded.

Their memory is collective and worn smooth, like the mountain. They do not keep records because records are for things that change and might be forgotten, and here almost nothing changes and so almost nothing is forgotten — the knowledge simply *persists*, passed hand-to-hand, the same knot tied the same way, the same stone re-shouldered, the grandparent's gesture in the grandchild's hands. What they have instead of history is *recurrence*. Their stories, such as they are, are not about events. They are about cycles. The long tales told in the amber dark over the roar of the climbing birds are not "and then this happened" but "and so it goes, and so it returns, and so it will go." They find this comforting in the way lower peoples find an ending comforting. To them, the ending is the worst imaginable shape a story could take. The good story is the one that closes its loop and begins again.

## What they fear, and what they love

They do not fear death much — death here is a slow tide, foreseen, folded into the cycle, the old leaf returning to the wall. A person grows long and grows worn and one warm dim day simply stops, and is wound in fiber and given to the deep moss of the north faces, where the green will take them gently apart over years, and the vines will fruit a little richer for it, and that is right. That is the loop closing as it should.

What they fear is *the wearing*. They know — it is the one thing they know that lies outside the comforting cycle, the single fact that does not loop — that the mountain is going. They feel it in the smoothness of the cliffs, in the dust that is the mountain leaving grain by grain on the wind. Every soul who has ever lived here has known that the stone beneath the town is, with infinite slowness, dissolving into the dry void, and that there will come a now — unimaginably far, past counting, but *coming* — when there is no more up to stand on. This is the one arrow in a world of circles. They do not speak of it often. When they do, they speak of it the way lower peoples speak of the sun going out: certain, distant, not for us, not for our children's children's children, but real. Some among them — there are always a few, in every generation, the ones who sit longest with the walls — carry the wearing close, and grow quiet with it, and tend a little more tenderly because of it.

And what they love is *the now*. They are, for all their slowness, perhaps the most present beings imaginable — a people with no past worth recording and a future that loops, who therefore live almost entirely inside the warm dim immediate. They love the heaviness of a ripe fruit in the dark green. They love the morning weight of a dew-net. They love the roar — the constant, ancestral, never-ceasing roar of the climbing birds, which to a stranger would be madness but to them is the sound of the world being alive and continuing to be alive, the audible proof that the loop has not broken. A silence here would terrify them more than any noise. Silence would mean something had stopped that was meant to go on forever.

They fill the lamps. The fat runs low and they render more and fill the lamps again, and this small act — performed ten thousand times by ten thousand hands across a span no one of them could hold — is the truest portrait of them: a people whose whole purpose, whose contentment, whose quiet courage in the face of the one slow arrow, is simply *to keep the warm light going a little longer, on top of the worn-down stone, under the smear of sun, for as long as there is up to stand on.*

And it is enough. It has been enough for a very long time.
