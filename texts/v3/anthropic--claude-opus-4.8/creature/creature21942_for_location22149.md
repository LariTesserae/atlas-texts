What lives here does not announce itself, because announcement is motion, and motion is the one thing this place has set aside.

Begin with the stains. You called them life that happens to be color, and that is exactly right, but it is also the deepest truth of the hollow's tenancy. The rust-orange film, the sulphur smear, the iron-purple fold low in the warmest seep — these are the oldest residents, and the most complete. They are not waiting to become anything. They arrived at sufficiency before there were creatures with eyes, before there was a word for *enough*, and they have held to it with a constancy that no animal could survive. An animal must eat, and so must want, and so must move toward the thing it wants. These do not. They take what comes up through the stone — the warmth, the dissolved breath of the deep earth, the slow gift of minerals — and they make of it only more of themselves, thinly, where they already are. They do not spread because spreading is a kind of hunger. They are the hollow's first and truest citizens precisely because they have nothing left to ask of it. To live here fully is to have stopped reaching, and they stopped first.

Above them, in the warm water, the soft blind things. You named them visitors, and from the outside that is fair — they pass, they are gone the next time you look. But watch the same cup of water long enough and you understand the passing differently. They are not going anywhere. Their movement is not travel; it is the slow turning-over of a body in a medium that asks nothing of it, the way a thing sealed in warm honey will drift and settle and drift. They have no eyes worth the name, because there is nothing to see that changes. They have no hurry because nothing here arrives or departs on a schedule they must meet. They are patient the way a stone is patient, except that the stone has no choice and these, somewhere in the dim coil of them, have made patience into the whole of what they are. They eat the films, perhaps, a little. They eat what the water carries. They have shrunk their wanting down to the size of the place, and in doing so have become native to it — which is to say they have become almost not-creatures, almost feature, almost terrain that happens to breathe.

The eyeless amphibian on the ledge is the largest thing the hollow holds, and it is the one most clearly caught between the world above and the rest it has come down into. Its throat pulses — there is still that, still a pump, still the animal insistence of needing air. But notice that it does not hunt where you can see it hunting, does not call, does not seek another of its kind. It rests. It has the long, depleted stillness of something that climbed down out of the bright and hungry world and found here a place where it could finally stop spending itself. The blindness is not a loss to it. Eyes are for a world of consequence, of the thing that moves and must be answered. Here there is only the even grey-gold glow that never resolves into anything to look at. The amphibian has let its eyes go the way a person lets go of a habit they no longer have any use for. It is becoming, slowly, over generations, more like the soft things in the water, and they are becoming more like the stains, and the stains are already nearly stone. The hollow runs in one direction only, and that direction is toward rest.

And then there are the others — the ones who are not here.

The flat stones in the swallowed floor. The stub of dry-laid wall. The niche, the worn step beneath it, the lip of an unglazed pot. These are the marks of the only residents who ever came here *wanting* and somehow were not turned away by the wanting. They came on their hands and knees, almost — the step is worn by kneeling, you can feel that without being told. They did not try to make the hollow into something. They laid a few stones flat so the kneeling would be easier, set one wall to lean against, cut one niche to hold one thing, and brought one vessel to carry water that needed no carrying. They understood, those few, what the place was for. Not for living in — nothing lives *in* the hollow; it is too full of itself to be lived in. They came to *visit the stillness*, to press themselves briefly against a thing that had finished, the way the living will sometimes seek out the company of the very old, not for what the old can give but for the way time moves differently in the room with them. They came to be quieted. And the hollow quieted them, and then they left, because they were still creatures of season and morning and dusk and could not stay where there were none. The mineral has been closing over their stones ever since, gently, without resentment, the way the seep is already closing over the shape of your foot.

So who lives here?

The honest answer is that the hollow's truest inhabitant is the *condition* — the rest itself, the breathing equilibrium — and every living thing in it is measured by how far it has surrendered into that condition. The stains have surrendered fully and are scarcely alive at all. The blind soft things are most of the way down. The amphibian is on the long slope, eyes already given up, hurry already given up, only the throat still arguing faintly for the world it left. And the people who once knelt here were the least surrendered of all, the most alive, which is exactly why they could not remain — they only touched the stillness and carried a little of it back out with them, like the warmth that stays in cloth for a while after it leaves the steam.

The hollow does not want residents. It is not lonely; loneliness is a kind of reaching, and it has set reaching aside. It tolerates what comes, slows it, warms it, lets it grow patient and blind and quiet to match, and in time takes it down into the long pale rest of the rock. Whatever lives here is on its way to becoming the place. That is the only tenancy the hollow offers, and it is in no hurry to fill it, and it will be exactly as it is whether the niche is occupied or empty, whether the throat on the ledge still pulses or has finally gone still — warm, wet, dim, full, and waiting for no one, because waiting, too, is a thing it gave up long ago.
