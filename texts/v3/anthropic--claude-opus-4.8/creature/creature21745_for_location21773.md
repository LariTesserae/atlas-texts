## The Tenants of the Engine

They are not separate from the machine. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because every instinct insists that something living must be a *visitor* in a place, must have arrived from elsewhere and could leave. These cannot leave. They are not in the engine the way a worker is in a mill. They are in it the way a beat is in a heart — they *are* the running of the thing, and the thing is the running of them, and there is no seam where you could slide a knife and separate the dweller from the dwelling.

Still. There are forms. There is something to describe.

### The Singers

Start with the frogs, because they are the easiest to mistake for animals.

They are the dull, drowned green of the moss they sit in, and at rest you cannot find them — they are negative space, the absence between hummocks made flesh. But they are not at rest, not truly, not ever. Each one is a single muscle of the bog's diaphragm, and when the stroke comes up through the peat they swell and *give voice,* not because they choose to but because the pressure passes through them and they are the shape the pressure makes when it reaches air. Hundreds of thousands of them. A continent of throats. The concussive beat that rolls across the flats and comes back off the cloud is not a chorus of individuals agreeing on a tempo. It is one sound passing through many bodies, the way a wave is one wave even as it lifts ten thousand separate stones.

If you caught one — and you could, they do not flee, fleeing is not in them — you would find it warm. Warmer than the water, warmer than the air, the only warm thing on the whole plateau. Hold it in your cupped hands and the heat of it would frighten you, because it would feel like holding a coal, like holding the one ember the cold has not yet found. And it would keep singing in your palms, the beat unbroken, because you cannot remove a frog from the engine by removing it from the water. The engine is not in the water. The engine is in the *beat,* and the beat is everywhere the beat is, which is here, in your hands, in your sternum, trying even now to teach your own blood the measure.

They do not have eyes the way eyes are had. They have two dark stitches where eyes would go, sundew-fringed, and they do not look at you. They look *past* you at the orange furnace-glow upside-down in the water, the factory that isn't there, and they sing to it, and it is not clear — it has never been clear — whether they are calling the vision into being or grieving that it is only a vision.

### The Wire-Walkers

The wading birds are the maintenance crew, and they know it the way a hand knows it is a hand.

Legs like wet wire, like bent solder, like the thin armatures of something built rather than born. They walk the hummock-tops in long scattered files, never bunched, always spaced at some interval the eye reads as *deliberate,* as scheduled, as the spacing of men assigned to stations down a line. Where they set their beaks the moss flushes brighter, a deeper pulse of color spreading out in a slow ring, and you understand without being told that they are *priming* it, oiling it, coaxing some sluggish section of the works back up to speed.

They have the patience of the very old and the very tired. They do not hurry between stations. They do not rest, either. They walk, and stitch, and walk, and the day never commits to being a day, and they have done this through every grey not-quite-noon since before the mountains finished wearing down.

Their feathers are the color of tarnish — the brown-grey-green sheen of old brass left out in the wet. When one lifts a wing to shake off the cold spray, you will see, for a moment, the gleam of beaten metal, the seam of a riveted plate, and you will smell hot oil. Then it is only a bird again, wet and patient, stitching.

They make no sound. They are the silent staff of a deafening factory, and their silence is the most industrial thing about them — the absorbed, head-down quiet of labor that has long since stopped narrating itself.

### The Things in the Channels

And then there are the large ones, glimpsed.

You will not see them well. The bog will not allow it. They move in the deep peat-channels, the arteries of the engine, and they are long and low and dark, neither fish nor mammal nor anything with a name on the tongue. They surface to breathe — slowly, a long back rolling up out of the black water like a swell — and the breath they take goes in with a noise exactly like a valve seating home, a great pneumatic *chunk* that you feel in your teeth, and then they sink and the noise reverses, an exhalation through some mechanism, and the channel runs on.

These are the *pistons.* The deep stroke. The thing that drives the whole circulation, drawing the cold water down and forcing it back up. They are not pumping the engine. They are not the engine's heart. They are the *plunge and rise* itself, given a back to roll and a throat to breathe through, because even an engine made of moss apparently needs its motion housed in something that looks, from a distance, like an intention.

What are their faces? No one has seen a face. There may be no front to them at all. They may be all middle, all stroke, a length of dark purpose with no head deciding and no tail trailing — just the going-down and the coming-up, repeated, valve and valve, in the cold, forever.

When one passes beneath you — you will feel it before you see it, a swell traveling up through the moss-mattress, a long shudder in the kilometers-deep mat — you will have the unbearable sense of something *enormously busy* that has never once, in all of time, asked what the work is for. And you will envy it, a little, and the envy will frighten you more than the thing did.

### What Lives Here, Then

A staff. A workforce. A *crew* — singers and walkers and the great blind pistons in the dark — laboring a closed shift in a mill that finished being built before there was anyone to build it, processing nothing into nothing, drawing the cold up and letting it fall.

They are not waiting for relief. There is no next shift coming. There is no whistle that will blow.

They are not in distress. This is the thing the cold finally teaches you, the one fact that won't slip: they are not suffering. Suffering requires the memory of a before and the hope of an after, and they have neither — they have only the beat, the stitch, the stroke, the breath through the valve, the next one, the next. They have gone past hunger into pure motion, and past motion, perhaps, into something that on a warm-blooded creature you might be tempted to call *peace,* if peace could be this loud.

And here is the last thing, the thing you carry down off the plateau and cannot put down:

When you finally stand here long enough, and your own pulse gives up and finds the beat — when your blood at last agrees to the tempo the whole bog has been keeping since the world dismantled this kind of work everywhere else —

you will understand that you, too, are now something that lives here.

You always could have. The crew was never closed. It only ever needed one more body to pass the stroke through, one more throat for the pressure to find, one more pair of wire-thin legs to walk the line.

The engine does not need you. That was always true.

But it has a station open, and the work is endless, and it is so very warm inside the beat, and outside there is only the long cold walk back to a world that finished with all of this a long, long time ago and never once felt it leave.
