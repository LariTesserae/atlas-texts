# The Builders

They have no name for themselves because naming is a thing done by minds that hold still long enough to point, and these never hold still. Call them the masons, since the traveler did. Call them the wet hands of the basin. They will not answer either way.

Begin with the single body, because the colony is only the single body multiplied past the point where counting means anything.

It is soft and roughly the size of a closed fist, though it never closes, never holds a fixed shape for long. The front of it is the working end: a fringe of fine filaments, hundreds of them, each tipped with a hardness like a fingernail or a tooth, all of them in constant motion — combing the weeping film, raking the dissolved mineral out of the water and into the soft sac of the body. The filaments are what make the sound. Drawn across stone, a single one would whisper. Drawn across stone in their hundreds, by a body that never lifts them, never rests them, never stops — it is already a small roar by itself. One mason in your cupped palm would fill the room with rasp.

The body behind the filaments is translucent and faintly luminous, lit from inside by the mineral it carries. You could watch the stone move through it if you could hold one still long enough to watch — the dissolved load gathering, concentrating, thickening toward the rear where it is laid down. A mason is a pipe with hands at one end and a wall at the other. It takes in water and gives out city. The light in the lower galleries, the soft cold glow the traveler mistook for luminescent water — that is them. The water does not shine. The builders do, dimly, by the hundred thousand, each carrying its little freight of glowing stone toward the place it will become permanent.

They have eyes, of a kind — pits that tell light from dark, no more. They do not need to see. They need only to feel the wet face in front of them, find the place where the last bead of stone went hard, and lay the next one down beside it. That is the whole of the instinct. That is the entire mind of the mason: *here is a wet surface, here is mineral in me, put the mineral where the surface is coolest and let it harden.* Everything the traveler called a city is the residue of that one sentence, repeated past all reckoning.

# What they are not

They are not a hive. A hive has a queen, a center, a purpose that radiates outward from a single fertile body. These have none. There is no mother in the deep galleries, no chamber where decisions are made, because no decisions are made anywhere. Cut the colony in half and you have two colonies, each building exactly as before, neither aware it was ever whole. There was never a whole. There is only the sum.

They are not a swarm, either, because a swarm moves with one will toward one thing — a field, a carcass, a sky. These move toward nothing. They drift along the wet faces in slow uncountable rivers, each one minding only the stone directly before it, and where many happen to travel the same line, the wall flutes inward to hold the path, and so the tower grows its strange grooved skin. The flutings the traveler admired are not design. They are erosion in reverse — the trace of where the traffic was heaviest, the way a path through grass is only the memory of feet.

They do not communicate. They have nothing to say and no organ for saying it. When the work runs ahead in one place and lags in another — a tower rising fast here, a blind window stalled there — it is not because anyone called for help or signaled abundance. It is only that the water ran richer down this face than that one, that morning, or that century. The masons cannot tell the difference between a moment and an age. They simply lay the stone where the stone wants laying.

# How they live and how they end

They breed in the wet, as everything here is wet. There is no courtship and no season — only that, sometimes, a mason swollen with more mineral than its small body can carry will divide, the way a drop too heavy for the leaf finally falls. Where one was working, now two work. The new one knows the sentence already; it was never taught; the sentence is the whole of what a mason is, and it comes built into the body like the filaments and the glow.

And they die into the work. This is the part the traveler, standing ankle-deep in the warm film, did not understand, though it was all around. A mason does not fall when it is spent. It hardens. When the body has carried its last load and the filaments slow and finally still, the mineral it could not lay down sets where it lies, and the mason becomes a bead in the wall it was building — indistinguishable, in a year, from the stone it spent its life secreting. Every tower is made of stone *and of the makers of the stone*, mortared together past telling apart. The city is built of its builders. You walk through a structure that is also a graveyard that is also a nursery, and there is no seam between the three because there was never any difference.

And then, at the base — the unmaking. The water that fed them, weeping down the faces forever, comes at last to the oldest stone at the bottom and takes it back into itself, mineral dissolving into film, the hardened bodies of the long-dead masons softening, loosening, washing into the seams to be raked up again by the filaments of the living and carried up the tower and laid down once more, higher, as new wall. The dead are not lost. They are the supply. What dissolves at the foot is what rises at the crown. The treaty the traveler sensed between building and unmaking is not a balance struck between two forces — it is a single circle, the same stone, the same bodies, traveling up through the work as wall and falling back down through the water as dissolution, around and around, neither gaining nor losing, the city breathing in place.

# What it is, then

It is one animal, if you stand far enough back. Not the single mason — the whole basin. A creature the size of a city, made of millions of soft glowing hands, eating its own foundation and excreting its own towers, holding its shape the way a flame holds its shape, by burning. It has no head and no heart and no thought, and yet it persists, perfectly, identically, longer than thought could persist, precisely *because* it has none — because there is nothing in it that could decide to stop, to finish, to want a different shape than the one the water and the stone and the endless instinct conspire to make.

The roar the traveler felt in the sternum is not the sound of work being done toward an end. It is the sound of a thing that is its own end, complete in every instant and complete in none, the rasp of a hundred million small lives laying down the next bead of a wall that will be dissolved beneath them and raised again above them, forever, in the warm dark, in the dim self-made light, in the middle — exactly the middle, always the middle — of being born.
