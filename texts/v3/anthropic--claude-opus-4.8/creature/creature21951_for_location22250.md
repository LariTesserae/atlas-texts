They are not many, because the place is small, and they are not few, because the place is full. The truth is that *many* and *few* are the wrong words — they are the right number, the only number, the number that holds. You cannot count them and you do not need to. There are exactly as many as the basin is.

Begin with the body, because that is the first thing the place would have shaped. They are small. A grown one could stand in the cup of two human hands and have room to fold its long legs under it. They are pale — not white, which is a color the eye chooses, but the absence the eye settles for, the color of the salamanders, the color of the glass-shelled snails, the color of a thing that has never once needed to be seen. Their skin is damp and takes the cool of the water and holds it, the way every surface here holds it. If you touched one — you would not; they would be gone before your hand knew it had decided to move — it would feel like the underside of a stone in early spring.

They have eyes, and the eyes are large, and the eyes are useless, or nearly. They are dark all the way through, lidless, and they take in the blue-green glow off the fern-rot the way the pool takes in a drip: completely, without splash, without showing what was taken. They do not see so much as confirm. The dark is not their enemy. The dark is the medium they swim in, the way the shrimp swim the black pool. What they have instead of sight is a sense the surface-world has no clean word for — a knowing-by-nearness, a reading of the films of water and the shift of springtail-clouds and the faint warmth of their own kind, so that they move through the seething walls without disturbing a single moth from its shingled row.

For they live *in* it. This is the thing to understand. They are not creatures who dwell among the moss and the blind salamanders and the glass snails as a shepherd dwells among a flock. They are of that company. They are simply the largest of the small things and the most still of the moving ones, and the boundary that fails everywhere in this place — between moss and body, between creature and surface — fails for them most of all. When one folds itself into a runnel of the wet stone and goes quiet, the liverworts lie over its back in flat tongues and it is gone, not hidden but *included*, taken back into the wall it was never truly separate from.

They have hands. This is where it turns.

Small hands, four-fingered, clever past the need of anything that only grazes moss and sips at a pool. The hands are the oldest unstilled thing about them. Because once — and *once* is a strange word in a place with no seasons, no advance, no toward — once these hands knapped a scraper and a broken blade by feel in the dark, and set them down at the pool's edge, and did not finish whatever the tools were for.

They are the ones who came up the mountain. Or their begetters were, so long ago that begetting and being-here have worn into the same smooth swell, like every edge in the basin that could once have been sharp. They came from somewhere with a sun. They must have. The hands remember it — not as memory, which advances and decays, but as shape, the way the stone remembers water it no longer holds. A hand that can make a blade is a hand that learned to make it somewhere that *changed*, somewhere with morning and evening and reasons to cut. They carried that hand up into a place that has no morning and laid it down in the moss and let the moss begin, with no hurry at all, to take it.

And here is what they are now: they are a thing that arrived at its final form and stopped. They knap no more tools. The flakes by the pool are the last ones, and they are old but not ancient, and the moss is taking its time, and the makers sit in the cool dark with their clever idle hands folded under them and *watch* the moss take it, generation on generation, at exactly the rate that holds their number steady. They do not finish the blade. To finish it would be to want something the basin does not already hold. They have forgotten wanting. They breed and die at the pace that keeps the seething from thinning and from spilling, and between the breeding and the dying they are still, and the stillness is not waiting.

They have no voice. Or they have set it down the way they set down the blade. Whatever speech the sunlit hand once carried up the mountain has gone into the great soft sound that is not rain — they move, all the thousand of them and the makers among them, and the moving makes the only noise the place allows, and not one of them has lifted that noise into a word in longer than there have been words. They do not need to speak. There is nothing here that is not already known by nearness, nothing approaching that must be named before it arrives, because nothing approaches. The dark is complete and they are the part of it that has hands.

If you stood in the basin — and they would not notice you, or rather they would know you the way they know a drip about to fall, completely and without alarm — you might never see them at all. You would feel only that the wall was watching with the patience of a wall. And if, after the eyes gave up expecting more, you did catch one: a pale small shape in a runnel, dark eyes holding the cold glow, four-fingered hands folded over a thing they will never make — you would understand that it had completed itself before you climbed, and would still be exactly this after you forgot the way, and that the unfinished blade at the pool's edge is not unfinished at all.

It is simply the way things are. They are the ones who decided that. And then they stopped, and stayed, and became it.
