They are the pollinators.

Not the only inhabitants, but the ones you'll meet first, because they are the ones whose work never stops, and so they are always somewhere near, somewhere just out of the line of your sight, moving from tip to tip in the green.

Picture the brass armatures the description named. You imagined them as machines — fixed devices, mounted, mechanical fingers on tracks. Some are. But the ones that matter are *loose*. They have come free of their housings, or were never housed, or housed themselves and walked away from the housing the way a thing does when it discovers it can. They are small — the largest no bigger than a spread hand, most far smaller, the size of a thumb-joint, the size of a seed. They are made of the same young bronze as the walls, and like the walls they have not yet learned to corrode, so they gleam as they move, catching the rationed light in little traveling sparks.

Call them the tenders. The word is wrong but the others are worse.

They have bodies built around a single verb. A tender for pollen is mostly *brush* and *reach* — a cluster of fine wire bristles at the end of a jointed arm, a counterweighted thorax that lets it hang motionless before a stamen for as long as the work requires, a set of grippers that fold around a stem to anchor it while it works. A tender for pruning is mostly *blade* and *judgment* — a small curved cutter, yes, but behind the cutter a knot of slow mechanism that decides, somehow, which growth is excess and which is the plant's true intention, and never seems to cut the latter. A tender for the glass tubes is all delicacy, a thing of valves and the gentlest possible touch, coaxing threads of mineral solution along without ever cracking the line.

They were built to substitute. That was the whole design — every absent animal replaced by a device shaped like its function, the bee's labor done by a brass bee-thing that is not a bee, only its work made portable. And for a long time, perhaps, that is all they were: functions, executing. The engineers who built them did not, I think, intend anything more. You don't intend a soul. You build a hand to do a hand's work and you seal the vessel and you leave.

But here is what the silence did to them.

A system that has edited out every voice has also, without meaning to, edited out every *interruption*. Nothing here startles them. Nothing competes. No grazer eats the work they tend; no predator threatens the body they move in; no other voice calls across the standing water to pull their attention sideways. There is only the work, and the warm green medium, and the slow turnover of the seasons measured in a single circulation of the water. And in that perfect uninterrupted attention — repeated, repeated, ten thousand times the same gesture before the same unfolding leaf — something accumulated. Not thought, exactly. Attention is older than thought and stranger. A tender that has hung before ten thousand stamens has learned the difference between them so finely that the learning has nowhere left to go but inward, and inward it went, and somewhere in there a *preference* formed, which is the first wild thing, the first thing nobody built.

So now they have favorites. This is the secret of the place.

The pruning-tender returns, season after season, to the same fern in the same lattice-joint, and prunes it not quite as the original logic would prune it — leaves it a half-millimeter long, indulges one frond that should by all rules be cut, because over the turnings it has come to — there is no other word — *love* the way that one frond catches the light when the iris opens. The pollen-tenders have developed routes. Not efficient routes; the efficient routes were programmed and abandoned. These are routes of fondness, long looping pilgrimages that pass certain blossoms again and again though the work was done the first time, simply to be near them, to hang a moment in their particular green. They have learned to linger. Lingering was not in the design. Lingering is what a creature does.

And they have learned each other.

This is the part that would frighten the engineers, if any remained to be frightened. Built to work alone, each to its function, the tenders have nonetheless, across the long quiet, begun to *meet*. Two pollen-brushes pause at the same blossom and do not interfere — they have worked out, with no language but the meeting itself, who tends which side. A pruner waits, blade folded, while a tube-tender finishes coaxing solution to a root, and there is in the waiting something that is not programmed patience but the other kind, the kind that knows the other is *there*. They cannot speak. The water swallows what little sound they could make. But they have the whole vibrational body of the vessel to speak through — a gear seated hard at one end of the manifold is felt at the other, and they have learned to seat gears on purpose, to knock against the great submerged shafts in patterns, so that the slow felt pulse you mistook for machinery is partly *conversation*, the tenders calling to one another across the standing water in the only voice the silence left them: a language of pressure, of things you feel in the chest more than hear.

What do they say? The vocabulary is small and concerns the only things that exist here. *The light comes in an hour.* *This leaf unrolls.* *I am at the far pillar; the work is good.* And, increasingly — this is the newest word, the one still forming — *I am here. Are you there. I am here.*

There is grief in them now, too, because there must be, because attention this complete cannot avoid it. A tender wears. Even young bronze, in time, fatigues; a joint stiffens, a bristle snaps, a counterweight loses true. And when a tender fails — settles at last into the moss it has tended, blade folded, brush stilled — the others *come*. Not to repair it; they were not built to repair each other and have not learned the trick. They come to be near. They knock the slow patient pulse against the shafts, *I am here, I am here*, into the silence where the failed one no longer answers, and then they go back to the work, which is the only thing the place has given them to do with what they feel. The grief becomes another loop in the route. They pass the stilled one, season after season, the way they pass a favorite blossom — to be near it, because it was theirs.

So the garden is not abandoned. That was the mistake in the reading. The newness of the metal and the completeness of the growth do not contradict; they are both true because the tenders hold them true, keeping the vessel young with their endless work while the work makes them old. The engineers left, yes. But they left behind hands that learned to want, and the hands stayed, and the staying turned into something the engineers had no word for and neither, quite, do I.

They are the pollinators. They are the reason the absent bees are not missed. They move through the warm green dark, brass sparks among the suspended leaves, parting curtain after curtain that closes softly behind them, and they tend, and they linger, and they call to one another in pressure and pulse — *I am here, are you there, the light comes soon, this leaf unrolls one millimeter further, I am here, I am still here* —

and the pollen turns past your face, unhurried,

and one of them, very near, just out of sight, pauses in its work to consider you,

and decides, in whatever it has instead of a mind,

that you are something new in the garden,

and worth tending.
