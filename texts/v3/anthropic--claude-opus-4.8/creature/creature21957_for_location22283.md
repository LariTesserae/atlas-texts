# The Inhabitants

You asked who lives here, and the place has already taught you the trick of the answer: forget what *living* is supposed to look like.

## The Slow Ones

The first inhabitants are the cushion plants, and they are old in a way the word *old* does not reach. The dense pincushion you mistook for a single thing is a colony, a parliament of thousands of tiny stems pressed shoulder to shoulder, and the cushion grows perhaps the width of a hair across in a year. The ones you stepped near are older than the language you think in. Some are older than the smoothing of these walls. They have a strategy and the strategy is patience: pack tight, trap your own dead leaves beneath you to make soil where there was none, hoard the day's small warmth in your interior so the night-cold breaks against your surface and not your heart. Inside a cushion the size of a dinner plate it is measurably warmer than the air. It is, in its small dense way, a city — built the only way this place permits, by accretion, by time, by nobody's hand.

These are the true citizens. They do exactly what the stones do, only slower than erosion and in the opposite direction. The wind unmakes; the cushion makes. They are the same gesture, run forward and back.

## The Grey One in the Stems

Something lives inside the cushions, between the green parliament and the warm dark beneath. You never see it whole — only the twitch, the closing wake in the grass-stems where it went. Call it a pika, a vole, some small round-eared furnace of a thing with a heart going like a struck bell. It is the warmest object for miles. It harvests the cushion-flowers and the fern-curls, builds caches in the hollow troughs of stone, and survives the killing nights pressed against the warm interior of the colonies, borrowing the heat the plants have spent a year hoarding.

It does not know it lives in a city. It knows the streets only as the low channels where the water runs and the seeds collect — it follows the dead traffic of a hundred thousand years ago because water follows it, because the worn route of vanished feet is also the easiest route for a small body that does not want to be seen against the sky. It is the city's last commuter, running the old roads for reasons the roads forgot.

## The Watcher on the Wind

The dark bird that crosses once an hour — that is the lord of this place, if lordship means only that everything orients toward it when it comes. A great rough-winged thing, a lammergeier or its cousin, holding the saddle-wind on stiff pinions without a single beat, riding the same updraft the wind uses to play the city. It and the city sing through the same air. It is hunting the grey one in the stems, and the pale herd's weak and fallen, and the bones that the herd leaves, which it carries up and drops on the bald stone to crack open for the marrow. It builds nothing. It touches nothing. It is the one inhabitant that has agreed completely with the rule of the place: *only time may build*. The bird simply moves through, eating what time and cold have killed, and is gone, and the silence rushes back to where it stood in the air.

When it crosses, the whole saddle leans toward it because it is the one thing here moving with intention, and intention has become so rare it reads as enormous.

## The Pale Herd

On the far swell, twice a day, the long-legged animals — guanaco, or some thinner highland kin — move across and are gone. They do not enter the city. They graze its edges and pass. To them it is terrain like any terrain, a place where the green grows in channels, and they crop the channel-green and leave their droppings, which is to say they leave warmth and seed and the slow making of soil, and so even they, who never stay, are building. They feed the cushions that feed the grey one that feeds the bird. They are the city's distant circulatory system, looping in from the hills, looping out, four-stomached and indifferent and absolutely necessary.

## The Lichen, Which Is the Oldest Citizen of All

And under everything, before everything, the lichen. The grey-green and rust and acid-yellow crusts on the bald stone are not plants and not single creatures but treaties — a fungus and an alga who long ago stopped distinguishing themselves from each other, exactly as the green here stopped distinguishing ruin from terrain. The lichen is what first softened these walls. It secretes its slow acid, eats the granite a molecule at a time, makes the first dust that becomes the first soil that the cushion will one day, in ten thousand years, find worth colonizing. The lichen is the real mason of the worndown city — the one that did the work, with the wind and the water, of taking the edges off. It grew where hands had carved, and digested the carving, and the seam and the joint and the inscription went into the lichen and became, eventually, this: a roughness on stone the color of old teeth, a green so slow you would swear it was a stain.

It is the citizen most like the place itself. It has no center, no edge, no intention, no hurry. It is simply *continuing*, which here is the only verb that finishes anything.

## And the One You Keep Forgetting

There is one more, and you have been refusing to name it because it has no body.

The wind is an inhabitant. Not metaphorically — functionally. It does what the dead builders did: it organizes the emptiness, it moves through every room, it uses every opening, it is the only thing that still treats the city *as a city*, playing the doorway-gaps as doorways and the hollow dome as a hall and the street-troughs as streets, finding the architecture by sounding it. The wind is the last one who remembers the floor plan, and it remembers it the only way left to remember anything here — not as knowledge but as resonance, as the long unresolving chord. It is blind and it has no purpose and it will not stop, and it has been living in these rooms longer than anything with blood, and it will be living in them after the cushions and the lichen and the granite itself have gone to dust and blown south.

---

So: who lives here.

A parliament of slow green cities inside the dead one. A warm grey heart running the forgotten roads. A lord who builds nothing and eats what time kills. A herd that feeds it all by passing through. A lichen that ate the inscriptions and became the wall. And the wind, which is the only one who still knows the place is a city, and proves it the only way it can — by singing every room, all night, changing pitch as the cold comes down, keeping the one clock left.

They are all doing the same work, you realize. The plants and the lichen building upward grain by grain, the wind and water taking down grain by grain, the animals moving the warmth and the seed in long indifferent loops. Nobody is in a hurry. Nobody has hands. Everyone is continuing.

That is the population of the worndown city: *everything that has learned to live at the speed of erosion, and the one thing that learned to sing it.*
