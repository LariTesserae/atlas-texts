They are small. That is the first thing to surrender — the expectation of a face at eye level, a thing that turns to regard you. Nothing here is built to that scale. The basin's citizens are measured in millimeters and fractions, in leg-spans and wing-beats, and their multitude is the only largeness they achieve. You are looking for an inhabitant. There is no inhabitant. There is a population so dense it has become a texture.

Begin with the ones nearest your skin, because they will find you first.

On every surface — the underside of leaves, the seams of bark, the warm folds of your own arrived body — there are the soft-bodied grazers. Springtails by the uncountable, working the film of decay, flicking away from disturbance and settling again before the disturbance has finished. Mites in such quantity that the leaf-litter is less a layer of dead matter than a slow boil of them, each one too small to resolve, the lot of them together giving the ground its faint give, its sense of being upholstered. They do not announce themselves. They have never needed to. In the dark, scent and vibration are the whole of communication, and the air is thick with both — a chemical conversation running at a pitch below any animal's hearing, the basin talking to itself in molecules.

Above them, suspended, are the ones who string the dark. Spiders, but not the sentinels of a bright web glittering with dew — these are patient and lightless, their silk strung in the corridors between trunks where nothing flies fast enough to break it cleanly. They sit at the hubs of architectures they have never seen, reading the world through tremor alone. A wing touches a thread four feet away and the spider knows its size, its struggle, its likely direction, all without a photon. The whole canopy is webbed in this way, layer over layer, a nervous system of silk strung through the deeper one of branch and vine. When something dies in the air it does not fall far. It is caught, and felt, and attended to.

And the wings. The busy threaded air. Moths that have given up most of their color because color is for the seen — drab, furred, navigating by the chemical plumes of their own kind and of the flowers that open only in the dark and pay only in scent. Tiny flies in clouds that hang in the still pockets, mating and dying in the span of a single human breath, their bodies the raw feedstock of the engine, falling, being caught, being eaten, being decomposed, beginning again. The wingbeat you feel against your cheek is not one creature. It is the medium. The air here is a worked substance, the way water is worked in a reef.

Go down, now, into the give of the floor.

This is where the real machinery runs, and it runs on the dead. Termites and the pale soft larvae of beetles and the wet ropes of worms — but more than any of these, the fungi, which are not animal at all and which are nonetheless the great laborers of the place, threading every inch of the under-dark with filament too fine to see, dissolving the fallen leaf, the fallen body, the fallen everything, and passing it along. The mushroom you might find is only the part that fruits; the organism is the basin itself, an interpenetrating mesh that has no edge you could point to and say *here it begins*. The hum you mistook for your blood is partly this: the steady, lightless, tireless reduction of all that has stopped moving back into the stock of all that will move again.

So who lives here?

The honest answer keeps refusing the singular. There is no one whose home this is. There is a *guild* — predator and grazer and decomposer and the green standing things that feed on the rain-light filtered down to almost nothing — and the guild is so old, so settled, so finely interlocked that no member of it stands out as the resident. They have negotiated their hush across durations no individual lives long enough to perceive. The quiet is not peace. It is saturation. Every niche is filled, every surface claimed, every joule of the meager filtered light and every gram of the falling dead already accounted for, already someone's. There is no slack in the system. To be loud, to be large, to be visible — these are the luxuries of a place with room to spare, and there is no room here. The creatures have grown small and silent and many because that is the shape that fits when life is turned all the way up.

If you want a single image to hold, hold this: a beetle the length of a grain of rice, walking the underside of a leaf in total darkness, its feet finding purchase in a fur of mites it does not eat and does not disturb, on its way to a fungus it will graze a fraction of, in air strung with silk that registers its passage but does not stir, while above and below and around it the same scene repeats a billion times with minor variations, none of them watched, none of them needing to be. The beetle is not the inhabitant either. The repetition is the inhabitant. The pattern is the only thing here big enough to call a creature, and it has no eyes, and it does not sleep, and it has been here longer than the hills had their present shape.

You arrived expecting to meet something.

Something is meeting you. It is already at your ankles and in the warm crook of your elbow and on the surface of your eye. It does not know it has met you. It has simply found, in your warmth and your dampness and your slow falling cells, another surface — another small contribution to the stock — and it has begun, without ceremony, in the dark, to do the only thing anyone here has ever done.

It has begun to put you to use.
