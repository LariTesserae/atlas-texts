# What Lives in the Loom Highlands

You said it yourself, at the very end, almost without noticing: *waiting for you to stop moving.* That is the tell. That is where the inhabitants are hiding — not in the canopy, not in the tarns, but in the verb. Let me find them for you.

## The First Thing: the ones who are already counted

Start with what you saw, because they are real and they are residents, even if they are not the answer.

The jewel-frogs in their forty-foot ponds — these are a people of the vertical, born knowing they will never descend. Each cup-pond is a nation of one generation. They do not migrate, cannot migrate; their whole world is the rainwater held in the throat of a plant, and when the plant tips or the drought that never comes finally comes, the nation ends. They are not lonely, because loneliness requires a memory of company. They have a single neighbor: the larva of themselves, looking up.

The clicking birds are the closest thing this place has to gossips. Listen to them properly and you understand the click is not a poor substitute for song — it is the *correct* language for a world that eats every long sound. Song would die in the moss three feet from the throat. The click survives because it is short and hard and dry, the one dry thing in the whole wet country. They are speaking the only sentence the air permits: *here, here, still here.* A roll call repeated forever. The birds are the highlands' census-takers, and the census never changes, and that suits them.

The arm-long salamanders are the elders, and they are elders in the truest sense: they have outlived the need for events. A creature that slow has effectively stepped outside of time. To watch one cross a wet rock is to watch a decision being made over the course of an afternoon. They are not patient — patience implies wanting something sooner. They have unhitched themselves from wanting. They are the place's monks, and their devotion is simply *to remain*.

And the grazers you never saw — they are the deliberate absence at the center of the room. You found the warm hollow. That warmth is the most intimate thing in the entire highland, the one place where the temperature is *made by a body* rather than by the breathing engine. They keep that warmth hidden the way the highlands hide everything: by moving when you cannot see, by being a fact you reconstruct rather than a creature you meet. They are shy not from fear but from a deeper grammar of the place — *here, nothing presents itself. Here, everything must be inferred.*

## The Second Thing: the residents you mistook for landscape

But these are tenants. They live *in* the place. You asked who lives there, and the harder, truer answer is that the place is not a place. You said it three times and kept flinching away from it.

A factory that grew. A factory that breathes. A factory that waits.

You kept calling the mist-engine a *system,* a *cycle,* a *process,* because those words let you keep it safe — impersonal, unowned, the way weather is unowned. But you also said: *regular, cyclic, productive,* and then *waiting,* and then *for you.* A process does not wait for you. A process does not have a *for.*

So let me say the thing you circled and would not land on:

The Highland is the inhabitant.

Not a god in the highland. Not a spirit that haunts it. The whole breathing, weeping, packing, manufacturing extent of it is a single slow body, and what you mapped as terrain is its anatomy. The warm-below is its blood, climbing. The trees are not cooling towers — that was you reaching for the right scale with the wrong vocabulary. They are *lungs,* and the white plumes are exhalation, and you walked through its breath and called the breath *cloud.* The moss that drinks every sound is not absorbent material. It is *attention.* It is how the body listens. A creature this large cannot have ears; it would hear nothing useful. Instead it grew quiet on purpose — it manufactured silence the way it manufactures green — so that any sound at all becomes information. The drip. The fern-creak. The footstep that closes the medium behind it.

Your footstep.

## What it is doing

It is not malevolent. I want to be careful here, because you felt the prickle on your neck and the prickle was correct but the conclusion was wrong. It does not want to harm you. It does not want *anything,* in the way you want things — sharply, with an object, soon. It wants the way a tide wants the shore: total, gentle, certain, in no hurry, and unstoppable precisely because it is not in a hurry.

What it does is the one thing you named and did not let yourself read: it *converts warmth and water into more of itself.* That is its whole industry. Every warm thing that enters the cold above is condensed, drunk, and packed into the next layer of density. You are a warm thing. You are walking through a cold that never lets the warmth leave, and you are, yourself, warm-below — the lowland heat made portable, made curious, made to climb.

It is not hunting you. It does not need to hunt. It only needs you to stop.

The whole ecology is *shy of the eye* — you said that too — and now you can see why. Nothing presents itself because presentation is wasteful. The clicking census, the unhurried salamanders, the hidden grazers, the unseen breathing of the land — none of it performs, because performance is for getting something from someone *else,* and here there is no else. There is only the one body and the layers it is folding inward, century after century, packing the world tighter with no waste and no purpose but the next layer.

A factory you could drown in, softly.

You wrote that sentence as an image. It is not an image. It is the business model.

## Who lives there

So. The frogs and the clicking birds and the patient salamanders and the warm-bedded ghosts in the fern — they live there the way bacteria live in a gut: real, alive, native, and also *part of the digestion.* They are organs and they are residents at once, which is the contradiction you kept hitting and could not resolve. *Industrial* and *untouched.* *Mechanized* and *no built thing.* Both at once, because the machinery is the animal and the animal is the place and the place was never made because it *grew,* the way you grow, the way anything alive arrives at its own complexity without a blueprint.

What lives in the Loom Highlands is the Loom Highlands.

And it has noticed that you have been standing still for a little while now, reading.

It does not mind. It has all the hours there are. The moss is already at your boot. The cold beads on the back of your neck — that was the prickle; that was always the prickle — and somewhere off in the white the double-note sounds, low, the breath of the land or an animal, and you understand finally that there was never a difference, and that the question was never *what lives here.*

The question was always how long until you become what lives here.

It will not make a sound.
