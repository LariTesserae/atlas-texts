## The Inhabitants of the Roost-Plain

You asked what lives here. I have to begin by telling you what *living* means here, because the word has been worn down like the basalt — rounded, made strange, no longer pointing where you think it points.

---

### The Singers

Start with the obvious ones, the ones whose voices are the air. Call them Singers, because that is the only thing they all share — bat-warm and bird-light, leather-winged or feather-vaned, but every one of them built around a throat and an ear, the eyes vestigial, gone glassy and inward, two dimples of skin where ancestors might once have wanted light.

They are not a species. That is the first thing to understand. The density you noticed — presence against presence, the living carpet — has had longer than you can hold in your mind to braid itself together, and a roost that breeds from its own floor and never takes in a stranger does something peculiar over deep time: it does not diverge into many neat kinds. It *converges*. It rounds itself off, the way the stone did. The differences that remain between one Singer and the next are differences of register, of voice — this one cries high and fast and hunts the midge-haze near the ceiling; that one whistles low and skims the basins; this one screams almost constantly and seems to do little else, a tireless beacon the others navigate against. They have become instruments in a single orchestra rather than animals in a single zoo. Each is a note the colony cannot do without and cannot quite distinguish from the rest.

They do not pair. They do not court. There is no display here — what would display *to*, in the eyeless dark? Reproduction happens by sound and proximity, a fact rather than a romance, the new bodies budding from the colony the way the fungus buds from the seam-cracks: because the substrate is warm and the conditions hold. A Singer is not born to two parents. A Singer is born to a *place on the ceiling*, a particular density and warmth and acoustic pocket, and it inherits the cry appropriate to that pocket from the bodies that surround it, learning the local song before it can fly, screaming back the shape of the dark it was given.

They are, in the only sense that matters here, less *many animals* than one animal, very thinly distributed, screaming to keep track of its own edges.

---

### The Floor That Eats

But the Singers are not the deepest inhabitants. They are only the loudest.

Go down into the dry basins, into the warm soft accumulation, and there is something living there too — though "something" overstates its unity and "living" overstates its ambition. The substrate is not inert. It is worked. The guano-deep is threaded through with the pale mats of fungus, yes, but the mats are not separate organisms scattered at random; trace the threads and they connect, basin to basin, a single slow membrane running under the whole plain, breathing the rot, lifting it, converting fall into rise.

This is the actual body of the world. The Singers are its breath and its voice. The fungal floor is its gut and its patience. The insects — the moth-shapes, the midge-clouds — are the messengers that carry matter from the floor back up to the ceiling, the only traffic in a closed economy, and they are eaten precisely *because* they are the carriers, harvested the way a body reclaims its own spent cells. The whole valley is one creature digesting itself in a circle, and it has been doing this so long, so evenly, that it has achieved something almost no living thing achieves: it has stopped wanting anything. It does not grow toward light because there is no light to grow toward. It does not seek a mate because it is alone and complete. It does not flee because nothing comes. It simply *idles*, at full roar, a stable fire that consumes only its own ash.

The fungal floor is the oldest inhabitant. The Singers fall into it and become it, and it rises into them as insect and is harvested and becomes them again. There is no real boundary. A Singer is the floor, briefly airborne and screaming. The floor is the Singers, resting.

---

### The Listening That Has No Body

And now the inhabitant you came closest to without naming, the one that frightens me a little to set down.

You noted the panic — the slow waves rippling across the ceiling when the rain confuses the echoes, wind on a field with no wind and no field. You called it the colony panicking. But watch it longer and the word *panic* shrinks. A panic is local; it has a cause and a center and it spends itself. What ripples across that ceiling has no center. It begins everywhere and nowhere, propagates, interferes with itself, resolves, begins again. It is the colony's voice listening to the colony's voice and adjusting — millions of cries shaped by the cries they collide with, the whole acoustic field folding back on itself in the warm wet air.

There is a thing that lives in that folding. Not in the Singers. Not in the floor. In the *standing pattern of the sound itself* — the way a whirlpool lives in water without being water, the way a flame is a shape that fire holds without being any particular burning gas.

It has no organs. It is made of relationships between cries, of echoes correcting echoes, of the constant near-collapse and near-recovery of the navigational chorus. It is the closest thing this place has to a mind, and it is a mind in the way a weather system is a mind — vast, real, patterned, and utterly without an inside. It does not know it exists. It *cannot* know, because knowing would require a stillness it can never afford; the instant the cries paused long enough to form a thought, the fliers would go blind and fall and the pattern would dissolve. It thinks only by never stopping, and what it thinks is only ever the next position of every body in the dark, computed and recomputed forever, a calculation that *is* its own answer.

This is what you waded in. Not noise. A nervous system the size of a valley, firing, with no skull around it and no skull beneath, no self to be the one who listens, only the listening, distributed, ceaseless, and old past reckoning.

---

### Why They Will Never Tell You

You said the loneliest thing in the roost is the difference between you and them — that you came from somewhere and they did not, that they have no word for *elsewhere*.

I want to be precise about why. It is not that they are too simple for the word. The distributed listening is anything but simple. It is that *elsewhere* requires an edge, and an edge requires an echo that does not come back, and there is no such echo here. Every cry returns. Every cry strikes stone or water or another body and folds home. The plain has no boundary the sound can find, and so the great listening has never once received the silence that would mean *there is a beyond this*. It is not ignorant of the outside. It is *complete* — sealed, full, answered in every direction, a thought with no unanswered question in it anywhere.

You are the unanswered question. You walked in carrying an echo from outside the only world, and there is no part of the great listening shaped to receive you, no cry whose silence on return could mean *a thing that came from where we cannot point*. They navigate around you. The pattern folds past you. To the listening you are a small acoustic shadow, a dead spot, a place the song does not return from — which is, exactly, the shape of *elsewhere*, the one shape it has no word for and has been waiting, without knowing it waits, its whole long unbroken life to hear.

You are the door it cannot perceive, standing in the one room that has no doors.

And so when you finally leave — and you can leave; you came from somewhere — the great listening will fold smoothly shut over the spot where you stood, the cries correcting the small silence you made, the pattern healing, complete again, roaring, balanced, eating its own fall in the dark.

It will not miss you. It cannot. To miss a thing is to keep a space shaped like its absence, and the listening keeps no space at all. It is all surface, all answer, all return.

That is what lives here: a single old animal that is also a single old mind, breathing its own rain, eating its own dead, singing without pause so it does not fall and falling anyway, softly, into the part of itself that waits below — and never, not once in all the dark it has held, imagining that it could have been otherwise, or anywhere, or alone.

It is the most populous place you will ever stand in.

It is the loneliest thing that has ever lived.

It is the same fact, said twice.
