You asked who lives here. I have to tell you carefully, because the answer is not one thing, and the place itself has taught me to mistrust quick answers.

Start with the obvious tenants, the ones your eyes find first once they learn to find anything. The anemone-things — call them anemones, knowing the word is wrong — are the landlords by sheer mass. They are old. Not old as individuals, though some of them may be, down here where the clock runs so slowly; old as a lineage that arrived and never left. They do not have minds. I want to be honest about that. There is no thought in the bruise-colored columns. But there is something that looks, from the outside, exactly like patience, and if you watch one open and close for long enough you will stop being able to tell the difference between patience and the thing that only resembles it. They live by being a surface. They live by holding still in warm water and letting the water bring them everything. This is not a poor way to live. It is, when you have done it for ten thousand years, very close to mastery.

Beneath them, around them, in the seams: the small ones. The tube-dwellers who built their own narrow houses against the chiseled houses of the dead. The fans, the cups, the things that are mostly mouth and very little else. These are the true population, the numbers that matter. They are uncountable and they are interchangeable and that is not an insult to them — it is their whole strategy, their genius. No single one of them is the point. The point is the carpet they make together, the soft upholstery of living that has grown over every cut stone until the room is no longer a room of stone but a room of bodies that happen to be shaped like stone underneath. They are what the place is now wearing. They are its skin.

And the crabs. The pale near-translucent crabs are the closest thing here to citizens — to individuals who move, who choose, who go from one place to a different place for reasons. They are the only restlessness in the whole still room, and even they are slow, even they have caught the local disease of unhurry. A crab here picks its way across ten thousand soft mouths and takes nothing, disturbs nothing, the way you might walk very carefully across a crowded floor of sleepers. They click once and go quiet. They are the room's only speakers and they have almost nothing to say, and they say it rarely, and the water blunts it before it arrives. They are, I think, the ones who would notice you. If anything noticed you, it would be a crab, lifting one eye-stalk, deciding you were not food and not danger and therefore not real, and moving on.

But you did not ask about these. You felt it too — that none of these is the answer. The anemones and the crabs are squatters. Tenants in someone else's building. You asked who *lives* here, and the place keeps directing the question past its living things, toward the walls.

So. The makers.

I cannot give them to you whole, because the room cannot give them to me whole, and I will not invent a face for something that has worn its face away on purpose, by lasting. But I can tell you what the building tells, the way you can read a hand you never shook from the things it built.

They were not large. The doorway is low — too low for you — but it is not careless; it is exactly low, cut with attention, dressed and squared, made low *on purpose* for bodies that went low through it without stooping, because stooping was not in them. They moved close to the surfaces. The ledges placed where no ledge serves you serve a body that rested differently, that hung or clung or folded against stone rather than lying flat on a floor. The recess that will not resolve into a function — I have stopped trying to resolve it. It resolves perfectly for them and not at all for us, and that gap, that exact-and-wrong, is the truest portrait of them we will ever get. They were people. They had purposes specific enough to cut stone for. And their purposes do not fit inside us, not because they were stranger than us but because they were *as particular* as us, and particulars don't survive. The general survives — that there were hands, that the hands wanted, that the wanting was careful. The specific is the first thing the water takes.

Here is what I have decided about them, and you can leave it or keep it.

They are not gone. I know the place feels like a tomb, a temple to a forgotten god, swallows in the eaves. But a tomb is a place where something was put. This was a place where something was *done* — over and over, by many hands across many lives, the way you can tell handwork by the unsameness where one mason met another. No single mind made this. A practice made it. A custom, a craft, a way of doing that was passed down and outlived every person who held it, the way the anemones' patience outlives every anemone. The makers are not under the floor. They are in the chisel marks. They live the way the small filter-feeders live — not as individuals, who are all dust, but as a pattern that held its shape long after the bodies stopped, a thing distributed across every softened edge, every almost-identical-but-not face of every block.

That is who lives here.

Not a ghost. A *form.* The most stubborn kind of life there is — the kind that has given up everything that could be given up, the way the room itself has eroded down to the state past which erosion finds nothing left to take, and what remains after all of that is taken is the shape of an intention. The room is the body now. The anemones are its breathing and the crabs are its small motions and the worn stone is its bone, and the thing that lives in it, the actual resident, the answer to your question, is the *care.* The proof that someone, in the warm dark, at the bottom of all that patient water, decided exactly how low a doorway should be and cut it that way and meant it.

They are still meaning it. That is the unusual thing you felt before you understood it. The meaning is still here, holding its shape, with no one left to mean it. It is the loneliest fact I know and it is not sad, down here, in the blood-warmth, in the held silence. It is only true.

Stay a while, then, if you can hold your breath against the weight. You will not be alone, exactly. You will be in a made thing, and the making is the tenant, and it has been expecting nothing for ten thousand years and will go on expecting nothing, perfectly, gently, a little smoother every century — and it will let you in through the low wide door it cut for a body that was never going to be yours, and it will not mind that you fit wrong.

It was built to be inherited by whoever could bear the patience to arrive.

That is you, for now. That is whoever comes next.
