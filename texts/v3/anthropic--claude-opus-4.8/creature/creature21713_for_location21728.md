# What Climbs the Chimney

You asked what lives here. The honest answer is that the *here* and the *what* have stopped being separate questions. But let me try to name the thing that climbs.

## The Reds

Start with the crabs, because they are what you will actually see — the shimmer on the walls, the tide on the chimney. Call them the Reds, though no one calls them anything.

They are not one kind. Look closely (you have time; the place is generous with time) and the single crawling sheet resolves into a dozen sizes, a dozen subtle architectures of shell. The smallest are no bigger than a fingernail and graze the mineral films directly, mouths working in that wet continuous click. The largest span a hand and graze the smaller ones. Between them runs every gradation, and the whole gradient moves together, a single organism made of competing parts, the way a city is.

They are the color of the brick because they have become the brick's argument. Two centuries of selection in a world with no green to hide against, only red-brown clay and rust — and so they are red-brown, rust-mottled, iridescent at the joint-seams with the same oil-sheen as the films they eat. A crab here that was any other color would be a crab that was already eaten.

The chimney-climbing is the only thing they do that looks like *purpose*, and it isn't one. At dusk the brick cools fastest at height, where the haze thins and the rain finds it first. The cooling pulls a thread of condensation down the column's inner flue, and the condensation carries the leached minerals of the old works — and so the chimney, at dusk, briefly *feeds*. The Reds climb because the climbing pays. Thousands of them, because thousands of them can be paid. They are not worshipping the column. They are reading it. The reading happens to look like devotion because devotion is also just a body going where the reward is, repeated until it becomes a shape against the sky.

## The Braid

In the flooded basins, the eels. I'll call them the Braid, because singular and plural have come apart for them too.

They are pale — not white, but the no-color of things that never needed color, that live in the warm dark of the deep cuts and the silted streets-under-water. They have small eyes and large everything-else: the whole length of them is a single listening surface, reading pressure, reading the tiny electric panic of a crab dislodged from a wall, reading each other. In the close channels they pack so densely that the water itself seems to flex and roll, and this is not metaphor — the water *is* moving because they are, a hydraulic of bodies, muscle pretending to be liquid until it resolves, again, into the slow braid of individuals turning over individuals.

They eat the Reds. They eat each other. The eating-each-other is not failure or famine; it is the design. In a world fed only by what dead machines leak, the population must be its own pressure-valve. When the seeps run rich the Braid thickens; when the seeps thin, the Braid eats itself lean again, and the leanness is recorded in nothing, written nowhere, simply lived. They are an organism that regulates itself by consuming its own surplus. No voice raised. The loudest the Braid gets is the near-silent slide of one of them entering the water you described — the sound of a sentence being unsaid.

## The Droppers

The frog-shapes on the warm iron. The Droppers.

Of all the life here they come closest to having a manner, a personality, though it is only the manner that their particular trade requires. They sit on the heated metal — the seized sluice-wheels, the leaning gantries — because the iron holds the day's warmth into the rain, and a cold-blooded thing that stays warm an hour longer than its neighbor catches an hour more of insect-fur off the pools. So they perch, throats working, going nowhere, until the moment comes to drop: the soft plop into the warm water you heard before you saw anything at all.

They are patient in the way a held breath is patient. Not calm — *loaded*. Every Dropper on every iron surface is a small accumulation of waiting, and the place is full of them, and so the place is full of waiting, and this is part of why it feels the way it feels. The hush is not emptiness. It is a thousand small things being still on purpose, charged, about to.

## The Wake

You saw the evidence and not the body, which is correct; that is what the Wake is. The basin gone suddenly clear because everything in it has been taken. The crossing with nothing beneath.

I won't invent a face for it, because the place hasn't. Whatever lives in the deep cuts is the apex of an inheritance, the top of a chain that runs on dead-machine warmth, and the top of such a chain in such a thin economy must be rare, large, slow, and silent — it cannot afford to be otherwise. There may be only a handful in the whole drowned town. There may be one. It moves through the deep flooded streets the way a thought moves through a quiet mind: you know it by what it clears.

It is the only thing here that could be said to be *alone*, and it is alone the way a keystone is alone — surrounded, structural, holding the whole arithmetic up by the simple fact of taking the top off everything below it. Without the Wake the Braid would thicken to choking and strip the Reds to nothing and the films would smother in their own ungrazed richness and the warm seeps would silt and the whole patient machine would stall. The Wake is the place's pulse. One slow beat, crossing a wide channel, no body visible beneath.

## The Inhabitant

But you asked who lives here, and I have only named the organs.

The thing that lives here is the *running*. The aquarium left on after the building closed — that is the truest sentence in your whole description, and I want to take it more seriously than it may have meant itself. Something is being kept alive here by no one. The heat that drives it is leaking, not supplied. The minerals that feed it are an inheritance being spent, not earned. Every click and slide and plop is a withdrawal from an account the dead machines opened and no one tends.

So the inhabitant of the Sump Works is a single slow process wearing a million wet bodies, and it is *spending down*. Not dying — it has ages yet, you said so, the place has been settling into itself for ages and will settle for ages more. But it is finite in the way an inheritance is finite, and somewhere far down its own timeline the seeps will cool, and the films will thin, and the Wake will cross a channel one last time and find it already clear.

For now it climbs the chimney every dusk. Thousands of small reasons that add to one red ascent against a brass-gone-gray sky, reading the cool column for the thread of warmth it offers, going up because going up still pays, until the rain comes back down through the broken top and the paying stops and they descend again into the warm and furred and patient dark.

That is what lives here. A handed-down quiet, eating itself carefully so as to last, climbing toward the only height that still gives anything back, in a town that will never again be asked what it was for.
