# The Tenders

You asked who lives here. Not the salamanders — they are residents, not inhabitants; the place does not need them and they do not answer it. I mean the ones the place has made, the ones who *belong* to it the way the moss belongs, by being shaped to its single overwhelming fact.

They are the ones who keep the engines.

---

## What They Are

There are never many. Perhaps forty across the whole shelf, perhaps fewer now — the work does not require crowds, and crowds could not survive the spacing this country demands. Each one lives close to a cluster of machines, in a hut grown half into the moss, and most will go a season without seeing another face that is not their own reflection in wet iron.

They are not the people who built the works. The builders came, riveted, ran the lines, and left — that was a campaign, an arrival, a thing with an end. The Tenders are what the campaign left behind to mind what it could not bring itself to abandon. They were sent, originally, some of them. Some were born here to others who were sent. By now the distinction has worn smooth. They are simply the ones who are here.

You would know one at distance by the gait. Everyone on the shelf walks the same way: a slow testing tread, weight rocked onto each foot before it commits, because the ground gives and weeps and you must ask it every time whether it will hold. They do not walk *across* the moss so much as *negotiate* with it, step by step, a lifetime of small courtesies paid to a surface that is always almost solid. The walk marks them. Set one down in any lowland city and you would see it instantly — that careful rocking gait on pavement that does not need it, a body still asking permission of a ground that long ago stopped answering.

## How They Hear, Having Stopped

The text told you the truth: stand here long enough and you stop trying to hear. The Tenders stopped generations ago, and the stopping went all the way down.

They speak very little, and when they do it is close to the ear and flat and short, a held-down murmur for the rare still pockets — the inside of a hut, the lee of a pump-house wall — where speech can survive at all. Out on the open shelf they do not bother. They point. They have a whole grammar of pointing, far past the lowland repertoire: the chin-jut for *over there, you know the one*, the flat hand pressed downward for *wait, stay, the ground is bad*, the two fingers laid against the other's forearm that means *I have seen it, I agree, we need not say so*. A conversation between two Tenders looks, from a distance, like two people barely acknowledging each other while their hands do quiet certain work in the air.

But here is the thing the silence did. Because they gave up the ear, they took up the other reading the place offers. They feel the turbines through their feet — the throb that comes up through the spongy floor — and they read it the way a lowlander reads a voice. A healthy machine has a certain note in the soles, a steady deep evenness; a sick one drags, or flutters, or develops a hard edge in the vibration that a Tender will feel three hundred meters off and turn toward without knowing she has turned. They diagnose by the ground. They have words for these vibrations — *the drag*, *the catch*, *the long shudder*, *the good hum* — words they almost never speak aloud and mostly only sign, pressing a flat palm to the moss and then making the shape that says which trouble it is.

And the light. With no shadows to read time by, with the mist throwing the brightness back flat and even from every quarter, they have learned to read the *quality* of the glow — the faint warming and cooling of that white ceiling, the slow tidal shift no instrument they were given could measure but their eyes have been calibrated to over years. They know the hour by the temperature of the light. They know weather coming by a thinning high up that has no name in any language the builders spoke.

## What They Have Become Inside

The cool keeps everything slow, the text said — the plants grow at the pace of stone. The Tenders have caught it. Not in body but in *tempo*, in the inner clock. They are patient in a way that unsettles visitors badly. Ask one a question (you must come close, you must put your mouth near the ear) and the answer may arrive a full minute later, unhurried, as though the question had to travel down through the moss and back up. This is not slowness of mind. It is that they live among processes — the building of a moss-cushion across a thousand years, the grinding extraction of the turbines, the ten-thousand-year arrival of the stone itself — beside which any human urgency reveals itself as a kind of comedy. They have been recalibrated to longer durations. They wait the way the moss waits. They have all the time the shelf has, which is more than enough.

This makes them, by lowland reckoning, strange to the point of frightening. A Tender does not startle. A Tender does not hurry to news. A Tender receiving word that someone has died will receive it the way the ground receives a footstep — accept the weight, give a little, hold. Grief here is not a storm; it is a season, and seasons on the shelf are very long and very quiet and have no edges you could point to and say, *here it began, here it broke.*

## What They Believe, If You Can Call It That

They do not pray to the water, exactly. But they have an attitude toward it that is not far off, and it is the central fact of their inner life.

The water is the only thing on the shelf that *acts*. The moss waits. The stone waits. The Tenders themselves have learned to wait. But the water comes from everywhere at once — up through the floor, down off every edge, sideways as standing mist — and it never stops and it answers to nothing, and the machines exist solely to drink a thread of it and turn that thread into the hum that justifies the Tenders' being here at all. So the water is, to them, the nearest thing to a will. Not benevolent. Not cruel. *Indifferent and total*, falling and falling, louder than thought — and they have organized themselves around it the way pious people organize themselves around a god whose only attribute is that it does not stop.

Their few rituals are water-rituals, and they are small. When a Tender takes over a station from one who is leaving or dead, she presses both palms to the moss until it weeps up around her fingers, and holds them there, and the place gives her its water, and that is the whole ceremony. It says: *I have asked the ground, and it has answered, and now I am the one who is here.*

They are not unhappy. I want to be clear about that, because everything I have described — the spacing, the silence, the flatness, the enormous patience — reads to a lowlander as deprivation, and it is not. The Tenders have what the place gives, which is: a clean relentless light that forgives nothing and asks nothing; a ground that always almost holds; a sound so total it has become the same as silence; work that matters and is never finished; and the company, spaced wide across the green, of a few others who walk the same testing walk and read the same throb in the floor and know, without needing to say it, the two-fingered touch on the forearm — *I have seen it. I agree. We need not say so.*

They are the moss's idea of a person. Total, and sparse, and patient, and saturated, and standing very far apart in a great loud green room, completely at home.
