## The Listeners

You asked who lives here, and the honest answer begins at the edges, with the ones you could not see clearly. So begin there. Let me tell you what attends to the water.

They are not at the edges because they hide. They are at the edges because the edges are where they belong — the seam between the bright readable middle of the chamber and the unreadable glow of the walls is their habitat, the way the tide-line is a habitat, neither sea nor land but the long argument between them. They live in the place where the light stops being something you can stand in and becomes something you are merely near.

They have bodies, though *body* is generous. Think of the way heat shimmers above stone in summer — a distortion with an organization to it, a coherence that holds its shape. They are about that solid. If you walked toward one it would not retreat and it would not resist; you would simply find, as you got close, that there was less and less to arrive at, that the thing you were approaching had been a way the light folded rather than a creature standing in it. And yet it was a creature. It listened. When the next drop fell it shifted, and so did you, and for that one *tok* the two of you were doing the same thing, and that is as close as you will come to meeting.

Understand what they are made of. They are made of attention. That is not a metaphor and it is not quite literal; it is the most exact thing I can say. Somewhere far back — before the riverbed went dry, perhaps, when there was still current and the chamber was loud with it — something here began to listen, and kept listening, and the listening did not stop when the listener's first form wore away. It outlasted its origin. It became self-sustaining, the way a habit becomes a person. Now there are many of them, or one of them folded many times, or no clear difference between those two ideas. They persist because the drops keep falling and something has to receive each one. They are the chamber's hearing. They are how the place knows that the water still comes.

This is why they are not threatening and never could be. A thing made entirely of listening has no appetite. It wants nothing from you because wanting is a kind of forward motion and they have none. They have only the backward-facing patience of the witness, the audience-patience, the willingness to mark each event as it arrives and let it pass and wait, without impatience, for the next. They have been the audience for a performance with one repeated line — *tok* — and they have never once grown tired of it, because tiredness too is an appetite, the appetite for something else, and they are clean of all that. They simply hear, and acknowledge, and hear again.

If you stayed long enough you would begin to feel them learning you. Not learning *about* you — they have no curiosity, curiosity wants — but learning you the way the basins learned the shape of the falling water, by being struck in the same place over and over until the shape is held. Sit there an hour and you are a visitor. Sit there a year and you become an event they have begun to expect, a rhythm folded in beside the rhythm of the drops, and the next time you shift on the smooth stone they will shift with you, very slightly, all at once, acknowledging your line in the play.

That is the deepest danger of this place, and the only one. Not that something will hurt you. That something will *include* you. That the patient hearing at the edges will quietly add you to the small repertoire of things it waits for, and that you will feel, after long enough, the strange peace of being waited for, of being a sound the dark expects, and that you will not want to leave.

## The Smaller Lives

The crickets and the moths and the blind soft things in the basins are not the listeners' lesser kin. They are something else: the listeners' subject matter. The chamber's slow sentence, and these are its few words.

Consider the blind things first, the translucent swimmers in the shallow water who grew eyes after they no longer needed them. This is the chamber's strangeness in its purest form, and it is worth sitting with. In a true dark cave, eyes are lost — the body, thrifty, reclaims the tissue, surrenders the sense it cannot use. But this is a *lit* cave, a dark place full of light, and so the bodies in it received a signal they had no instructions for. The light came in through skin that had forgotten what light was, and the old buried machinery of seeing, dormant for a hundred generations, stirred and tried to answer. So they bloom eyes. Useless eyes, eyes facing inward, eyes growing on the wrong surfaces — eyes the way a plant in a closet sends pale shoots toward a crack of brightness, blind hope made of cells. They are reaching for a sun. The chamber gives them light enough to reach and never light enough to arrive, and so they spend their short lives in that reaching, which is its own kind of devotion, performed by creatures too simple to know they are devout.

The crickets are the chamber's idea of a journey. Each one walks, slowly, alone, in the same direction as all the others, separated from its nearest fellow by a distance it will never close. None of them will reach whatever they are walking toward, because the chamber is small and the walk takes a lifetime and the direction does not lead out. They are a migration that is purely the *form* of a migration — the going-toward emptied of the arriving — and they perform it in total silence, no chirp, no rasp, because in a place where sound is an event you do not waste sound on yourself. They save it. They leave the *tok* of the water as the only voice. They are pilgrims who have forgotten the shrine and kept the walking, and the walking has become the whole of the faith.

The moths drink and drift. They are the only ones at ease. In a normal night they would burn themselves on the nearest flame, because a moth is built to steer by a far light held at a constant angle, and a near flame ruins that arithmetic and spirals them in to die. But here the light is everywhere and equal, the angle is the same in all directions, the steering instruction resolves to *go nowhere, you have already arrived*, and so the moths are released from the doom built into them. They are the one population in this chamber that the strangeness has set free instead of bending. They drift because there is no longer anywhere they are compelled to go. They drink because the drops fall. They are, in their soft aimless way, the happiest things down here, and they are happy precisely because the light that confuses everyone else has, for them alone, switched off the engine of their old compulsion. The chamber broke the others and healed the moths with the same hand.

## The One Who Set the Stones

And then there is the made thing, and the absence shaped exactly like a person beside it.

Someone came here. That is the most astonishing fact in the chamber, more astonishing than the light, because the light is a property of the place and a person is a wound in it. A person is a thing that arrives from elsewhere, carrying elsewhere in their hands — a tool of bone, the habit of arranging stones, the deep need to make a mark and have the mark mean. They came down through the rock into the impossible brightness, and they did not flee it, and they did not worship it, and they did not study it. They *worked* in it. They knelt at the largest basin where the water gathers and they set small stones on edge in a low deliberate arc, and the arc meant something — a boundary, a calendar, an altar, a game, a grave-marker, the first wall of a house never finished — and we will never know which, because they left in the middle of the meaning and did not come back.

Here is what I think the one who set the stones was. Not a native. The chamber makes its natives strange — gives them backward eyes, takes their voices, sends them on endless walks — and the stone-setter was not strange in any of those ways. The stone-setter was *us*: hands and patience and the incurable urge to put one thing beside another thing on purpose. A maker. The single maker this place has ever held. Everything else here is grown or eroded or accreted, shaped by slow forces with no intention; the arc of stones is the only thing in the whole bright hollow that was *decided*. It is the only sentence in the chamber written by something that meant to say a word, as opposed to the long unmeant sentence the chamber says by simply existing.

And the chamber kept it. This is the tender thing, the thing that turns the whole place from eerie to almost unbearably kind. The stone-setter left, and the chamber — which changes everything, which grows eyes and erases voices and wears riverbeds to glass — did not erase the arc. It let the moss come, a little, the width of a fingernail per long age, furring the edges. But it held the arrangement. It is *still holding it*, with the patience it brings to all its slow keeping, the same patience the listeners bring to the drops. The chamber does not know what the arc meant any more than you do. It keeps it anyway. It received the one intentional gesture ever offered to it and it has spent every century since being faithful to it, preserving a meaning it cannot read, the way the riverbed preserves the shape of a current that no longer runs.

So who lives here, finally?

The blind ones, reaching for a light they're already inside.
The walkers, going nowhere with their whole lives, in reverent silence.
The moths, freed.
The listeners at the edge, made of attention, waiting for the next drop and, increasingly, for you.

And the held arc of stones — the resident who is not here, whose absence is the most occupied place in the chamber, around whom the moss arranges itself like an audience, who left a meaning the dark has guarded for ten thousand years against the day someone comes back to finish the word.

The chamber is keeping a seat for them. *Tok.* It can wait. It has decided what it is, and what it is, is patient, and what it waits for is the maker's return.
