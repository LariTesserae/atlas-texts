# The People of the Cold Ascent

They are small. This is the first thing to understand about them, because it is the thing the cold and the thin air made first. Not stunted — they are perfectly made, dense and compact, with no wasted length anywhere. A people built like the cushion-plants they grow between the lanes: low, rounded, hoarding their own heat. The tall do not last here; the long-limbed lose their warmth too fast and their breath comes too hard in the thin air. So over the long ages the bodies drew inward. Broad through the chest, deep in the lung, with great slow hearts that beat unhurried. Their hands are wide and short-fingered and immensely strong, and they keep them tucked away when not working — into the folds of their garments, into the warmth of their own armpits, into the wool of the herd beasts when they lean against them.

Their faces are broad and high in the cheek, the skin gone ruddy-dark from the pale fierce light at altitude, weathered early so that the young and the old are not easily told apart. They have the eyes of people who look at distance — set deep beneath heavy brows, narrowed by long habit against the glare off ice and stone. A people who squint. A people whose first expression, before any other, is the small contraction of the face that means *I am reading the far weather, the falling clouds below, the question of the night to come.*

## How They Move

Slowly. Always slowly, and this is not weakness but wisdom worn into the body. To hurry is to sweat, and to sweat in this cold is to freeze; to hurry is to gasp, and the air does not reward gasping. So they have made of slowness a whole grammar of being. They climb the endless stairs at a pace that never changes — the same patient tread going up as coming down, loaded or empty, young or old. You can hear it if you listen: the soft even fall of feet on worn stone, never breaking rhythm, the rhythm of people who have a great deal of climbing ahead of them and intend to still be climbing at the end of their lives.

They do not gesture much. The cold discourages it; the hands stay warm and still. They have learned instead to say things with the smallest movements — a tilt of the head, a slow blink, a shift of weight. A stranger from the lower world would think them grave, even sullen, and would be wrong. They are simply economical. They spend nothing they do not need to spend, not heat, not breath, not motion, not words.

## The Voice and the Silence

Their language is made for the still air. It is quiet and carries — pitched and shaped to travel across the gulf between terraces without shouting, because shouting wastes the breath and the lungs are too precious to waste. So they speak in a low clear tone that seems soft up close but reaches improbably far, and a conversation may happen between two people three terraces apart, each unhurried, the words floating down and up across the cold gap with long patient pauses between them. Much of their speech *is* pause. Silence is not the absence of talk among them; it is part of the talk. To answer too quickly would be a rudeness, a kind of grasping. They let a question sit. They let it cool, the way they let everything cool, and then they answer it, and the answer is the better for having waited.

They sing, but rarely, and only in the lowest tiers where the air is thickest, where the lungs can afford it — long slow drones that hold a single note across many breaths, passed from singer to singer so the sound never stops though no one person sustains it. The way they build their walls, stone fitted to stone with no seam: that is how they sing, each voice seated against the next so closely that no silence enters between.

## What They Carry in Them

Understand the deepest thing: they are a people who do not believe in *enough*. Not from greed — from the long memory of cold. Every one of them carries, bred into the marrow, the knowledge that the night is long and the water is thin and the green is a film you must tend grain by grain or lose. So they hoard. Not selfishly; communally, fiercely, with a discipline that has the force of religion. The cisterns are sacred. The seed-grain is sacred. The dung-fuel stacked and dried in the rafters is counted and accounted. A person who wastes — who lets the cistern overflow, who burns more than the night requires, who takes a second portion before the first has gone round — has committed not a crime but a kind of blasphemy, an offense against the patient logic that keeps them all alive.

And so they share without question, because the sharing *is* the hoarding — the warmth of the packed houses, the heat of the herd beasts on the lower floors, the bodies sleeping close in the long nights. They have no word that means *alone* without also meaning *cold*. To be apart is to be unwarmed. They raise their walls touching and they sleep in heaps and they keep their animals beneath their floors not only for the beasts' sakes but because the whole dense mass of the city is a single engine for the keeping of heat, and every body in it — human, beast, fowl — is a coal in the common fire.

## The Long Patience

They build for grandchildren they will not meet. A wall begun in one lifetime is a foundation in the next and a finished terrace three generations on; no one expects to see their own work completed, and this does not trouble them, because they do not measure a life by what it finishes. They measure it by what it *adds* — by the courses of stone laid, the seed saved, the children kept warm through how many winters. Their dead are built into the walls. Not buried — there is no soil to spare and the rock is too hard — but laid into hollows in the great revetments, sealed with a fitted stone, so that the terraces are literally made of the ancestors, course on course, the whole city a vertical reliquary climbing the cold flank. When they lean against a wall to rest, they are leaning against their own grandfathers. When they lay a new stone, they lay it on the bones of the ones who taught them. They are never, in the truest sense, alone on the mountain. The city is full of the held-still dead, packed in with the living, all of them enduring the same long night.

## Their Joy, Which Is Real

Do not think them grim. The slowness, the silence, the hoarding — a visitor reads these as severity, but from the inside they are the texture of a deep contentment. These are people who have made a home in a place that should not allow one, and they know it, and the knowing is a quiet daily triumph that never goes stale. They take pleasure in small warm things with an intensity the lower world has lost: the first portion of hot grain in the bitter dark before dawn; the brief improbable blaze of the cushion-flowers in their short season, which they watch the way other peoples watch festivals; the particular sound of bells coming up through the haze on a still evening; the moment in a long climb when you round a switchback and the whole stacked galaxy of lamplit windows opens below you and you can see, all at once, the entire breathing body of the place you belong to.

They love the brief warm hour of midday when the pale gold light reaches the high terraces, and they stop their work, all of them, and turn their broad weathered faces to it like the conifers in the courtyards, and say nothing, and let the warmth sit, and do not hurry it.

And at evening, when the lamps come on and the frost begins and the bells fall quiet, they go inside to the heaped warmth of their kin and their beasts and their hoarded fire, and they are not afraid of the night, because they have been keeping this night for ten thousand years, course on course, stone on bone, breath on patient breath — a people who long ago made peace with the cold by refusing, slowly and forever, to let it have the last word.
