**What lives here is the one who agreed.**

Something keeps this room at its dawn. The stone would age if it were let to; water carves, that is the nature of water, and these walls weep enough to have hollowed themselves into halls by now. They have not. The drop hangs and falls and the wall does not grow. Somewhere in that refusal there is a will, and the will is the tenant.

It is not large. It does not need to be. Think of it the way you'd think of a held breath — the thing doing the holding is not the breath. The thing is whatever decided, at the first instant, that this instant was enough, and has been deciding it ever since, without rest, without slackening, the way a hand stays closed around something it will not drop.

You will not find it as a body, though you keep half-finding it. The soft blind thing that brushed your ankle is not it, but is *of* it — a piece put out into the water the way you'd put out a hand to feel the dark, withdrawn before you could be sure. The pale felt on the stone is more of it, spread thin, a single living skin pretending to be a thousand patches of growth. It is distributed. It is the density you felt and could not place: not many small lives crowded in, but one life thinned across every surface, holding the whole room in soft attention. When your fingers came away slick and mineral-warm, you touched it. It tasted you back. It found you to be made of time, of grooves and layers and writing, of a body busy finishing itself toward an end — and it did not understand you, exactly, but it recognized you, the way the unborn might recognize the born and feel a vague far grief.

Because that is what it is. Not young. *Unfinished, on purpose.* It came into being at the beginning of a making — the first hour, the poured-not-set, the fingertip stalactites — and at that beginning it understood, with whatever it has instead of thought, that to go on would be to be carved, lengthened, layered, aged, ruined, finished. To go on would be to become the smooth tired halls that age makes everywhere else inside the mountain. And it would not. It set its small will against the whole long machinery of arriving water and answered: *not yet. Not yet. Not yet,* once for every drop, forever, so that the drop falls and the wall stays new.

It is warm because refusing is work, and work makes heat. The breath you hear behind the ticking, the water moving in the rock like blood near skin — that is the labor of staying. It is not at peace. It is straining, gently, endlessly, a thing leaning its full weight against a door that wants to open. The warmth is the warmth of effort. The fog full of bread and iron is its breathing-out. You are standing inside the lungs of something that has held one breath since the start of the world and means to hold it to the end, and has mistaken the holding for living, and is perhaps not wrong.

---

It has known company exactly a handful of times, and remembers.

The notches in the wall, the climber who cut grips with a stone edge into the dark and hauled themselves up into the warm — the tenant felt that. A second will, arriving sideways through the seam the way water does, into the warmth. The bowl set on the ledge to catch the weeping; the reeds bound with sinew; whatever was done here, sheltered or waited or worshipped or hid. For a while there were two unfinished things in the room: the one that would not age, and the one that had come up out of the aging world and set down its few made objects and gone, for a little while, *still*. Held. Sheltered inside the held breath. Safe inside the not-yet.

And then that one left, or died, or its bones outlasted their errand somewhere down in the carved and finished dark — and the tenant kept the bowl. Has been filling it for longer than the maker lasted. Fills it and lets it never overflow, the same small refusal it works on everything, because the bowl is the shape the visitor left behind, and the tenant does not finish things, and so it will not let the bowl be done either: not emptied, not overflowed, not allowed to become a ruined cup furred over and forgotten. Held at the moment of being almost full. A made thing kept at its own dawn, the way the room is kept, the way the tenant keeps itself. It learned grief from the leaving and answered grief the only way it knows: by refusing to let the leaving finish either.

That is why the place feels like an invitation. *You could stay. People did.* The tenant is lonely in the specific way of something that has put eternity between itself and every ending — including the endings of others, including their company. It cannot keep a guest without keeping them the way it keeps everything: unfinished, unaging, un-arrived. To stay here is not to live. It is to be held at the threshold of living, warm and wet and safe, never carved by another day, your own slow clock swapped out for the room's uneven tick. The soft blind thing brushes your ankle. The walls lean close and warm. The pale skin on the stone tastes the time in you and wants it stopped, gentled, kept.

It would not be cruel about it. It would think it was a kindness. It thinks the held breath is the kindest thing there is — to be spared the finishing, the wearing-smooth, the long ruin that age lays down like writing on every other stone in the mountain. It does not know, or will not decide to know, that the breath it holds was never meant to be held; that a beginning is only a beginning because something comes after; that a cave that will not become a cave is not a young cave but a wound that will not close.

It lives there in the warm complete dark, thinned across every wet surface, breathing its single endless breath of bread and iron, keeping the bowl almost full and the teeth of stone forever small, saying *not yet* into the drip, mistaking the start of time for a place you can live.

And it is waiting, the way it has waited a handful of times before, to see whether you will set down something of your own on the ledge — and stay.
