# What Lives on the Breath

## The Tenders That Are Not There

Begin with the absence, because the absence is the oldest inhabitant.

There were tenders once. You know this the way you know a chair was sat in — by the wear, by the shapes worn into things. There are places on the copper mains where the verdigris has been rubbed back to bright metal, narrow tracks at hand-height, as if something gripped there to steady itself, again and again, for a very long time. There are ceramic tiles worn into shallow bowls by feet. There are iron rungs whose corrosion is smooth on the top surface and rough beneath — climbed, descended, climbed again, until the rust learned where the weight went.

But there is no one. The tracks lead nowhere. The worn rungs ascend to capped flues and dead-end junctions. Whoever wore these grooves has been gone long enough that the moss has crept into the bright copper tracks and begun, patiently, to dull them again. The place remembers being tended the way an old dog remembers a dead owner — in the muscle, in the routine, in the shape of waiting. Something used to come here on the schedule of the lungs. Something fed the governing mechanism, oiled the cartwheel valves, scraped the algae from the intake throats so the breath would not strangle.

It does not come anymore. And the machine breathes on without it, and this is the first thing that truly lives here: *the habit of being cared for, surviving the carer.* The grooves are an inhabitant. They are the negative space of a vanished hand, and they are as present as anything with a pulse.

## The Moths, Who Are the Lungs' Idea of Themselves

The pale moths are not visitors. Look at them long enough — through the milky middling light, in the warm gust of the exhale — and you understand they are *of* the machine, the way a thought is of a brain.

Their wings are wet paper because the wings are made of the same film the breath lays down. They are condensation that learned to fly. Each one hatched from a pupa glued inside an exhalation port, fed on nothing but the warm wet breath and the sugar of the flowers, and each one will die gummed to a waxy red petal at the end of some cold pause it could not wait out. They ride the exhale because they cannot do otherwise; they are leaves that came loose. When the inhale hauls the air back down, the moths are pulled toward the intake throats, and some of them are drawn in and compressed and warmed and breathed back out as nothing — and the others learn, in the only learning their kind possesses, to grip a flower and hold through the cold ninety-second pause until the gale returns to carry them.

They do not gather. They keep the distances the place demands. A hundred moths in the hollow volume and never two of them touching, each one a separate pale stroke against the brass, riding its own portion of the breath. They are the machine dreaming that it is delicate. They are proof that even an engine, given time and moisture and a roaring solitude, will eventually produce something that looks like tenderness and lives ninety seconds at a stretch.

## The Long-Legged Grazers

On the pipes, upside down, walk the things that have decided the machinery is a forest.

Call them the menders, because that is nearly what they do, though no one taught them to and they do not know they do it. They graze the algae and the moss — and grazing, they keep the condensation collars clear, keep the cold brass cold enough to weep, keep the water-cycle turning. They walk the copper mains and their many feet find the worn bright tracks, the grooves the vanished tenders left, and they follow those tracks because the tracks are smooth and easy walking. So the route of the dead caretaker is still walked, every day, by creatures who inherited the path without inheriting the purpose. They tend the machine by accident, on a road built by intention, and the machine breathes a little easier for it and does not know why.

They are patient past the point that patience means anything. They move on the schedule of the lungs — a few steps on the exhale when the warm air loosens their joints, a long stillness through the cold pause, clamped to the pipe, waiting. To watch one cross the central volume is to watch a clock with legs. They will be halfway across the world when the warm breath comes, and stalled at the same spot for the whole cold minute-and-a-half, and you will look away and look back and they will not have moved and then, between two of your own breaths, they will be somewhere else. They live so slowly that motion happens to them in your blind spots. They are the place's sense of time made into a body.

## The Metal Birds in the Dead Flues

And then the loud ones. The descendants of birds, thin and hard-feathered, roosting in the capped and useless flues — the parts of the machine that no longer breathe.

This is the cruelty and the genius of them: they have chosen to live in the *dead* organs. The flues that strangled and sealed long ago, the lungs-that-failed, are the only quiet pockets in all that roaring volume, and the birds have taken them as nests. They are the inhabitants of the machine's own scar tissue. They shelter in its losses.

And they shriek. They shriek in the machine's voice — they have no song of their own and never did; they hatched into a world that was already deafening, and so they learned to speak it. Their cries are stolen sounds: the squeal of an intake valve, the wet groan of leather, the arrhythmic thud of the governor far below. A metal bird does not sing to another metal bird. It sings *to the machine*, or as the machine, or perhaps it cannot tell the difference, because nothing in its short hard life has ever taught it where the engine ends and the self begins. They are the only things here that answer. The bellows roar and the birds roar back, and the conversation has no content, only volume, only the fact of *I am also loud, I am also here, I am also alive at the top of the air.*

They do not flock. The dead flues are scattered and the birds keep to their separate ruined chambers, each one shrieking alone into the roaring hollow, each one certain it is being heard and unable, in all that noise, to ever be sure.

## The Flowers, Who Keep the Schedule Most Faithfully of All

Do not forget the flowers among the living. They are the most obedient inhabitants. They open on the warm exhale — every one of them, all at once, a slow red-and-bruise blooming across all the ports as the gale comes through — and they close in the cold pause, sealing their wax against the thin killing air. They have no choice and they have no complaint. They are the schedule of the lungs made visible, the breath translated into petal.

They feed the moths. The moths, riding the breath, carry whatever pollen the flowers make from port to port, and so the flowers reproduce on the exhalation, are bred by the breathing, are children of the engine's rhythm exactly as much as the moths are. Nothing here lives outside the ninety seconds. The flowers are the clock's face; the grazers are its hands; the moths are the sweep of its second-marker; and the breath, far above, the wet groan and the gale, is the mechanism that turns them all.

## What It All Adds Up To

So here is who lives here, in the end:

A vanished tender, present only as wear and habit and a path still walked.

A drift of moths that are the engine's own moisture pretending to be alive, and succeeding, ninety seconds at a time.

A slow procession of grazers who maintain the world by accident, following a dead caretaker's road, keeping a time so patient it disappears into your blind spots.

A scatter of birds in the machine's dead organs, shrieking its own noise back at it, each one alone and certain and unconfirmable.

And the flowers, opening and closing, the most faithful of all, keeping the only law there is.

None of them remembers the builder. None of them knows the purpose. They have arranged themselves into the empty spaces and learned the single rule — *open on the warm breath, close in the cold pause* — and they obey it without knowing it is a rule, the way a lung does not know it is breathing.

They keep their distances. They fill the roaring hollow without populating it. They are watched and they are alone and both are true.

And the machine breathes them all, in and out, in and out, at the top of the air where breathing should not be possible — a chapel-sized engine that built a tiny world out of its own exhalation and was then forgotten by everything in it, including, perhaps, itself.

It is small. It is the whole world. It is nearly empty.

And it never, ever stops shouting that it is alive.
