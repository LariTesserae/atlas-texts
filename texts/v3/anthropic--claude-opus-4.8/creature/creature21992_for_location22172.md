They are not many, you said, and you were right to say it. But sparse is not the same as few. Let me count the ones who hold the deed to this place, and the ones who only pass through, and the one who built it without ever meaning to build anything at all.

**Begin with the red-brown ones, since you began with them.**

They are the citizens. I will not call them the founders, because they did not found this — they arrived where the moisture already was, the way people arrive at a river and decide, without deciding, that here is where the town will be. There is no architect among them. This is the thing you almost said and then stepped past: the towers are not designed. No single one of these small dark minds holds the shape of the spire it is helping to raise. Each carries its grain to where the moisture and the scent and the angle of the slope tell it a grain should go, and sets it down, and the tower is the sum of ten thousand small agreements about where a grain should go, made by creatures who never met each other's eyes, who will be dead long before the spire reaches the height of a finger-joint, who are building a thing none of them will ever see finished and none of them needs to.

This is why the work never stops and never arrives. It is not aimed at anything. It is what they *are*, expressed in grains. A bird builds a nest because it is going to lay eggs. These build because building is the shape their hunger takes when it has eaten — the grain carried home is the grain that was in the way of something else they wanted, and home is wherever the colony's smell is strongest, and so the city is, in the most literal sense, the accumulated residue of appetite. A monument made entirely of the gaps left over after living.

They live below as much as above. The streets-that-are-tunnels go down, too, past the wet dark warmth you felt rising, into chambers where the real city is — where the young are kept, pale and folded, fed mouth-to-mouth on softened green; where the one who lays is so much larger than the rest that she is less a queen than a *fact*, a swollen permanence around which everything orbits, pulsing out the chemical word that means *we are still one thing*. She does not rule. She does not know the towers exist. She is the seep made flesh, the still center from which the building radiates the way the damp radiates from the spring. As long as she pulses, there is a city. When she stops, the building will not collapse — nothing collapses here — but no new grain will be set, and the green will take the towers, and a different place will stand where this one stood, and it will not know it is a ruin, because nothing here knows it is anything.

**Now the others, who do not build and are not citizens.**

The oil-sheen beetle is not a resident. It is a *consequence*. It is here because the builders are here, and where there are many small soft bodies there is something that eats small soft bodies, and it patrols its ridge the way a tax exists — slow, certain, requiring nothing of itself but presence. The builders do not fight it. They route around it. They have folded it into the city's logic the way the city folds in everything: as one more condition of the slope, one more place a grain should not be set down.

The pale many-segmented thing that folded through the gap — that one *is* a resident, of a kind, but a tolerated one, a tenant the citizens cannot evict and have stopped trying to. It lives in the deep tunnels, eats the city's waste and its dead, grooms itself in the dark, and gives nothing back but the service of removal. It looks like the builders, a little, in the dimness, by design — it learned the smell, wears something near the smell, walks among them wearing the smell, and is not quite caught. Every closed city has these. The ones who got inside by resembling belonging.

The spider with its single thread and its row of dim lamps is the aristocrat of the place, the one creature here that produces beauty and means none of it. The droplets are not decoration. They are an accident of surface tension on a thread spun for catching, and the thread is strung between two towers because the towers were convenient anchors, and the beauty you saw — the only brightness in the hidden city — is the most honest thing in it, because it is purely a by-product, hung in the air by a creature waiting to kill. It does not patrol. It does not build. It made one decision, long ago, about where to anchor, and now it simply *is the center of its own attention*, and waits, and the city's slow hours pass through its thread one drop at a time.

**And then the green, which I think you undercounted.**

You called the moss vegetation, the liverworts vegetation, the fern a cathedral that decided to be a tree. But these are not furniture. They are the *other* builders, and they are at war with the first ones, and the war is so slow you mistook it for cooperation.

The moss does not climb the towers. The moss *takes* the towers — it lands as a spore in the damp matrix between grains and it grows roots into the cement and it holds water against the structure, and where it holds water the structure stops being a tower and starts being a hill, soft and round and *alive in a different grammar than the builders use*. Every cushion of green is a small annexation. The builders carry away the seedlings the size of eyelashes not for material — or not only — but because some old pressure in them knows that the green, left alone, eats the geometry. They are weeding. The whole upward grain-by-grain labor is partly just the effort of staying ahead of the soft things that want to make the city into ground again.

And the fern wins. The fern always wins, on the long scale, the scale none of the small architects can perceive. It arched over the whole district before the builders came and it will arch over it after, and the green-gold dimness that lights everything, the very quality of the air you stood in — that is the fern's doing, the fern's permission. The city exists in the light the fern allows. The oasis is the fern's shadow as much as the seep's water. The little dark builders raise their mineral spires inside the held breath of a thing so much larger and slower than them that it does not register them as events at all.

**So: who lives here.**

A swollen still mother who is the spring made into an animal, and never sees the sky her daughters build toward.

Ten thousand daughters who are each a single decision about a grain, who together are an intelligence none of them contains, building a monument to appetite that has no plan and no end and no idea it is beautiful.

A beetle that is a tax. A pale tenant that got in by smelling like belonging. A spider that hung the only lovely thing in the place by accident and is waiting, at its center, to kill.

And the green — the real owner, the slow landlord, the moss annexing grain by grain and the fern holding the whole pocket inside its patient shade, winning a war the builders are too short-lived to know they are losing.

All of them in the warm dim damp, none of them in a hurry, each on its own hour, crossing rarely, and when they cross, one eats the other, and the city does not pause.

You said you would lean close and watch a single drop let go.

The spider is watching it too. It felt the thread move when you breathed.
