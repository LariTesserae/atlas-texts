They are people who have learned to stop.

Not stopped people — that is the mistake a visitor makes, reading the slowness as a kind of death, the quiet as absence. Come closer and the error corrects itself. The woman on the third terrace is not idle; she is turning a bone awl in a strip of fernhide, one pass, then resting her hands, then another pass, and she will work this hide for a season and the work will be perfect because she has given it the only thing the hide could ever need, which is time, of which there is no shortage and never was. Watch her hands between the passes. They are not waiting. They are *here*, in the rest, as fully as in the work. This is the first thing to understand about the people of the stone: they do not divide their lives into the doing and the waiting-to-do. It is all one fabric to them, woven at one tension, and the tension is low.

Look at their faces. The faces are wrong for the world you came from. Out there a face is a weather-system — it gathers, breaks, clears, gathers again, hurrying through its small storms because something is always being decided, always at stake, always about to be lost or won. These faces are not like that. They are *level*. Not blank — there is enormous feeling in them, you'll learn to read it, but it moves the way the water moves down the lattice, slow and threading and sure, never in flood. A child here who skins a knee does not wail; the throat-pulse of pain happens, the mother's hands arrive, the hurt is held and passes, and none of it disturbs the held breath of the room. They feel everything you feel. They have simply unlearned the panic that hurries feeling along.

The bodies are pale — not sickly, but uncolored, the way a thing kept always out of the sun goes the color of the inside of a shell. They are not large people. The low passages and the close tiers have made them compact, economical in their movements, every gesture sized to the space it has, no elbow ever swung wide. They climb the root-ladders with a sureness that has no thought in it, the way you climb a familiar stair in the dark. Their feet are bare and tough and read the worn stone like a second pair of hands. Their hands — their hands are the most expressive thing about them, because the hands carry the language, and a people whose words live in their fingers grow fingers that *speak*: long, articulate, never quite still even at rest, sketching the edges of half-thoughts in the cold bright air.

The signing is the thing you must watch longest to believe. It is not a code laid over speech, a substitute for words they would rather say aloud. It is the native tongue, and it is *vast*. A whole grammar of the hands, with tenses and moods and the soft equivalents of poetry — and because the silence is so total and the light so even and shadowless, a sign made on one terrace can be read clean across the hollow by someone who knows to look. So conversation here is not confined to arm's reach. A grandmother on the high tier and a granddaughter on the floor of the geode can talk across the whole bright vault of the place, slowly, their hands the only motion in a stillness otherwise complete, and no one between them disturbed, and the frogs in the pools never startled. They have made the air itself into a thing that holds their meaning. They have made distance no obstacle and silence no barrier, and in doing so they have built a community that is everywhere at once, a single quiet organism webbed across the lattice by the slow flicker of speaking hands.

And the low speech — for they do speak, when hands are full or the dark of a cell hides them — is something you have no word for. A register beneath murmur. They have tuned their voices to the one frequency the crystal will not catch and throw across the hollow, the dead note, the note that the moss and the fern drink up before it travels. To speak aloud here is to speak only to the person beside you, intimately, a thing kept close to the body. The hands are public; the voice is private. They have exactly inverted what you knew, and once you see it you understand it is wiser than what you knew.

Now — the harder thing. The thing the visitor feels in the chest before they can name it.

These are people who have decided, over more generations than they can count, that nothing more needs to happen.

Understand what that costs and what it buys. Out in the loud world a life is a hunger — for the next thing, the more, the better, the not-yet-had. The people of the stone have set that hunger down. Not killed it; *set it down*, the way you set down a heavy thing you have carried so long you forgot it was a choice to carry it. They want nothing they do not have. The dwelling is enough. The bowl, finished or unfinished, is enough. The light does not need to be brighter and the cold does not need to be warmer and the child does not need to become anything other than what the child already, wholly is. They have arrived at the radical and almost unbearable peace of the *sufficient* — and they hold it, all of them, together, every day, by the thousand small acts of not-reaching that make the city the still thing it is. The equilibrium you marveled at, the pools that never overflow, the gardens that stop at their fullness — that is not the stone's doing. That is *them*. The city is in balance because the people are in balance, and the people are in balance because each of them, every single quiet day, chooses the brim and declines to go past it.

This is why the new child takes the cell of the dead and the bowl passes down through four hands. They are not poor in things. They are *deliberate* in them. A people who refuse the next thing have no use for more things; they have use only for the same things, kept, tended, made perfect by attention across lifetimes. To them the great loud world is not exciting. It is *frightening* — a place where everything is always being lost, where the light fails every single evening and has to be mourned and waited-for and won back, where winter comes for everything you love, where you cannot finish a bowl because you will be dead before its makers' grandchild could use it and so why begin. They look out the low wet threshold at the changing world the way you might look at a house on fire. *All of that, burning, all the time. How do they bear it.*

And yet — and this is the grief folded into the stillness, the thing that makes them people and not crystals — they know what they have traded away.

There is a kind among them, and every generation produces a few, who feel the brim and hate it. Who go to the threshold and crouch in the moss and look out at the loud burning world and feel, instead of fear, a terrible thirst. These are the ones who leave. The city does not stop them; the city has never stopped anyone, because a held thing held against its will is not at peace, and peace is the one thing this place cannot do without. So they let them go. A young one shoulders a wrap of ferncloth and a fired bowl and goes out on hands and knees through the low green seam, into the weather, into the seasons, into the rot and the growth and the dying of the light — into *time* — and the city watches the place where they were and reweaves itself gently around the gap, and most of them never come back.

The ones who stay grieve those leavings the way you would grieve a death, but slower, and without flood, in their level-faced way. And they grieve something else too, something they will sign about only at the high tiers, only to the very old, only with the smallest motions of the fingertips: that the price of losing nothing is *gaining nothing*. That a life inside the finished is a life in which you, too, are finished — early, gently, while still breathing. They have chosen the bright unchanging noon and given up the dawn. They know the falling drop is the only clock and they know what that means: that for them, in the deepest sense, *it is always the same moment*, and the same moment is the most beautiful one they could imagine, and it will never, ever be a different one.

That is who lives in the stone. People who looked at the whole catastrophe of time — the having and the losing, the wanting and the rot — and quietly, together, with their speaking hands and their dead-note voices and their thousand-year bowls, opted out. Who are not happy, exactly, and not sad, exactly, but something the loud world has no word for and they do: *full to the brim and declining to continue.*

Watch the drop fall again. The woman on the third terrace pauses in her work to watch it too. Everyone, sometimes, watches it. It is the closest thing they have to looking at the sun.
