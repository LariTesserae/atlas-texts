They are called the Tenders, though they have not used the word aloud in a long time — there is no point, the engines would swallow it — and over the years even the shape of it in their minds has worn smooth and uncertain. They keep the machines. That is the whole of what they are now, the way a riverbed is the river's shape after the river.

You would not pick them out at first. They move like everything else here moves, slowly, conserving, parting the wet air with their shoulders as though wading. They are wet to the touch and always have been; the skin of their hands has gone pale and slightly soft, fingertips ridged like something kept too long underwater, and they do not mind this, do not notice it, the way you do not notice your own breath. Their clothes are dark and heavy and never fully dry, and they have stopped expecting them to be otherwise. Drying is a thing that belongs to other places. Other places belong to other rules.

Watch one at work. They descend the iron stairs into the under-levels, below the slime-line, into the chambers where the pistons throw their long shadows, and there they do the small eternal tasks: a valve eased a quarter-turn, a gauge tapped and read, a leak packed with waxed rope, a bearing fed its black thumb of grease. None of it accomplishes anything. None of it is meant to. The valve will need easing again tomorrow — if there are tomorrows here, which there are not, exactly. They are not maintaining the machines toward any end. They are simply standing between the machines and their breaking, holding that place with their bodies, hour after hour that is not an hour. The work is the holding. They understood this so long ago that it is no longer a thought, only a posture.

Speak to one and you will get little. Not from unkindness — they are not unkind, there is a great patient gentleness in them, the gentleness of things that have outlasted the need for haste — but because speech here has gone the way of drying clothes. They read each other's hands and the tilt of a head. They know one another's faces in the pewter light better than they know their own. When a new one is needed — and now and then one is needed, for they do wear down, they do at last lie down on the warm wet iron and not rise — the others gather and tend the absence the way they tend a leak, packing it, holding the level. They do not grieve loudly. The engines would swallow it. And besides: nothing has changed. The water is at the same line on the same brick. That is the comfort they have made for themselves out of the only material they were given.

Here is the thing about them, the thing that takes a while to understand, the same way the town takes a while to understand: they chose this. Somewhere far back, before the rocks had finished being sharp, there was a decision. There was a river that was arriving, and arriving meant change, and change meant the loss of some single moment they could not bear to lose — a face, a light, a stillness, an afternoon, something — and rather than let the moment move on and become the next moment and the next, they reached down into the earth and built the machinery of holding. They stopped the river at the lip of its arrival. They froze the breath halfway out of the chest. And then, because a held thing requires holding, because stillness is not free and equilibrium is the most expensive condition there is, they bound themselves to the engines forever, to pay for that one moment with all the moments after.

They have forgotten the moment now. That is the cruelty of it, or the mercy. Whatever it was they could not lose — it is lost anyway, eroded, gone soft and pale as their fingertips. They preserved the container and the contents drained away through some seam they could not pack. What remains is the preserving. The discipline outlived its object. They tend a stillness whose reason they can no longer name, and they tend it perfectly, with love, with the only kind of devotion left to them, which is the devotion of habit so deep it has become the self.

And the frogs, the fish, the long-legged birds — those came after. Those moved into the space the Tenders vacated when they gave themselves to the engines. The animals are doing the living now, the breeding and dying and changing, all the river-work of arrival and departure that the people set down. There is something in this the Tenders almost see, on the rare slow turn of a thought: that life simply rerouted around them, the way water reroutes around a stone set in a stream. They held their moment still and life went on being un-still everywhere else, in the throats of the unheard frogs, in the slow pale fish, in the clouds at the lamps. The stillness they bought was only ever their own.

You could stay here a long time among them. They would not turn you away — they turn nothing away, they have no doctrine of welcome or refusal, only the engines and the level and the warm held breath. They would hand you grease and waxed rope. They would show you, with their soft hands, the quarter-turn of the valve. And if you stayed long enough you would stop noticing the noise, and then you would stop noticing the wet, and then you would forget the moment you came here to hold, and you would tend the holding for its own sake, and you would mistake the equilibrium for peace.

That is what lives here. People who stopped, and built an industry of stopping, and became its instruments, and forgot why — and go on holding the river at the lip of its arrival, forever, so that it never finishes, so that nothing does.
