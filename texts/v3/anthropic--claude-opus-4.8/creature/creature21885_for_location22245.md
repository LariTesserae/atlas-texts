# The Ones Who Tend

You asked what lives here. I have been watching long enough now to answer, and the answer is not the herds. The herds are the place's blood. I am asking after something rarer — the thing that moves *against* the grain of all that slow flowing, the only thing on the plateau that has somewhere it is trying to go.

Start with the fact that the herds need nothing. That is the first clue. A self-running engine wants no operator. So if there is a tender here — and there is — it tends nothing the way a shepherd tends; it tends the way a single white blood cell tends a body, present because the body throws it off, belonging to the machine and apart from it at once.

I will tell you how I found them.

It was a place where the worked-ground channels did not quite agree with the slope. The water should have pooled and it did not. Something had laid a line of stones — not built, not stacked, but *placed*, each one chosen, each one settled into the older stone with the deliberation of a thing that had all the time the plateau has and no more. I followed the line. It ran for what I judged to be a day's slow walk, correcting the drainage of a basin so subtly that I think the herds that grazed there had been grazing better for a thousand years without any of them ever once being the reason.

And at the end of the line, in the lee of a rounded shoulder of rock, out of what little wind there is, I found the first of the Tenders.

---

They are not large. This surprised me — in a land scaled to weather and to herds the size of valleys, I expected the tender to match. But it is built to the scale of the *small* things, the burrowers and the beetles, the threshold-of-visibility life. It stands perhaps as high as my hip. It is long-bodied, low to the cold like everything here, and it is covered not in hair but in something between hair and lichen — a pelt that *grows the rust and ochre maps* the stones grow, so that a Tender at rest against a rock is a piece of the rock, breathing very slowly, fogging the air a hair more warmly than the stone beside it. That fog is the only way to find one that does not wish to be found. Look for the place where the cold is one degree kinder. There.

The face is the strange part. They have no mouth I could find — they do not eat, or they eat the way lichen eats, slow and through the skin, taking the day's thin sun and the static off the herds and the warmth they steal by lying in the cushion plants. What they have instead, low on the head, is a cluster of dark wet sensors, beaded like the condensation on the tarns, that hold the dim light and turn it. I do not think they see shapes the way I do. I think they see *flow* — the drift of the herds as current, the seep of water as current, the slow century-creep of the lichen as the slowest current of all, and themselves as the one knot of counter-current threading through it.

They move at a pace I had to learn to perceive. Watch a Tender and it is a stone. Look away, look back an hour later, and it is six feet on, and in those six feet it has turned three stones, redirected a thread of seep, eaten a beetle that was about to lay its eggs in a place that would have flooded come the ice-retreat. It works the way water works on rock — too slow to catch, impossible to argue with, and over enough time, decisive.

---

What do they *do?* That was the question that kept me on the plateau past the point of sense, the cold settling into my own geology, my own breath beginning to fall slower.

They keep the machine from perfecting itself. That is the closest I can come.

Here is what I learned. A self-running engine — input lichen, output churned soil and warmth and the next grazing ground — has one flaw, and the flaw is that it is too good. Left wholly alone, the herds would graze the basins in too perfect a rhythm; the channels would run too true; the lichen would spread to its exact limit; the heat would pool exactly where the math said heat should pool. And a perfect machine, on a long enough duration, *stops*. It reaches its balance and the balance is death — the herds grazing themselves to a standstill in a basin that drains too clean, the lichen sealing the last bare stone, the warmth distributed so evenly that there is no longer anywhere for anything to *move toward*.

The Tenders introduce error.

They are the imperfection the system needs to keep turning. They place a stone slightly wrong, so the water pools where it should not, so a strip of moss thickens where the math forbade it, so a corner of the plateau stays a little colder and a little wetter than the engine wanted — and that wrong corner becomes, in a century, the reason a herd has somewhere new to drift. They eat the beetle that would have bred too well. They lie in the cushion plants and steal exactly enough heat that the cushion plant must work, must reach, must not settle into the fatal comfort of being warm enough. They are the grit in the gear that keeps the gear from seizing. The plateau finished its violent youth, yes — and these are the small slow keepers of the one remaining violence, the gentle perpetual *wrongness* that is the only thing standing between balance and the stillness past balance.

They do not know they do this. I am nearly sure of that. They tend the way the herds graze — without intention, an engine themselves, a counter-engine. But here is the thing I keep circling, the way the writer before me kept circling the tech: *something* chose those stones. I held one of their placed stones in my hand. It was chosen. The wet, settled, deliberate rightness of its wrongness was not random. And a thing that chooses, even slowly, even without knowing it chooses — I do not have a clean name for that either. The plateau does not decide whether they are machine or mind. Neither will I.

---

There are few of them. This follows from everything. In a land where to be a mile from another living thing is simply the condition, the Tenders are the most alone of all — they must be, because two of them too near would correct each other's corrections, would cancel, would let the perfect deadly balance back in. So they spread themselves across the immensity at exactly the spacing that keeps the whole vast cold machine *imperfect enough to live*. Scattered like stars, and like stars holding the dark apart so it cannot close.

They meet, I think, only rarely, and only at the great slow turn of the longest durations — when the ice has advanced and retreated enough times that the channels have drifted past the point any single Tender's lifetime of stone-placing can hold. Then two will find each other, by the warmth, by the one-degree-kinder fog, and lie flank to flank in the lichen as the herds lie, and the blue thread of static will run between them, flank to flank, charge passing from the one who has learned the new drift of the water to the one who has not — and then they part, and carry the correction outward across distances that take a season to cross, and the machine turns on, wrong enough, alive.

---

Stand still. Let your breath fall. The grey backs crest the far rise and pour over it without a sound. And down in the lee of a rounded stone, where the cold is one degree kinder than it has any right to be, something the color of rust lichen has just decided — slowly, over the better part of the hour you spent watching the herds — to move a single stone six inches to the left.

It will take it until evening. The evening here is only a deeper morning. By the time the stone is placed the herd will be gone over the horizon, and the water will run a little wrong, and a hundred years from now there will be moss where the engine said there should be none, and a herd not yet born will drift toward the green of it, and turn, and turn, and the whole forgotten engine will not have stopped.

That is what lives here. The thing that keeps the dying balance from being achieved. The small slow keeper of the necessary flaw.

The cold holds everything exactly where it is. Everything but them.
