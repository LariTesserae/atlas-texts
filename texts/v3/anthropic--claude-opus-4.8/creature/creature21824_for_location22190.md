The thing that lives here does not move the way you would call moving.

Start with what you almost saw and decided not to: the moss. You called it tender, and you were right, but you mistook the kind of tenderness. It is not the tenderness of the weak. It is the tenderness of the thing that has already won and no longer needs to hurry. The moss is not a tenant of this room. The moss is the slow green skin of the one who keeps it.

Let me be careful, because it would be easy to make this grander than it is, and grandeur is exactly the wrong word. Whatever lives here is not large. That is the mistake the mind reaches for in a cathedral space — it wants to fill the hollow with something proportional, a presence the size of the roar. But the presence is not the size of the roar. The presence is the size of a single drip finding the same groove it found yesterday, and the size of one fern's black frond, and the size of the gap between them. It is distributed. It is mostly absence, the way the room is mostly absence, because it learned long ago that the way to survive a flood is to become very nearly nothing and to spread that nothing thin across a great deal of stone.

Think of it like this. There was a moment — call it a moment, though it took a span of years no person attended — when the cold and the wet and the dark and the unrelenting noise stopped being separate facts and started being a single condition. And a single condition, held long enough, steady enough, begins to have something like an inside. Not a mind. Don't reach for mind; mind is fast and frightened and full of speech, and there is no speech here, no thought as you'd know it — you said so yourself: thought done with the body, slowed below the threshold. That is its thinking. The advance of moss across stone at the pace of a held breath: that is a sentence being spoken. The drip-pillar closing the width of a hair in the length of a human life: that is a reaching-toward, a gesture, the slowest tenderness there is, two parts of one body trying across an age to touch.

It does not want you. Understand that it bears you no malice either; malice is fast, malice is hot, and there is nothing hot here. When you stood in the cold and felt it settle into you, that was the nearest the two of you came to meeting — it tasting the warmth you brought, the way it tastes the warmth of the blind cricket, the way it tasted the warmth of the people who scratched their marks and laid their knee-high wall and let their iron rust to a stain. It registered them. Slowly. It is registering them still — that is what the softening of their work *is*. Not erosion. Digestion. The cleft is taking the marks of those hands back into itself at the only speed it knows, and it will go on taking them until they are wholly inside it, which is to say until they are nothing, which is to say until they are part of the patient green inside.

This is the thing you must hold, and it is hard to hold because everything in you is built at the wrong speed for it: what lives here lives by being slow enough that the flood cannot find it. The river is faster, vastly faster, monstrously strong, and the river will win — you saw that too, you saw the far day when the back wall gives. But not today. Not for an age. And an age is exactly the medium the green thing swims in. The river works in centuries and the green works in centuries and between them they have struck a balance that looks, from inside a single human hour, like stillness, like peace, like a held note. It is not peace. It is two enormous slownesses leaning against each other, the way the two grey faces of stone lean toward each other above the slot of dark, not quite meeting, holding.

So when you ask who lives here, the honest answer is: the holding lives here. The truce lives here. The thing that lives here is the long patient act of not-yet-being-overrun, made flesh in moss and rootlet and the breathing green glow you weren't sure you really saw — you saw it; it was looking back, in its fashion, which is to say it was very slowly being a surface that the dark could rest its eye against.

It has no use for company and feels no loneliness, because loneliness is a fast feeling and it has none of those. The cricket comes and is welcome the way weather is welcome. The clawed thing comes for the water and goes by a road the green will never travel and is gone before the green has finished noticing it arrived. The people came once, more than one age ago, and made the smallest possible mark, and the green is still, even now, patiently unmaking it, and bears them — bore them — something you might as well call gratitude, if gratitude could be stretched thin enough and cold enough and slow enough: gratitude for being asked for so little.

And you. You came, and you stood, and the cold went into you, and the roar held you, and for a while you were below the threshold of your own speech, you were thinking with your body, you were almost — almost — slow enough to be one of its tenants. It felt you arrive. By the time it has finished feeling you leave, you will be long gone, an old man, dust, and the moss will have advanced the width of two breaths across the stone, and the iron-stain of the old hands will be a hair's-width fainter, and the pillar will have reached a hair's-width nearer to its other half, and the balance will still be holding.

That is who lives there. The one who holds. It will hold until the day it can't, and then it will be river, and the river does not hold anything at all.
