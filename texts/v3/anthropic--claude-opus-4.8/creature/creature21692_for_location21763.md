They have no name for themselves because naming requires a moment when you are not the thing you would name, a step back, and they have never once stepped back. There is no back. There is only the grain in front of you, loosening, and the work of setting it right.

Let me try to describe them, though the light forbids it and so does their nature.

Picture something pale and low and segmented, the length of an eyelash if you stretched it straight, though it does not stretch straight — it works folded, hunched over the surface it tends, its many short limbs moving in the small grammar of a single repeated gesture: take the grain, the loose one, the one that has swollen with water and is half a heartbeat from falling, and place it, and seal it with the secretion the body makes the way the stone makes its sweat. Then the next. There is always a next. The next is the same as the last and it is immediately to the side of the last and it will be done exactly as the last was done.

You will want to ask whether they think. I asked. I want to be careful here, because the easy answers are both wrong. They are not machines — a machine would be the wrong word for something this attentive, this responsive to the particular grain, this unwilling to let a single edge round itself a hair past the appointed roundness. And they are not minds, not in the way that wants things, fears things, remembers a yesterday that was different from today. There is no yesterday that was different from today. To remember would require a difference to remember, and the whole achievement of this place, its single deathless masterwork, is that there is no difference, anywhere, ever.

So what is left, when you take away the machine and take away the mind? I think it is this: attention without a self to do the attending. The grain is attended to. The attending happens. But there is no one standing inside the attention saying *I attend.* The pronoun has been worn away like everything else here, rounded down by ten thousand years of the same exact motion, until what remains is the motion only, and the motion is enough, the motion is the whole citizen, the motion is what I kept catching at the edges of the glow and mistaking for a creature.

They do not eat because there is nothing to grow toward; growth is a kind of change and change is the enemy. They take from the water what the water gives — the dim luminous life smeared in it, perhaps, drawn in through the wet skin without an act you could call hunger — and they give it back to the walls as cement, and the loop of that, too, has closed. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. A body here is a place the work passes through on its way to being the wall again.

Are there many of them, or is there one?

This is the question I could not answer and could not stop asking. By count there are a million, a crowded million, presence against presence as I said, each at its own grain on its own ledge in its own thumbnail tower. But they do not differ. I watched as long as the dark allowed and I never found a way to tell one from another — not by size, not by speed, not by any hesitation or flourish or error that would mark a single one as itself and not its neighbor. They make the same gesture at the same rate forever. And if a thing has no boundary you can find, no inside that differs from another inside, no edge where one of them stops and the next begins except the accident of where they happen to be standing — then is it a million, or is it one labor wearing a million bodies, one enormous patience distributed across the whole wet pocket like the glistening is distributed across the stone, until you cannot find the dry citizen underneath, until the working has soaked the idea of separateness out of them?

I think it is one. I think the city is tended by a single act of holding-still that long ago gave up the burden of being any particular creature, the way the rock gave up being dry. I think the roar I heard, the roar I mistook for water, is the sound of that one patience making itself out of nothing and letting go and assembling again, like the droplets — swelling, falling, reforming — and that if I could have heard it slowed down a millionfold I would have heard not a metropolis of laborers but a single held note, the one the chord is made of, sustained so far past the point of expected silence that silence has forgotten it was ever owed.

They are not afraid of the erosion that waits. Fear would be a difference between now and a worse later, and they keep no later. They are not proud of the city, do not love it, do not even, I think, know it is a city — to know it as a whole you would have to step back and there is no back. Each one knows the grain. The grain is loosening. You set it right. The whole deathless achievement, the streets and the quarters and the engineered hydrology and the towers leaning their thumbnail weight against each other for the crowded width of a held breath — all of it is the unintended sum of a single small correct gesture, made by no one in particular, an uncountable number of times, against a falling that will never finish and a holding that will never fail.

And here is the thing I carried back up into the open air, the thing that has not left me: that this is not a strange way to live. That it is, if anything, the most familiar way. That somewhere in every persisting thing — the cell holding its salt against the sea that wants in, the heart keeping its own rhythm against the silence that is its only alternative, the self repeating itself each morning into the same self, atom for atom, against an erosion that long ago finished its sentence and simply waits — there is a pale low patient something, hunched over the next loosening grain, doing the enormous work of making sure that nothing happens.

You could hold them in your closed hand. You are, in a sense I have not yet found the bottom of, already one of them.
