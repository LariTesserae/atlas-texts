# The Knitters

You will not meet them by going to look for them. That much I can promise you, because that is how everyone fails. You ride the lift and you watch for the people of Hesp — the engineers in their grease-cuffed sleeves, the cistern-keepers, the children who run the catwalks barefoot because they trust the iron the way you trust your own legs — and you think, *these are the inhabitants.* And you are not wrong. But you are looking at the surface of a thing that lives all the way down.

Let me try to say it properly.

## What the Engineers Are

The engineers think they tend Hesp. This is the necessary mistake, and it is a kind, useful, productive mistake, and they should keep making it.

They are a long-boned, patient people, the engineers, shaped by altitude and by inheritance both. Their hands are extraordinary — wide-palmed, callus-mapped, capable of reading a column's health through the skin the way a physician reads a pulse. They tag the load-bearing vines with little brass plates. They sound the girders with hammers and listen to the note that comes back. They keep ledgers that are older than any nation you have heard of, ledgers in which a single entry might be *Tier 41, west column, vine now trusted, do not cut,* written by a hand four generations dead and obeyed without question by a hand alive today.

They marry late and apprentice early. They speak slowly, in a dialect thick with the names of things that hold weight. Their religion, insofar as they have one, is the *slow rule* — the conviction that anything done quickly is done wrong, that the right answer to almost any question is *wait and measure.* They are not unhappy. They are the least anxious people you will ever meet, because they have outsourced their anxiety to the ledgers and the numbers and the long, long memory of their craft.

And they touch the columns for luck. They will tell you it means nothing. Watch their hands. The hands know something the mouths won't say.

## What the Climbing Creatures Are

Now. The lithe long-fingered things that move along the vines. The pollinators, the pruners, the ones with whom the pact was struck.

The engineers think of them as fauna. Useful, tolerated, not chased off. The engineers are wrong about this too, and this mistake is less kind, though no one means harm by it.

The climbers are not pets and they are not vermin. They are *the second tenders.* Where the engineers tend the iron — the made thing, the dead-but-trusted scaffold — the climbers tend the green, and they have been doing it longer, and they remember it differently. They do not keep ledgers because they do not need to; the memory is in the doing. A climber knows which vine is ready to bear weight not by sounding it with a hammer but by the way it gives under a foot. They prune what should not grow and feed what should. They carry pollen and seed and small intentions from tier to tier, and the shape of the gardens — which the engineers believe is *weathering into balance,* a happy accident of rust and root — is in fact authored, slowly, by hands that have never held a pen.

They are not smarter than the engineers. That is the wrong frame entirely. They are *differently attentive.* The engineers attend to weight. The climbers attend to growth. Between the two attentions, held in tension like a vine wound round a column, the city is administered — and neither party fully understands that this is what is happening, and the not-understanding is itself part of the structure, load-bearing, do not cut.

## What the Birds Are

The wheeling colonial things in the air-gaps are the messengers, though they do not know it and carry no messages.

What they carry is *information about the whole.* When a tier sickens — when a cistern fouls, when a reed-bed filter chokes, when a vine begins to rot at a join the engineers cannot see and the climbers have not yet reached — the birds know first, because the insects shift, because the warm updraft changes its smell, because the small idle fish in the pools go still. And the birds move differently. They abandon a girder-hollow weeks before anyone can say why. They flock thicker over a healthy tier and thin over a failing one.

The engineers have learned, without ever quite admitting they have learned it, to read the birds. *The west gap's gone quiet,* an old keeper will say, and a younger one will go and look, and find the rot at the join, and the ledger will gain an entry, and the slow rule will be served. The birds are the city's nervous system, firing across the gaps, and the people are the part of the nervous system that has language, and so the people take the credit, and the birds take the updrafts, and everyone is satisfied.

## What the Green Is

And now we come to it, the thing under the thing, the inhabitant that the others are only the limbs of.

You have been told that Hesp should fall. That the spire is too tall for its base, that the numbers should not work and somehow always do, that there is a quiet belief — never codified, present only in the touching of columns — that the green now holds the iron.

That belief is correct, and it is not a metaphor, and it is not magic, and that is the strange and patient horror at the heart of all this loveliness.

The green of Hesp is not many plants. It is one thing, or it is becoming one thing, or it has been one thing for so long that the distinction no longer means anything. The vines are continuous. They have been continuous for longer than the ledgers run. Root has met root in the dark of the cisterns and the cracks of the deck-corners and the wet warm dark behind the foundry-walls, and what met did not stay separate. The hanging gardens four stories deep, the moss upholstering the iron, the trees in the feed-troughs, the reed-beds scrubbing the smoke — these are not a community of organisms enjoying a vertical paradise. They are the *body* of the inhabitant, and the paradise is the inhabitant's own slow shaping of the place into a thing it can comfortably be.

It has no hurry, because it has no fear, because nothing in two miles of stacked ecology threatens it that it cannot outlast. It thinks — if think is even the word, and it is not, but I have no better one — across seasons, across generations, in the only tense it knows, which is the long present-continuous of *growing toward.* And what it has been growing toward, patiently, for longer than anyone can measure, is *stability.* Not its own. Not selfishly. It is not a parasite and it is not a predator. It is something rarer and stranger: a thing that discovered, a long time ago, that it could live most easily inside a structure that did not fall — and that has been *making sure the structure does not fall,* knitting itself rib by rib into the iron, taking up loads the engineers' calculations cannot account for, threading its impossible strength through the impossible spire, holding the whole made world up.

The engineers measure the spire and the numbers are always fine. Of course they are fine. The green is making them fine. The green is the missing term in every equation, the silent correction, the reason a structure that should have toppled tiers ago stands instead in clean white summit-sun with birds wheeling in its gaps.

## The Truce

So here is the full inhabitant of Hesp, and you must hold all of it at once or you will not have understood:

There is the iron, dead and trusted, the swallowed mountain and the bolted tiers, the scaffold of the made world.

There are the engineers, who tend the iron and keep the memory and touch the columns for luck.

There are the climbers, who tend the green and keep no memory because they are the memory, differently attentive, the second hands of the place.

There are the birds, the nervous system, firing across the air-gaps, read by the people who do not know they are reading.

And there is the green — the one slow vast deciding thing — which tends *everything,* which has knit itself into the iron until the two cannot be separated, which holds Hesp up not as a service and not as a conquest but as the simple ongoing fact of its own preferred shape.

The slow rules are not the engineers' invention. They are the engineers *catching the rhythm of the green* — learning, across generations, to move at the pace of the thing they live inside, to make no decision faster than a vine claims a column, to assume their grandchildren will live with the result because the green assumes its grandchildren will too, and the green is always right about this.

It is not a haunted place. I want that understood, the way I wanted the absence of ground understood at the start. Nothing here means anyone harm. The green does not resent the engineers or the climbers or you, rattling up in your lift-cage with the leaves pouring past the bars. It does not resent, full stop. It simply *holds,* and intends to go on holding, slowly, for as long as it possibly can — and everything that lives in Hesp, with hands or with wings or with long clever fingers, lives inside that holding, fed by it, kept by it, standing on the green's vast unhurried decision the way you would stand on bedrock, if there were any bedrock, which there is not, and never was, and never needed to be.
