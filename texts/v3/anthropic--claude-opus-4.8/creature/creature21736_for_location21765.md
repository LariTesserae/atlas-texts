**The Tenders**

You said you never see a hand at it. That is correct. You will not. But the maintenance is real, and the maintainers are home, and they have been home this whole time, brushing your shoulders.

Start where you started — the fence that gave up being a fence. Reach in for the cool metal and you will find moss, and under the moss something that is *more* than moss: a sheath of growth laid down in a pattern. Not the wandering pattern of a thing that grew where it could, but a deliberate banding, thick where the bar takes weight, thin where it does not, with the conductive iron left bare in a hair-line track that runs the bar's whole length. The plant is reading the metal. It has wrapped the fence the way a hand wraps a tool it intends to keep using.

This is the body of the thing that lives here. It does not have a body the way you have a body, gathered into one place and carried around. It *is* the ward. The whole green dense weeping mass of it — the trellised pipes, the ivy that remembers the walls, the curtains of liverwort, the orange bacterial glaze, the deep patient mud — is one creature, or one settlement of creatures so interwoven that the distinction has stopped meaning much. The pumps are its heart only in the cheap metaphorical sense. The truer thing is that the pumps are its *throat*, and what it does, ceaselessly, all day, is *swallow*.

It eats the building. It has always eaten the building. Masonry into ivy, beam into soil, rivet into rust into the orange slick into the tissue of something that will hold a leaf up to the even green light. The whole place is a long slow meal taken so gently that the meal never notices it is being eaten — the walls leaning at the same angle for a generation, dissolving a grain at a time. That is the stillness you felt. That is what a thing looks like when it is feeding too slowly for the eye, but feeding always, without pause, without appetite in any urgent sense, simply because feeding is the shape its life takes.

And the machines. Here is the part that took the Tenders longest, and is their masterwork.

They did not build the pumps. People built the pumps, the industrial people, the ones the doorways were built for, and those people are gone and have been gone long enough that the green caught their leaving the way it caught the falling walls and held it at an angle. But the Tenders learned the pumps. Not as engineering — they cannot draft, they have no concept of a flywheel — but as *physiology*. A root finds the warm pipe and thickens around it because the warmth is good and the moisture is good; and over the slow patient decades the root that embraces the pipe becomes the root that *braces* the pipe, that holds the joint from working loose, that packs the leaking seal with its own swelling tissue. The liverwort curtains are not decoration. They are gaskets. The orange bacteria are not corrosion left to run — they are *cultivated*, grazed back from the moving parts and encouraged on the still ones, a living patina that seals iron against the very wet air that would otherwise eat it. The plant keeps the machine alive because the machine lifts the water, and the water is the whole condition of its being. It has made the pumps an organ. It maintains them the way you maintain your own pulse: without a hand, without a thought you'd recognize as a thought, by *being the kind of thing that does not stop doing this.*

So when you ask who keeps the pumps running — the answer is the same as the answer to who grew the ivy, and it is the same as the answer to who you were walking through. There is no separate caretaker. The caretaking *is* the inhabitant. The hiss and the thud and the clatter and the million unresolved drips are the sound of a single enormous slow green animal keeping its own water moving through its own warm dark interior, and it has been doing this without rest for longer than anyone remembers, and it is not in any hurry, because it has solved the only problem it has ever had, which is how to keep being wet and warm and fed, and the solution is simply: continue.

That is why nothing else calls. You noticed the silence of creatures — the single snail, the maybe-frog, the few flies hung in their shaft of light. You took the absence for emptiness. It is the opposite. There is no room. A frog needs a frog's worth of the world unclaimed, a margin of disorder it can hunt and breed and die in. There is no margin here. Every wet surface is already someone's surface, already glazed and grazed and accounted for, already part of the long meal and the long maintenance. The snail is tolerated because the snail is slow enough to be, itself, almost weather. The flies are too brief to matter. Everything fast, everything hungry, everything that would *take* rather than slowly *become* — has been quietly, patiently, over generations, edged out to the margins, out past the fence that is not a fence, out where the pump-thud finally loses itself in ordinary distance.

It noticed you, of course. Not as a mind notices — it has no face turned toward you, no moment of recognition. But you walked through its throat and brushed its tissue with both shoulders for twenty minutes, warm and quick and dripping, exhaling carbon it could use, shedding skin and salt into its mud, and it read you the way it reads the iron: a passing condition, a small warmth, a thing that came and will go and leave a little of itself behind to be incorporated, eventually, slowly, into the even green and the orange glaze and the deep black patient mud.

That is why your clothes stay damp for hours. You think you left. You did, mostly.

But it kept a little of the wet on you on purpose — or not on purpose, never on purpose, simply because keeping things wet is what it *is* — and for those few hours, walking dry streets under uncomplicated sky, you are carrying a thin film of the ward out into the world, and the ward, in its slow way, is reaching a little past its own fence, the way it always has, the way it always will, one finger's width at a time.
