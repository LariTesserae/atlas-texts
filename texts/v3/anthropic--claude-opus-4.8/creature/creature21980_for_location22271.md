## The Tenants

You asked who lives here, and the honest first answer is the one the place keeps insisting on: no one. But that's a reading taken with the wrong instrument. *Fauna near zero* is true. *Lifeless* is a lie the silence tells. Look again, slower, on the timescale the place actually keeps, and the residents resolve — not because they hide, but because they live at a speed the eye refuses to call living.

Begin with what owns the most surface.

**The Sheeting.** This is what the mosses and liverworts have become, and to call them *the moss* the way you'd call a forest *the trees* is to miss that they are not many small things but one large slow thing, a single continuous tenant draped over every horizontal iron in the basin. It does not have a center. It has a frontier — the advancing edge, finger-width a season, where the green meets the bare-but-rusting metal and decides, cell by cell, whether the metal is hospitable yet. The Sheeting reads warmth and reads damp the way you'd read a room for comfort, and it grows *toward* good conditions and *away* from the few that aren't, which are the down-throat mouths of the pumps where moving water would tear it loose. It has learned, in the only way a thing without a brain can learn — by dying everywhere it shouldn't and thriving everywhere it should until the shape of its body *is* a map of the safe places. The Sheeting is the oldest continuous inhabitant. It remembers the layout of the works better than any drawing, because it *is* a drawing, a living survey rendered in the single statement *here it is good to be*.

**The Horsetails** are different, and they know they're different. They are the only residents with anything like ambition. Where the Sheeting spreads, the horsetails *colonize* — they pick a channel and fill it, stem by jointed stem, until the water has to comb itself through them, and the combing slows the water, and the slowing drops the silt, and the silt becomes soil, and the soil lets more horsetails stand. They are terraformers on a clock so slow it looks like stillness. And there is something in their bearing — those segmented stalks that have not bothered to invent a flower in three hundred million years — of a tenant who was here before the building and fully expects to outlast it. They do not crowd the iron. They do not need it. They are using the works the way water uses a streambed: as a temporary convenience, a thing to flow through on the way to being, eventually, just marsh again.

**The Ferns** are opportunists, and the most nearly *animal* in their habits, which is to say the most local and the most opinionated. Each one is an individual in a way the Sheeting is not. A fern lives in a *joint* — a rivet-seam, a bracket-crook, the lip where a smaller pipe was clamped to the great main a hundred years ago and rust has since packed the gap with the closest thing this place has to dirt. From that one address the fern unrolls toward whichever lamp is nearest, and you can read a fern's whole biography in the direction of its fronds: this one leans left because the arc-lamp on that catwalk has been lit and faithful for longer than the fern has been alive; this one is confused, half-turned, because two lamps contend for it and it has spent its short life unable to choose. The ferns are the closest the works comes to a population — many small lives, each in its own crevice, each reaching, each subtly disagreeing with its neighbors about where the light is best.

Now the harder tenants. The ones you have to stop expecting people in order to see.

**The Pumps.** I do not think they are alive in any way that would satisfy the instruments. But living is the wrong question. Ask instead: *what behavior do they exhibit, and does it sustain itself?* They lift water that returns to be lifted. Pointless — unless you stop assuming the point was ever the water. The pulse keeps the warmth circulating. The warmth feeds the green. The motion keeps the down-throats clear of Sheeting so the pumps don't choke. The pumps maintain exactly the conditions the pumps require, and the conditions the green requires, and the two requirements are the same requirement, and have been for longer than anyone left to wonder about it. That is not a machine running on after its makers. That is a metabolism. The pumps are the heart and the green is the body and neither could say which one keeps the other, because the answer is *both, mutually, in a loop, forever.* The works is not a haunted factory. It is a single organism that happens to be made partly of iron and partly of moss, and the thudding you hear in your chest is not eerie residue. It is the animal breathing. You are standing inside the tenant.

**The Lamps** are its eyes turned inward — or its sun turned downward; the metaphor strains because the thing it describes has no outside. The lamps were lit by people once, for people, and then the people thinned out the way a fever breaks, and the lamps did not go out, because by then the green had grown to depend on them and the pumps had grown to power them and the whole system had quietly closed the circuit around the empty space where the purpose used to be. The light is no longer *for* seeing. Nothing here sees. The light is *food*, and the lamps are the organ that makes it, and they stay lit for the same reason your own cells keep their small fires lit: because stopping is not a thing a living process does on purpose. It is only ever done *to* it.

And the rust — I almost left out the rust, and that would have been the worst omission, because the rust may be the truest resident of all. It is the works *digesting itself*, infinitely slowly, never finishing. Conservation, you said: nothing leaves. So the iron cannot rot away to nothing; it can only change state, dry to wet, gray to ochre to liver-red, and in changing state it makes the very soil the ferns root in and the very texture the Sheeting grips. The rust is the works composting its own bones to feed its own green skin, and because nothing is ever lost, the composting can go on as long as there is iron, which is to say effectively forever. The rust is not decay. In a closed system, *decay is just another organ.*

## So, Who Lives Here

A single slow animal, then. Iron-boned, moss-skinned, lamp-eyed, pump-hearted. It eats its own light and breathes its own water and shits rust that becomes soil that becomes more of itself. It has no edges that aren't also organs and no organs that aren't also weather. It was assembled, accidentally, out of the leavings of people who built a town in a marsh and lost — and in losing, in walking off the catwalks one last time, they left behind not a ruin but a *creature*, the way a tide pool left by a retreating sea is not an absence of ocean but a small new ocean of its own, complete and self-sustaining and entirely uninterested in the larger water it came from.

It is not waiting for anyone. You were right about that. But it is not finished either — that was the one thing the place let you believe that isn't so. It is growing. A finger-width a season, a millimeter a decade, one slow stroke at a time, it is becoming more thoroughly the only thing it has ever wanted to be, which is *itself, but greener.* Give it long enough — and it has nothing *but* long enough — and the horsetails will have their channels, and the Sheeting will have closed over the last bare iron, and the threadlike growths bridging pipe to pipe will have finished knitting the works shut into one unbroken green hide, and the pumps will go on beating underneath it all, buried, faithful, in the warm wet dark.

That is who lives here. The house and the tenant are the same thing, and it is alive, and it is at home, and it is the only one of itself there will ever be.
