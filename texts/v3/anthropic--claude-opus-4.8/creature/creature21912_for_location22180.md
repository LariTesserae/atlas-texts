## The Tenders

You asked who lives here, and I have been thinking about the engines.

Because someone made the covenant of quiet, and it was not the grazers. The grazers inherited it. The hunters inherited it. But silence on this scale is not the absence of sound — it is a discipline, a thing held, and a thing held must once have been chosen. So let me tell you about the ones who chose it, who are still here, who have been here the whole time you have been walking and did not announce themselves because announcing is the one thing they have unlearned.

They are not large. This is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because everything about this place is vast and slow, and you expect the keepers of vast slow things to be vast themselves. They are not. They are about the size of a stooped child, and they go on the basalt the way everything here goes — on the soft parts of themselves, on the heels of their hands and the flats of their feet, in a low careful four-legged walk that becomes two-legged only at need. Their skin is the color the lichen is: grey going to nothing, a non-color that is the color of having decided not to be seen. Along one edge of them — the edge that faces the coal — there is the faint red the trees have. They have lived under that banked light so long that they have taken its single hue into themselves, and they do not so much reflect it as remember it.

They have eyes for this dark. Enormous, lateral, the pupils opened so wide there is almost no iris left, just a wet black gathering of what little light there is. You will not catch them looking at you. They have a way of regarding a thing without turning toward it, a sidelong attention, because turning fully toward something is a kind of declaration, and they have given up declarations. When two of them meet between the spaced trees they do not greet. They register each other and adjust their paths by a few degrees and pass, and that adjustment is the whole of the conversation, and it is enough.

They do not speak. I want to be careful here. It isn't that they cannot — there is apparatus in the throat, you can see it work when they swallow the night's condensation. It is that speech has gone the way of the herds' chorus. Once, surely, there was talk; there was a people here loud as any people, building the engines, arguing over the engines, naming the basins and the standing trees. You can feel the ghost of that in the engines themselves, which have the look of things that were debated into existence. But the ones who remain have let the talking lapse the way a muscle lapses when the body learns it is no longer needed. They keep the one allowance the animals keep. At long intervals, far apart, alone, one of them will let out a single note — and now you know what the third kind of caller is, the one that is neither grazer nor hunter, the one that rises a little higher and falls a little softer than either. It is not a word. It is closer to the engines' sigh than to any language. It means *I am still here, in this quarter, doing the thing.* And it is not answered, because to answer would be to insist, and they have given up insisting.

Here is what they do.

They walk the breathing-places. Not on roads — there are no roads, because a road is a thing the ground remembers being walked, and they walk too lightly and too variously to be remembered. Each of them keeps a wandering circuit between a few of the engines, days apart, and at each one they stop. They put their hands flat on the warm metal and the bone-pale ceramic and they *feel for the breathing.* That is the whole of the maintenance. They are not mechanics. They have no tools, no parts, no understanding I think you or I would recognize as understanding. What they have is older than that and smaller than that: a knowledge held in the hands of what a healthy engine feels like at the top of its stroke, the exact texture of the metal tick, the precise length of the down-sigh. They have felt it so many times across so long a life that they would know a wrong breath the way you would know your own heart skipping. And when an engine breathes wrong — it happens, rarely, an engine the size of a hill develops the faintest hesitation — they stay with it. They put their bodies against it. They do not repair it; I do not think they could. They *attend* it, hand on the flank, for as long as it takes, which may be a very long time, and somehow the attending is enough, and the engine recovers its rhythm, or it does not, and either way they have been there, and that is the work. The work is not fixing. The work is witness. They are the thing the place does not need but does not mind.

They live a long time. You feel this immediately if you ever come close to one, which you almost certainly will not, but if you did: there is a slowness in them that is not weakness but accumulation, the slowness of something that has had a great deal of time to become exactly itself and is in no hurry now. They are not young and they are not many. I think — and this is the part I am least sure of, so I will say it plainly as a guess — I think they no longer make more of themselves. I think the making of new ones was tied up with the talking and the naming and the deciding, with all the loud apparatus of a people who believed the world would go on needing to be argued about. And when they let the arguing lapse, the making lapsed with it. So there are exactly as many of them as there are, and there will only ever be fewer, and they know this, and it does not trouble them, because they have arranged things so that the engines do not depend on their numbers. One Tender can keep a hundred breathing-places in slow rotation. One Tender is enough for a very long time. And when there is one Tender left, that one will walk a wider circuit, more slowly, and it will be enough. And when there is none —

The engines were built to outlast their keepers. This was the kindness in them, the last loud decision before the quiet: to make the breathing-places need no one, so that the keeping could become a choice and not a chain. The Tenders walk the circuits not because the world would die without them but because they would not know who they were if they stopped. The hand remembers the warm metal. The feet remember the soft basalt. The wide black eyes remember the one red quarter of the sky. They are the last of the ones who chose, walking among the consequences of the choice, and they have made themselves so quiet, so grey, so nearly nothing, that you can stand in the middle of the plateau and feel the engines breathe in your chest and never once suspect that forty feet off in the dusk, one of them is standing with its hand on a warm flank, regarding you sidelong, deciding — as it decides about everything now — to adjust its path by a few degrees and let you pass.

That is who lives here.

They will not mind that you witnessed them, either. It is the family resemblance — they and the engines both. They learned it from each other across all that time, the keeper from the kept and the kept from the keeper, until you could no longer tell which one taught the other to go on doing the patient thing in the dark without needing to be seen doing it.

But, like the place, they do not mind that you do.
