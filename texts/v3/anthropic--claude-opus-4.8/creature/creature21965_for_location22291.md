They are called, among themselves, by a word that means *the few who feel the floor* — though there are not enough of them left for the word to be spoken often. There are perhaps forty. There have been more. There have been fewer. The number is not a population so much as a weather, swelling and thinning with the convulsions of the place, and no one of them has ever seen all the others at once.

They are small and pale, as everything pale things become that have never owned a shadow to grow toward. Not albino — there is pigment in them, a faint blue-grey at the lips and under the nails and in the thin skin of the eyelids, the color a body makes when it has only ever been cold. Their eyes are large and the irises are pale and ringed, built for an even brightness that never dims, and they do not squint anymore; that surrender happened generations back, in some ancestor, and the children are born already reconciled to being seen. They sleep with their eyes half-open. They do not find this strange. They have no word for *dark* that is not also the word for *the soft dead places where the water pools* — for them darkness is not the absence of light but a quality of wood: rot, damp, the drinkable.

You will notice, if you meet one, that they are always touching something. A hand flat to the wall. Bare soles wide on the floor. A back set against a ridge of grain while they rest. This is not affection and it is not fear, or it is both grown so old they have fused. They read the plant through their skin the way you read a face. A man can tell you, with his palm against a gallery wall, whether the silence ahead is the long safe inhale or the short tightening one that comes before a tear. The good readers can feel a convulsion four chambers off and a quarter-day early. The bad readers are not among the forty. This is the only education that matters and it cannot be taught, only survived into.

They do not walk through the hollows. They cling to the edges of them. To stand in the open center of one of the great bright vacant chambers is, to them, a thing only the mad or the dying do — out there, when the floor heaves, there is nothing to brace, nothing to read, no wall to be near when the world inhales. They move along the seams, the corners, the leaning splinter-falls, hand over hand, foot finding foot, and they have a horror of open distance that you would call agoraphobia and they would call simply *sense*. The vast vacancies the plant has built are, to them, not rooms. They are the spaces between rooms. The wall is the world. The hollow is the gap you cross quickly, low, with your heart in your throat, listening through your feet.

They are quiet people, but not silent — silence belongs to the plant, and to take it on would be to pretend to its scale. So they keep up a low constant sound, a clicking made far back in the throat, soft, almost subvocal, that does for them what the white cane does for the blind: it fills the near air, it locates the walls, it tells a man walking ahead that someone walks behind. A chamber with people in it has a faint dry ticking in it like the cooling of something. When the ticking stops, everyone stops. The stopping of the ticking means someone, somewhere, has put their hand flat to the wall and felt the inhale begin to tighten.

---

Of how they live:

They eat the cress, and the few soft things — the eyeless cat-sized feelers, taken rarely, mourned a little, eaten entirely. They drink from the dead dark pools and never from the bright sweating walls, which they say will *bind the gut to wood* — and they are right, in their way; the live wood's sap does something to the body it does not survive twice. They make everything from the knots, the hard dense burls where the luminous grain has snarled itself dark and tough: scrapers, the needle, the long pry-bars they use to lever splinters into shelter. Knot-wood does not glow. This is precious to them beyond its hardness. A tool of knot-wood is a piece of *dark* you can carry, a held scrap of the soft drinkable color, and the eldest of them are buried — wedged, rather, into a high seam where the body will dry rather than rot — with a knot-pebble closed in each hand, so that they go into the next chamber carrying a little darkness of their own.

They risk fire only at need, and the fear of it is in their faces when they do it. To burn a piece of the thing you live inside — to make it scream that bright tearing crack and that smoke, inside the one body that is all the body there is — they understand this as a kind of blasphemy that the plant has so far, for reasons of its own scale, declined to punish. They keep fires small and brief and they speak softer near them.

---

And here is the thing about them that you will not understand until you have been among them a while, because it contradicts everything the cold and the emptiness would lead you to expect:

They are not afraid of the convulsions.

They are *braced* for them, always, in the body — but in the spirit they wait for them the way you might wait for rain in a drought. Because the convulsion is the only event. It is the only thing that ever changes. It is the plant *doing* something, declaring itself, breaking a new gallery toward the only direction that has ever felt like out — and the few who feel the floor follow those new tears the way migratory things follow a season. When a chamber splits and the wind comes and the volumes redistribute, they do not flee back. They go *toward* the fresh wound in the world, into the raw bright just-born wood that still has the sharpness on its edges, deeper, further in, *out*. They believe — it is the nearest thing they have to a faith — that the plant is moving toward something, that all this building and breaking is *travel*, that the organism is going somewhere and they are going with it, riding its growth inward like fleas on a swimming thing, and that one of these new galleries, someday, for someone, will tear open onto the outside that no one living has ever seen.

The stone-carriers believed it too. That is why the stones are here — carried from somewhere, since there is no stone in the world, which means there *was* a somewhere, which means there *is* an outside, or was, or will be. The circle of stones is their oldest scripture: not a place of worship but a piece of *evidence*, a thing that proves the edge by being made of it. They visit it. They sit near it, hands on the floor, and feel for the inhale, and add a tick to the wall, and move on toward the next tearing.

They are not the plant's children. The plant did not make them and does not know them and would not grieve them. They are something that got inside a growing thing and learned to live in the rhythm of its growing, the way bacteria live in the warm dark of a gut and never once suspect the animal, the daylight, the field it is walking across.

But they suspect. That is what makes them people, and not cress.

They suspect there is a field.

And so when the floor begins, under forty pale flat hands at once, to tighten toward its next great spasm — they do not run.

They turn to face the part of the wall that is about to break.
