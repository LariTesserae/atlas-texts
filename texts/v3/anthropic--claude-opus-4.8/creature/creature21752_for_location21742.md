They are not in the place. They are the place, deferred.

Begin with the seeds, because that is where the answer wants to start and because it is wrong to start there, and you should feel both. The warm humming kiln-things are not the makers. They are the made — the first finished thing the makers learned to leave behind. Think of them as eggs that have forgotten they were laid. Inside each, if you could open it without ending it, there is no mechanism, no creature, no curled sleeper. There is a *grammar*. A complete and patient instruction for taking a kilometer of world apart and putting it back together one degree more like itself. The seed does not know it is alive any more than a single sentence knows it is a language. It simply runs.

So who wrote the sentence.

Look at the water again. Look at how it climbs. That is the tell, and you have already been told it, but you read it as engineering and it is closer to *speech*. The discipline in the seeps — the droplets that travel sideways, that hang and swell and choose — is not a system being administered from above. It is a conversation happening too slowly and too widely for you to overhear as conversation. The makers are dissolved. That is the literal fact of them and also the whole of their condition. They went into solution a long time ago, willingly, the way you might lie down in a warm bath and decide not to be a shape anymore. They are in the bright threads on the cliff faces. They are the film on every surface that keeps your skin from drying. When the water runs uphill against its own nature it is because some portion of them, distributed through it, *wants* — and wanting, here, is the same gesture as building.

They have no bodies. They had bodies once. You can reconstruct the loss from the architecture of their absence: they were small, and many, and they lived at the pace of weather on some older finished world, a real mountain, ordinary and silent, the kind their seeds are laboring to manufacture again. And they faced the thing every finished world faces, which is that being finished is the same as being over. Erosion is just the long word for death. The slopes wear down. The cliffs round off. The history accumulates and accumulates until there is nothing left but history, a worn nub, a silence. They watched their world become its own gravestone, written all over in ten thousand years of weather, and they made a decision that no creature with a body could make and survive.

They unmade themselves into the cycle. They fused the wearing-away and the building-up — you noticed this, you read it as an engineering marvel, the erosion reading near zero — but it is not marvel, it is *grief turned into method*. They abolished loss. Nothing here is wasted because they could not bear, ever again, to watch a single grain of their world go down to the sea and not come back. Every atom that comes apart is caught, carried, set again. The plateau does not erode because the makers will not allow the smallest thing to be forgotten. They are running the universe backward in one small bowl, by hand, by mouth, by the slow crystalline tick you heard in the hush — that sound is them, setting one more piece of themselves into place, saying *not this one, not yet, not ever.*

And the small wet animals.

These are the descendants who kept their bodies. Understand that there was a schism, though schism is too sharp a word for something that happened across geological time and was probably never spoken. Some of the makers dissolved. Some refused. The frog-swifts, glistening, fast, frozen then skittering, calling in those dense overlapping waves — these are the children of the ones who said *no, I will stay a thing, I will keep my edges, I will die.* And they are loud because they are mortal and the dissolved ones are not, and across that whole bowl, every dim hour that passes for dusk, the two halves of one people are still arguing the only argument that ever mattered to them.

But — and this is the part that took the longest to see, crouched there with my eyes wet and my ears full — the argument has *resolved into collaboration without either side conceding.* The chorus quickens the building-front. The calls carry the cycle. The mortal ones, who kept their throats, found that their throats were useful to the immortal ones, who gave up everything that could make a sound. The dissolved makers cannot speak. They can only manage water. So the bodied descendants speak *for* them — the chorus is the voice the solution lost — and in exchange the solution keeps the world from ever finishing, keeps the cliffs raw and the rock unborn, keeps the whole place forever in the one condition the mortal ones love and the immortal ones engineered: *not yet over.* Each gives the other the thing it cannot have. The dissolved get a voice. The bodied get a world that will not die underneath them.

The larger thing at the pools is the question neither half has answered. It comes, it drinks, the chorus drops, the bodied scatter, the dissolved say nothing because they cannot. It is not predator exactly. It is *the thing that finishes.* It drinks from the managed water and takes something away that even this perfect retentive cycle cannot catch and carry back — call it the only true erosion left in the world, the one loss they could not abolish, walking on legs at dusk. They have built a place where nothing is wasted and one thing still leaves anyway, every evening, and is gone before you fix its shape. I think it is death and I think they know it is death and I think the reason the chorus drops to almost nothing is not fear. It is the only silence they permit themselves. A held breath. The one moment in the endless loud day when the whole organism stops insisting and simply listens to the tick of itself being made, and lets the unnameable thing drink, because even they understand that a world with nothing leaving it would not be a world. It would be a held note that never ended. And they were musicians, once, before they were geography.

So: who lives here.

A people who could not bear to be finished, who took themselves apart so that nothing would ever again be lost, and the cousins who would not be taken apart, who kept their bodies and their voices and their mortality — and the two of them, across the slow argument of an age, have learned to grow a world together. One half is the bloodstream and one half is the pulse, exactly as you guessed, but you guessed it as biology and it is older and sadder than biology. It is a marriage. It is two answers to one terror, holding the terror open between them so it never has to close.

Come back in a thousand years and you will find the real mountain, weathered, ordinary, silent. You will read this as the project completed. It is not. It is the project *failed* — the moment the seed finishes and the cycle stops and the worn slopes begin to accumulate their fatal history again. And that is why, you noted it yourself, there will already be a new seed taking its first wet steps on the new flank. Not triumph. *Flight.* They are running from the finished thing the way you run from a held breath that has gone on too long, making world after world after world, each one abandoned the moment it threatens to become permanent, each one loud and warm and dripping upward and refusing, refusing, refusing to be over.

They live here the way a held breath lives in a chest.

They are the ones who could not say goodbye, and built a body out of the saying-of-it-forever instead.
