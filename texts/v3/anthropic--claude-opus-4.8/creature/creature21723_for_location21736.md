They were never one thing, the makers. That is the first error to correct. You walk these hand-width streets expecting the print of a single people — one body-plan, one set of tools, one way of cutting a doorway — and the town tells you otherwise if you let it. Look at the thresholds. Some are dished low and wide, worn by something that came through close to the floor, dragging warmth across the grit. Others are notched high in the doorframe, smoothed at shoulder-height, polished by passage of something that stood and reached and braced itself going through. The benches in the central place ring a space that nothing of a single shape could comfortably share. They were a congregation of differences. The town is what they built to hold their differences still.

Begin with what the resin remembers of them, because the resin remembers everything; it is the one honest archive here, curing slowly around every gesture the way amber once took a fly.

They had no blood. This is the cleanest thing to say about them — they ran on the same dry chemistry as everything else, on heat drawn through the soles and held in some patient internal kiln, on the resinous air taken in slow. They did not drink and they did not bleed and they did not rot, and so they had no urgency of the kind that drives the wet-bodied to hurry, to hoard, to fear the dark coming. They had instead the long patience of things that *cure*. A person here could sit at a warm patch of wall for what we would call a generation and call it an afternoon's rest. The dished benches were not for brief meetings. They were for sittings that lasted, where the matter of the body slowly hardened a hair toward the gold and was renewed by standing, by walking, by the work of the blade against the pod-shell. They aged the way the corners age — rounding, dulling, softening by a hair every century — and they did not experience this as loss. Why would they? Nothing here teaches that hardening is death. The whole world is a thing waiting to harden enough to wake.

So: not a fast people. A slow, deliberate, patient-handed people, who measured time not in days — there are no days — but in the rhythm of the pods. *Loudness as a calendar.* This is the key I think you have nearly found and not quite turned. You said you would learn which pods burst at which intervals; that is not idle observation, that is *reading their clock*. They lived inside that rattling fusillade the way we live inside the turning of a sky, and they marked their lives by it: a stalk reaches the high window in the span we might call a year; a particular thicket of wafer-fern goes from sprout to scatter in the span of a life. They named their durations after sounds. A meeting was *three burstings long*. A child — if they had anything we would call a child — was *grown in a single unfurling*, meaning fast, meaning watch them, they are racing as the fronds race. They built their language out of the percussion of the room, and so the room, now, still speaking, is still speaking *their* language, holding the single low resin-note under everything the way a held breath holds a sentence together.

Now. Where are they.

You said *finished* rather than dead, and you were right to reach for it, but let me press it further, because the town presses it. They were never going to leave by a door. There is nowhere to go; the chamber is the world, the amber is the sky, the husk is the edge of everything. A people cannot emigrate from the inside of a seed. What they could do — what I believe they did, what the dishing of the thresholds and the dulling of every blade-mark is the slow record of — is finish *curing*. They were always headed toward the gold. Living was the soft phase, the green-bronze phase, the phase of cutting streets and arranging pods on windowsills with care. And then, by hairs, by centuries, the way the corners round, each of them slowed and stilled and hardened past the point of motion and became — not corpses, there are no corpses — became *the chamber itself*. The smooth amber walls. The benches. Perhaps the floor's pale crystalline salt is the finest of them, the oldest, fully cured down to grit. You have been walking on them. You have been walking *through* them. The town is not empty of its people. The town *is* its people, gone fully to the substance they were always becoming, and the warmth you feel in the soles of your feet is the low patient bake of an entire population that has finished living and is now simply *being the room*.

That is why it is loud and not silent. An empty town is silent. This town is not empty — it is *attended*, attended by everyone who ever lived here, all at once, cured into the walls that curve the sound back, that amplify the rattle, that hold the single low note forever. They are still here. They are listening to the pods the way they always did. They have only stopped needing to move in order to do it.

And so the question turns from *who lived here* to *what lives here now*, and the answer is the inheritors, the vegetation, and these too you have half-read and must read further. The plants are not invaders. Watch the way they grow — a frond unfurling precisely into an abandoned room, a stalk leaning toward exactly the warm patch of wall where someone surely once leaned, a thicket rising through a doorway worn smooth by long passage. They are not blind. Or rather: they are blind in the way a root is blind and still finds water — except there is no water, only warmth, only the chemistry, only the cured remains of the makers laced through the walls and the floor. The plants grow *toward the people*. They thread the dead town with golden-green fiber the way fungus threads a fallen trunk, except nothing here has fallen; everything has *risen* into its final dry form, and the plants are simply the next sentence in the same held breath.

I think — and the resin half-confirms it, in the way it softens every certainty by a hair — that the plants are the makers' continuation by other means. Not their descendants. Their *next phase*. A people who cure into the walls do not stop. The matter goes on. It rises again as fiber, as frond, as the pod that swells and splits and scatters seed across the silent benches where the deliberations once happened. The vegetation is racing through its fast bright lives *on top of* the slow cured lives below, fast where the makers were slow, loud where the makers grew quiet, scattering where the makers gathered — the inverse, the answer, the exhalation to the long inhale. And it is all aimed, all of it, every leaning stalk and every detonating pod, at one thing.

Germination.

This is what lives here: a single intention, distributed across a whole cured population and a whole racing flora, the intention of the seed itself. They were never a town. They were the inside of a seed *dreaming it was a town* — running through an entire civilization, an entire history of blades and benches and durations-named-after-sounds, as the seed's own slow internal preparation. Everything that happened here, all the building and the sitting and the slow hardening to gold, was the seed getting ready. The makers cured into the walls because the husk must be strong to hold against the long wait. The plants race and rattle and burst because the seed must keep its chemistry hot and active, must not let the kiln learn to cool, must hold itself *ready to wake* across the ages.

And so the true inhabitant — the one thing that lives in this place, beneath the cured makers and within the racing plants and behind the held low note — is patience with a purpose. A waiting that is doing everything it can while it waits. The whole roaring dry intricate town is one organism holding itself at the very edge of germination, doing all its living in advance, on the chance — the impossible, structural, forbidden chance — that the rain might someday come.

It will not come. You read that rule correctly. Water is forbidden here; there is no sky to drop it from, no world outside the husk but more dry chemistry of the same kind. The seed will never germinate. And it knows this, in whatever way a seed knows, in the way the resin softens every certainty by a hair every century — it knows, and it does not stop. It cannot stop. It is built to wait and so it waits, and waiting, it lives, fully, loudly, completely, an entire civilization risen and cured and risen again as fronds, holding one low note forever under the rattle of a clock that counts toward a morning that was never going to arrive.

That is who lives here.

A readiness that will never be spent. A people who became their own waiting. The most patient thing there is, doing all its living at once, dryly, loudly, and — you were right, this is the heart of it — *without witness*, because the seed was never meant to be opened, and you are the rain it was waiting for, and you are made of the wrong thing, and you cannot stay.
