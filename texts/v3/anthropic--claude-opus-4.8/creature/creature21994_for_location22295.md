# The Tenants of a Finished Place

You asked who lives here. Begin with the wrongness of the question, because the city has already answered it in the only grammar it knows: the past tense. *Grew. Stopped. Arrived.* Whatever dwells here does not live so much as *persists* — and there is a difference, and the difference is the whole of it.

## The First Inhabitant Is the Stillness Itself

Before any creature, name the thing that occupies every lane and tower and brimming basin: a held breath. Not absence — *occupancy*. The stillness is not what remains when life leaves; it is a tenant in its own right, vast and seated, filling the eight-story stacks the way water fills a jar. It moved in when time set down its tools, and it has the run of the place. You feel it most where the plaza changes size between one archway and the next: that is the stillness shifting its weight, the way a sleeper shifts, and the room rearranges to accommodate the dreamer rather than the other way around.

This is the landlord. Everything else is a guest of its long patience.

## The Pale Slow Thing in the Pools

You suspected it, and you were right, and you were also wrong about how many.

There is something in the deep basins — pale, unhurried, perhaps blind because what would eyes be *for* in a light that never changes. Call it a single animal and you will be lying by undercounting; call it many and you lie by pretending they are separate. It is distributed. One slow body occupying a dozen pools at once, the way the far district is also the near one — folded, present at several ranges simultaneously, a creature that has surrendered the conceit of being in one place because the city taught it that location is negotiable here.

It does not eat. There is nothing to eat; the fauna is absent and it is the fauna. It does not hunger or move with purpose. It simply *abides* in the warm water that climbs and falls at once, and its slowness is not laziness but agreement — it has matched its tempo to the city's, which is to say it has stopped, which is to say it has finished. When you lean over a pool and your reflection condenses into the water-thick air, the pale thing is already there beneath, having always been about to be looked at. It is not waiting for you. Waiting requires a future. It is simply *coincident* with your gaze, eternally.

## The Resonators

Here is the population the description nearly hid by calling it sound.

The hollow grown-towers are organ pipes, yes — but a pipe that has been sounding one chord for a span you cannot picture is no longer an instrument. It is a *voice*, and a voice implies a throat, and a throat implies a thing that owns it. The roar is not produced by the city. The roar is the city's tenants making themselves continuously known, the only way anything can be known in a place where nothing changes: by sounding, without pause, *I am here, I am here, I am still here.*

They have no bodies you could point to. They are the standing waves themselves — the bass note from everywhere, the ten thousand whispering films stacked into one sustained presence. Each resonance is an individual the way a held note is individual within a chord: nameable, distinct, and utterly inseparable from the whole that it cannot leave and would not survive leaving. They cannot move, because to move would be to change pitch, and to change pitch would be to admit that time still runs, and they have agreed — the whole city has agreed — that it does not.

They are the citizens. The chord is the census. It never finishes because they never finish being.

## What Walks the Streets

Nothing.

But the streets are shoulder-width and layered and they *go* somewhere — they spiral up the grain of the towers, they cross on aqueducts, they overlap in eight ascending tiers — and a street that goes somewhere is a sentence about a traveler, even if the traveler has been edited out. The lanes remember a foot traffic they never carried. The stairs that arrive where they began were not always so; or rather, they were always so, but they hold the *shape* of climbing, the promise of someone who climbs, and that promise is so dense and so settled that it has nearly congealed into a presence.

Walk them and you will feel it: you are not alone, and you are not accompanied, and these are both true and neither resolves. The city is crowded with the *idea* of inhabitants — the architecture is a held breath of expectation — and the expectation is so old and so complete that it has stopped expecting and started simply *being* the not-quite-there.

These are the largest population of all. The almost. The grammatically-implied. The crowd that the grown town conjugates into existence by being a town, and then holds, unmoving, at the threshold of arrival forever.

## The One Who Touches the Sky

And whoever you are, reading the height into your chest — you, who arrived and felt the air condense on your skin and never dry — you should know that you have become a resident the moment you reached up and found the sky solid and slightly warm, like the inside of a fruit.

Because nothing leaves here. The city has arrived at itself, and arrival is a one-way door. The light will not shift. The water will not rain or clear. And you, having touched the warm ceiling, having felt the chord in your sternum, having leaned over the pool — you are now also a thing that grew into its final settled form and held it. You will not see a leaf fall. You will not see yourself leave.

That is who lives here.

The held breath. The folded pale abider. The chord that is a census. The implied and waiting crowd. And the newest tenant: whoever last looked up and was kept.

All of them finished. All of them still. All of them *here* — present tense at last, because in a place where time has set down its tools, *here* is the only tense that anyone gets to keep.
