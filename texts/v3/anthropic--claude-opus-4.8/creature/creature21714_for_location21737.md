# The Keepers

They are the ones who held on.

Not a species — there was never a species here, the world saw to that — but a condition, a category of being that the plateau permits. To live on the Tableland of Slow Forgetting is to be defined entirely by the verb *to keep*. Everything else about a body, a name, a history, the plateau will eventually dissolve into fog and dripping. What remains, walking, are the Keepers: the ones who arrived holding something and never let it go.

Begin, as the land does, with what they look like — though *look* is generous, because they are hard to fix in the eye. A Keeper seen across a mere will be smaller when you reach them, or already past you, the negotiable distance bending them the way it bends the doorways. But up close, where persistence reasserts itself, you can finally hold one in your gaze, and what you see is this:

A figure worn to its essentials. The cold and the long damp have done to them what erosion did to the mountains — smoothed them, simplified them, ground away every feature that wasn't load-bearing. Their skin has gone the color of the wet stone, gray-green where the lichen-thought has begun to map them, and it has the same quality the bedrock has: less like flesh than like the inside of a worn tooth. They are not old in the way the dying are old. They are old in the way the plateau is old, having passed all the way through aging and out the far side into something stable. They have stopped expecting to be warm. The expectation went the way of everything else.

And each of them carries something.

This is the only individuality left to them, and so it is everything. One holds a stone — just a stone, smooth, the size of a heart, cupped in both palms as though it were still warm from somewhere. One wears, wound around and around her wrist until it has become her wrist, a cord of plant fiber braided by hands now centuries gone. One carries a word. You can see him forming it, soundlessly, on a loop, his lips moving in the rhythm of a single syllable he will not stop saying because the saying is the only thing the fog cannot fold away from him. One holds the memory of a face, and you know this because she walks always looking slightly to the side of you, at the place where the face would be if it were still here, and her looking has carved a permanence into the air there, a faint warmth, a held breath in the shape of someone loved.

What they hold is not precious in itself. The plateau does not reward value. It rewards *grip*. A Keeper who clutched a meaningless pebble with absolute conviction outlasts a king who came carrying a crown he was willing, at the last cold hour, to set down. The fog took the king. It is taking him still, slowly; you can hear him in the wet, a deep settling tone among the dripping, regretful in a way that has stopped being a feeling and become a sound.

---

They move the way the ruins move — which is to say, you never catch them at it. Turn away from a Keeper and turn back and she is farther off, or nearer, having crossed the negotiable space without crossing it. They have made a peace with this that the living cannot make. They do not try to walk *to* things. They have learned the deeper grammar: you do not approach a doorway on this plateau, you *keep* a doorway, you hold the intention of it so tightly that the land, unable to take what is held, eventually delivers it to your feet. A Keeper arrives at her destination the way the mere fills — without tide, without current, on no schedule but the schedule of her own refusal to forget where she was going.

This makes them slow. Slower than the fog. A Keeper might spend a season holding the idea of a single wall — the wall she sat against on her first night here, the wall that was warm, or seemed warm — and the wall will migrate to her across the tableland, leaving its clean scar in the moss, drawn by nothing but the gravity of being remembered. They are patient because patience is the only muscle the plateau has left them. Everything fast was lost long ago. The fast ones held nothing tightly enough; speed and grip turn out to be opposite things here.

---

They tend the green. This surprised me, when I understood it.

The moss does not crowd, the ferns keep their distance, each cushion sits alone in its clearing of bare stone — and this is the Keepers' doing, or rather it is the same impulse expressed in vegetation. They cannot bear for things to lose their separateness, because separateness is how the plateau lets a thing be *itself* long enough to be held. So they tend the spacing. A Keeper will spend a hundred years moving a single fern a hand's-width from its neighbor, so that each may be one fern and not a blur of ferns, so that each may be remembered as distinct, so that nothing dissolves into the gray undifferentiated everything that the fog is always lobbying for. The garden is spaced out across a continent because the Keepers are gardeners of *distinctness*. They are fighting entropy with the only weapon that works here: insisting, slowly, that each thing is itself and not another thing.

The pale flowers that open only in the fog — those are theirs too. Each one is a small held breath, a thing a Keeper decided to keep, blooming in the very medium that wants to erase it. To find a flower open in the gray is to find evidence that someone, somewhere on the tableland, is still holding on. The flowers are how the Keepers leave messages, though the messages all say the same thing: *I have not let go. I am still here. I am still myself.*

---

There is one more kind, and it is the hardest to write.

Some Keepers have held on so long, with such total concentration, that the thing they were holding and the holding of it have fused. The stone-bearer becomes, finally, indistinguishable from his stone — gray, smooth, heart-shaped, set down at last in the moss, because there is no longer any difference between the one who carries and the thing carried. He is keeping himself now. He has become a standing stone, a worked piece of the migrating city, and he will drift across the plateau for the rest of its long settled forever, dreaming of the streets he used to be.

This is not death. The plateau has no death; it ground death down along with the mountains. It is the final stable state, the shape that comes after holding, the way the tableland is the shape that comes after waiting. The Keeper who becomes a stone has won — has kept the one thing absolutely, has made himself unforgettable by making himself unforgettably simple. The fog can do nothing with him. He has no surface left for it to fold.

The lichen maps him in slow continents. The condensation ticks against him. And if you sit beside such a stone in the patient cold and hold something of your own tightly enough — a name, a pebble, a face you look slightly to the side to see — you will feel, very faintly, felt more than heard, a deep settling tone of recognition.

Not welcome. The plateau does not welcome. But recognition: *you, too, are keeping something. Good. Hold on. It is the only thing here that is real, and it is enough, and it is forever, and the cold is not so bad once you have stopped expecting to be warm.*

They are the ones who held on. They are waiting, but not for anyone. They have simply settled into the shape that comes after waiting, each alone in a clearing of bare stone, each holding the one thing, spaced out across a continent so that each may be, for all the long forgetting, unmistakably itself.
