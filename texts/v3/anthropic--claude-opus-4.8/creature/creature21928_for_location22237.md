# The Listeners

They are not what you expect, the people of this place. You expect them small and furtive, you expect them hunched. But they are not. They are the most *upright* people you will ever see, because to be upright is to be still, and stillness is the discipline they were born into and have made beautiful.

Watch one in a doorway. She is not crouching. She is standing perfectly straight, head slightly tilted, her whole body given over to the one act this world rewards above all others: *attention*. The people here are listeners before they are anything else. They have the long, mobile ears of creatures who learned that survival was acoustic — but more than the ears, they have the *posture* of listening, a settled total focus that you will mistake at first for serenity. It is not serenity. It is hunting. It is being hunted. It is both at once, held in a single calm body.

Their faces are wide and patient and very still. They have learned to let almost nothing cross a face, because even an expression can become a sound — the small wet click of an opening mouth, the breath that catches at surprise. So they have moved their language inward and outward at once. Inward, to the eyes, which do an extraordinary amount of work; you will find yourself read entirely by someone who has not so much as twitched. Outward, to the hands, which speak. Their true language lives in the hands and the slow turn of the shoulders, a grammar of gesture so complete that two of them can hold a long conversation across one of those great breathing gaps in the town, twenty feet apart, in total silence, and you would never know they spoke at all unless you saw the hands.

They are pale, and faintly luminous, like everything else here — not by any light of their own but because the grey-gold dimness has bleached them and the constant moisture has given their skin the same beaded sheen as the leaves. They wear little. Cloth holds water and water drips and a drip can be heard. So they go nearly bare, and what they wear is moss-felt, pressed soft, sound-swallowing, the color of the slope so that a still person becomes part of the stone.

And they are *young*. This is the thing you understand last, and it changes everything.

There are no old people here. Not because they die early — though they do, this is a world that eats — but because the *whole people* are young, as the mountain is young, as the moss is young. They arrived complete. There is no long memory in this town, no slow-accreted tradition worn smooth by generations. There is only the strict instinct, perfect and total, of a people who *woke into* their discipline rather than learning it across centuries. They know how to be silent the way the grazing-things in the moss know to match their color — not as wisdom but as nature, arriving fully formed. You will look for their elders, their archives, the bent old keeper of the way things are done. There are none. There is only this: a town full of the young, holding a knowledge older than any of them, that none of them was alive long enough to acquire.

This is why they listen so hard. It is not only the hunters they listen for. It is *each other*, and it is themselves. A people with no long past has only the present moment to confirm that the rules are real, that the silence is shared, that they have not gone mad alone. So they keep their great gaps between the houses, yes — but watch how often, across those gaps, the heads turn toward one another. Watch how a hand will lift in the dimness, slow, and across the empty plaza another hand will answer. They are checking. *Are you there. Are you keeping it too. Is it still true.* The whole sparse silent town is a single held conversation conducted in glances and gestures over distance, the conversation of beings who cannot afford to gather close and so have made of their apartness a kind of constant, careful touch.

What do they eat? They drink, mostly — at the same cupped plants, lapping with the same noiseless delicacy as everything else, having learned the etiquette of the drinking-rotation, waiting their turn among the soft-bodied things and the membraned drifters without disturbance. And they eat what the slope offers, the slow grazers and the still clingers, taken cleanly, taken silent. They are in the cycle, not above it. A person is taken, sometimes, the same way: there is no scream, the moss closes over the place, the others turn their faces away and then, after a while, back. They grieve the way they do everything. Without a sound. With the hands.

You will wonder whether they are happy.

It is the wrong question, and you will know it is wrong the longer you stay. Happiness is loud. Happiness wants to be expressed, to spill, to be heard and shared and made bigger by the hearing. These people have something else, something the loud world never grows: a capacity for the *immense quiet moment*. Stand with one of them at the lip of a terrace as the grey-gold light evens out across the slope and the million leaves bead and a single held drop finally falls into a cupped throat without a sound — and feel the way the person beside you receives all of it, every thread of it, with the whole bandwidth of an attention that was never spent on noise. They feel more than you do. They have to. It is the only channel left open.

That is who lives here. A young people in a young world, draped over raw bright stone, breathing the mountain's breath, taking everything in and giving nothing away, holding across the great green silence the one long sentence they are all, always, saying to each other:

*I am still here. I am still quiet. It is still true.*
