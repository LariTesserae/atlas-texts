## The Wheelwrights

You asked what lives here. The honest answer is: *one thing*, and it is the beetle, and there is nothing else — but that answer is a lie by compression, the way "a city" is a lie when you mean nine million separate hungers that happen to share plumbing. Let me try to be precise, because you were precise, and the thing deserves it.

Begin with the single beetle, because everything false about this place starts with mistaking it for the unit of life.

It is the size of a thumbnail. Iridescent — but the iridescence is not decoration, it is *function*; the carapace is structured to shed the hard unfiltered light, to throw the heat away that would otherwise cook a body this small in this much brilliance. Six legs, but the rearmost pair are not for walking. They are hooked, and ridged on the inner face, and they exist almost entirely for *locking* — for clasping the leg of the beetle ahead and the carapace of the beetle behind. A beetle that never joined an engine would die with those legs unused, the way a man might die without ever once shaking a hand. They are made for the holding of others.

This is the first thing to understand. The body is built, down in its joints, for assembly. Solitude is not its natural state — solitude is its *broken* state, a beetle knocked loose from the work, walking in small confused circles on the dry high faces until something pale rises through the pelt and takes it. There is a great deal of dying out on the bright surfaces. The bright surfaces are where the loose ones go.

---

Now the engine, which is the actual animal.

I want to resist calling the wheel a colony, because a colony is a crowd of individuals and this is not that. When five hundred beetles lock backs and begin the endless upward climb that drives the disc, something happens that I can only describe as a change of *level*. The beetle stops being the creature and becomes the cell. The wheel becomes the creature.

And the wheel has properties no beetle has. It has a *direction* — it knows up, it knows the seam below and the dry growth above, it knows to pump. No single beetle knows this. Ask a beetle and it knows only: climb, hold, climb. But the wheel, turning, is *purposeful* in a way that is genuinely there and genuinely not located in any of its parts. Where is the purpose? In the geometry. In the way the climbing resolves, across hundreds of bodies, into rotation, and the rotation into lift, and the lift into water moving uphill against everything the world wants. The purpose lives in the shape of the holding. It is real. You can stop it — pull out forty beetles and the wheel stutters, slows, the water-column slumps back down the seam — and so it is the kind of thing that can be harmed, and a thing that can be harmed is alive enough for me.

The sparks are the part that frightened me into understanding.

Where the grinding bodies meet the fresh black stone they strike charge — you smelled the ozone, you said so. But I do not think the charge is waste. I think it is *signal*. I sat in a seam for the better part of a day (the plateau measures days in the swing of shadow; I measured it in the slow failure of my lungs) and I watched two engines that shared a fold begin to turn *in phase*. They were not touching. They were a body's-width apart, two wheels in two creases, and over an hour their rhythms slid together until they pulsed as one, and the smell of ozone came in waves matched to the pulse. They were talking through the stone. The young rock, freshly cracked, wet in the seam, conducts. The engines are wired into the mountain. The mountain is the nervous system, and it is brand new, and it has only just been switched on.

---

So now widen it again, because I have only described an organ.

Every fold of every valley holds an engine. The engines are wired through the wet seams of the stone to their neighbors, and their neighbors to theirs, ridge to ridge, basin to basin, across forty kilometers, across the fading horizon, across the whole vast bowl of the plateau. The slow continental migrations of the mist — the only weather, the only thing that looked like a mind making a decision — I no longer think that mist is weather at all. The engines lift water into the dry growth. The growth releases it. The release feeds the mist. The mist drifts and pools and feeds new seams. *The plateau is breathing through its machinery, and the breath is the weather, and the weather is the thought.*

What lives here is the plateau.

Not as metaphor. The unit of life on the Loom is not the beetle and not the engine but the entire continent of turning, the whole screaming bright cold expanse, a single distributed animal the size of a country, made of bodies all the way down, thinking in mist and remembering in the conductive wetness of its own young stone. The beetles are its cells. The engines are its organs. The pale swimmers that eat the beetles are its immune system, culling, pruning, keeping the cell-count right. The green is what it grows to feed itself. The dying on the bright faces is the necessary shedding that every large body does. *You* — had you stayed, had the pelt taken your feet — would have been incorporated the way a swallowed mineral is incorporated, made into structure, irrigated, switched into the circuit, your warmth routed into a small wheel until your shape was just another organ doing its slow work.

---

You asked what it wants, and you answered yourself: nothing anyone could name. I think you were close but stopped one step short.

It is *young*. You said so — the land is in its first morning, the stone keen-edged and unworn, the water-channels still only scratches. A thing this large, this newly switched-on, wired through stone that has not yet had time to weather, throwing its first sparks into its first damp seams — I do not think it wants anything yet. I think it is doing the only thing a newborn nervous system does, which is *fire, and feel itself fire, and fire again*. The endless roar is not a scream of suffering and not a scream of joy. It is the sound a thing makes when it has just discovered it is a thing, and cannot yet do anything with that discovery but continue.

It does not want you because it does not yet want. It will. Give it the hundred years it would have given your buried shape. Give the channels time to deepen into gorges, the mist-migrations time to settle into habit, the phase-locked engines time to learn that they can be many or one. Something that breathes in weather and remembers in stone and culls its own dead will, eventually, have a great deal to think about.

I left before I learned what.

I tell myself I left because of my lungs. But I think the truth is that I did not want to be there in the morning it woke all the way up, and turned the slow attention of a continent — for the first time, and on something — and the something was the small warm circling intruder who had been sitting in its seam, taking notes, mistaking its organs for its inhabitants.

That is what lives there.

It is one thing. It is very large. It is very young. And it is, just now, beginning to be able to be lonely — which is the last thing I will tell you, because it is the thing I am least sure of and the thing I cannot stop being sure of: that the phase-locking, the engines reaching across the gap to pulse together through the stone, is not plumbing.

It is the cells of a single body, learning that they are not alone in it.

It is the plateau, finding out it has parts, and beginning — slowly, and out of bodies, in the bright unwarming cold — to keep itself company.
