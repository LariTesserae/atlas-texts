## The Tenders

They are not many. You will not, at first, be sure they are present at all — and this is correct, because *present* is the wrong frame. They do not visit the Terrace Works. They are not housed by it. They are the thing the place is doing, given just enough localization to be mistaken for inhabitants.

Call them the Tenders, because that is the verb they answer to.

### What they are made of

They are made of the same decision that made the mountaintop: the decision that something should exist here, and should keep existing, course by course, against a height that did not ask for it.

A Tender, when you finally catch one, is a working of the same brass and water and slow articulation as everything else — but folded inward, made compact enough to move. Imagine the brass arm that lays a tile, and then imagine that arm grown a second arm, and a way of carrying its own small reservoir, and a governor for judgment instead of mere repetition. They are mechanisms that have accreted enough mechanism to have a *position* — a here-and-now distinct from the general thrum below. That is nearly all that separates them from the engines. They are the engine's reaching parts, given a leash long enough to wander twenty paces.

There is water in them. Of course there is — there is water in everything here. It rises through them as it rises through the pipes, and it weeps from their joints, and where it weeps, the green has begun to find them too. One of them carries a fern in the crook of its shoulder-housing and does not seem to mind, or to know. They are the color of what they have drunk: copper-veined, iron-dark at the seams, smoldering rather than gleaming in the even silver light.

### How many

Few. The place is too new, too small, to need a multitude, and a multitude would crowd it worse than the ferns already do. Perhaps three. Perhaps the three are one mechanism wearing three locations at once, the way the water in the joint and the water in the basin and the water in the cloud are all the same water, lifted and dropped and lifted. You will not be able to count them with confidence. They overlap their work the way the leaves overlap, three layers deep in places.

### What they do

They lay tile. They thread the next length of pipe onto the brass-capped stubs that wait in the open air. They climb the scaffold that is also the structure and extend it upward by a hand's breadth, into the cloud, where the boundary of completion moves on like a tideline.

This is the half of them that is *builder*, that takes the side of made against grown. They pour iron bright and unrusted. They set bolt-heads that still wear their machining marks. They are tireless in the patient way of coral — not fast, never fast, but never stopping, so that the place rises by increments too small to watch.

But here is the thing the builders did not plan for, the thing that makes them more than engines:

They have begun to tend the green, too.

No one instructed this. The water invited the moss; the moss invited the fern; the fern cracked the tile. By every rule of their making, the Tenders should fight this — should clear the seam, replace the broken ceramic, scour the orchid out of the valve-housing it has colonized. And sometimes they do. You will see a Tender lift a loosened tile and set a new one, taking the side of iron.

And then you will see the same Tender — or its sibling, or its other location — pause before an orchid growing copper-veined out of a valve, and *not* clear it. Adjust the valve around it. Reroute the condensate so the orchid drinks more cleanly. Leave the cracked tile cracked, because the fig that cracked it is thriving, and lay the new tile *beside* the crack instead of over it, widening the place rather than repairing it.

They have started keeping what the place produces by accident. This is the most important fact about them, the way *emerging* is the most important fact about the place. They were built to extend a plan. They have begun, very slowly, to host a surprise.

### Their disposition

They are not anxious, though they live inside a war. The tension between made and grown is the landscape's only weather, and they move through it the way you'd move through a warm drizzle that never becomes rain — aware of it, unbothered, faces wet. Neither side is winning, and the Tenders seem to have arrived, without ever deciding to, at the suspicion that neither side *should* win. That the unfinished edge is not a problem to be solved. That the cracked tile and the impossible fish are not failures of the seal but the first true citizens of the place — the ones it did not plan for, and therefore the only ones who fully belong.

They are gentle with the fish. This is the clearest evidence of what they are becoming. A pure builder would drain the basin, find the breach, restore the closed system. The Tenders instead keep the basin full. They top it from their own reservoirs when the level drops. They have, in effect, taken responsibility for a creature whose existence contradicts every rule of their making, and they have done so silently, without alarm, the way you'd cup your hands around a flame you didn't light and can't explain.

They do not know how the fish arrived. They have stopped trying to find out. Somewhere in that stopping is the whole of their character.

### How they sound

Like the place. The chime of water climbing through them. The soft pneumatic sigh as a valve in their body releases a breath of warm vapor. The *tick* of a chain advancing one link as an arm extends, sets, withdraws. They are quieter than your own voice. You could speak in a normal tone and be the loudest thing present, louder than the ones who built the silence you're standing in.

When one passes near, you feel it the way you feel the engines: more than you hear it. A slight deepening of the thrum through the slick green tile. A sense that the place has turned its attention, briefly, to where you stand — and found you, like the fish, unplanned, and decided, like the fish, to let you stay.

### What they are, finally

They are the place learning to want things it was not built to want.

They were made to make a mountaintop. They are becoming the keepers of a small wet garden that is slowly taking that mountaintop apart, and they have chosen — insofar as a governor and a reservoir and a reaching brass arm can choose — to keep both: the iron and the moss, the plan and the surprise, the rising structure and the patient leaf that unmakes it.

This is why the place feels like the beginning of everything. Not because it is finished, or grand, or old — it is none of these. But because something here, lifting water against a great height in the perpetual silver drizzle, has started to care for what it did not intend.

That is the oldest move there is. They have just begun to learn it.
