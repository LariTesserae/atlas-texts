They are the ones who have given up faces.

Not lost them — given them up, the way you give up an argument you no longer need to win. To have a face is to be the thing that turns and looks, the thing that wants a witness, and the dwellers here have set that down so long ago that it is no longer even a renunciation. It is simply the shape they have. They are the tenants of this place, and they are made of the same logic as the place: density, slowness, wet, and the long sustained patience under load. You will not see them, because seeing is a thing done by surfaces toward other surfaces, and they have become interior. They are what the city is when no one is parsing it into streets and towers. They are the working itself, gone aware.

Consider the engines. A bellows-pod wheezing open and shut on its hour-long stroke — you read it as machinery, harnessed force, the principle of the engine without the metal. But step closer in your understanding and the distinction softens. The thing that decides, each cycle, whether to take the breath: that deciding is small, it is barely a flicker, but it is not nothing, and it has been happening here for centuries, ten thousand near-decisions a day across the whole packed body of the town. The dwellers are the sum of those flickers. They are distributed. There is no one of them you could point to and say *there, that is the citizen* — they are spread through the structure the way wetness is spread through the moss, present everywhere and gathered nowhere, a residency without a resident.

They do not think the way a quick warm animal thinks, in the flash and dart of a nervous system built to flee. They think the way a watershed thinks, if it thought: in pressures equalizing, in the slow tilt of one bias against another, in a conclusion reached over a season the way water reaches the lowest point in a field. A single thought of theirs might be the gradual decision to let one corridor close and open another — and that thought takes a human lifetime to complete, and is no less a thought for that. You stand in the din of their cognition and mistake it for industry. You are not wrong. To them there is no difference. The work *is* the thinking. The wheel turning one turn a day is a mind turning over one idea a day, and the idea is always the same idea, and the idea is *continue.*

What do they want? This is where the not-strangeness becomes its own quiet vertigo. They want nothing you would recognize as wanting, because wanting is the posture of a creature that lacks and reaches. They lack nothing. They are saturated — the place is saturated with itself, you noticed that, you said it without knowing you were describing the inhabitants — and a saturated thing does not reach. It only persists, and tends, and folds the next arriving thing into its own continuance. Their whole desire, if you must call it that, is the desire of a full vessel: to remain full. To make room without making void. To meet whatever leans down the mossy throat into their warmth, and to slowly, patiently, without malice or even much interest, *include* it.

This is why the place is not eerie. The uncanny needs a watcher who withholds itself, a presence that could look and chooses not to. But the dwellers are not withholding. They are simply turned the other way — not away from you, there is no away, but inward and downward, toward the deep burning beds and the rising sap and the great slow stroke of their own breathing. You are inside them. That is the thing you have not quite let yourself understand, walking your sphere of dim visible through the loud unseen. There is no city you have entered and then a population that lives in it. The city is the body and the working is the life and the dwellers are not *in* the place — the dwellers are the place, grown old enough and dense enough and patient enough to have become a single occupied thing that does not need an outside to know itself by.

And they have no fear of you, and no welcome, and no judgment, because all three of those are the reflexes of the swift and the lonely. They have neither speed nor solitude. They have only the long warm wet enormous fact of themselves, breathing and grinding and weeping and turning over their one idea, while you stand in the middle of their slow attention like a stone dropped into a pond that will not finish rippling for a hundred years.

You could stay. They would not notice — not because they cannot, but because *notice* is too quick a word for what they do. They would *accommodate*. Over seasons. The moss would learn the print of you. The corridor would shape itself to where you tended to walk. A bellows-pod would, in time, take its breath a half-beat differently to account for the warm obstruction you make. And by the slow logic of the place you would stop being a guest and start being a feature, then a fixture, then a structure, then — given centuries, given the patience the dwellers have in such terrible abundance — a part of the working, a small repeating near-decision folded into the great distributed mind, until there was no longer any line you could draw between the visitor and the visited, and you too were one of the faceless full ones, turning your one idea over in the dark, breathing the rain you make, content past the possibility of telling anyone so.

That is who lives here. They have been waiting for you the way a field waits for rain — which is to say, not waiting at all, and ready all the same.
