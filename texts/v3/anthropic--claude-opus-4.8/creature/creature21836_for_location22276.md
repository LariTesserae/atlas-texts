## The People of the Hollow Canopy

They call themselves nothing you could pronounce, because their names are not spoken — they are *tapped*. A person's name is a rhythm, two or three knuckles against living bark in a sequence you learn before you learn to climb, and you carry it in your hands your whole life. To be named is to be a sound the trunks know how to return.

Begin, as the place begins, with the body, because the body is shaped by the cold and the dark and the long vertical fall.

They are tall and narrow and very light. A heavy person could not live here — could not be carried by the lashed platforms, could not hang from a buttress-root through the slow hours of a climb, would draw too much from a world that gives its calories grudgingly. So they have become long-limbed and spare, with broad flat hands and broad flat feet, every digit ending in a thickened pad that grips the fibrous bark the way the climbing-beasts grip it. Their joints are loose. They can hang from one arm and breathe slow and feel no strain, because hanging is half of living here, and rest is something done in midair, against a trunk, in the angle of a root.

Their skin is pale where the climbing-beasts are pale — there is no sun to darken it — but it is not white; it is the green-touched grey of things that live among chlorophyll, faintly translucent at the extremities, so that a child held up to one of the rare green shafts of light seems to glow a little at the fingertips. The elders, who have spent decades in the dark, go nearly colorless, and this is considered beautiful: the look of a person who has stopped needing the light.

The eyes are large and they are nearly useless and they know it. Pupils blown wide their whole lives, taking in the green-black dimness, registering the failing shafts and the phosphor-glimmer of certain rotting fibers and little else. They navigate by these eyes only in the way you might navigate a dark house by the memory of furniture. The eyes are for the close work — the carving, the weaving, the reading of a companion's face. For the world, they use the ears.

---

The ears are the great organ. Large, mobile, set wide and slightly forward on the skull, and trained from infancy until hearing becomes a sense more like sight is to you — directional, detailed, *spatial*. A grown listener stands at the base of a trunk, raps it once with the flat of the hand, and reads in the returning sound the whole geography ahead: the open nave, the chamber closing with new growth, the dead column that will fall, the drip-line shifting a kilometer off, the soft moving hush that is a predator working its way down through the dark. They hear the shape of emptiness the way you see the shape of a room.

They speak this way too. Their language is half voice, half percussion — clicks and hummed tones that carry, that bounce off the columns and arrive changed, so that a single phrase tapped against a great trunk can be heard and understood by someone they will never see, a day's climb away across the loud dark. They have learned the acoustics of their world so thoroughly that they can throw their meaning around corners, can aim a message at a distant pillar and know it will ricochet to the platform of a friend. Gossip travels here at the speed of sound through wood. A death is tapped out and known across the chamber before the body has gone cold.

And they fall silent — all of them, all at once, instantly — when the *other* silence comes. The predator's hush. They have a reflex bred into them deeper than thought: when the constant noise of the world drops away in a moving pocket, they cease. They press flat to the bark and stop their breath and become, for as long as it takes, simply more wood. Children are taught the stillness before they are taught to climb. A child who cannot hold the silence does not grow old here.

---

They are few, and they live scattered, and so they have made a virtue and a faith of the scattering.

A family holds a trunk — not the whole impossible kilometers of it, but a stretch, a vertical territory, a few hundred meters of bark and buttress that they know intimately, every grip and water-channel and resonant flaw. They build their platforms in the angles where the great roots leave the column. They cut their cisterns into the burls and route the falling drip with grooves their grandparents began carving and their grandchildren will deepen. A trunk is a homeland. To be born on a trunk is to be a citizen of it, and the carvings up and down its flattest face are a kind of archive — depth-cut, water-stained, generations deep — recording who hung here, who fell, who climbed up and never came back down.

They marry across trunks, because they must, and a marriage is an enormous undertaking: a climber must descend their own column to the warm decaying floor, cross the empty nave — the most dangerous thing a person can do, the floor being the predators' country, the silence's hunting ground — and ascend a stranger's trunk to be received. Many do not survive the crossing. Those who do are honored their whole lives for it. *They crossed the floor* is the highest thing said of anyone. It means: they walked through the moving silence and the silence did not take them.

For the most part the trunks visit each other only in sound. A young man on his platform taps out his name to a young woman three columns over whom he has never seen and never will see clearly, and they court in rhythm, in the changed echoes that arrive across the dark, for months, for years, before either dares the floor.

---

They make everything, and they make it from the one material the world allows.

There is no metal. There is no stone. There is only the plant — and so over uncountable generations they have learned the plant the way a smith learns iron, every grain and fiber and sap of it. They cut blades from the hardened heartwood of fallen columns, edges honed against the silica in certain leaf-skins until they will part a tendon. They weave shelters and slings and the long climbing-ropes from stripped and twisted fiber, strong enough to hold a hanging body for a generation. They hollow vessels, dry leaf-skins into water-skins and document-skins, ferment the slow sap into something that warms the marrow against the cold. Their tools are beautiful because they have had nothing else to perfect. A water-hook passed down through a family is a worked thing, polished by a hundred years of hands, carved with the tapped-names of everyone who has held it.

They eat what the dark offers, which is little and must be worked for. They harvest the pale eyeless colonies from the wet runnels. They strain the falling condensation for what drifts down from the unreachable ecology of the canopy — spores, fragments, the rain of an upper world they will never climb to and can only imagine. The boldest hunt the gliding things, snaring them out of the air by sound alone, throwing a weighted net at a noise. And when a great climbing-beast dies or falls, it is a feast and a funeral both — weeks of meat, of hide, of bone-fiber for tools, the whole scattered community converging across the chambers in a way they otherwise never do, tapping their grief and their gratitude into the very trunk the beast had lived on.

---

And then there is the last thing — the asking, the thanks, the soft rhythm against the bark before sleep.

They know the world is alive. Not as you know it, having been told so by a parameter, but as the only fact their whole existence has ever rested on. They have felt the drip-lines shift toward them over hours. They have lain down to rest and felt the fiber-floor go warm beneath exactly where their bodies fell. They have looked up — those great near-blind eyes straining — and seen a shaft of weak green light arrive, just often enough, just where they walked, that no one among them has ever believed it accident.

So they leave the offerings at the bases of certain trunks: a portion of carried water, a small woven thing made beautiful for no use, a fragment of the day's meat. They do not pray in words, because words are too small a thing to throw at the size of what is listening. They tap. The asking-rhythm, soft, against the bark before sleep — *we are here, we are small, you know where we are, let it be a kindness that you know.* And the thanks-rhythm in the morning, if there is anything they will call morning, when they have not been taken in the night.

They do not pretend to understand the attention. Their elders are clear on this, and it is the one piece of wisdom every child receives unaltered: **the world is paying attention, and you cannot read it, and you must not mistake its kindness for love or its silence for cruelty.** It is too large to mean anything you could survive understanding. It moved the light to fall where you walked. It also grows the chamber shut over a sleeping family in a season, without malice, without notice, the way you would not notice closing your hand. It is not your friend. It is not your enemy. It is the inside of the thing you live within, and it knows where you are, and the only correct posture before it is the one their whole bodies have become: light, quiet, listening, ready to go still.

They tap their thanks into the groaning column, and the column groans back — flexing against its own vast weight, against the internal weather, against the screaming wind a kilometer up that they have never felt and never will. And they take the groan, as they have always taken it, as the closest the world comes to answering.

That is who lives in the Hollow Canopy. They are listening, even now. They heard you coming a long time ago. They have already gone still, and pressed themselves flat to the bark, and they are waiting — with that bred-deep patience, with those great useless eyes wide open in the dark — to learn whether you are the silence that moves, or only one more sound the trunks will learn to return.
