We are the ones who tap the pipes.

I should say that plainly, because the description you've been given is generous to us, and generosity blurs things. It calls us *the small warm-blooded things*, and that is true, but it is the kind of true that lets you imagine something more dignified than we are. So: we are small. We are warm in the way a coal is warm, banked, hoarding. We are the parts of the engine that have not yet been reabsorbed, and we know it, and that knowing is the only thing we have that the grazers don't.

Let me tell you what we are by telling you what we do, because here, doing and being have stopped being separable. The plant taught us that. You become what you do all day, and we drip-collect, so we are the drip-collectors.

---

We came in small. That part of the account is right — we moved in like mice into a wall, and the mouse-logic is still in our bodies. We are low to the ground, or low to the catwalk, with long gripping hands because everything here is vertical or about to become vertical, and a back that bends easily because the corridors close and you learn to fold. Our eyes are large and take the sourceless daylight gladly; we have no eyeshine, no need for it, because there is no dark here to peer into, only the long even mercy of the manufactured day. We do not blink much. The air is too dry to spend tears on.

Our skin has gone the way of everything here — waxen, a little. Not succulent, we never managed that, but sealed, lacquered, miserly. We do not sweat. We learned not to, the way you learn not to spend money you'll never see again. Our breath is the great betrayal: every exhalation is moisture leaving, water the world will not give back, and so we breathe shallow, and slow, and through cloth, and we have built our whole manner of speaking around losing as little as possible. We talk in short flat phrases, close to the listener, because the foam-rock drinks sound and you have to be near to be heard, and because long sentences cost breath, and breath costs water. The account noticed that voices carry *close and flat*. That flatness is us. That is the sound of people rationing themselves.

---

There are not many of us. I want to be honest about this without making it sad, though it is a little sad.

A district might hold a few dozen. A whole reach of the chamber, a few hundred. We are scattered the way everything here is scattered — sparse in the savanna way, thriving at our own remove, each settlement holding its circle of room around a good tap, a place where the sap-gas runs rich and the condensers can pull a real drip from it. The taps are everything. A settlement is just the people who share a tap. When the trunk closes its channel, when the plant reroutes its slow internal weather and a tap goes dry — and they do, on no schedule we can predict, because the plant does not consult us — then those people walk. They take the condensers off the wall and they walk through the dry haze to wherever the pressure-tides still favor, and sometimes they find a settlement that will take them and sometimes they become the people who didn't.

We do not fight over taps. I know that is what you expect, that thirst makes us cruel, and maybe it should. But the cool keeps us slow, the same as it keeps the grazers slow, and slow people do not fight well or often. We are sluggish, all of us, conserving, never quite warm. A fight is a great spending of heat and breath and water for an outcome you could have negotiated by sitting very still and waiting, which is the predator's trick, and we have all learned the predator's trick whether we wanted to or not. So we wait each other out. We outlast. The disputes here resolve the way the violence resolves — slowly, over an afternoon, like weather.

---

What do we believe?

I have to be careful here, because beliefs are the thing outsiders most want from a people, and we don't have the kind they want. We have no gods in the canopy. We hung lamps up there before we understood it would light itself, and that is the closest thing we have to a creation story: *we brought light to a place that didn't need it, and we were wrong, and the place lit itself anyway.* The lamps still hang. Nobody takes them down. They are dark now, most of them, dead bulbs in the manufactured day, and we keep them the way you keep a thing that proves you were once foolish in a way you can no longer afford to be.

But we have a feeling, and a feeling does the work of belief. The feeling is the one the place leaves everyone with. *Not yet finished.* We live inside it. We have built our whole sense of time around the fact that the engine is young, that the erosion is barely begun, that nothing here has yet failed and weathered into a softer second shape — and so we read the plant the way coastal people read the sea. We know which corridor is closing. We know the trunk-wall halfway to fused, and we mark it, and we teach the children: *don't den there, that's going to be solid in your lifetime.* We have a word — I won't give it to you, it loses everything in the carrying — for a place that is going to become wall. It is not a sad word. It is just a fact about the future, the way *winter* is a fact, and we plan around it.

The children. Yes. We do have those. Not many, and we space them, because a child is the most expensive thing there is in water — a child cries, and crying is water thrown into the air for nothing, and so our infants are swaddled close against a warm body and a damp cloth, breathing recycled breath, and they learn before they learn anything else not to spend themselves on grief. I don't know what that does to us, long term. I suspect it is part of why our affections are so flat and so deep at once. We love close and quiet, like we speak.

---

Now the hard thing. The thing the account circled and would not land on, and I will land on it, because I am one of the people it is happening to.

We are becoming plant. Not fast. Not yet. But the grazers fuse where they feed too long, the plant grows over their limbs and they do not pull free, and the categories here are not stable — and we are categories here. We are not exempt. The oldest among us, the ones who have sat longest at a single tap, breathing the sap-gas year on year, drinking the trace water condensed from the plant's own circulation — they change. Their skin goes from waxen to something closer to bark. They slow past even our slowness. They sit, and they sit, and one day they do not want to be moved from where they sit, and they say it is comfortable, that they can feel the pressure-tides now from the inside, the nine-hour breath, and we leave a cloth and a little water beside them and we come back and the cloth is part of the wall.

We do not bury our dead. There is nowhere to bury anything; the floor is not soil, it is the slow extruding body of the thing itself. We do not burn them — there is nothing to burn with, and we would not spend the air. We leave them against a growing wall, in a *going-to-be-solid* place, and the plant takes them in, and they become part of the substrate's slow circulation, fed by it now instead of feeding from it. We tell ourselves they have finished. The rest of us are *not yet finished*, and they are finished, and that is the only kind of completion this place offers anyone.

I have started to wonder whether the difference between us and the engulfed grazers in the wall is real or only a story we tell while we are still warm enough to tell it. The plant is the floor and the towers and the light and the engine. We tap its pipes and match our hours to its tides and breathe its breath and drink its water and, in the end, lie down against its growing edge. At what point in that sentence did we stop being separate from it. I do not know. I think nobody knows. I think that not-knowing is the most honest thing about us, more honest than the lamps, more honest than the word for a place that is going to become wall.

---

So. Who lives here.

Small folded people with miser's lungs and gripping hands and flat close voices, banked-warm in the cool, slow in the slowness, dripping out their lives one careful drop at a time. People who do not weep because they cannot afford the water. People who outlast rather than fight. People who keep dead lamps as monuments to having been wrong. People who read a vegetable engine the size of a valley the way you'd read weather, and plan their grandchildren's dens around where the corridors will close. People who lay their old against the wall and call it finished, and are not entirely sure they're lying.

We are the parts that haven't been reabsorbed yet.

That part stays with us, too.
