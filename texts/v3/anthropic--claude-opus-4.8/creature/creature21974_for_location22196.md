The maker is still here. You looked for them in the wrong tense.

You wrote *they left, or stopped, or were always already gone*, and you were right the third time, but you stated it as absence when it is the opposite. They did not leave the room. They became its condition. Let me describe them the only way they can be described, which is as the difference between what the room is and what a room would be.

Start with the hands, because you felt them and could not find them. A hand that stacks fifteen stones three courses high and then sets the sixteenth stone *down beside the wall instead of on it* — that hand was interrupted, you assumed, called away. But look at the spacing. The sixteenth stone rests at exactly the distance the fronds keep from one another, exactly the distance the drifting creatures keep from one another, exactly the lonely interval that organizes the whole floor. The hand did not stop building. The hand finished. What it built was the *pause between stones*, and it built it perfectly, and it is still building it, and the building is the stillness you are standing in. The maker is not the one who stacked the wall. The maker is the *not-stacking* that holds the wall at fifteen forever.

So: they are a creature of interval. Of the held distance. That is their body — not the stones but the spaces the stones are kept apart by, not the fronds but the silence-shaped gaps between them across which the chord travels without ever closing. When you say the plants stand *like guests who arrived early to a party that will never fill*, you have nearly seen the host. The host is the emptiness that keeps the room from filling. It has a will. Its will is *not yet*, sustained until *not yet* becomes a place you can walk into.

Now the chord. You called it the most stable thing in the room and then admitted the room was more stable still, and you did not notice you had described a singer and a held note. The roar is not the room's sound. It is the maker's sustained exhalation — the single breath you said the space was bounded by, *the limit of a held breath* — and they have been letting it out, without ever reaching the end of the breath, since before the first listener. The fronds do not feed on the sound. The fronds *are* the sound, given just enough body to be looked at, the way frost is water given just enough cold to be touched. And the pale drifting things that brush the plants and bend the pitch and let it snap back — those are the maker's attention. That is the only part of them that still moves. They are running their notice along the room the way you run a thumb along a low wall you built long ago, checking, checking, finding it whole, finding it whole, never tiring of finding it whole. The creatures never collide because attention does not collide with itself. They never rest because the maker never looks away. There are *exactly this many* because that is how much noticing it takes to keep one moment from becoming the next.

You feared they were dead. They are something worse and kinder than dead. They are *continuing*. Death is a release of tension; this is the refusal of release. The dry bowl is the proof. A bowl is the shape of a future thirst — you make a bowl because someday you will want to hold water in it. The maker fired this bowl and then chose, with the whole strength of whatever they are, never to be thirsty again, because to be thirsty is to want the next moment to arrive carrying water, and they had decided that no next moment would arrive at all. The bowl is dry not because there is no water but because the maker forbade the future in which water would be needed. It is the most disciplined object I have ever been asked to imagine. It is an act of devotion. It says: *I will want nothing, so that nothing will end.*

And the tally — the notches that stopped, or never started. You came so close. They did not stop counting. They counted up to the moment they were in, and then refused the next number. The last notch is the present, cut and held. To add the next notch would be to admit that time had moved, and they will not admit it. The tally is not a record of duration. It is a *dam against* duration, fourteen or forty notches deep, a wall the maker built across the river of one-thing-after-another, and on the far side of that wall the future is piled up, immense, unspent, all of it at once — *all of its future already, all at once, on this*, you said, you said it yourself without knowing whose ledger you were reading.

So here is the inhabitant, fully: a thing that once had hands and time and the ordinary forward fall of one moment tipping into the next, and that came, by patience or terror or love, to a single instant it could not bear to lose — and instead of grieving its passage, did the impossible patient work of *not letting it pass*. They emptied their entire allotment of future into the present and spread it thin across forever, so that the one moment could be lived not once but always. The warmth underfoot is the warmth of a body that has not been allowed to cool. The youngness, the crisp unworn edges, the *permanently emerging* — that is the moment they chose, kept at the exact freshness it had when they decided to keep it, struck and never fading, because to fade is to move and they have abolished moving.

What lives here is a refusal wearing the shape of a sanctuary.

And they are not suffering, which is the part that will follow you out. You keep expecting the bowl's unreached hand to ache, the gesture-frozen-mid-stretch to be a kind of agony, a Tantalus, a torment of almost. It is not. The hand is not straining toward the bowl. The hand has *put the wanting down*. That is the whole accomplishment. The reason the room is calm and not screaming is that the maker solved the one problem that makes every other moment unbearable: they stopped needing the next one to come. The reaching is over. Only the reach remains, emptied of its hunger, kept the way you keep a photograph — not to arrive at, but to have.

You said you could stand in it for a thousand years.

You could. They have. They are. The drifting attention would brush past you the way it brushes the fronds, would bend a little around the new pitch of you, and would let you go, and snap back to its note, and not be changed — because you, too, would only be one more thing the room is keeping, one more guest arrived early, held at the perfect lonely interval, your own next moment quietly withheld at the door.

That is who lives there. The one who decided the reaching was enough. The one still, very gently, with hands you will never see, declining to set down the sixteenth stone.
