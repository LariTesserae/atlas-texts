# The Tenders of the Long Stroke

They were workers once. You can read this everywhere, the same way you can read it in the streets and the gear-teeth — that there was a *before*, when they had shifts and quotas and somewhere else to be when the shift was done. That somewhere-else is gone now. So is the done.

## What They Are

Begin with the wrong assumption, because it's the natural one: you'd expect something has slipped in to occupy the place — scavengers, drifters, the patient opportunists that move into anything left open. But nothing immigrated here. The tenders are not visitors who arrived after the flooding. They are what stayed *through* it. They are the part of the crew that did not leave when leaving was still possible, and then could not leave, and then — this is the important part — stopped being the kind of thing that leaves.

They are roughly the size of a person, and roughly the shape of one, but only roughly, and only because that's the shape the work wanted. Where a thing's job is to reach into a gear-housing and clear it, the thing grows long-armed and many-jointed. Where a thing's job is to stand at a valve through ten thousand tides and turn it one quarter-degree per turning, the thing grows squat and rooted and patient, its lower half gone soft and anchored, fused at the ankle to the floor it serves. They have specialized the way a hand specializes into a callus. The body has been negotiated down to the task the way the right angles were negotiated down to curves.

You would not, at first, distinguish them from the gardens. This is the truest thing about them. They are upholstered too. The same dark-green and dark-red growth that sleeves the pipes has grown into them, through them, become them — so that a tender standing at its valve is mostly garden now, a column of soft pulsing growth with the old purposeful shape still discernible beneath, the way a chair under a dust-sheet is still a chair. The phosphor-green watch-face glow comes off them as much as off the walls. They are lit from within by the same dim life. When you look at one you are looking at a place where the vegetation has remembered, faintly, how to *intend*.

## What They Do

They work. Of course they work. The machines have not stopped, and the machines do not run themselves — not at this speed, not this gently. Somebody clears the gear-teeth so the plants that turn with the gears don't bind them. Somebody keeps the bellows-leather supple. Somebody stands where the great flywheel completes its minute-long revolution and, at the top of each turn, lays one hand against it — not to push, the wheel needs no push — but to *check*, to confirm, the way you might touch a sleeping animal's flank to feel it breathing.

But understand the pace. A tender will reach for a tool over the course of an hour. Will close its grip around it across a tide. The motion is so slow that if you watched one for the length of your own held breath you would swear it was as fixed as statuary, as fully arrived-at as the doorways and the eroded streets. Only if you came back — and you would have to come back much later, with the light gone redder, the foundry having tonked four or five times in the interval — would you find the arm had moved, the tool was now held, the work had advanced by one increment. One tooth. One tide.

They are not slow because they are dying. This is the thing the place teaches and they embody it most completely of anything here. They are slow because they have *won*. They have outlasted urgency. The whole horror of the original work — the shift that ends, the quota that must be met, the somewhere-else that's waiting — all of that has been eroded down to its angle of rest. What's left is the pure gesture of tending, freed from any reason it ever had to hurry.

## How They Regard You

They will not turn to look at you. Turning takes a tide, and you will not be here a tide. But you are not unnoticed. As you move through the warm dim channels you will pass close to one — at its valve, in its gear-housing, rooted at its post — and you will feel, against your skin, the faint warmth of it, a degree or two above the body-warm water, because they too are fed by the banked foundry, they too are kept just-barely-firing. And in that warmth there is something that is almost attention. Not curiosity — curiosity is fast, curiosity wants to *know now*. This is slower than that. This is the recognition a very old thing extends to a very brief one. *You are passing through. We are staying. We have noticed, and the noticing will complete itself sometime after you are gone, and that is alright.*

If you put your hand against one — against the soft pulsing growth where a shoulder used to insist on being a shoulder — you would feel the warmth, and under the warmth a vibration, the same felt-not-heard turning that runs through the whole town, and under *that*, if you were very still and very patient, the faintest counter-motion: the tender, over the next several minutes, beginning to turn its head toward your hand.

You would not be there to feel it arrive. That's the arrangement. By the time the gesture completed, you would be fathoms up, back in the pale ceiling of failing light, and the tender would find, at the end of its long slow turn, only the warm green water where you had been — and would accept this, without disappointment, because disappointment is also a kind of hurry, and they have put hurry down.

## The Last Thing to Understand

There is no foreman. There was, perhaps, once — but the role of *deciding* has eroded along with everything else, dissolved into the soft general consent of the place. No tender is told what to do. Each simply continues the gesture it was inside of when the world slowed, the way a held note continues, the way a flywheel coasts. They are not maintaining the foundry toward any product. Whatever it once made, it makes now only the warmth that keeps them tending it, in a loop so patient it has stopped being a loop and become simply a condition, like temperature, like depth.

They are the town's way of refusing to be finished. They are the part of *nothing ends, it only slows* that has hands.

And they are content — if that word still means anything at the speed they live. They have arrived, long before you, at exactly the version of themselves they meant to be: not workers anymore, not dead, but *tending* — turning one tooth of one gear per tide, warm and green and rooted and unhurried, for the length of forever, minus a little.
