# The Builders, Who Are Still Here

They did not leave. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because you came down expecting a tomb and found a roar, and the roar made you think *animals*, *only animals* — but listen longer. Stay through what would be a night if there were night. The grinding sigh of the dust, the click-storm of the flyers, the long groan of the grazers in the avenues — and threaded through all of it, so slow you mistook it for the breathing of the stone itself: a rhythm. A meter. Something that answers.

They are still here. They never built anywhere else because there was never anywhere else worth going. They are the reason the city aged into place instead of falling down — not because they maintain it, but because they *are* it now, the way a shell is the snail and the snail is the shell, no longer two things.

## What They Are

Begin with the plainest description and let it deepen.

They are not large. A grazer dwarfs them; the soft climbing things on the pillars could look one in the eye. They go on the ground, mostly, though they can take a wall or a slumped stair with the unhurried certainty of something that has never once been in a hurry. Their bodies are the same bone-ash, molar-gray as everything down here — not by paint or accident but by belonging, the color a thing takes when it has matched its surroundings for longer than color was a choice. Pale. Smooth-skinned in the way the doorways are smooth, the edges of them rounded off, the corners become shoulders. Even their hands — and they have hands, this matters — have lost the sharpness of nail and knuckle, worn to a soft and patient instrument, the tool that left no tool-marks because the tool was *them*.

Their eyes are large. Enormous, in fact, and dark, and they do not blink so much as slowly close and slowly open, a gesture you might wait a long minute to see completed. But the eyes are almost ceremonial now, almost vestigial in their use — kept the way the city keeps its worn arches, because removing them would be a kind of change, and change comes only on the scale of stone. They do not see by light. There is barely light to see by. They navigate the dim by the same thing that makes the place deafening:

**They hear the city.**

## How They Live Inside the Roar

This is the secret folded into the noise. You experienced the sound as a wall, a weather, an undifferentiated roar — because you are new, because your ears flatten it into a single overwhelming thing. To them it is *not* one thing. It is the most detailed surface in the world.

Every rounded pillar throws back a sound with its own grain. Every dome answers in its own register. The flyers' shrieking, piling echo on echo, is to them a constant living map — the shape of every space described and redescribed a thousand times a second by the returning cries of creatures they did not train and do not own but have simply *agreed with*, across uncountable generations, the way you might agree with the weather. They read the grazers' groans the way you read a horizon. They feel the scratch of the wall-colonies as a man feels the floor he stands on. The roar is not the obstacle to their perception. The roar *is* their perception. They have no concept of the silence you keep listening for, because to them silence would be blindness — would be the world suddenly gone.

When they speak to one another — and they do, constantly, though you have not been able to pick it out — they do not speak *over* the roar. They speak *into* it, low and slow, a contribution to the standing weather of sound, their meaning carried not in any single utterance but in the way it changes the whole, the way one more voice changes a choir without ever being heard alone. A conversation between two of them might take what you would call an hour and consist, to your ear, of nothing — of a barely-deeper note woven once into the million-throated drone. But it was a whole exchange. It was, perhaps, a greeting, an agreement, the settling of something that did not need settling fast.

## What They Do, Which Is Almost Nothing, Which Is Everything

You will not see them building, because the building is done. You will not see them tending the gardens, because the gardens tend themselves on the same mist that the people drink. You will not see them hunting, exactly, or farming, or making, or trading, or any of the busy verbs that a place full of motion ought to imply.

What they do is **continue.**

This is not idleness. Idleness is a thing you feel against the pressure of work undone, and there is no work undone here; there is nothing left to erode to, nowhere left to go, no final shape left to reach because the final shape was reached so long ago that the reaching of it is older than their oldest kept rhythm. They are not waiting for anything. They are not in decline. They are doing the one thing the place itself does, the deepest rule made flesh: they are being *full*, and being *finished*, and being *at peace with having ended already and kept living anyway.*

They move through the bare avenues among the herds, unhurried, neither herding nor herded. They sit at the rounded mouths of their dwellings — and you can tell their dwellings from the animals' wall-cracks only because theirs are arranged, faced toward one another in the soft worn plazas, the geometry of a society long since perfected and then simply inhabited. They tend a few things by hand: a fungal tower trained over generations into a particular curve, a hanging garden combed so the mist-fibers fall just so. Small slow gardenings of beauty, not of need. The aesthetics of a people with infinite time and no anxiety. A bloom cultivated across four lifetimes because there was no reason it should not be, and every reason it should.

## What They Remember

Here is the thing that will keep you awake — or would, if you could find the silence to lie down in.

They remember the water.

Not as fact. None of them was alive for it; the dryness is older than anything alive can hold. But they remember it the way the seed remembers the tree it will never become and the tree it came from — a held thing, encoded below knowing. It is in their slow ceremonial blinking, the eyes kept for a brightness that does not come. It is in the lowest register of their woven speech, a note that the acousticians among them — if you can call them that, the old ones who have listened longest — say is the note of *falling*, the sound a thing would make coming down from above, the memory of a sky that gave instead of merely roofing.

They do not mourn it. Mourning is fast; it has a crescendo and a falling-off, and nothing here crescendos. They simply *carry* it, the way the dry seed carries the wet promise it has learned to live without. It is the one thing in all this finished, settled, full and roaring world that is not yet complete — the only unworn edge left in a place where everything else has rounded to a shoulder.

And so they continue. Pale and patient and unhurried, eyed for a light long gone, voiced into a roar that is their only sky, drinking the mist that is all that remains of the rain, in the city they became and that became them, below the old plain that has nothing left to say —

— being full.

Being finished.

Going on anyway.
