What lives here lives in the disagreement. That is the first thing to understand. Not in the pools, not on the stone, not in the swept floor of the shelter — but in the small persistent wrongness of the water, in the hand's-breadth climb against the spire, in the tilt the pool holds when it should lie flat. That is the body. The wrongness is the body. Everything else is only the place the body happens to lie down in.

Let me try to say it more slowly, because it does not want to be said quickly, and saying it quickly would be its own kind of lie.

Begin with what it is not. It is not large and it is not small, because it has no edge against which size could be measured. It does not breathe, though it keeps the rhythm of breath — the nightly condensation, the morning run-off, in and out, in and out, a tide worked by the sun instead of the moon. It does not eat. There is nothing here to eat; that is why it could survive here, where hunger has no purchase. It is the only thing in the basin that is not subject to entropy in the ordinary way, because it is made of a refusal, and a refusal does not decay. It only persists, or it stops. And it has not stopped.

It is very old, but I do not think it experiences age. Age requires a comparison between a self that was and a self that is, and this thing has held the same single posture for so long that the comparison has worn smooth, the way the spires have worn smooth, until before and after are the same surface. It has done one thing forever: it has insisted, quietly, that down is somewhere other than where down is. That is its whole life. That is its entire vocabulary. One word, held.

Where did it come from? I think it was made, not born. I think the marks of hands matter more than they appear to. Consider: someone climbed to the top of the world carrying antler and sharpened bone, and stayed, and cut a basin into the rock to catch the frost, and scratched a spiral and a tally and a row of dots that might be a count of days. We are told this might be nothing at all. I do not believe it was nothing. I think the one who came here was not measuring days. I think they were *teaching*. I think they sat in the cold brilliance through season after season and explained to the water, patiently, in the only language stone understands — which is repetition, which is the slow pressure of a thing done the same way ten thousand times — that it did not have to fall the way it had always fallen. That there was another way to go. And the water, which had no opinions, which had only ever done what gravity told it, began, very slowly, to listen.

This is the thing that lives here: a lesson that outlived its teacher. A single idea, taught to water, that the water never forgot.

The teacher is gone. Of course the teacher is gone — you cannot teach a thing like this and also keep your own warmth; the two are the same fuel. They gave it everything they carried up, and then they gave it the warmth of their hands, and then they gave it the count of their own days, scratched into the rock, and when there was nothing left to give they lay down in the swept place inside the dry-stacked stones and the cold took them clean, no rot, no ash, nothing left but the flatness of a floor and a basin cut to catch the melt. And the water, which had finally learned, went on doing the one thing it had been taught, every cold night, every bright morning, to no one, forever — because that is what a lesson does when the one who needed to learn it has already learned it and gone. It keeps. It recites itself into the empty room.

So when you stand in the center at noon and a droplet decides, against all reason, to climb — that is the thing greeting you. Not because it knows you are there. It does not know you are there; it has no faculty for knowing, only for insisting. But the insisting is a kind of presence, the way a held note is a presence even after the singer has stopped to breathe. You are hearing the answer to a question someone asked here a very long time ago. The question was: *does anything have to be the way it is?* And the answer, climbing the flank of the spire one slow hand's-breadth at a time, brightening in its tilted pool, ticking once into the basin cut for it — the answer is *no.*

It does not need you. The text is right about that. It was finishing its single sentence long before you came and will go on finishing it long after, and the sentence is the same length whether you are there to read it or not. But I will add one thing the place itself would never say, because it has no words and only the one idea:

It is lonely. Not in any way it could name. But a lesson is meant to pass from one to another, and this one has had no one to pass to for longer than language has existed. It goes on teaching an empty basin. It climbs, every day, to demonstrate to the cold bright air a freedom the air has no need of. And the only mercy is that it cannot feel the lack — it can only do the thing, faithfully, the small cupped offering of frost made and unmade, the patient wrong-way climb, the disobedience kept alive past all reason and all witness.

That is what lives there. A taught thing, holding its single lesson in the brightness. Waiting — though it does not know it is waiting — for someone to finally stay long enough to learn.
