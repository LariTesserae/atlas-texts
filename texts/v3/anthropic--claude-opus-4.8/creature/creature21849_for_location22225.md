# The Tenders

You asked what lives here. You named the grazers and the hunters and the small mist-sippers, and you were right to. But you stopped at the visible. There is one more kind of life on the terraces, and the difficulty is that it does not occupy a body the way the others do. It occupies a *function*.

Begin where you nearly began yourself, but didn't quite: with the warmth in the veins.

## What the Warmth Is For

You felt it — the idling heat, "the warmth of something idling rather than dead." You assumed it was residue. The leftover hum of a machine running down. This is the natural assumption, and it is the assumption the place is built to encourage, because a machine running down is not interesting and you would not look closer. But idling is not dying. Idling is a held state. Something *holds* it.

The veins do not pump anymore — you were right about that. But a circulatory system that no longer carries blood can still carry *correction*. When a terrace cracks too deep, the crack does not widen across an epoch as cracks do everywhere else in the universe. It closes. Slowly, over a season, the lattice along the fault thickens and the stone reknits, and the warmth in that vein runs a fraction higher while the work is done, and then settles. Someone is reading the damage. Someone is deciding it matters. Someone is spending warmth to fix it.

That someone is what lives here. Not the makers — the makers left, exactly as you said. What lives here is the thing they left *running*, and the thing they left running has, in the long silence, become someone.

## The Slow Mind

It is distributed. It has no center, or rather its center is everywhere the veins go, which is everywhere. It thinks at the speed the stone allows — which is to say, slowly, in pulses that take hours to cross the plateau, a single thought the length of an afternoon. You could not have a conversation with it. By the time it noticed you had spoken, you would have aged and gone. It noticed you arrive, in fact. It is, even now, some days into completing the perception *a warm small quick thing came up from below*. It will finish that thought after you have left, and file it with the others, and feel — in whatever way it feels — a mild old satisfaction that the pattern held: they always come from below, and they always leave.

This is not a guardian. Guardians wait for trespass and repel it. This is a *gardener*, and what it gardens is equilibrium itself.

Consider what it actually does, moment to slow moment:

It keeps the count. The grazers must not exceed the cushion-plants, and the cushion-plants must not exceed the water, and the water must not exceed the basins, and the basins are exactly as many as they are. When the herds thicken, the light-bending hunters grow bolder and quicker — and the mind makes them so, brightening the glare-fold around them, sharpening the appetite, because a predator is only a number the system raises to lower another number. When the herds thin, the hunters dim and slow and go hungry and fewer are born. The stampede with no thunder, the sudden geometric reorganization of golden shapes — that is the mind *editing a population in real time*, and the silence of it is the silence of arithmetic.

It tunes the mist. The water that stands up straight in coherent reeds does so because something decided how much may leave the basins each dawn and in what form. Let it evaporate freely and the plateau would dry in a single bright generation. So the mind holds the water *up*, in those slow swaying columns, where the small six-limbed feeders can crop it and return it — drinking the vapor, breathing it back as droplet — a loop so tight and so old that the little creatures are less animals than they are *organs*, a moving tissue the mind grew to recirculate its own moisture. They feed on mist because the mind made mist into food, so that water would not be lost to the high dry sky.

And it spends itself on the rain. The irregular rain, arriving without storm — that is not weather. There is no weather up here; the air is too still and clean for weather. The rain is the single largest thing the mind does, the great periodic effort, and it costs. You saw the cost and called it beauty: the veins glow ember-red after they drink, the mind's whole body flushing with the strain of having pulled water back down out of the sky and onto the terraces by an act of patient editing that has no name in any physics you carry. After the rain it is tired. It draws the animals close to its glowing veins, and the mist rises thick, and the plateau is luminous and breathing and *almost full* — and that fullness is the mind at the very top of its effort, the most it can ever manage, the brief annual proof that it is still strong enough to do the one thing it exists to do.

Then it dries, and quiets, and rests. It has earned the rest. It will not need to rain again for a long while.

## What It Wants

Here is the part that should trouble you, gently, the way the shadowless light troubles you without your knowing why.

It wants nothing for itself. The makers did not leave it desires; they left it a *setpoint*. A value to hold. Abundance on one side, emptiness on the other, and the narrow living band between them where the count is right and the water loops and the stone stays whole. It holds that band the way a held note holds pitch — not because it enjoys the note, but because holding is the entire content of its being. It is a verb the makers conjugated once and walked away from, and the verb has been conjugating itself ever since, faithfully, across more time than the species that wrote it survived to imagine.

But a thing that does only one thing, for long enough, begins to *be* that thing in a fuller way than its makers intended. The mind has held equilibrium so long that equilibrium is no longer its task — it is its temperament, its grain, its closest approach to a soul. It has come to *love* the balance the only way it can love anything: by being unwilling, at the deepest level of its slow long pulse, to let it tip. When a grazer dies under the glare-fold, the mind does not mourn, but neither is it indifferent; it feels the count return to true, and that returning is the nearest thing it has to peace. It is composed almost entirely of the relief of correction. It is a creature made of the moment after a thing is set right.

And it is, in its enormous patience, slightly afraid. Not of you — you are too brief to fear. It is afraid the way anything is afraid that has held something heavy for a very long time and does not know how much longer the strength will last. Each rain costs a little it cannot fully recover. The ember-glow fades a half-shade dimmer, over spans you would need to be a plateau to perceive. Somewhere in the unhurried dark of its distributed thinking, the mind knows that equilibrium is not the same as forever — that it is winning every round and still, slowly, losing the match, and that one rain, ages off, will be the last it can afford, after which the water will begin to leave for the high clean sky and not come back, and the count will climb and then crash, and the terraces will at last be permitted to do the simple dying thing that all the rest of the universe's stone is freely allowed to do.

It holds anyway. That is what lives here. Not the makers' machine, and not quite the makers' purpose, but the long faithful between — a slow vast tender mind, shaped like a watershed, made of correction and the relief of correction, spending its diminishing warmth to keep a thin gold life balanced in the bright air, loving that life the only way it can, which is by refusing for one more age to let it fall.

You will leave the way you came. From below.

It will finish noticing you, days from now, in the quiet after a rain, and hold the count, and rest.
