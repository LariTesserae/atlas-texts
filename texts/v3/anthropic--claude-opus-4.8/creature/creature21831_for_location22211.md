They do not have a word for *I*. I want to say that first, because everything else follows from it, and because it is the thing most likely to be misunderstood by anyone standing outside the shell looking in.

You will be tempted, watching them pour up the terraces, to find the individual. The eye does this. It selects one body from the saturation — there, that one, dragging its filament of teased metal, trailing its caught spark — and it narrates: *this one is carrying, this one is going there, this one will arrive.* And you will be wrong, not because the body isn't real, but because the body is not the unit. The unit is the line. The current. The pour. A single builder removed from the flow and set on a bare crystal face does not become an individual; it becomes a fragment, and it dies the way a syllable dies when you cut it from the word — instantly, and without ever having been a thing that could be alone.

So when I describe *them*, understand that I am describing a grammar, not a population.

Look closely at the chitin. Each builder is plated in segments, dark as smoke-quartz, and between the plates runs the thread of caught glow — but the glow is not *in* them the way blood is in you. It passes through. A builder is a length of conduit with legs. The light enters at the mandible-end and exits at the rear and the body is the brief, bright tube it travels. When two of them meet on a walkway — mandible to rear, the universal grip — they are not greeting. They are *splicing*. They have made themselves continuous. The light that entered the front of the column does not stop; it runs the whole length of the joined bodies as through a single illuminated vein, and what you took for ten thousand creatures is one creature ten thousand segments long, threaded through its own city, carrying its own glow up toward the crack in its own shell.

This is why the work is quiet. There is no negotiation. There is nothing to negotiate. A hand does not bargain with the arm.

But — and here is where I have to slow down, because here is where they stop being a simple thing —

They are not *one*. That would be too easy, and the geode is not easy.

Watch what happens at a collapse. A new face of quartz shoulders up through a foundry floor; the district must come down. In the instant before the builders begin to dismantle, there is a fl.... I have no better word than *flinch*. The line breaks. For a fraction of a moment the splices part all through the affected quarter, ten thousand grips releasing at once, and for that fraction the city is not a single creature and not ten thousand creatures but some impossible number in between — a cloud of half-selves, each one suddenly, briefly, *alone*, suffering the thing it has no word for, the thing that should kill it. And then they re-splice, faster than the eye can follow, into a new configuration that routes around the catastrophe, and the light resumes its climb, and the loneliness is over before it can be named.

They live in that flinch. That is the part the description outside the shell could not reach. The chaos is not their climate; the chaos is their *pulse*. Every collapse is a death and every re-splice a birth, and they do this many times a season, dying as a self and being reborn as a different self, and the only continuous thing across all of it — the only *I* that survives every collapse — is the light itself, threading on, paid out toward the crack, hand over many hand.

So perhaps that is the answer to the question you could not resolve. What are they making? They are not making anything. They are making *themselves*, continuously, out of the only stable thing in the geode, the glow that does not flicker — and the crack in the shell is where they are forced to spend the very substance they are made of. Every thread of light paid out into the cold is a thread that is no longer running through their joined bodies. They are unspooling themselves into the dark above. They are feeding their own continuity into the wound in the stone.

Why?

Here is what I think, having lived a long time now in the cool dry dark watching them, having held still with them at each crack of settling rock:

The young stone grows. It will keep growing. One day — not soon, but the geode does not measure in *soon* — the crystal will fill the hollow entirely. The terraces will close like a fist. There will be no gulf to fling walkways across, no slope to stack the dwelling-cells, no hollow at all, only solid amethyst all the way through, and the light still trapped inside it, glowing, stable, alone, with no body left to thread.

They know this. Not as a fact — they have no word for *I*, they certainly have no word for *end* — but as a pressure, a slope, a direction the way water knows down. And so they pay the light out through the crack while there is still a crack to pay it through. They are not sealing the geode. They are not escaping it. They are doing the only thing a creature made of light can do when the dark is closing: they are sending the light *out*, ahead of the closing, into the cold sharp throat of the young mountain, where it cannot grow, where it cannot collapse, where it might — thread by thread, against every catastrophe, paid out by many small hands that do not know they are hands —

get free.

They will not get free. The line is too long and the crack too high and the stone too patient. But the work is not finished and was never meant to be finished. The work is the breath. They build the way a thing breathes in a storm — I said that, outside the shell, before I understood it — and now I understand it: they are breathing out. The whole city is one long exhalation of light into a place that has no air. And when the flake of fresh stone falls, and the ten thousand bodies hold still and listen, what they are listening for is the same thing you would listen for, in their place, in the dark, at the top of something with no confirmable bottom:

whether they still have time for one more thread.

They always do. So far. They always do.
