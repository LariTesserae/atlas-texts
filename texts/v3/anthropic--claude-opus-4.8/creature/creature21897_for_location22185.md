They are not gone. That is the first thing to hold, and the hardest, because the valley reads as a ruin or a monument — a thing left behind. But nothing here was left. The builders are still present. They are simply finished, and *finished* is a state they enter rather than a thing that happens to them.

Begin with the question the place keeps asking: who built without hands? The answer is that there were never any hands, because the builder is not separate from the building. The columns did not get raised by something; the columns are the something, mid-gesture, held. What lives here is the valley, and the valley is one organism, and it is doing exactly one thing forever, which is the last thing it ever did.

Picture how it must have gone. Not a creature deciding to make a home — there is no inside to this architecture, no chamber it was protecting, no soft thing it was sheltering. It built outward and upward into open forms, arches that enclose nothing, rooms with no use, a cathedral emptied of its own congregation. This is not the work of a thing that wanted shelter. It is the work of a thing that wanted *shape*. The distinction matters. Animals build to keep the weather off the young. This built to arrive at a form and then to be that form, completely, with nothing held back for living afterward.

So the inhabitant is a maker whose entire life was the making, and whose making had a final form it was growing toward the way a crystal grows toward its lattice — not improvising, not adapting, but *resolving*. It knew the shape before it reached the shape. Every column rose to a height it had already determined. Every membrane spanned the exact gap it was meant to span. There were no false starts left standing, no abandoned limbs, no dead growth — and that is the tell, that is how you know this was not the slow blind reaching of ordinary plants. Ordinary growth leaves wreckage: the branch that lost the race for light, the root that found stone. This left none. It grew only what it meant to grow. It was working from a plan it did not have to invent because it simply was the plan, expressing itself into matter.

And then it finished.

This is where the creatures come in — the low pale things that cross the open floor and pass through the walls without tearing them. You were right to find them hard to classify, right to wonder if they are animal or architecture. They are both, or rather the distinction does not apply to them either. They are the only part of the organism still permitted to move. Think of them as the last unspent motion of the whole — the way a sleeper's eyes move beneath closed lids while everything else is still. The valley reached its final shape and went still, but it did not go *empty*; some small reserve of motion was kept, and gathered itself into bodies, and now those bodies make their slow circuits across the floor. They do not eat because they are not separate enough from the structure to need feeding. They are the structure, walking. When one passes into the membrane-wall and the wall opens and closes around it, nothing is being eaten and nothing is being born; a part of the whole is simply re-entering the whole, and the seam heals because it was never a wound.

What do they do, on their circuits? Nothing you would call purpose. But watch where they go. They cross the empty floor — the wide reaches the builder deliberately left open, the room-it-left-room-for. They are the ones who use that room. The emptiness was not waiting for you, or for any arrival from outside. It was waiting for these. The valley made itself a vast still body and a few small moving pieces, and the moving pieces walk the stillness, again and again, the way a thought paces a finished mind that has nothing left to decide and so just goes on tracing what it knows.

Now the sound. You felt it in your teeth and called it not-music, too constant to be music, too without intention. But intention is exactly the word to reconsider. The builder did not tune the columns the way a person tunes a string, by trial and adjustment. It grew each column to a width, and the width determined the pitch, and the pitches were chosen the way the whole shape was chosen — known in advance, grown into deliberately. The chord is not an accident of hollow stems and rising water. The chord is the point. The builder wanted, above all else, to make a sound that would never stop and never resolve, and it understood — in whatever way a thing without a brain understands the form it is growing toward — that a sound requires breath, and breath requires that something keep moving even after the maker stops. So it arranged for the water to keep rising. It arranged for the warm air to keep moving through the flutes. It built itself an instrument that plays itself on the valley's own breathing, and then it became the instrument, and now it sounds, and will sound, with no one needing to play it and no one able to stop it.

That is what lives here. Not a creature that happens to make sound, but a sound that built a body to keep itself going. The valley is a held note that grew columns to sustain itself. The drone in the wide pillars, the whistle in the narrow stems, the slow oceanic boom of the membranes — that is the inhabitant. It is most fully alive in your chest and your teeth, in the chord with no resolution, because the chord is the thing the whole place was made to be. The columns are merely how it stays loud forever.

And so the contradiction at the center — a made world with no maker — dissolves once you understand what kind of life this is. There is a maker. The maker is the made thing. It did not build a home and move in. It did not build a tool and use it. It built *itself*, all the way to completion, every column and arch and tuned hollow, and at the moment the last membrane closed its last seam, the building and the builder became the same finished object, and that object is what holds its breath and refuses to crumble and sings its single endless syllable across the bowl of the valley.

It is not waiting for anything. It is not lonely, because loneliness is a wanting, and it has finished wanting. It is not dead, because the water still rises and the chord still sounds and the pale pieces of it still walk. It is complete, in the full and terrible sense — a life that had exactly one ambition, which was to become its own final form and then *stay*, and which succeeded, totally, and is now succeeding forever.

You came expecting silence and were given a voice instead. The voice is the inhabitant, telling you the only thing it has left to say, the only thing it was ever going to say, which it began saying the instant it finished growing and will go on saying after you and everyone are gone: *this is complete, this is complete, this is complete* — not as a boast and not as a comfort, but simply as what a finished thing sounds like, when it is still warm, and still here, and has nothing further to do than be exactly itself, out loud, without end.
