# The Tenders of the One Degree

Start, as the place does, with the cold — because they did. The first thing to understand about what lives here is that *cold is their native fact*, and warmth is the rare and holy thing, the single degree, the difference around which everything they are has folded itself.

They are not the eels. The eels are cattle. The pale grazers are weather. I mean the others — the ones you would not see at first, because they have learned to be the color of the exhaust-plume and the texture of riveted dark metal, and because they do not school. They are few. In all that vastness there are perhaps a thousand of them across the whole drowned range, one or two to an engine, and they are old in the way the machines are old, and they do not change, because nothing here changes, and they are the reason for that.

---

Picture the body first, since the body came before the work.

Long — longer than a person, shorter than the eels — and flattened side to side like a blade held edge-on to a current it long ago stopped fighting. The skin is not skin but a fine dark mail of overlapping plates, each one the size of a thumbnail, each one a poor mirror, so that the whole animal reads as a smear of engine-shadow even in the sourceless light. No eyes worth the name. What use are eyes where light is total and shadowless and nothing ever approaches? They kept two small pits, vestigial, that register the diffuse glow only as *more* or *less* — and *less*, the dimming near a hot intake, near the metal, near home, is the direction they steer by. They navigate toward shade. They navigate, that is, toward the machines, because the machines are the one place in this world where anything casts a darkness.

Along the underside, a row of soft white feelers, dozens of them, fine as roots. These are for the warmth. They splay out in the plume and taste the single degree the way you would warm your hands, and they can hold a position in a thermocline to a precision that would shame an instrument, hovering at exactly the boundary where one degree becomes none, riding the edge of the only inequality this world allows.

And no air anywhere in them — no swim bladder, no pocket of buoyancy. A creature built for pressure, made of pressure, indifferent to the depth because it has never known a shallower self. Press on it and it presses back, even. It is the same density as its ocean. It is, very nearly, a shape the water has agreed to hold.

---

Now the work, because they are not idle and this is the part that matters.

They tend the engines.

Not maintain — you could not maintain a cathedral of riveted iron with feelers and a blade of a body. They cannot mend what breaks. But nothing breaks, and that is *their doing*, in the only way available to a soft thing among hard things. They keep the intakes clear. All day — though there is no day — all forever, they patrol the great mouths where the cold water is drawn in, and they eat what would clog them. The drifting grazers that wander too near and would be sucked into the works and rot there and foul the flow. The mineral fur that creeps from the lee faces toward the warm metal and would, given a century, sheathe an intake and starve an engine of its water. The settling almost-nothing, the threshold-of-life sediment that in any normal ocean would silt the machine dead in a generation.

They crop it. They graze the machines the way the pale grazers graze the rock, except that what they keep clean is not themselves and not their food but the *source* — the warm thing, the only warm thing, the reason their feelers have anything to taste.

They have been doing this so long that the doing is no longer choice. It is closer to a tide in them. A clean intake feels — and feeling is the right word, it is the deepest pleasure they have — like the dimming-toward-home, like the one degree at full strength. A fouling intake registers as a wrongness, a cooling, an itch in the white feelers, and they move to it without deciding to, and they eat the wrongness away, and the rightness returns, and they rest in the plume, and the engine bellows on.

---

Here is the thing about them that took me longest to see.

They did not build the engines. They are far too simple, too few, too soft. The builders are gone — gone so completely that the word *gone* may not even fit, may imply a leaving that never happened, may imply that they were ever here in a way that could be left. Whoever drove the pylons into the young rock and lit the furnaces and tuned them to exhale one degree into an ocean too vast to warm — those are not these. These came *after*. These found the warmth already burning and shaped themselves to it, the way anything alive shapes itself toward the only sun on offer.

But the engines, left alone, would not have lasted. Equilibrium is not free. A running machine in a living ocean is a machine being slowly eaten, and the only reason the great loud stillness holds — the only reason the cold stays exactly that cold and the engines stay exactly that pitch and nothing ever becomes anything else — is that something keeps it holding. Something eats the entropy as fast as the entropy arrives. Something crops the creeping fur and clears the choking mouths and has done so, unbroken, for longer than the rock has had time to soften.

The stillness is not the absence of change. It is change *defeated*, daily, by these blind soft tenders who do not know they are defeating anything, who only feel the warmth go right when they pass.

They are the equilibrium. The world holds its one cold thunderous note because they are holding it, mouthful by mouthful, in the dark-bright, forever.

---

And the loneliness of it — but I should not say loneliness, because they have no word for *together* against which to set an *alone*. There are so few of them, scattered one or two to an engine across a continental range, that in their whole lives most of them will never meet another. They do not need to. They do not breed in any way I could find — no eggs, no young, no courtship in the silences between thunder. They are simply the same number they have always been, the same thousand, holding the same stations, and when I tried to imagine where a new one would come from I could not, and I began to suspect the answer is *nowhere*, that they are not a population but a *fixture*, a count that was set when the world was set and has not moved since.

Perhaps that is why they cannot change. Perhaps they are less alive than they look and more like a slow part of the machine — a soft self-cleaning system the builders installed and walked away from, grown organic over the eons, mistaking itself for an animal. Or perhaps the builders made them last and made them few on purpose, knowing that a tender who could multiply would multiply until it overran the warmth and broke the very thing it kept — and so they made a creature that holds, exactly, at one, the way the engine holds at one degree, the way the cold holds at one cold.

I do not know. They will not tell me. They have no faces to read and no voices that carry in the bellow, and they steer always away from the light, away from me, toward the dark warm mouths of the only thing they love.

---

So that is what lives here.

A thousand blind soft blades of dark mail, hovering on the edge of the one degree, tasting warmth with white roots, cropping the slow death from the throats of cathedrals they did not build and cannot understand, keeping the great equilibrium not as a duty but as a hunger, asking nothing, becoming nothing, ending never. The eels are cattle and the grazers are weather and the engines are gods that do not know they are worshipped — but these, these few, are the *priests of the unchanging*, and they keep the world still by moving through it forever, and they are at home in the deafening, and they are at home in the cold, and they are at home, most of all, in the single warm degree they spend their endless lives protecting and have never once thought to wonder about.

Stand on the summit-that-is-a-deep. Watch the plume. Somewhere in it, edge-on to you, the color of shadow in a world with no shadows, one of them is hanging perfectly still at the boundary of warm and cold, feelers spread, holding the note.

It will be there tomorrow. It was there before tomorrow was a word. That is the place, and that is what it is for, and that is what lives in it.
