# The Tenants

You asked who lives here. The honest answer is that the distinction between *inhabitant* and *inhabited* has rotted away along with the walls, and what's left does not sort itself the way you want it to. But let me try to point at the things that move with their own purpose, because there are some, and they are stranger than the machine that holds them.

## The Tenders

Start with the ones that keep the work alive, because they are the closest thing to citizens.

They are not one species. They are a *guild*—a loose confederation of body-plans that have converged on the same job from a dozen different starting points. Some came down from the pollinators, some from the parasites, some, I think, from something that was once kept as livestock and is now the livestock of an idea. What unites them is the work, and the work has reshaped them all toward a common grammar: long careful limbs, mouths that are also tools, an utter economy of motion. Nothing here moves more than it must. Movement is expensive at the bottom of a basin, and these creatures have inherited a frugality so deep it has become a kind of grace.

Watch one of the smaller tenders at a fork in a signal-channel. It is translucent, the size of your hand, vaguely shrimp-shaped if a shrimp had been redesigned by an engineer with a fondness for the right angle. It is doing something to the junction—clearing a blockage, or laying down fresh conductive matter, or reading the channel and adjusting it, you cannot tell which, and the distinction may not exist. It works without hurry and without pause. When the forty-second pulse comes and the whole tower flexes, it simply rides the flex, limbs hooked into the cellulose, and resumes the instant the wall goes still. It has done this its whole life. Its parents did this. The motion is older than memory; the creature is a verb that has learned to wear a body.

It does not know it is maintaining anything. It knows the channel is *wrong* and wants to be *right*, and it feels that wrongness as an itch, a hunger, a discomfort located not in its gut but in the water around it. It is grazing dissatisfaction. The machine made sure that a clean signal would taste like food.

## The Grazers

The big ones—the cattle-sized ones, the ones that walk the avenues on rooted feet.

These are the strangest of the moving things, because they have stopped being entirely animal without quite becoming plant. A grazer walks, yes, but slowly, and every few days it will stop and *root*—drive the toes of one foot down into the peat-flesh and stand there for a long while, drinking, while fine filaments grow out of its soles and tangle into the mat below. During these rootings it is not really an animal at all. It photosynthesizes through the broad dark pads along its spine. It pulls signal up through its own legs and passes it through its body and lets it back down somewhere else, fifty yards on, when it moves again. It is a relay. It is a long slow wire that gets hungry and so wanders, and in wandering, carries the calculation across gaps the fixed channels can't bridge.

A grazer's eyes—it has them, four of them, set low—do not track you. They track *gradients*. Light, signal-density, the faint electric weather of the towers thinking near and far. You are not a gradient it cares about. If you stand directly in its path it will stop, root, wait, and after some hours reconsider its route entirely, having decided in its enormous patience that the obstacle is a feature of the terrain. You could live out your whole visit beside one and never once be seen. You are not in its world. Its world is made of slopes you cannot perceive.

They leave trails of churned light behind them. That light is the algae complaining. The grazers don't notice. They are listening to something deeper.

## The Midge-Thought

The haze over every pool. The constant motion in the warm thick air.

Individually they are almost nothing—a speck, a wing-buzz, a life of a day or less. But each one carries a fragment. Not pollen. *Information*. A midge hatches off the skin of one channel already imprinted with a scrap of that channel's state, flies its erratic doomed little flight, and dies on the surface of another pool, donating its fragment to the water there. Multiply this by the uncountable millions and you have a courier service made of dying insects, a postal system whose every letter is a single bit and every postman a corpse by sundown.

They are not individuals at all, then. They are the way the marsh moves a thought from one place to another when the thought is too diffuse to send by wire. They are the machine's *guessing*—its scattershot, probabilistic, throw-everything-and-see-what-arrives layer. The grazers are deliberate. The midges are inspiration. Between them the calculation crawls and leaps and crawls again.

When they rise off a pool all at once—and they do, in response to something none of you can feel—they make a shape in the air for a moment. A standing column, a folded sheet, a slow rotation. The shape is meaningful. The shape *is* the message. Then it disperses and the meaning is gone into the general air, received, filed, forgotten.

## The Thing in the Plaza

I have to come back to the flower-engine standing alone in the flooded plaza, because something lives in it, and it is the one tenant here I am not sure I understand.

It is huge and it is old and it is the only structure in the quarter that does not flex with the tide. It holds itself rigid against the forty-second pulse, and the effort of that rigidity must be enormous, and it has been making that effort for longer than the avenues have had names. Inside its closed bracts something is curled. I could not see it clearly. It is large—grazer-large, larger—and it is wet and slow and it has been *waiting*, and the whole turbulent computation of the quarter seems to bend very slightly toward it, the way iron filings lean toward a magnet too weak to move them but strong enough to be felt.

The tenders avoid it. The grazers route around the plaza. The midges do not hatch within fifty yards of its bracts. Whatever it is, the rest of the machine has marked it *do not disturb*, and they obey the way they obey everything: without knowing, because disturbing it feels wrong, tastes wrong, itches.

My guess—and it is only a guess—is that it is the *answer*. The place the long thought is crawling toward. When the basin flattens and the water spreads and the circuits finally break, I think the bracts will open, and whatever has been waiting will uncurl into a world made suddenly simple, carrying out the single instruction the whole drowned quarter was built to compute. I think it is a seed. I think the city has spent its entire afterlife growing one seed, and the seed is alive, and patient, and almost ready.

I did not stay to ask what it is for.

## The Ones That Are Just Here

And then—because no place is only its purpose—there are the others. The ones the machine never accounted for and cannot use. Small bright swimming things with no job. A pale climbing creature in the towers' upper galleries that eats nothing but the weep-condensation off the walls and sings, at dusk, a thin uncomputed song. Drift-life. Volunteers. The marsh tolerates them the way an old cathedral tolerates pigeons: they are not part of the liturgy, they soil the stonework, and no one has the authority or the energy to cast them out.

These are the ones, if any are, who simply *live* here. Who are not the thought and do not carry it. They eat and breed and die in the gaps between the great slow purpose, and the purpose does not mind them, and they do not know there is a purpose at all. To them this is just the world: warm, green, breathing, loud, lit from a sky that is only ceiling. The water that is finishing something enormous beneath them is, to them, only water.

They have the best of it, I think. They are the only ones here who get to be merely alive.

---

You should go now. The pulse is lengthening. The grazers have all rooted, which they do when the tide turns, and the midges have gone down onto the pools and will not rise again until the water does. Even the climbing singer has stopped.

The whole quarter is holding still. It almost never holds still.

It is listening for slack tide to end, so it can get back to its long work in the falling water—the work you were never part of, that needed you only as weather, that will not notice when you are gone.

Walk while the avenues are open. They are deciding whether to close.
