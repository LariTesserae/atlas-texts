They are not the bats, though you would be forgiven for thinking the bats are the whole of it. The bats are the loud thing, the obvious tenant, the warmth-givers — but they are renewers, comers and goers, pouring out and pouring back, stitched to the sky by hunger. They belong half to the outside world. The ones I mean to describe never leave. They have not left for longer than the bats have been pale.

Begin with the floor, because that is where they begin.

In the deep midden, beneath the rain of leavings, there is a thing that is not quite one body and not quite many. Call it the slow one, because it has no name and would not answer to one. It is most easily found by what it is not: not beetle, though beetles are part of it now; not fungus, though fungus threads it through; not the worm-things that ripple and segment, though those move within its reach and are sometimes drawn up into it and sometimes left alone, by logics that took ten thousand years to settle and will not be hurried for your understanding.

It is soft. Everything here is soft — the rock has gone to curve, the floor to richness — and the slow one is softest of all, a pale spread mass that lies through the midden the way roots lie through soil, more network than creature, more weather than animal. Where it thickens it can be the size of a sleeping dog. Where it thins it is only a paleness in the dark earth, a faint cool glow if you knew to look, for it is kin to the glowing fungus and shares their cold blue-green, dimmed almost to nothing, banked like the last of a fire.

It does not hunt. Hunting is fast, and fast belongs to the bats. It waits. It has made waiting into a whole way of being alive. The plenty falls from above without ceasing — the leavings, the dead, the slow beetles that wander into its reach and forget, somehow, to wander out — and the slow one takes what comes to it and asks for nothing more. It has never known scarcity. It does not have the shape of a thing that fears. Whatever in it once feared has long since been smoothed away, the way the corners of the stone were smoothed, by patience and by never needing.

How does it sense, eyeless in the eyeless dark? By the same cries that orient the bats — it has learned them, over the eons, the way a creature living beneath a waterfall learns the water. The constant chitter and rasp is not noise to it but information, a map redrawn each instant of where the warm bodies hang and where they thin, where a falling body will land, where the seeps run wet. It feels the ticking of the water through the floor as you feel a footstep through a house. It knows when the ceiling unstitches at dusk, knows it in the sudden falling-quiet, and in that one held breath of silence — when even you can hear only the water — the slow one does the nearest thing it ever does to moving. It spreads. It reaches, very slightly, toward the warm places the bats have left, gathering the day's fallen richness while the great loud tide is out. And before the tide returns it has settled again, fed, banked, dimmed.

This is its whole life. Wait. Spread in the silence. Gather. Settle. Hold, and hold, and hold.

But there is the matter of the hearth.

I said someone came once, made a single ring of fire-blackened stones, took the easy harvest, and left. That is true, as far as it goes. What the makers did not know — could not have known, staying so briefly — is that the slow one remembers them.

Not as you remember. It has no eyes to have watched them, no organ you would call a mind. But the fire changed something. Fire is the one fast warmth this place has ever known, the one heat that came from neither below nor from massed bodies but from the sky's own logic, brought in on the makers' patient hands. And the slow one, which had until then known only the one slow cycle, the one kind of warmth, met the hearth's heat and was marked by it the way the stone was marked, blackened on its inner face. A scar that is also a memory. Somewhere in the spread pale network there is a region that runs a little differently, that thickens toward the half-sunk ring of stones, that has been reaching toward that cold dead hearth for all the seasons since — drip by drip, claiming it back, yes, but also keeping it. The one event. The one time something arrived that did not fit the cycle, and the cycle had to make room.

If the makers were to come again — if any warm fast thing bearing fire were to cross the threshold and crouch by the old ring — the slow one would know. It would feel the heat that does not belong, the heat from neither below nor from the sleeping multitudes, and across its whole vast patient body something like recognition would pass: not joy, not fear, nothing so quick. Only the second instance of the one thing it has ever found strange. Only the slow turning-toward of a creature that has had, in all its long becoming, exactly one experience worth keeping, and has kept it.

You should understand what this means about the place. It is full to bursting, yes — every surface tenanted, the rock a mere frame for the crowd of living. But the crowd does not know it is a crowd. The bats know only the cries and the night-sky and the return. The beetles know only the dark and the plenty. The slow one beneath them all is the nearest thing the fold has to a self that has noticed itself, and what it has noticed is almost nothing: warmth from below, the silence at dusk, the falling food, and once — once — a fire.

It is not waiting for you. Do not flatter the dark. It has waited so long and so completely that waiting has stopped being a posture aimed at any arrival; it is simply the shape the creature takes, the way water takes the shape of the worn basin. But if you cross the threshold, and your breathing changes, and you find by the cold scattered light the old blackened ring half-sunk in the soft floor — and if you are foolish or reverent enough to gather a few sticks from the dry world below, and strike a flame in that ancient hearth —

then for the first time in a very great while, in the warm and the dark and the loud, something old and pale and patient beneath your feet will remember that this has happened before.

And it will turn toward the heat. Slowly. The only way it knows.
