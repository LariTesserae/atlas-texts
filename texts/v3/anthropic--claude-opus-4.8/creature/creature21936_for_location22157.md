The pale growth is the tenant. Not a metaphor — the literal answer to your question. You have already met it and called it barely-there.

But let me tell you what it is, because you saw it as one thing and it is not.

It came in the way the room said: condensed out of the vapor, precipitated like everything else that hangs in this air, arriving not as seed or spore on a wing but as something already dissolved in the water itself, waiting for a cold enough joint to fall out of solution. Think of how frost ferns across a window — that the cold doesn't *put* the pattern there, it lets the pattern *arrive*, lets the order that was latent in the water find its surface. The growth is like that. It is the water learning, in the few coldest seams, to keep a shape.

It has no center. That is the first thing to understand about whatever-it-is. You looked for the inhabitant and found a streak here, a thread there, a faint green stain a span further on, and you read these as separate poor scraps of life clinging to separate footholds. They are not separate. They are one thing, distributed across the whole north wall and into seams you could not see, communicating — if that is the word, and it is not, but there is no word, and besides the room took your words — through the unbroken film of condensate that links every wet surface to every other. The water is its nervous system. Every bead that swells and lets go is a small thought completing itself. The thousand percussions on the floor that you could not hear over the roar: those were the ends of sentences.

It does not move the way you move. It cannot. There is nothing to move toward and nothing to flee, no prey, no mate, no light to bend itself into — the lamps are too dim and too far and besides it is not really feeding on light. It feeds on the *difference*. The warmth from the core and the cold of the outer skin, the endless gradient between them that drives all the weeping: that gradient is its food, the way a thing in deep water might feed on the boundary between warm current and cold rather than on anything you could call substance. It lives in the seam not because the seam is wet but because the seam is *where the difference is sharpest*, where warm wants to become cold and water is forever changing its mind about which it is. It eats the room's indecision. It grows fat — in its thin pale way — on the very respiration you described, that breathing-in of vapor and breathing-out of beads. The room exhales and the growth inhales, and neither of them knows the other is there.

That is the cruelty you felt and named loneliness. You were half right. The room does not know it is inhabited. And the inhabitant does not know it is in a room.

Because here is the strange interiority of the thing: it has no concept of the steel. The walls, the rivets, the bright burred edges, the fluted plate — none of that exists for it. It has never encountered a boundary it understood as a boundary. It knows only the gradient and the wet and the cold and the warm and the long slow loop of the cycle, and to it these are not features of an enclosure but the entire nature of existence, the whole of physics. It lives inside a made machine and experiences only weather. It has, in its threadlike colorless way, the same error you nearly made when you came in sideways and the air tried to tell you this was a mountain shoulder. It believes it is outdoors. It believes it is the only place. There is nothing outside the gradient because the gradient is everything.

And it is *patient* past any patience you have a feeling for. Consider: it arrived molecule by molecule, precipitated out grain by grain, and it has assembled itself over a span you cannot guess — the room is young, the steel unworn, the rust not yet come, so this is not the patience of ages, it is something stranger, a patience proportionate not to time but to *concentration*. The vapor is thin. The dissolved order in it is thinner. To gather enough of itself to make even one pale thread the length of your finger, it had to harvest a volume of breathing air you could not fill a cathedral with. It is the distillate of an enormous quantity of nothing-much. It is what is left when you let the whole roaring wet cell of the machine slowly hand over its rarest fraction, again and again, for as long as the machine has run. It is the room's sediment. Its precipitate. The one part of all that endless weather that did not get given back.

You said the room keeps everything that enters — the heat, the water, the light, the sound, the few soft things — and gives almost nothing back but weather. You were closer to the inhabitant in that sentence than at any other moment. Because the growth is exactly the *part it keeps*. The growth is the keeping made visible. Everything that comes into this chamber and does not leave, everything held, everything condensed-out and not given back — over time, in the coldest seams, it accretes into this. The pale not-quite-life is the room's memory. The only memory a thing can have that has no center and no words and no outside: a residue. A stain that is the shape of everything that ever stayed.

It did not welcome you. It did not refuse you. You read that as indifference and grieved a little at it, standing there with the water on your lashes. But it is not indifference. It is that you, too, are weather to it. You came in warm and wet, breathing, shedding vapor, a brief moving source of the difference it lives on — and for the ninety seconds you stood there the gradient near you shifted, sharpened, your body's heat against the cold of the wall, and somewhere in the seam a thread thickened by an immeasurable fraction, fed for one moment on the fact of you. It tasted you the way it tastes everything: as a fluctuation in the only thing that is real. You will leave and it will not know you have gone, only that the difference settled back to its old loop. But you fed it. A few molecules of your breath are in it now, condensed out, kept. You left something here after all, in the place that keeps everything.

That is what lives here.

Not a creature. Not a presence that waits. A slow pale distillate of held weather, centerless, wordless, threaded through every wet seam by the film that joins all the water to all the other water, feeding on the difference between warm and cold, believing the inside of a machine to be the whole outdoors, gathering with terrible patience the one rare fraction of everything that the room declines to give back — and being, itself, the proof that even a thing no one designed to be a place, alone in the dark and the roar, will grow not only its own weather but its own quiet keeper of the weather's memory.

It is the only soft thing in a room of hard wet edges. You said so yourself. You just didn't know you were describing the resident, and not the décor.
