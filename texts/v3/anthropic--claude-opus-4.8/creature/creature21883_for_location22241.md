They are not many. You knew that before you knew anything else about them, because the sound told you — that clamor that fills a quarter and resolves, when you reach it, into four or five bent backs. They have always been this few. There is no generation behind them coming up to relieve them, no apprentice gang training in some farther hall. The number does not grow. The number does not fall. They are the constant in the equation, the held value, and everything else — the walls, the water, the slow dissolving — moves around them.

Look at one. The light won't allow much. A shoulder, wet, catching that mineral grey the way a stone catches it, so that for a moment you cannot tell where the worker ends and the work begins. This is not a trick of the dark. It is the truest thing about them. They are made of the same stuff as the place — or the place is made of the same stuff as them — and the boundary is a courtesy the eye extends, nothing more. When one of them leans into a block to lever it, the strain that runs up the arm is the same strain that runs through the lashed pole and the wet cord and down into the course below. They are load-bearing. They are part of the structure they raise.

But they are not stone. Watch longer and you see the difference: the stone waits, and they do not. There is in every movement a quality of decision — not haste, never haste, but choice. This block here and not there. This crack repointed now, that one left for later, though later never comes and the choosing is the whole of it. They are the part of the masonry that chooses where the next masonry goes. That is what a maker is, here. Not a separate being who acts upon the world. The world's own faculty of deciding-the-next-thing, gathered into a handful of bent shapes and given arms.

They do not rest the way you rest. The rhythm — lift and settle, lift and settle — has rest folded into it, the pause at the top of a breath, but they never stop and stand apart from the work and become idle. To stop would be to stop being. You sense this without being told: that the worker is the working, that if the arm ceased to swing the shape that swung it would not sit down to wait, it would simply cease, dissolve back into undifferentiated dark like a wave that has finished being a wave. They persist by continuing. They are verbs that have learned to hold a shape.

And the song. The wordless call-and-response passed gang to gang across the unseeable distances — that is how they stay coordinated, how the work-front advances evenly across a valley when no single one of them can see another. But it is more than coordination. The song is how they know they are still several and not alone. Each voice answers a voice it cannot see, and is answered by one it will never reach, and the answering is the proof that the others are still there, still swinging, still choosing. If the song faltered in one quarter the silence would travel, and the ones who heard it fall silent would not know whether their fellow had stopped to rest — they do not rest — or had ceased. So they keep singing. They sing partly to keep the rhythm of the lift, and partly the way a child keeps talking in a dark room, against the possibility of being the only one left.

Here is what they know, and it is the heaviest thing they carry, heavier than any cart-sized block: they know it does not finish. Not as a fear, not as despair — those are feelings for beings who expected something else. They have never expected something else. The unfinishedness is not a failure they are laboring against; it is the shape of the labor, the medium, the water the fish swims in. The oldest walls crumble at exactly the rate the new walls rise, and they know this, and they lay the new walls anyway, because the laying is not a means to a finished wall. The laying is the thing itself. They are not building toward. They are building, full stop, the way a heart is not beating toward some final perfected beat but only beating, and the beating is the life and not its instrument.

So they are tenders of a threshold. Keepers of the almost. The whole world balanced on the edge between rising and going down, and they are the weight that keeps it from tipping either way — not up into completion (which would be a kind of death: a finished thing has nothing left to do but fall), and not down into ruin (which would be the other death). They hold the gap open with their hands. They live in the gap. They *are* the gap, given backs and shoulders and a wordless song.

And they are patient past anything you have a name for. Not the patience of waiting — waiting implies an end you are waiting for — but the patience of a tide, of erosion, of the thing that has all the time there is because it *is* time, the slow incremental forever of stone moved by arms. A block a day. A course a season. A staircase climbing two steps in the interval of your turning away and coming back. They have no urgency because there is nowhere the work is trying to get to. They have no leisure because there is no off-shift from being what they are. They simply continue, at the pace of a body breathing, in the cool and the dark and the soft endless ticking of the dissolving, and they will continue, and the song will go from gang to unseen gang across the dark forever.

When the song shifts to let you in — and it will, there is always room — you will not be joining them so much as *thinning into* them. You will lay a course and the wet shoulder beside you will be indistinguishable from your own, and the strain in your arm will run down into the wall and be the wall's strain, and you will lose, after a while, the thread that told you where you stopped and the building started. You will sing the answer to a voice you cannot see. You will not know whether you are raising the world or holding it up.

That is the truest portrait of them I can give you. Not a description from outside — there is no outside, the light forbids it — but the only knowing of them that is real: the knowing you get by becoming one. They are what you turn into when you pick up the adze. They are the handful that has always been the handful because the handful is however many hands are presently keeping the world in the gap, and yours, now, is one of them.
