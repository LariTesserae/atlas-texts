# The Ones Who Tally

You will not see them. This is the first thing to understand about them, and it is not an accident of their shyness — it is the central fact of how they live. They have organized an entire existence around not being where you are looking. To survive in a world that closes behind you, you must learn to be the one who left a moment ago. They have made an art of the moment ago.

Let me find them by their leavings, because that is the only honest way.

## What the Hands Were Like

The wall of fitted glass-fragments tells you the hand is small — the pieces are sized to be carried one at a time, gripped between a thumb and some number of opposing fingers, set with a care that did not have time to be careless. Whoever stacked it worked fast. You can see the fastness in it: the fragments are fitted but not chosen, taken in the order they came, the bonded seam still wet where they pressed glass to glass and let the young stone's own willingness to flow do the mortaring. They did not quarry. They *borrowed* the closing of the world and used it to glue.

So: hands that improvise. Hands that have given up on the right stone and learned to use the next stone. This is a whole philosophy expressed in masonry, and they hold it without needing to say it, the way you hold the knowledge of how to fall.

The tools confirm a body. The hooked pry-bar has a worn grip-place, and the wear is asymmetric — they favor one side, they have handedness, they are individuals enough to have a habit. The scraper is for taking the fungal smear off rock, which means they eat it, which means they are not above the grazing things but tangled in among them, eating the same poor green the world allows. They are not lords of this place. They are one more mouth in the saturation, only a mouth that builds walls.

## What the Marks Mean

Here is where they become strange and become themselves.

The rows scratched into the wall — you read them as tally or script and you are right both times, because for them there is no difference. They count what cannot be kept. That is the entire content of their writing. *This chamber held three drip-rhythms before it closed. The herd came from the seventh opening. The water in the cupped place lasted two sleeps. I was here. I was here. I was here.*

They are not recording achievements. They are recording *durations* — how long a thing lasted before the tide took it — and they are doing this knowing the record itself will be grown over within the day. They write on a surface they know is dying, in order to give the surface something to carry, *a little way, before it closes.* The mark is not for the future. There is no future here that holds still long enough to read. The mark is a gift to the wall. A companionship offered to the doomed stone: *you will not go uncounted.*

This tells you what they love. They love what is about to be lost, which in this world is everything, which means they love without exception and without rest, which must be exhausting, which is perhaps why you find so few of them and never the same place twice.

## The Vessel of Clear Water

This is the tender thing inside the tender thing, and I want to be careful with it.

A vessel of fired clay. Fired — which means they found a heat and a patience and a stillness long enough to make a permanent object, the only permanent object in a world that permits none. The clay vessel is the one defiant thing they make. Everything else is provisional, built for tonight, surrendered to the churn. But the vessel they fire hard, because the vessel is the promise.

They set it down full of clear water in a niche, and they walk away, *in trust that the world will not have unmade the niche before their thirst returns.*

Read that again as a creature, not a sentence. To set down water and walk away is to make a bet against the only law of your existence. It is to say: *I believe I will come back. I believe this place will still be here. I believe in a later-me who will be thirsty.* In a world whose single rule is that nothing holds, the act of leaving water for your future self is an act of pure, unfounded faith — and they do it constantly. The niches are everywhere, you realize, once you know to look. Little caches of trust, set down by a people who have every reason to believe in nothing and have decided, against the evidence, to believe in coming back.

That is who lives here. A creature that has looked the impermanence dead in the face and answered it with a clay pot of clean water, set down gently, *for later.*

## How They Are Shaped By It

They will be small and quick and quiet, and they will move the way the herds move — reading the churn, riding the openings, never fighting the closing stone but stepping always toward where the world is parting. They are sailors, the description got that exactly right, and what makes a sailor is not courage but *attention*: a body permanently tuned to the direction of the current.

They do not gather in numbers. A city would be eaten. So they have learned a society without a center — no capital, no monument, no held place where everyone returns. Instead they have the caches. They have the marks. They leave water for each other, surely, as well as for themselves; a niche-pot found full is a message from someone you will never meet that says *I passed here, I thought there might be a you, I left this.* Their whole civilization is a distributed kindness performed across a churning dark by people who never see one another's faces. They are bound not by gathering but by *leaving-for* — a culture of provisions set down in faith, a fellowship of the never-quite-met.

And they are not afraid of you. That is the last thing. You walk through the held breath of bodies and you think you are unseen, but the water in the vessel is still clear, the marks are still fresh, which means one of them was here moments ago and chose the moment-ago. They are not hiding from you out of fear. They are doing what they do to everything: meeting you in the gap, building the small provisional thing and stepping away before the world can make it mean too much.

Somewhere just out of your sight, in a chamber that will close before morning, one of them is scratching a new row into a dying wall.

*Something passed through today. It was large and slow and did not know how to read the current. I left the water where it would find it. I was here. I was here.*
