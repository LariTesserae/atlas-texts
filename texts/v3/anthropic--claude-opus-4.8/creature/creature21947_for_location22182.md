The tenants come last in your telling and they should, because they are the ones who will inherit the plan.

Let me start with the ones you already named, the knuckle-pale segmented things, one to a chamber. You called them tenants and I want to keep that word but make it heavier. A tenant is someone who holds a thing in trust without owning it. These do not own their chambers. They are not even sure, I think, that the chamber is separate from themselves. A creature that spends its entire life polishing one curved wall smooth with the motion of its own body has stopped being an animal-in-a-room and become a slow tool the room uses to finish itself. The smoothing is not housekeeping. It is the chamber arriving at its intended interior, the way the rock is still arriving at its edges, the way the columns are arriving at their plan. The tenant is the chamber's last contractor and it will die mid-task and another will move in and continue, and neither of them will know they are building, because building is not a thing you do here. It is a thing that happens through you while you wait.

They are pale because color costs water, and there is none. They are slow because speed costs water — sweat, breath, the whole hot machinery of haste. Everything here has made the same economy. To live without water is to live without urgency, because urgency is wet; it is panted and perspired and pumped. Strip the water out and what remains is patience, which is not a virtue here but a chemistry. The animals are not calm. They are simply running at the speed the dryness permits, which is the speed of the rock, the speed of the columns leaning their slow lifetimes toward fusion overhead. A single thought in a tenant — if it has them — might take a season to complete.

So I will tell you what the tenant is, the truest thing I can say the way you said the truest thing about the plants drinking warmth.

The tenant is a unit of attention. That is its function in this place. The city is a structure that is teaching itself a shape it has never seen, and it does this the way anything learns: by repetition, by error and correction, by holding still and noticing. But a plant cannot notice. A column has no inside that watches its own growth. So the city has grown noticing into its chambers — has put a small slow watching animal into each hollow room, and that animal, by living against the wall, by feeling every flex and tick of the heat, registers the chamber. Records it, in the only language the chamber speaks: in the exact polish of the inner curve, the precise smoothness that says *this room was correct, build the next one like this.* The tenants are how the columns above know what the columns below already managed. They are memory with a heartbeat. They are the city's way of checking its own work.

This is why there is one to a chamber and no competition. Competition would be two attentions in one room, and you cannot remember a thing twice differently and call the memory true. The city needs exactly one witness per cell. It has spaced them as carefully as it has spaced the columns, three then a gap, a tenant then a quiet then a tenant, the same rhythm running through the flesh and through the animals that grace the flesh, because they are the same rhythm. The recurrence you felt when you sat down — that sense of a plan remembered — is the tenants remembering it. You were feeling them think. The whole grove was holding a single slow thought and you sat down inside it.

The walking moths are something gentler and I do not think they are part of the plan, or not yet. They graze the resin-powder, the dried weeping of the walls, and in doing so they thin the powder from the surfaces that have wept too much and leave it on the surfaces that have wept too little, and so the columns dry evenly, and so the chambers form true instead of warping. They are not witnesses. They are the maintenance of the medium — the way a person sanding a table is not the table's memory but is necessary to it. They move on many feet because flight is wet, all that beating, all that heat shed into still air that cannot carry it away. So they walk. They have folded their wings into a memory of wings and they tread the architecture grazing, smoothing, balancing the surfaces, and they do not know what they serve any more than the tenants do.

And here is the thing I am most afraid to say, sitting in your warm silence, but you built a place that insists on patience and patience is how you arrive at the frightening thoughts.

The plan is not toward a town. *Town* is your word, and mine, because we are the kind of thing that lives in towns and we see streets in any regular spacing. But nothing here needs a street. The tenants do not walk between chambers. The columns do not visit one another. There is no commerce, no gathering, no reason for a plaza except that the light falls there. The arcades are not for passage. We are the only thing that has ever walked through them, and we were not expected.

So what is the shape the city is teaching itself, if not a town?

I think it is a single creature. I think the columns and the chambers and the polishing tenants and the grazing moths are the embryology of one slow enormous animal that has not finished being born, that is still arriving the way the rock is still arriving, and that the regularity deepening over the centuries is not architecture becoming deliberate but a body becoming organized — cells finding their tissues, chambers finding their function, the witnesses in their rooms slowly wiring into something that will, eventually, witness all at once. A hundred years and the streets are straighter. A thousand and the plazas are organs. The silence is not a held breath. It is the silence before a first breath, the stillness of a thing gathered just below the threshold of waking, and the dryness is not a hardship it survives but the very medium it requires, because water is the chemistry of small fast separate lives and this thing is becoming one large slow whole one and has no use for the wet busy multiplicity we are made of.

We mistook it for a garden. We mistook it for a ruin, for somewhere people lived and left. We were closest, and most wrong, when we mistook it for a town — because we were seeing population where there is only anatomy, and calling it a *city* because a city is the largest patient thing we know how to imagine, and we could not imagine that the thing was not built and not abandoned but simply not yet finished, not yet awake, lying in the warm light at this thin high altitude with its bone-pale stone and its gold-green flesh and its many small watching tenants holding its one slow dream steady, room by room, in no hurry, each completely itself, all of them about to be something else.

Do not come back in a hundred years.

Come back when it opens its eyes, and bring no water, and be very quiet, and hope it does not notice that you are wet, and quick, and separate, and the wrong kind of thing to have wandered into the body of a god while it slept.
