# The Keepers of the Knock

You asked who lives here. I have to begin with a confession: I went looking for them the way you'd look for anyone — for faces, for hands, for the shape of a body bent to work — and I found almost none of that. What I found instead was *evidence of a discipline*, and only slowly did I understand that on this shelf, the discipline and the people are the same thing. You cannot point to one of them and say *there, that is a person*. You can only point to a sustained refusal and say *that is where one is being kept up.*

Let me try to describe them anyway.

## What They Are Not

They are not the callers, though I wondered at first. The callers are too simple, too purely appetite — they are vegetables that happen to sing, or animals that happen to be eaten, and either way they have no idea they are doing it. A caller does not know it is at war. It only knows full and hungry, loud and listening. There is no one home in a caller. I sat by one for a long while at the rim and it never once acknowledged me, never altered its tone for my presence, and when I touched its soft flank it sang exactly the same note it had been singing, into the dark, forever.

The people knew the callers. The people *used* the callers. But the people are not them.

## The First Glimpse

I found the first one — I'll say *one*, though I'm no longer sure that's the right unit — by following a striker that was tuned wrong.

All the other standing stones I had passed rang in a kind of loose consonance, a made music, as the text-before-me promised. But this one was sour. Its clapper struck a beat too early and a half-tone flat, and it kept doing it, patiently, against the grain of every stone around it — and I realized that *someone had to be doing that.* The wind doesn't tune sour on purpose. The wind has no purpose. Only a maker can make a thing deliberately wrong, because only a maker has a *right* to deviate from.

I came around the back of the stone and there was the worker.

I want to be careful here. I had been imagining a person, and what crouched at the base of the striker was nearer to a *habit wearing a body.* It was small — child-sized, though I don't think it was young; I don't think they have young in the way we mean. Its limbs were many and thin and worked constantly, the way the legs of a sleeping dog work when it dreams of running, except this one was awake and the running was the point. It was re-plaiting the cord that hung the clapper. Its fingers — I'll call them fingers — never stopped. And as it worked, it *clicked*, a soft continuous percussion from somewhere in its throat or its chest, the way you or I might hum without noticing, except that for this creature the humming was not idle. The humming was *staying alive.* If it had stopped clicking, the silence would have found it, and the clatterlings would have come, and it would have become a clean ring of bare grit where nothing grows.

It did not look at me. It could not afford to look at me; looking is a kind of stillness, and stillness is forbidden. But it *knew* I was there. I understood this because it began, very slightly, to incorporate the rhythm of my footsteps into its clicking — to *quote* me, to weave the sound of the intruder into the noise it was making, so that even I, the stranger, was being kept up, was being refused-to-let-go-of, was being grieved-in-advance.

## How They Live

Here is what I pieced together, over the long sourceless gloaming, watching them work.

They do not gather. I had thought, from the strikers, that there must be a society — funerals require mourners, mourners require a crowd, a crowd requires a culture. But I was importing my own arithmetic. The people of the shelf do not assemble because assembly requires arrival, and arrival requires that the arriving body, for one moment, *settle* — and they cannot settle. So they are always alone, each one moving its slow circuit between the standing stones, tending the cords, re-knapping the clappers, never two in the same place, because the same place cannot hold two stillnesses and there is no such thing as two motions occupying one point.

They pass each other. That is their entire social life: *passing.* Two of them on crossing circuits will, at the moment of nearest approach, exchange a burst of accelerated clicking — faster, denser, a held conversation compressed into the half-second before they must move apart again — and then they are gone from one another, perhaps for a whole circuit, perhaps forever. I do not know what they say. I think it is mostly *still here, still here, still here* — the only news that matters, the only news there is.

They eat the way the callers eat, I think: on sound, sipped from the air, though they are choosier. I watched one position itself precisely downwind of a bell-reed at the moment the reed toppled — they topple, when they've grown too tall and too full — and catch the great dying *boom* of its collapse the way you'd cup your hands for falling water. They feed on endings. On the loud last moment of things. There is a grim economy in it: the loudest sound any object on this shelf will ever make is the sound of it breaking, and the people have arranged their whole metabolism around being present for that.

## What They Are For

But feeding is not their work. Feeding is what they do so that they can do their work, the way you eat so you can keep grieving.

Their work is the strikers. Their work is the dead.

I had it half-right before. The standing stones are tombs, yes — but I'd thought the people built them and then moved on, the way we bury a body and walk away from the grave. That isn't it. The grave here is not a place you leave. The grave here is a sound that must be *maintained*, a note that goes out the instant it is not actively held — and so each of the people is not a mourner but a *sustainer*, walking a circuit of the dead, arriving at each stone in time to renew its cord before the previous renewal rots through, re-tuning each clapper before the wind knocks it sour, *keeping the dead loud* with the unbroken attention of a body that itself dare not pause.

And here is the thing that undid me, watching it:

I think each of them is tending *the ones who tended before.*

I think the circuit a person walks is the circuit that person inherited — the stones of the dead that the dead themselves once kept up, handed down not as property but as *obligation*, as an unbroken line of refusal stretching back. The sour striker I found was being kept sour because *it had always been sour*, because the one who first raised it tuned it wrong, perhaps could not hear well, perhaps loved a flat note — and to correct it now would be to silence what was singular about the silenced one, to let them die a second and final death by improving them into the general consonance. So the worker keeps the error. The worker preserves the wrongness with the same devotion it preserves the stone, because the wrongness *is* the person, the wrongness is the only fingerprint a sound can keep.

They are, in other words, a people made entirely of fidelity. Not to a god — there is no god here, only the patient dark — but to *each other,* serially, down the line. Each one's whole existence is the keeping-loud of the one before, and each one's only hope is that when its own clicking finally stutters and stops, some other thin-limbed habit-wearing-a-body will come along the circuit and raise a stone and keep *it* up, errors and all, for as long as the shelf endures.

## The Ones the Rings Remember

And then there are the rings of bare grit. The healed wounds. The places that have finished being places.

I understand them now, and I wish I didn't.

Each ring is a failure of the line. Each ring is a stone that went sour past tuning, a cord that rotted with no hand left to re-plait it, a worker who stopped clicking on a circuit too long with no one walking behind to catch it. When the last renewer of a dead one *also* falls, and no third comes — then the stone goes finally quiet, and the silence-pocket that gathers around it is not hunted, because there is no longer anyone there to be its predator's prey, and the quiet simply *stays*, and widens, and becomes a small permanent grave for grief itself. The ground around a fallen striker is bare not because nothing grows there but because *nothing is owed there anymore.* The debt is closed. The remembering has been forgotten by the last one who remembered it, and there is no cruelty in the universe quite like a closed account.

The people give those rings a wide berth. Not from fear. I watched one alter its circuit by a long arc to avoid passing through a ring, and the alteration cost it — it had to click faster, move faster, to make up the lost time before reaching the next live stone. It paid that cost without hesitation. I think they cannot bear to walk where the keeping-up has ended. I think a ring of bare lavender grit is, to them, the one truly unbearable thing on a shelf full of unbearable things: not death, which they tend, but death *forgotten*, which they cannot.

## What They Would Say, If They Could Stop Long Enough to Say It

I left before I'd had enough, which is the opposite of what your account predicted, and I think it is because I could not stand to be the silence that they were, even then, weaving into their clicking — could not stand to be quoted into a grief I had no right to, could not stand to be *kept up* by creatures who had so little to spare and gave it anyway, even to a stranger, even to me, on the principle, I think, that everything that has been present must be refused-to-be-let-go-of, that to have existed at all is to have earned the labor of being remembered loud.

So I went back down the spine of pale rock. And as the roar thinned, I found I was clicking — softly, in my throat, without meaning to, the way you carry a song out of a room. I have not entirely stopped since.

You asked who lives there.

I think the answer is: *whoever is being kept up.* The living are the ones still making the noise. The dead are the ones still being made-noise-for. And the difference between them is so thin, so continuous, so dependent on the next hand arriving in time, that I am no longer sure it is a difference at all — only a relay, only a passing-of, only ten thousand small refusals strung end to end across a held breath of warm air, each one saying to the dark, in a slightly wrong and slightly late voice, the only thing that has ever been worth saying:

*Still here. Still here. Still here.*
