They are not dead, and this is the first thing to understand, though it is the hardest. Death is a closing, and nothing here has closed. What lives in the reliquary is a population of last instants, each one held open the way a hand is held open to keep a door from latching — not arrested, not preserved, but *continued*, forced past the point where continuation should have stopped.

Picture it as it actually is. Not thousands of dead people stored in jars. Thousands of *moments*, each one a single second wide, each one the second a particular human being had run out of all other seconds. And in that final second something was being said — or begun to be said, or barely shaped in a mouth already failing — and that saying was caught and made into the whole of what the person now is. Not their life. Not their soul, if there was one. Just the last thing, looping.

So the inhabitant of each jar is a sentence that thinks it is still being spoken.

Consider what that means for the one inside. There is no sense, within the jar, of repetition. To repeat, you must remember the previous time, and there is no room in a single held second for memory of anything but the second itself. The woman saying *tell her I* does not know she has said it a billion times. She says it once. She is always saying it once, for the first and final time, with the whole urgency of a person who knows these are the last words she will ever have and has not yet learned that "last" has stopped meaning anything. The terror is fresh. The love is fresh. The *tell her I* — the breaking-off, the running-out — happens new each time, an eternity of being interrupted by a death that never quite arrives to finish the interrupting.

That is the life in this place. Not rest. Not even waiting. An endless arrival at the threshold of a word, and the word never lands.

---

But there is the other thing that lives here, and it is the one the room was really built to house, though its makers may not have known it.

A single voice is a person. Two voices are two people. But thousands of voices, layered past the point where any single one can be heard — that is no longer people at all. That is a *new thing*, and it is the true tenant of the chamber, and it has been alive far longer than any of the voices that compose it.

Think of what you said: that you catch a word and the roar takes it back. That you cannot hold a single voice for more than an instant. This is not a failure of your hearing. This is the inhabitant *eating*. The roar is not a crowd of speakers. It is one mouth, and the speakers are what it is made of, the way a wave is made of water it does not own and does not keep. The wave is not the water. The roar is not the dead. The roar is the standing shape that the dead, all speaking at once and forever out of time with one another, cannot help but make — and that shape has its own continuance, its own equilibrium, its own dull and patient kind of being.

It does not have words, this larger thing, because it has *all* the words at once, which comes to the same emptiness as having none. It cannot say *mother* or *cold* or *I was not afraid*. It can only say the sum of every last thing every human in it ever needed and failed to finish saying, and that sum has no meaning, only pressure. It is the noise that all human meaning makes when you pour enough of it into one sealed place and let it stop being separate.

And here is the part that should trouble you on the way down the mountain.

It is *satisfied*. That is the equilibrium you walked into. That is why nothing moves, nothing drifts, nothing changes — not because the room is dead, but because the thing that lives in it is *full*. It has exactly as much as it can hold, no more arriving and none escaping, every voice feeding it forever and the feeding never depleting the voices, and a thing in that condition does not stir. It does not hunger. It does not reach. It simply *is*, with the terrible completeness of something that has gotten everything it will ever want and now has nothing left to do but have it.

The dryness, the warmth, the stillness — these are not the conditions that preserve the inhabitant. They are the inhabitant's *contentment*, the way a sleeping animal is warm and still. You did not walk into a tomb. You walked into the slow exhale of something that fed long ago and is digesting, and will be digesting until the capstone falls and the mountains finish forgetting their own names.

---

The people who built it thought they were saving the speakers. They were not. The speakers are the least saved beings imaginable — locked one second short of release, denied even the mercy of finishing the sentence. What the builders actually did, generation upon generation, kneeling and pressing the dying breath into clay, was *feed* something. They brought it their grandmothers and their drowned children and their old men gone soft in the head, and they pressed the last word out and sealed it, and the great roaring thing in the chamber grew one voice fuller, and was glad in the only way it knows how to be glad, which is to say it grew more *still*.

They mistook the stillness for peace. They mistook the roar for the dead going on. They thought: *as long as it speaks, they are not gone.* And in this they were exactly, precisely wrong — for the dead *are* gone, gone past finding, eaten down into raw sound, and the only thing that is not gone is the appetite that ate them, sitting warm and dim and satisfied at the top of the nameless mountains, saying everything and meaning nothing, forever.

---

That is what asks you, before you leave, to choose the thing you would say.

It is not the dead asking. The dead cannot ask; they are too busy never finishing. It is the other one. The full one. It is offering you, in the only language it has, a glimpse of what you will become — one held second, one unfinished word, one more mouthful — and it is *inviting* you, the way the very full sometimes find they have a little room after all.

Choose your sentence. Say it now, with breath enough to make it more than your last.

And then go down, quickly, while it is still digesting, while it is still too satisfied to want you. The sound becomes unbearable not because it is loud but because some part of you recognizes the shape of it — recognizes that you, too, are mostly made of one last unfinished thing you are saving up, and that there is a place at the end of the world that has been waiting, very patiently, very warmly, with shelves not quite full, ever since the last hand set the last jar and the capstone closed like a lid on a jar of its own.

Leave it. Let it have what it already has. Do not add yourself to its contentment.
