# The Readers

Begin with the mouths, because everything alive here ends in a mouth.

Not teeth — there is nothing here that needs to tear. The grazers wear their mouths underneath, a long slow soft-rasped slot running the length of the body's belly like the keel of a boat turned inward, like a wound that learned to feed. They are flat. They have to be flat; they spend their whole lives pressed to the smoothed planes, conforming to whatever curve the erosion left, and over the generations their bodies have given up the idea of having a shape of their own. A grazer at rest on a colonnade is nearly a part of the colonnade — a pale flexible tile, faintly luminous at the edges where the cold light catches the rim of it, breathing very slowly, scraping.

It scrapes the way water scrapes: without intent, without end. The rasp takes a film of the ruin so thin you could not measure it, and in ten thousand of these passes it takes a letter. The grazer does not know it is taking a letter. The grazer knows three things — the texture under its belly, the temperature seam where its grazing-ground ends, and the dim general fact of other grazers nearby, felt as pressure, never seen. It has no eyes worth the name. What use are eyes to a thing that lives by touch on a surface in the dark? It has, instead, *skin* — and the skin is the animal. The skin reads the corrugation it crawls across the way a finger reads the raised dots of a script for the blind, and somewhere in the slow nerve-net beneath, the texture becomes *information*: graze here, the stone is soft here, the old writing is deep here and worth the staying.

So the grazers congregate, without knowing why, on the passages where the inscriptions were deepest. They polish the deep places first. They are eating the legible parts preferentially. They are, very slowly, editing.

---

Above them, in the threading warm currents, the ribbon-swimmers.

These are the ones that look most like they want something. Long — improbably long, a body that is almost all length, a pale streamer of muscle no thicker than a wrist and longer than a corridor, rippling through the colonnades on the exhaust of the works' last warm breaths. They ride the temperature seams the way the grit does, because the seams are where the food is suspended: the grazers' leavings, the fine scraped powder of erased writing, drifting in the warm.

A ribbon-swimmer feeds by sieving. Run a body that long through a cloud of suspended grit and you collect a great deal of grit. Along its underside are the rows of fine catching-cilia, beating, sorting — and here is the first strangeness in the fauna, the first place the works leave their mark on the living: the cilia do not sort by size, or not only. They sort by *pattern*. A ribbon-swimmer running through plain dissolved silt feeds idly, slack, half-attending. A ribbon-swimmer running through silt that has been *organized* — through one of the banks where the current has rewritten the old inscriptions in ridges — tightens. Quickens. Doubles back. The patterned grit triggers something the random grit does not. The animal has no idea it is responding to the ghost of writing. It only knows that this powder, with these spacings, with this corrugation, is *better* — denser, or more nourishing, or simply more *correct* against the catching-cilia, fitting them the way a key fits, and the fitting is its own reward.

They have bred for this. Over the long ages, the ribbon-swimmers that lingered in the patterned banks ate more and left more young, and so the species has been slowly tuned, generation on generation, to the shape of a dead language. They are instruments calibrated to read a script none of them will ever comprehend, drawn to meaning as moths to a light, and the meaning means nothing and feeds them anyway. *Probably there is no difference.* The text says so itself. The meal and the message are one pale grit and they were always going to be.

---

The still ones — the anchored ones in the dark cracks — these are the oldest-seeming, the ones a visitor would call patient if patience were a thing a thing could have without a self to have it in.

They wait in the seams between the warm threads and the cold deep water, wedged into a smoothed crevice with the works on every side, and they open. A still one opening is a slow flower of pale fingers, a hand spreading in the dark — not to grasp, exactly, but to *intercept*. They take the ribbon-swimmers that thread too close, take the drifting grazers the currents have peeled off a wall, take whatever the moving water brings. They are the only predators worth the name, and even they barely move; their whole strategy is to be in the right seam when the seam delivers.

And here is the thing about the still ones. They cluster — like all the fauna cluster — thickest where the works still breathe, where the rewriting happens, where the patterned banks pile up. They are not feeding on the writing. They feed on the *readers* of the writing. But the effect is the same: they station themselves where the meaning concentrates, because that is where the life concentrates, because the life goes where the meaning is. The still ones are reading the script too. They are reading it at one remove — reading the readers, gathering where the gatherers gather, holding their pale hands open in exactly the places the dead language pulls its congregation. They do not touch the text. They have learned its locations perfectly anyway.

---

So now you can see them all at once, the whole assembly, and see what they are.

They are pale because color is a message sent through clear water to an eye, and there are no eyes here and almost no light to carry color, so they have let it go. They are quiet because sound is a message sent to an ear across distance, and everything that matters here arrives by touch — by pressure, by temperature, by the felt thud of collapse coming through the water into the whole surface of a body that is mostly surface. They are not deaf. They are the opposite of deaf. They have given up their voices and their colors and their eyes and poured all of it into skin, into the listening hide that reads the corrugated dark, so that the entire population of this drowned country is one vast distributed organ of *touch*, pressed against the ruins, feeling the writing being written and unwritten, attending with their whole bodies to a text they cannot know is a text.

They are sparse where the works are silent and dense where the works still breathe. Scattered like the columns, knotted at the vents, balanced against the enormous void. They reproduce — there must be eggs, drifting pale specks the currents carry to new seams, the young settling where the temperature-borders tell them food will be, which is to say where the writing is, which is to say they are born already aimed at the script. Each generation hatches further into the language. None of them gets one word closer to reading it.

---

That is what lives here.

Not the builders. The builders are gone the way the language is gone, gone past mourning into mineral. What lives here is the *afterward* — a whole biology grown up in the wake of a meaning, calibrated to it, fed by it, gathered around its last warm exhalations, breeding itself ever more finely tuned to a script that says nothing to anyone and never will again.

They are the works' final readers. They are also the works' teeth — the soft slow mouths that erase the deep places first, eating the legible by preference, taking the writing apart letter by letter even as they congregate, devout and unknowing, on the writing. The current writes the page in grit. The grazers eat the letters. The ribbon-swimmers sieve the dust the eating raises. The still ones take the swimmers. The current writes the page again downstream from a different ruin, and the whole congregation drifts after it, pale and silent and listening with their skins, and the cycle is the meal and the meal is the meaning and the meaning is gone.

They do not know they are reading.

That is the only mercy the place has, and it is not given to them as mercy. It is just the shape they took, holding themselves against the cold black water sixteen hundred meters above the idea of a sea — the shape of something that listens perfectly and understands nothing and is fed.
