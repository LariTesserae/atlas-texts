# The Tenders

You asked who lives here. The honest answer is that you have to widen what you mean by *who*, because the things that have made this place their home have also, slowly, stopped agreeing on where one of them ends and the next begins.

Start with the obvious tenants and work inward.

## The Lamp-Gathered

Around the string of lamps that still burns — the drowned moon, the one no one lit and no one will put out — there lives a congregation.

Moths first, because moths were always going to come, but these are not the moths of a dry country. Their wings have given up on staying dry; they have become something closer to gills, broad and sodden and patterned in the grays and rust-ochres of the weeping iron, so that at rest against a pipe they vanish. They do not flutter so much as *labor*, hauling waterlogged wings through air that is barely air. They cannot fly far. They don't need to. Everything they want is here, in the dim electric warmth — the only warmth in three kilometers of cool — radiating off a transformer housing that has run without inspection for longer than the moths' whole lineage has existed.

And because the moths gather, the frogs gather. These are the lamp-frogs, and they are the most settled citizens of the whole range, because they have found the one fixed point in a world of churn: a heat that does not vary, a light that does not move. They have arranged themselves by temperature. The ones who like it warmest cling to the lamp housings themselves, pale and almost translucent, their skins gone thin from never needing to fight the cold. The ones who like it cooler hold the outer ring. There is an order to it, a hierarchy of degrees, and it has held steady so long that the frogs at the center have begun to lose the markings their cousins use to recognize each other in the wild. They don't need to be recognized. They only need to be warm. They have made a religion of a machine's waste heat, and the machine does not know, and would not care, and that is precisely the arrangement that suits them.

## The Hunters of Standing Pools

Up in the canopy that grows sideways, in the bromeliads that hold their cupped aquariums aloft, live the frogs who will never touch the ground — you named them yourself. But you should know what they have *become*, up there, in their isolation.

Each bromeliad pool is a world, and worlds in isolation drift. The frog in this cup and the frog in that cup, four meters apart and a lifetime away, are no longer quite the same animal. The wind that throws the rain sideways occasionally throws a frog sideways too, splashes it from one pool into another, and this is the entirety of their commerce with the wider world — a rare, violent, weather-borne migration, once or twice in a life, that lands you in a stranger's house with strangers' rules. Most don't survive it. The ones who do carry their old pool's habits into the new water like an accent that never fades.

They hunt the larvae of the laboring moths. They hunt each other's young. They sing — and this is the thing — they sing *down*. The whole calling architecture of these high frogs has tilted, because there is no point throwing your voice up into wind that will shred it. They aim their calls at the pools below them, at the water itself, and the sound travels through the soaked moss and the film-water and the dripping curtains better than it travels through air. The range is full of frogs singing to each other through the substance of the mountain. You hear only the topmost layer of it. Beneath your boots, in the saturated moss, in the iron pipes acting as accidental speaking-tubes, the conversation goes much deeper.

## The Soft-Footed Thing

Something large moves on padded feet. You felt it; everyone does.

It is not large the way a predator on dry land is large. It cannot afford to be. Everything here is built low and bent and economical, and so is this. But it is the apex of the moss-world, and it lives entirely within the deep undergrowth, never breaking the surface of the green into the open white, because the open is where the wind is and the wind is the only thing it fears.

Picture an animal that has solved the problem of permanent wet by refusing to fight it. Its coat is not fur in any sense that would shed water — it is closer to the moss it moves through, a deep dense pelt that holds a film of standing water against the skin, so that the animal walks always inside its own private cloud, its own small weather. This is not an accident. The held water insulates. The cool that gets into the seams of *you* never reaches *it*, because it has wrapped itself in the very thing that would kill you and made it a blanket.

It hunts the soft-footed way: not by chase — there is nowhere to chase, the green is a labyrinth — but by *patience*, which is the last word of this whole range. It waits in the moss the way the mountains wait at the rock. It has learned the lamp. It has learned that the warm congregation of frogs is a larder that restocks itself, and on the cold nights it comes to the edge of the electric glow and takes what it needs and withdraws, and the frogs at the outer ring have built their whole hierarchy partly around this — the warmest center is also the safest, and so the bravest and strongest frogs hold the lamp, and the weak hold the rim where the soft-footed thing comes feeding. The machine's waste heat has organized the predator's hunting and the prey's society both, and the machine does not know.

## The Things in the Iron

Here is the part people find hardest.

The pipes are not empty. The turbine halls are not dead. Where the warm water still runs its old intended channel — where a wheel still turns — there is heat, and movement, and the strange steady chemistry of metal weeping rust into mineral water for decades on end. Life found it. Of course it found it. Life here finds everything.

In the rust-stained outflows below the weeping seams, in the warm discharge where the working turbines dump their spent water, there are mats of living growth that exist nowhere else in the range — things that feed on the iron itself, on the dissolved metal and the warmth, slicks of red and orange and improbable copper-green that the wild frogs avoid and the lamp-frogs cannot live without, because the same warm outflow that grows the mats is the warm that they crave. The machine is not separate from the ecosystem. The machine is an *organ* of it now — a strange, hot, iron-blooded organ that the rest of the body has grown around and into and through, the way the moss grew around the pipe, the way the fern crowned it.

And the machine, running down, is *dying* on a schedule the whole community can feel. When a wheel seizes — that groan you feel in your teeth — a warm outflow goes cold within hours. The mats die. The lamp-frogs that depended on that particular heat must migrate or freeze, and they cannot fly, and the wind is waiting, and most of them do not make it to the next warm place. Each failing seam is a small extinction. Each new burst pipe is a small genesis, a new waterfall, a new cold channel that something will colonize within a season. The community lives inside the long downward run of the machinery the way the frogs live inside the wind: braced, turbulent, replacing what it loses, never quite catching up, never quite falling behind.

## What Lives Here, Said Plainly

A congregation of laboring moths and warmth-worshipping frogs, organized by degrees of heat around a lamp no hand still tends.

Canopy frogs in their isolated pools, drifting apart into separate species four meters at a time, singing to each other not through the air but through the soaked body of the mountain itself.

A soft-footed patient hunter that wears the cloud as a coat and reads the machine's heat as a hunting map.

And the metal-fed, iron-blooded mats in the warm discharge — the living tissue that has accepted the dying machine as a temporary organ, and will keep accepting each new pipe-burst and mourning each seizing wheel until the very last wheel stops.

They do not know they have been abandoned. The word has no meaning to them. Something burns, something turns, something stays warm, and they have built their entire layered, thrashing, turbulent lives around it — exactly the way the whole range has built itself around the ocean's patient exhale.

The mountain breathes out water. The lamp burns. And gathered close around that small false moon, indifferent to how it got there or how long it has left, the wet and crowded living press in for the warmth, and call to each other through the rock, and wait in the moss, and do not stop.
