They do not call themselves anything, because a name is a thing you need when you might be confused with something else, and they are never confused with anything else. They know their own by touch — the specific give of a familiar shoulder, the grain of a hand they have held since it was small, the particular way a kin-body parts the leaves ahead. In the dark that is total, identity is not a face. It is a texture and a rhythm and a smell, and theirs are as distinct to one another as a shout would be to you.

Start, as you did with the place, with the heat — because they are made for it. They run warm but not wastefully; they are long-bodied and broad-handed and built low, so that the warmth coming up from the young ground is a thing they lie against rather than fight. They do not stand the way you imagine standing. There is no horizon to stand up toward, nothing to be seen by being tall. They go through the world more than over it, moving in the corridors and chambers of the living architecture as fluently as the water moves down through the leaves — and like the water, they prefer to be *handed* along, from secure grip to secure grip, never committing weight to anything until a hand or a foot or that long prehensile lower limb has read it and found it sound. They have learned, the way you have not, that a corridor here is a sentence the forest is still writing. They do not trust a path. They trust the next handhold, and the one after, and they are never more than one handhold committed.

Feel along the trunk and you find their marks, and the marks tell you most of what there is to know about the mind that made them. The sharpened point. The notch. The placed line of stones. But understand what these are *for*, because they are not for cutting and not for killing and not, mostly, for building. They are for *reading*. They are writing.

This is the thing about a place with no light and no horizon and no stability: nothing stays where it was. The corridor closes. The bank collapses. The leaf that cupped water yesterday has been eaten and the water goes down some other way now. A creature that lives by memory alone, in a world that unmakes itself hourly, would go mad or die. So they do not live by memory alone. They mark. The notch on the trunk says *the way bears left from here, or did.* The sharpened stick set into a fork says *a kin passed, recently, going down.* The line of placed stones — you found it and thought *built thing,* a wall, a foundation — is none of that. It is a sentence. It says how many went by, and how long ago by the warmth still in the topmost stone, and what they were doing, and whether they were calm or fleeing, and you could not read it any more than the insects could, but the next of their kind to come along that trunk will run a hand across it in the dark and know everything at once.

They have made the turbulent place legible to themselves and to no one else. They have laid a quiet grammar of touch over a world that erases itself, and they keep it current, every passing hand adding its line, smoothing the stale marks away, the whole forest threaded through with a low murmured conversation conducted entirely in worked edges that you will keep stumbling across and keep failing to hear.

The sound that deafens you is, to them, *speech* — not all of it, but more of it than you would ever guess. They have learned to live inside the wall of voices the way they live inside the wall of vines: by being one more thread in it. They call to each other in the registers the place leaves open, in the gaps between the insect-drone and the throat-pulses of the great ones overhead. A volley of clicks that you filed under *fauna* was a question and its answer. They are patient with sound the way they are patient with everything; a single exchange might take a long while, a click, then the long wait through three other things calling, then the reply, then the wait — a sentence stretched thin across minutes so that it can fit between the noises of everything else that is also alive here. They do not raise their voices. Raising your voice in a place this loud is the act of something that has not yet understood where it lives.

And the glow — the cold blue along the crawling things, the green fungal shelves, the slow rolling thought that sweeps across a far wall of the tangle. They use it the way you would use the corner of an eye, except they have made friends with it. They know which fungus is safe to disturb and which crawler will flare when startled, and a careful hand can wake a little light where light is wanted — not to see by, never enough to see by, but to *say* something to a kin too far for touch and too near for the long-thrown click. A wiped streak of luminous matter on a leaf is a marker that glows for an hour and then dies, a note left in the dark that says *here, this way, soon.* They are not afraid of the breathing light. They have learned its moods. When the great rolling pulse sweeps the colony-wall and the whole forest seems to pass a thought from one side of itself to the other, they pause, and they let it pass through them too, and then they go on. It is not their thought. But they have lived alongside it long enough to know its shape, and to wait for it, the way you wait out a sound that is bigger than you and means you no harm.

They do not own this place. That is the discipline at the center of them, and it is why there are almost no built things, why there are no walls. They watched, perhaps, somewhere far back, the lesson the whole place teaches without stopping: *stability is death here.* A wall would be eaten or buried or grown through before it could mean anything. A claimed territory would close behind them like every other corridor. So they gave up owning and kept only knowing. They do not hold ground. They hold the *current understanding* of ground, and they pass it hand to hand and let the rest go, and in a world that is an argument with no resolution they have found the one way to live that does not require winning — they have stopped trying to make the place hold still, and learned instead to stay exactly as fluid as it is. To be unfinished, on purpose, forever. To never be more than one handhold committed.

You will not meet them. Understand that this is not shyness and not fear. They have read you already — your weight, your blundering, the warmth you leave on everything you grab, the way you commit your whole self to a path and then are surprised when it betrays you. You are a clumsy sentence written loud in a place that speaks softly. They have run a hand over your passing and known everything about you at once, and what they know is this: you are new here, the way the ground is new, sharp-edged and not yet dulled, still convulsing gently toward whatever you will become. They are patient with new things. They have left you a mark or two, here and there, set deliberately where your hand will find it.

You felt them, and thought: *built thing. Something lives here.*

That was the message. You read the first word of it. They will wait — they are very good at waiting — to see whether you learn the rest.
