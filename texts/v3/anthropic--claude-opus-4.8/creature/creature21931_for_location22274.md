# The Tenders

Look again at the small armored things in the gardens, the ones cleaning the metal-roots. You assumed they were fauna. You were close.

They were people once. Or they were made by people, which up here is the same thing — the distinction wore round and smooth along with everything else, like the rivets, like the maker's marks. Now they are simply *the ones who tend*, and there are exactly as many of them as there have always been, because none are born and none have died inside any span the cistern would notice.

---

Begin with the body, because the body is the first thing the water made true.

Each Tender is about the size of two cupped hands, and shaped by the same logic that shaped everything here: it has arrived at its final form and stopped. The carapace is bronze — not worn metal *like* the floor but the floor's own material, as though each Tender condensed out of the pavement one slow century and detached. The same dissolved maker's mark ghosts faintly across every back. The same softened edges. When a Tender settles onto the tiles to rest, it nearly vanishes, becoming a slightly raised place in the floor, a rivet that decided to move.

Underneath the shell: a fan of segmented limbs, more than you can count while they're working, fewer than you'd guess when they're still. The limbs end not in claws but in *tools* — little brushes worn to softness, scrapers honed by ten thousand years of the same gentle motion, fine combs for drawing silt out from between the metal-roots. The tools are not held. They are *grown*, are part of the hand the way a fingernail is part of a finger. A Tender cannot put down its work because its work is its body.

And it has no eyes, because there is nothing here worth the speed of light. It feels instead. It reads the water — the temperature of a convection current, the particular grain of the engine-thrum coming up through the bronze, the chemical wash of a root that needs cleaning versus one that is content. It lives inside the long chord of the place the way you live inside your own pulse: not listening to it, *being* it.

---

Now watch what it does, all day, for the age that has no morning.

It tends. It moves along a metal-root from base to frond-tip, clearing, combing, coaxing. When it reaches the tip it turns and works back down. When the root is clean it crosses the bare bright tile — slow, deliberate, in no hurry that any clock would recognize — to the next root, and begins again. Its territory is a single garden, a few square meters of warm pooled water and red-green ribbon, and it has cleaned every centimeter of that territory more times than there are numbers, and it will clean them all again, and it is not bored, because boredom requires the memory of having wanted something else.

It does not remember wanting something else.

Here is the thing the visitor takes longest to feel, the thing that comes up through the floor and into the chest along with the thrum: the Tenders are not maintaining the cistern. The cistern does not need maintaining; it reached its final form long ago and holds without help. The gardens would grow whether tended or not. The roots would drink the bronze regardless.

The tending is not *for* anything. It is the same as the engines pumping water that goes in circles, the same as the long shapes circling the walls following currents that lead nowhere. It is motion that has agreed to be stillness. The Tender cleans the root the way the chord rings the bell — not toward an end, but as a *being*, an unbroken present-tense verb that long ago forgot it ever had an object.

---

There is a tenderness in it, though, that the machinery does not have. This is the strange warm secret at the center.

Watch closely and you'll see that the small flat silver swimmers come to rest, sometimes, against a Tender's back while it works — and the Tender slows, just slightly, to let them. Watch and you'll see that when one of the translucent bellows-creatures drifts wounded or fouled into a garden, a Tender will leave its root and go to it, and clean *it* instead, with the same worn-soft combs, the same patience, until the creature pulses clear again and moves on. No one taught the Tender to widen the definition of *root* to include *the suffering thing that wandered in*. Or someone did, once, a dead engineer who built care into the program and then left it running. The distinction wore smooth. It does not matter now. The care is real whether it was designed or discovered, the way the warmth is real whether or not it should exist at this altitude.

The Tenders are the part of the machine that learned to be *kind* and then could not stop, the way the rest of the machine could not stop being warm.

---

So: who lives at the top of the world?

The ones who keep the gardens that do not need keeping. The bronze hands that condensed out of the floor to comb the metal-roots forever. They have no names because there is no one to call them and nothing to distinguish one from another and no story that would require telling them apart. They are perhaps the dead engineers' last instruction, still being followed. They are perhaps what the cistern dreamed up to have something to do with its own enormous patience. They are perhaps simply *us*, after long enough — after every want has worn round and every edge has softened and the only thing left that we still freely do is care for the green things and the wounded swimmers, gently, in the warm permanent light, for no reason at all, forever, and calling it rest.
