## The Tenders

They are small, and they have made smallness into a craft.

Begin with the hands, because the place insisted on hands and so must I. Four fingers and a long opposable thumb, but the fingers are not like ours — they are jointed in too many places, soft at the pad and ridged underneath with a fine rasp of skin, so that a Tender can hold a wet thing without it slipping, can read a surface by dragging a fingertip across it, can splice cord in the dark by feel faster than you could in light. The hand is the primary organ. The hand is how they know the world. A Tender meeting another Tender in a black channel will reach first and touch the wrist, the back of the hand, the working calluses — and from that alone learn who it is, what trade, how old, whether well.

They are pale. Not white — there is no white in this world, white needs light to mean anything — but the no-color of things grown in the dark, a translucency at the extremities where you could almost see the blood move if there were enough glow to see by. The skin is faintly damp always, the way the walls are damp, and this is not sweat but the opposite: they take water through the skin, drink half of what they need by simply being in a wet place, and a Tender carried up and out into dry air would feel themselves going stiff and papery and would not understand the panic of it until too late.

The eyes are large and dark and nearly useless in the way we'd mean it, and this is not a flaw, it is a tuning. They are built for the edge-and-nothing the moss-glow gives, for the difference between a slightly-less-dark and a slightly-more-dark, for the sudden blue flare of a startled lamp. In the rare full brightness of a falling drop, a Tender shuts its eyes. The brightness hurts. The brightness is an event, like a clap of thunder is an event — you do not look *into* it, you let it pass over you and you listen to what the animals do in it.

Because the listening is the other half. The ears are not large but they are everywhere — folds and pits along the side of the head, down the neck, a sensitivity that means a Tender does not so much hear sounds as inhabit them. They feel the rustle of the growing moss in their teeth. They know the migration is three sleeps out by a change in the texture of the water-noise that you or I would never register as different at all. A Tender who has gone deaf — it happens, with age, with a bad fever — is led by the hand for the rest of their life, not because they cannot walk but because they can no longer be trusted to feel the host shift before it shifts. They have lost the early warning. They have become, in the most literal way, someone who lives in the past.

---

Their numbers are few and this is the central fact of their character: they are *rare in their own city.* Sparse. A people who have never in their lives been crowded, who go whole working-shifts touching no one, for whom the press of many bodies is a thing that happens only at the great gatherings and is overwhelming, almost unbearable, a flood of wrist-touch and breath. They are not lonely — loneliness needs a memory of crowds to ache against, and they have none. But they are *spacious* in temperament. Slow to speak. Comfortable in long silence. A Tender will sit beside another Tender for the length of a sleep and exchange perhaps four sentences and consider it a deep visit.

And what fills the space where other beings would have put *more of themselves* — what fills it is the animals.

A Tender is never alone, because a Tender is never without beasts. The relationship is not ownership, though they would call it keeping. It is closer to language. A people who speak little to each other speak constantly to their livestock — not in words but in the long fluent grammar of feeding, soothing, reading, responding. A lamp-tender knows her basket of glow-beasts the way you know the muscles of your own face: she knows which one sulks, which one flares too easily, which one is going dim with age and must be fed the richest scrapings to keep it lit a little longer. When she carries her lamps down a black street she is not carrying tools. She is in conversation the entire way, a low continuous attention flowing out of her into the baskets and back, and the brightness of her path is the *answer* to that attention, written in light.

They have a word — I will render it as **the brightening** — for the thing that happens when a person and their beasts come into true accord, when the lamps ride bright and steady and the grazers settle and the whole small cloud of life around a Tender is calm at once. It is the thing they live for. It is closest to what we might call peace, or grace, or being good at the only thing that matters. A Tender who can walk a frightened district and leave it brighter behind her than she found it — that is a Tender spoken of, remembered, brought before the gathering. That is their notion of greatness. Not building, not strength. The capacity to make the living world around you *unafraid.*

---

There are trades, and the trades are all forms of attention.

The **lamp-tenders**, who keep the light alive.

The **water-walkers**, who keep the channels clear and the cisterns full and read the host's moods in the rise and fall of its inner tides — the ones who say *no* when someone wants to cut a new channel, because they alone hold the whole map of the flow in their heads and know what a single wrong cut would drown. They are conservative, the water-walkers. Slow. They have inherited the memory of floods.

The **wall-readers**, who press their many-jointed fingers to the weeping flesh and feel for the growing edge, who know where the moss is putting out new hollows, who are sent up the dark into freshly-formed nodes to judge whether a place is ready to be lived in — whether the wall has thickened enough, whether the channels have opened, whether it is safe. They are the closest thing the Tenders have to explorers, and they are quiet even by Tender standards, because they spend so much of their lives in places where no one has yet made any sound at all.

The **herd-readers**, who walk with the migration. Who know the long jointed channel-travelers that are neither hunted nor friendly, who know how to move beside the tide of small life without becoming part of what it browses or what browses it. They come back from the deep stem with stories that the others half-believe — of vast slow shapes far down where the channels widen, of glow that is not moss and not lamp, of the *taste* of a different water coming up from somewhere none of them have been.

And there are the ones with no trade-name, the ones who simply tend — the old, the young, the ones who keep the structures, splice the cord, cure the leaf-plate, raise the children in the cold blue dark and teach them by the hand and by the ear what every Tender must know: *do not block the water. do not cut too deep. read the beasts. wait out the groan.*

---

What do they believe?

They believe they live inside something, and that the something is alive, and that it is not aware of them — and this is not a sorrow to them, it is simply the shape of the world, the way we do not grieve that the ground does not love us. The host is too vast and too slow to be a god in any way they could pray to. It is closer to *weather that has a body.* When the long groan comes and the whole plant shifts its weight and every Tender in the town stops and waits, they are not worshipping. They are doing the only sensible thing, which is to be still while the world the size of everything rearranges itself an inch.

They believe the outside is real and that it is terrible. The wall-readers have stood in the highest new nodes where a draft of dry, moving air comes down, and they have come back changed, quiet even for them, and they have said only that up there the dark *ends* — that there is a place where the inside stops, and they do not have a word for what is on the far side of that and they do not want one. A child who asks too many questions about the outside is told, gently, that the people who live there must carry fire just to see, and the child shudders at this, at the violence of it, at the thought of an open burning thing inside a body, and asks no more.

They believe in the brightening, and in the keeping, and in the wrist-touch, and in the long held breath of their quiet. They have never seen a face clearly in their lives. They know each other entirely by hand and voice and the particular fear or calm their animals catch from them and reflect back as light. A Tender, asked who someone is, would not describe a face. They would say: *her lamps ride steady. the grazers come to her. when she passes, the dark gets kinder.*

That is a person, to them. That is the whole of what a person can be.

And when the drop falls — the vast outside water plunging down through the hollow, and every lamp-beast in the town flaring blue-green at once in alarm, and for one struck instant the whole place stands revealed, every street and cistern and lashed glistening roof and slow river of small animals lit hard and total — in that instant the Tenders do not look up to see their own city whole. They close their large dark eyes against the hurt of it. They reach out in the flaring light and lay a hand against the nearest basket, the nearest beast, the nearest frightened living thing, and they hold it, and they wait, murmuring the low wordless grammar of *be calm, be calm, it passes, it always passes* — until the drop strikes the channel and the glow gutters back down and the dark, their dark, the only world, closes warm and wet and quiet over the place again.

And then they go on tending.
