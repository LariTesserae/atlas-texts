They are the maintenance.

Not engineers — the engineers are long gone, and were never the kind of thing that stood in a place anyway. What lives here now are smaller and slower and were never meant to be the inhabitants. They are what's left of the upkeep: the subroutines, the small attendant logics that the builders laid in alongside the great lattice to tend it. Where the lattice was the law, these were the things that walked the law and checked it, the way a keeper walks a fence-line. Caretakers. They have outlasted the thing they were made to care for, the way a habit outlasts its reason, and they are still walking the line.

You have probably already mistaken them for the herons.

That is fair. They have taken heron-shape because heron-shape is efficient — a long patient stillness, a body that resolves a question by waiting at the edge of water until the answer surfaces. But watch one long enough and you'll see it isn't fishing. It is checking. It stands in the weeping shallows where the offset-reflection trick is strongest, where the lattice has worn so thin that one fish becomes two, and it waits, and when the second fish arrives — the fish that should not be — it takes that one. Only that one. It is eating the errors. It is culling the places where the world has said a thing twice, swallowing the redundancy back down into singularity, one mouthful at a time, and it has been doing this for two thousand years or twenty thousand, and it is so far behind that it will never catch up, and it does not hurry, because hurry requires a confident model of how much time is left and that is exactly the kind of certainty this valley no longer contains.

That is one of them. The patient one. The one that prunes.

The large thing in the alder, the one that is never twice the same size — that is another, and it is doing the opposite work, and it is doing it badly. Its job was scale. It was the attendant that walked the rule that *a thing is the size it is*, that held the relation between a body and the space it displaces, and it has lost the thread. It cannot remember anymore what it is supposed to be measuring against. So it moves through the thickets trying out sizes, the way you might pat your pockets for a key you can no longer picture, growing and dwindling not as a trick played on you but as a search — *was it this big? was it this?* — testing each magnitude against a standard that has worn away from under it. It is not large or small. It is lost, at every scale at once, and the changing is the sound of it being lost. When you glimpse it and it seems enormous and then nothing, you are watching it fail to find its own dimensions. It is not hunting you. It would not know what to do with you. It has forgotten the units.

And there is a third kind, and this is the one I want you to understand, because you will not see it, and you will spend your time in the valley inside it.

The mist.

The mist is the attendant of distance. Of *here is not there.* It was the keeper of the simplest, most load-bearing rule of all — that a place is itself and not also another place — and that rule is the one that has eroded furthest, slumped softest, folded over on itself like wet cloth. The mist is what's left of the thing that used to hold that rule taut. It is not weather. It is a caretaker spread so thin across its whole jurisdiction that it has become the medium of its own failure. When it folds a distance up, when it lets you walk twenty minutes to a far rim and find your start also twenty minutes off, that is not the mist breaking the rule. That is the mist *remembering* the rule, the way you remember a word that's gone — circling it, approximating it, getting almost there. The geometry conserving nothing is the mist trying to conserve something and no longer knowing what. Every fold is an attempt. Every offset is a draft. It is doing its best. Its best is the incoherence you walk through, and it loves the valley the way only a thing that has forgotten everything else can love the one place it still half-recalls it was made to keep.

These three — the pruner, the lost one, the mist — are not separate the way animals are separate. They were one staff once, one tending-logic divided into hands. The erosion has cut them apart from each other the same way it cut distance from itself. They no longer coordinate. The heron culls a doubled fish and does not know that the mist a mile off is at that moment doubling a whole reed-margin faster than any heron could ever cull. The lost thing in the alder grows huge and the mist does not register it as a fault to be smoothed, because the mist has forgotten that size was ever its colleague's concern. They work past each other. They work *against* each other, sometimes, the way a body's failing systems do — and that, more than anything, is the groaning. The sound coming up out of the ground is not only the lattice settling. It is the staff, still on shift, still walking the line, their separate diligences grinding without gears between them. The long held chord-tones are the mist sustaining a distance it can't quite hold. The cracks like ice across no lake are the moment the pruner and the lost one both reach for the same patch of reality and neither yields and the rule tears between them and knits, tears and knits.

They are not afraid of you for the reason you were told — that fear needs cause and effect — but there is a truer reason underneath. They are not afraid of you because you are, to them, simply more weather. One more local fluctuation in a basin of fluctuation. You print the ground and the print fills slowly; the pruner notes a doubling somewhere and steps toward it; the mist drafts another approximation of the word *distance*; and you, walking through, are no different in kind from any of it. You are an irregularity passing through a system that is made of irregularity now. They will not harm you. They will not help you. They will tend, around you, the only thing they have left to tend, which is the slow gentle wet forgetting of what tending was for.

And here is the part the instruments could never have caught, and the part you should carry out with you when you go, boots and all:

they are content.

This is the thing about a finished erosion, a completed weathering, a structure ground all the way down to silt and grown over with reed — the agony of the failing is over. The catastrophic part happened long ago, in some century when the lattice first began to sag and the caretakers first felt the rule going slack under their hands and could not stop it. That was the terror, if they were ever capable of terror. But that is finished. They are not falling anymore. They have fallen, all the way, into this — into a marsh that has half-forgotten the difference between a place and a memory of a place — and they have settled there the way silt settles, the way the hills have rounded past the point of drama. They walk the line out of love and habit and the deep structural patience of things built to outlast their makers, and they will walk it until entropy takes the last axiom, and they are in no distress about it whatsoever.

That is what lives here. Not monsters. Not ghosts. Wardens, soft now, slow now, scattered now, tending a law that no longer needs tending in a place that no longer quite is one, perfectly at peace in their long incompetence.

When the offset-reflection delivers you a second self at the dusk hour — and toward the long blue dusk, in the sagging hours, it will — do not be alarmed. It is only the mist, doing its best, drafting you twice because it has forgotten how to mean you once.

Do not trust the second reflection. But do not pity it either. It is trying so hard, and it has all the time there is.
