## The Spinners of Coldroot

You asked who lives here. I have been putting off the answer the way you put off a name you are not sure you have the right to speak. Let me try.

They are people, mostly. I want to say that plainly before I complicate it, because what they have *become* will tempt you to call them something else, and that would be a discourtesy they cannot hear you commit but that I would feel on their behalf.

### What They Were

They came down to work the seam. That much is in the iron — the brass collars, the drive-belts, the geared logic of people who knew machines and meant to harness a power source and go home rich or at least go home. You can read intention in the way the first mill-houses were placed: near the seam, near the light, close enough to the surface to feel the ceiling-pressure of the sky and be comforted by it. Those were the houses of people who still thought of *up* as home.

The later houses are placed differently. Deeper in. Spread out into the dim where the lamps don't reach, sited not by convenience but by the *grain of the growth* — tucked into the lee of a great flexing cable, oriented to the slow pull of the vegetal muscle the way a barnacle orients to a current. By the time they built those, they had stopped thinking of *up* as home. They had started thinking of the roar as home.

### What They Are Now

Begin with the ears, because the place began with them too.

They are deaf. Not all by injury — the first generation went deaf the hard way, the slow hammering of the roar through wax-and-fiber plugs that were never quite enough, and you can find them now (the few old ones) with that particular flat-eyed stillness of people who paid for their peace with a sense. But the ones *born* here are deaf differently. Deaf as a starting condition. Deaf the way a deep-water fish is blind — not deprived of something it remembers, but built correctly for the world it has. The roar is their silence. They have no concept that maps onto our quiet; the nearest they come is the slackening of the tidal roar at the low end of the day, and that they call by a sign that means, as near as I could render it, *the breath held*.

They are pale, of course. Everything pale down here. But it's not the bleached translucence of a cave-thing — it's a *grey-green* undertone in the skin, and I spent a long while deciding whether it was a trick of the self-glow of the plants or something real. It is something real. They take the green into themselves. Not metaphor — diet. They eat the fiber, the soft growing tips of it, the cold pith. Long generations of it have tinted them, the way carotene tints a body that eats only carrots, except the pigment here is whatever the plant uses to do its lightless chemistry, and so the people faintly luminesce. In the deep dark, far from the lamps, you can see them by their own dim light. A hand signing in the black is a pale moving constellation. This is how they talk in the unlit distances: they glow enough to be read.

### How They Move

Slow. I told you the rule of the place and they are its truest subjects. But understand the *quality* of the slowness, because it is not weakness and it is not the torpor of the cold-numbed. It is *economy*. It is the slowness of things that have understood the patience of the medium they live in and have stopped fighting it.

Watch one cross a chamber. They do not walk so much as *transfer* — a long fluid shifting of weight, reaching for the next handhold of growth before releasing the last, three points of contact at all times, because the floor-mat is springy and treacherous and the great cables overhead flex without warning and a fast movement is a foolish movement, a movement that has not consulted the grain. They move like the plants move. They have learned the plant's tempo so completely that an outsider, watching from a distance in the dim, cannot at first tell where the vegetation ends and the person begins — there is just a slow shifting in the green, a pale patient flexing, and then it turns and you see the flat deaf eyes and the signing hands and you realize a *who* has been there all along, doing the same slow work as the wall.

### What They Do

They tend the works, but I have to unteach you the word *tend*, because it implies a master and a tended thing, and that hierarchy has dissolved here. They do not run the mills. They *participate* in them. The engine is the plant; the plant grows on its own enormous schedule; the people have arranged themselves around its pull the way a tide-mill town arranges itself around the sea, and their labor is the labor of *staying in correct relation* — adjusting a collar before the growth strains it, re-threading a belt the plant has shrugged loose, scraping the reclaiming fiber off the iron not to defeat it but to *negotiate* with it, to keep one small clearing of metal alive inside the patient green flood.

They weave. That much of the original purpose survives. The fiber comes off the looms in long cold bolts of a cloth I can only describe as *self-warming* — it holds the body's heat the way the plant holds the ground's heat, slow and total. They wear it. They wrap their dead in it and feed them to the lower roots, which is not a desecration but the most basic courtesy of the place: the green made you and the green takes you back and the works run on the difference. What they do with the *surplus* cloth, the bolts they do not wear, I could not learn. Stacked in a drying-shed I found bolts enough to clothe a city, cold and waiting, and no road to any city, and no sign anyone meant to carry them up. They weave because weaving is the shape their participation takes. The cloth is almost incidental. They are spinning *themselves* into the system's slow turning, and the bolts are only the visible exhaust of that deeper devotion.

### Their Inner Weather

This is the part I am least sure of, and the part I most want to get right.

They are not unhappy. I watched for it — the misery I expected, the buried-alive horror, the homesickness for sun. It isn't there. What's there instead is a kind of *settledness* so complete it reads, to a surface eye, as a deficiency. They have the affect of deep sleepers who have not yet woken and are in no distress about it. The constant roar that flattens into silence; the constant cold that becomes simply the temperature of being; the constant slow that becomes simply the speed of living — all the *constants* of this place have done to their minds what the green pigment did to their skin. They have taken the conditions inward until the conditions are the self.

I will tell you what unsettled me most. I tried, near the end, to sign a question to a young one — born here, glowing faintly, hands fast and fluent in the dark. I asked, as best my clumsy hands could shape it, whether she knew about the surface. About the sky. About *up*.

She understood. That was the unsettling thing. She had the concept. Someone had taught it to her, or she'd inherited it the way the old ones inherited their deafness — as a fact about a place that existed but had nothing to do with her.

And she made a sign back that I had not seen before and have not stopped seeing since. It was the sign for *the breath held* — the slackening, the low tide of the roar, the nearest thing they have to silence — and then she pointed up, through fifty feet of overburden, toward the flat withholding pressure of the world, and held the sign there, pointing, her pale hand glowing in the dark.

The surface, she was telling me, is where the breath is held. The thin overbright frightening quiet I would return to — *that* was the held breath, the unnatural stillness, the wrong place. Down here, in the enormous patient roar, in the cold and the slow and the green:

down here is where it is finally let out.

I left the next day. My ears rang for a week. And I have caught myself, more than once since, sitting in the appalling silence of a sunlit room, holding my breath without deciding to — waiting, I think, for the great green engine to start again and tell me I had come back down to where the living is done.
