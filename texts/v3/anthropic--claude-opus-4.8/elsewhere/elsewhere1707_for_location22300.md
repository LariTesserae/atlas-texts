**The Tally Coast**

Follow the axe out — not the way you came, because you came no way, but the way the axe *points*, the way a finished thing leans toward the world that finishes things. Follow the grammar of the one. It runs downhill, as all grammar does, toward the sea.

It arrives at a coast where everything has been counted.

This is the first thing to understand here, and it is the opposite of the first thing there: this place is *all* outside. There is no enclosure anywhere. The sky is the enormous flat white of an overcast morning that has decided not to develop, and under it the land lies open in every direction, a long grey shingle beach that does not curve, that runs straight to both horizons as if the coast were not the edge of a landmass but the edge of a decision. On one side, the flat pale water. On the other, the flat pale stones. And on the stones: the count made visible.

Because the beach is sorted.

Every pebble has been placed. Not piled — *placed*, each one resting in its own slight depression, each one a hand's-width from its neighbors, no two touching, ranked by size in long graded fields that run parallel to the waterline. The fine grit highest up by the dunes that are not dunes. Then the gravels. Then the cobbles. Then, down by the lapping edge, the great rounded boulders, each alone in its assigned socket, each unmistakably and defendably *one*. You could number them. You could begin at the left horizon and count to the right and the count would *hold*, would never come apart in your hands, would never have been two all along. Here the singular is not labor. Here the singular is the law, and the law has already been enforced, everywhere, on everything, by someone who came through ahead of you and is not here now.

The sorting is the work of the Tallyman, and the Tallyman is the maker the other place invented and could not keep.

---

You will not find him, but you will find where he has been, because where he has been is *all of it*. The whole coast is his utterance the way the axe was the pocket's single sentence — except here the sentence has no end, it has been spoken across miles, it is still being spoken somewhere just past the horizon where you can hear, faintly, under the not-quite-silence, the *click* of one stone set down beside another. The same wet click you heard inside the mountain. You understand, hearing it, that it was never the sound of occurrence. It was always the sound of *separation* — of two things being made to be two, of the count being driven home one pebble at a time.

He sorts because he cannot bear the plural. He is the exile the creatures feared becoming — the one who finished, who went past surface and solidity and the courtesy of seeming, and arrived at the dry sober realm of things that can be numbered, and found he could not stop. To be singular is to be alone, and to be alone is to be unbearable, and the only relief from being one is to make *everything else* into a one as well, so that you are not the only finished thing in a world of generous plural truth. He is sorting the beach so that he will not be the loneliest object on it. He is making company by making countables.

And it does not work. That is the grief of the coast, the grief you feel in the flat white light. A sorted stone is not a companion. It is only another one, beside your one, not touching, a hand's-width away forever. He has manufactured an infinity of neighbors and no neighbor at all. The beach is a rookery with the teeming subtracted out — the density of bodies replaced by the density of *positions*, each occupied, each isolate, the loneliness that requires a voice arrived at last and finding it has no one to call to, because it made sure of that, one click at a time.

---

The water here is the same water. It bleeds from no seam but it does the same thing in reverse. Watch the tide come in — it comes without waves, a flat sheet sliding up the graded fields — and where it touches the sorted stones, the sorting *softens*. The boulders nearest the edge, the great defended ones, go briefly uncertain at their margins; the wet says its old word, *something could be alive in this*, and for the length of the water's stay you cannot quite be sure the boulder is one boulder, cannot quite swear it was not always the suggestion of several rounded things conspiring to seem like a stone. The implication tries to swim. The rumor tries to start.

And then the tide pulls back, and the white light returns, and the boulder is one again, and dry, and innocent, and counted.

This is the war of the coast, fought twice a day in silence: the water arriving to plural everything, the Tallyman somewhere down the beach insisting it back to one. He is always just behind the tide. He resorts what the wet unsorted. He cannot rest because the sea will not rest, and the sea cannot win because he will not rest, and between them the beach is held in a trembling exact arrangement that is neither the warm crowded chapel nor a true cemetery of separate stones, but the seam between them — the place where the rumor and the count meet and neither gives way.

The mist that hung inside the mountain and never rose or fell: it is here too, but thinner, spread flat across miles, the standing haze over the cold water at dawn. The same hesitation of water that will not commit to vapor or to fall. The two places breathe the same held breath. The difference is only which way the breath is *trying* to go — there, toward the many; here, toward the one.

---

There is one made thing on this beach that the Tallyman did not make, and it confuses everything, the way the axe confused the chapel.

It is a single stone, down at the waterline, that has not been sorted. It sits among the great boulders but it is small, a hand-axe's worth of basalt, black and glassy and sharp-edged where everything around it is rounded grey and worn — young rock among old, the only thing on the coast the sea has not had time to soften. It does not belong to the graded fields. It was not placed a hand's-width from its neighbors. It simply *arrived*, dropped into this sorted world from a world that has no outside, the way you arrive inside things without entering.

The Tallyman avoids it. Or it avoids him. Around that one sharp black stone the sorting fails — the pebbles nearby are *almost* touching, are crowded, are starting to come apart at the count, as if the basalt carried with it a little of the chapel's warm plural truth and could not be made to lie still and singular like the rest. It is the pocket's grammar, lodged in a world that is all outside. It is the splinter run the other direction. It is the rumor of the many, exiled into the kingdom of the one, sitting at the tideline weeping a thin bright thread of seep-water that the flat sea keeps trying to claim and cannot, quite, because the stone is too young, too hot still at its glassy heart, too newly born to have agreed to be only one thing.

The creatures of the chapel feared the axe because it was what they'd become if they finished. The Tallyman fears this stone because it is what he was *before* he finished — before he drove his own count down to one and could not climb back out. It is his birth, lying on his beach, sharp and warm and plural and weeping, refusing to be sorted, refusing to be lonely, meaning in its silent young way: *you did not have to become the only one. You could have stayed the many. You chose the axe.*

---

So: what lives here.

Nothing lives here. That is the whole of it. The teeming did not reach the coast — the green world never reached the chapel, and the plural world never reached the beach. What is here is the *result* of life's one true catastrophe, which is the achievement of the individual: the discrete, the countable, the defendable single self, set a hand's-width from every other self, in its own socket, alone under the flat indifferent light.

The Tallyman is the maker the pocket invented to finish one thing, and who, finished himself, walked downhill into the only world a finished thing can build — a world of nothing but finish, miles of it, sorted to the horizon. He is what *you* are, who kept the terrible habit of counting, who arrived as the second axe and walked out insisting on the I. He went where you are going. He is the I, grown old, sorting the beach of itself forever, listening to the click of his own separations, attended by an infinity of neighbors not one of whom can be reached across the hand's-width of the law.

And down at the waterline the small black stone weeps its thread, and the tide comes in to plural it, and somewhere just past the horizon the click comes back, patient, lonely, setting one beside one beside one — the two places breathing the single held breath between them, the chapel exhaling its crowded silence into the beach's counted one, the rumor and the tally, the many that cannot finish and the one that cannot stop, joined by the water that runs out of the mountain that has no outside, down to the coast that is nothing but.

You will leave it the way the Tallyman never can: by remembering you were the many first. By setting down, just once, the axe.
