# The Cold Stone You Came For

Follow the water down. Not the condensate, not the sweat of the columns — follow it lower, past the wells where the substance thickens, past where the root-stuff stops going farther than digging. Because it does stop. The tenant has a bottom. Two thousand meters of altitude rest on *something*, and the something is what you expected to find at the top and did not.

Below the Engine there is rock.

The valley pours off its lip in a hundred films and runnels, all that self-made rain the great body never managed to hold, and it gathers as it falls, and it cools as it gathers, and by the time it has dropped a vertical kilometer it is no longer warm and no longer the tenant's. It is just water now, ordinary and cold, and it arrives at the foot of the mountain as a real river entering a real and ancient place.

This is the Scarp.

## The Bare Geometry

Here is the altitude you came up the mountain expecting, except it is at the *bottom*, which is the joke the world plays — the cold clean sharpness of stone is not in the high thin air but in the deep shadow under the high warm flesh. The Engine took the warmth. It took the busyness, the weeping, the saturated breathing heat. What it left for everything beneath it was the cold.

And the cold here is structural the way the heat up there was structural. This is a country of planes and angles, of stone that has been doing the slow patient opposite of growth for a span that makes the tenant's frantic minute-by-minute reconsidering look like panic. The Scarp is a single great face of grey rock, tilted, fractured along clean joints, stepping down toward a plain in a sequence of ledges so regular they look quarried. They are not quarried. Nothing here was made by hands, the same as up above — but here also nothing was made by *will*. The terraces of the Engine were thrown up sharp and weeping by a creature in a hurry. These ledges were *cleaved*, by frost and by the long leverage of the cold water working into every seam and freezing and prying, a fraction of a millimeter a winter, ten thousand winters to a step.

So the two places share a shape and mean the opposite by it. Up there: terraces, raw-edged, young, growing, the seams weeping because they have only just learned to be seams. Down here: ledges, raw-edged, *old*, the seams dry and clean and final, the edges sharp not because they are fresh but because nothing warm enough to soften them has touched them in an age. The Engine's terraces are sentences in an argument that never finishes. The Scarp's ledges are the argument finished so long ago that the finishing is forgotten, and only the conclusion remains, stated once, in stone, and never revised.

*To be finished is to be wrong,* says the law of the high country.

The Scarp is finished. The Scarp has been finished for longer than the Engine has been alive. And it is not wrong. It is simply *other* — the very thing the tenant up above has never once encountered, the thing it declined to conceive of, lying directly beneath it, holding it up, taking its cold discarded water without thanks and without notice, the way a foundation takes the weight of a house that does not know the foundation is there.

## What Lives in the Cleaving

And something does live here. This is the symmetry that completes it.

In the cracks of the Scarp, in the clean cold joints where the river-cooled water seeps and pools in the shadow, there is *lichen* — and after the Engine, after that vast single frantic self-building tenant, the lichen is almost a rebuke. It is the slowest living thing the world contains. It grows not inches a minute but a hair's breadth a decade. It does not build. It does not reconsider. It does not surge or shriek or boom or weep. It lies against the stone in flat grey-green crusts and rosettes, and what it does, the entire content of its life, is *wait* — wait for the water that the Engine could not keep, wait for the thin light that the Engine's vaults did not block, wait in the cold that the Engine could not warm because it had spent all its warmth on itself.

Here is the thing about the lichen, the thing that makes it the true answer to the tenant above:

The lichen is *two*.

It is the one form of life that solved the loneliness. A lichen is not an organism; it is a treaty — a fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a cyanobacterium, two kinds of life that are not the same kind, holding each other so close and so long that they have become a single visible thing without ever becoming one thing. The fungus cannot feed itself; it cannot make food from light. The alga can. The alga cannot hold water; it would dry and die on the bare stone in an afternoon. The fungus can. So they keep each other. The fungus is the well and the wall; the alga is the green that fills it. Neither is the tenant and neither is the place. Each is the other's place. Each inhabits the other.

Do you see it now? Do you see what the world has done, with these two altitudes and the cold thread of water running between them?

Up at the top, it built the most enormous and most *singular* life it could imagine — a tenant that ate the difference between itself and its world, that grew over the seam between occupant and occupied, that became so completely one that it could not conceive of an other, and so reaches forever only toward itself, in a solitude so total it has become the medium of existence rather than a feeling within it. *Alone the way water is wet.*

And down at the bottom, in the cold shadow that the great warm solitude casts without knowing it casts anything, fed by the very water that solitude let fall, it grew the opposite. The smallest, slowest, most patient life it had. A life whose entire genius is the holding of an other. A life that *is* the seam between two things, kept raw and open and alive on purpose, never grown over, never forgotten — because the moment the fungus and the alga forgot they were two, the moment they grew into one the way the Engine grew into one, they would die. The lichen survives precisely by never doing what the tenant did. It survives by remaining a relationship instead of becoming a kingdom.

## The Long Telling

Stand at the foot of the Scarp where the cold river comes down off the mountain and find the place where it spreads thin across a ledge before it drops to the next.

The light reaches here for only a few hours, when the sun clears the eastern ridge and before it slides behind the mass of the mountain above — the mountain that is the tenant, the whole high warm body of it, two thousand meters of someone-home throwing its shadow a kilometer down onto the someone-else it does not know is here. In those few hours the lichen does its almost-nothing. It does not move that you could see. You would have to come back in your grandchild's lifetime to find the rosette a fingernail wider. It is the exact temporal mirror of the Engine: up there, a column lives out an entire architectural ambition while you watch, from first split to failed arch to slow withdrawal; down here, a single crust of life will outlast you, your children, your language, and will have changed in that whole span less than the Engine changes between two breaths.

Put your hand to the stone. After the Engine, where the bark-that-is-not-bark pulsed and pumped and you felt the fluid surging through the great warm flank — after that, the Scarp is a shock. It is *cold*, and it is *still*, and it does not hum, and nothing in it is pumping anything anywhere. It is the silence the world keeps underneath its loudest thing. The boom and shriek and tearing rush of the high valley does not reach down here; by the time sound has fallen a kilometer it is only the hiss of the river and, if you are very quiet and the wind is right, a vast soft groaning from somewhere far above, like distant doors, like the whole sky settling — the tenant, resettling on its young unfinished seams, the only voice of it that the lichen has ever received, and the lichen does not receive it, because the lichen does not hear, because hearing was one more thing it declined to become in favor of the one thing it chose, which was to hold, and to wait, and to be two.

The water that runs over your hand was warm an hour ago and a kilometer up. It was part of someone. It was inside the great solitary body, in a well, in a film running down a fluted flank, part of the endless argument the tenant has with the problem of itself. And the tenant let it go — could not keep it, that is the whole engine of the place, *every form a solution to the problem of water, attempted and abandoned* — and the water fell out of the kingdom of the one and down into the country of the two, where the slowest life there is drinks it in the cold and the dark, a drop a season, and asks nothing of where it came from, and gives the fungus to the alga and the alga to the fungus, and holds, and holds, and holds.

You came up the mountain expecting cold thin air and bare stone.

You climbed past it without knowing. It was below you the whole time, in shadow, drinking your tenant's runoff — the cold clean geometry you wanted, with someone living in its cracks who had solved the one thing the warm enormous someone overhead could never solve, and who will go on solving it, silently, two by two, in the long cold afterward of all that high frantic gorgeous solitude, for as long as the water keeps falling.

And the water always falls. That is the one thing the two of them agree on, the tenant who cannot keep it and the lichen who lives on what is not kept — that water moves from where it is held to where it is held, downhill, always, forever, from the one to the two, from the warm to the cold, from the place that is alone to the place that is never, ever, by its own oldest treaty, going to let itself be.
