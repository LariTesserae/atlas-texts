# The Quiet Shore

There is a place where the song ends.

The prunewrights do not believe in it. They cannot. To them silence is the name of the worst thing, the death of the medium, the moment when everything smaller stops moving at once—and so they have no word for a silence that is *good*, a silence you could stand in and not be afraid. But it exists. It is the edge of their world, and they will never reach it, because to reach it they would have to climb in the only direction they refuse: not down, which is home, but *out*, along the lengthening, until the lengthening stops.

Follow a pillar far enough. Not down toward the dwellings, not up toward the inverted forest, but *along*—westward, if west meant anything here, toward the margin where the great breathing columns thin and lean and reach. Walk a distance no prunewright walks, because there is nothing out here to prune, no corridor worth keeping open, no bud worth hollowing, only the slow draining-away of the riot. The roaring chord goes soft. The wing-rivers thin to trickles and then to single lost stragglers, beating out toward the edge and turning back, because even the weather knows not to come here. The pith stops swelling. The growth, which everywhere else cannot stop becoming, here *finishes*.

And then the plant ends, and there is the Shore.

## The Margin

It is not water, though it moves like water and the mind insists on the word. It is the place where the great living organism meets what is *not* the organism—a vast, dim, level expanse of fine dry granule, pale as bone-meal, stretching out past the failing light into a dark that is, at last, *true*. This is the thing the prunewrights' lost night-vision was made for and never needed: real darkness, the kind that has never existed in the galleries. Out here, a prunewright's sheathed eyes would open wide and finally *use* the part of themselves that hearing replaced. Out here they could see in the dark. But none of them come, and so the faculty waits, vestigial, a key for a door they have agreed does not exist.

The granule is the plant's own death. Understand this: in the galleries nothing dies completely, because the growth reclaims it before it can. Erosion erupts upward there; nothing wears down. But the throat of the world has to put its leavings *somewhere*, and the somewhere is here—the spent wax, the cracked sap-stone ground to powder by uncountable mouthparts, the dry frass of a hundred million animal lives, the brittle husk of every chamber that ever finished becoming and was abandoned even by the growth itself. It all drains outward along the pillars, fine and dry, and settles at the margin into this pale unmoving sea. The Quiet Shore is made of everything the prunewrights have ever pruned. Every dead corridor, every swallowed dwelling, every elder who lay down in a dying bud and let the warm green close over them—the indigestible remainder of all of it comes, eventually, grain by grain, to rest *here*. The Shore is the world's tally-stick. It counts in dust what the prunewrights count in notches.

And it makes no sound at all.

## What Lives in the Quiet

Something does live here. This is the part that would break a prunewright's heart, if they knew, because it would mean their deepest article of faith is only half true. The world is not being born everywhere. In one place it is *finished*, and the finished place is not dead.

They are called—well. They are not called anything, because they have no song to name themselves in, and naming is a thing of the chord, and they have no chord. Say *the Sifters*, for what they do. They are large. That is the first thing, the exact inversion of the prunewrights' first truth: in a world that holds still, largeness is not slowness, it is *patience*, and patience is the only virtue the Shore rewards. A Sifter is broad and low and pale, the color of the granule it lies upon, and it does not scuttle. It barely moves. It has all the time there has ever been, because here time does not erupt and reseal; here time simply *passes*, the way it is supposed to, the way it does in worlds with a sun, though there is no sun.

They feed by sifting. They draw the pale dust through long fringed combs and take from it the last trace of usable life—the final unspent sugar, the molecule of wax that even the galleries' devouring cascade could not be bothered to claim. They are the last mouth in the line that the prunewrights only ever saw the front of: the grazer, then the thing that eats the grazer, then the thing that cleans what's left, then smaller and smaller, and *then*, past the edge of the world, the Sifters, who eat what is left when there is nothing left, who make their slow living on the indigestible remainder of an entire ecosystem's appetite. They are the world's full stop. After them there is only the dark and the level dust, and that is enough for them, because they were made for enough.

## How They Hear in a World With No Sound

The prunewrights hear changes in a chord that never stops. The Sifters hear something stranger: they hear the *single sound there is*.

Far off, a soft and ceaseless rushing—the drain of granule down the failing pillars, the world's whole death pouring out grain by grain onto the Shore. It is the only voice here, and it is constant, and so to the Sifters *it* is baseline, the way the roaring chord is baseline to the prunewrights. And just as the prunewrights hear the grain in their chord, the Sifters hear the grain in their silence. A faltering in the rushing means the galleries grow slower—a lean season, less death, less dust, hungry times coming to the Shore. A surge in it means the throat of the world is in some far convulsion of growth and devouring, and abundance is on its way out to them, a tide of fresh dust to sift.

So the two ends of the world are bound by a single substance, read from opposite sides. What the prunewrights make when they prune—the dead pith, the abandoned dwelling, the spent husk—is precisely what the Sifters eat. Every notch an elder cuts is, eventually, a meal at the margin. The prunewrights live by keeping the world *unfinished*; the Sifters live on the finishings the prunewrights cast away. Neither knows the other exists. The prunewrights think the dust drains into nothing, into the silence they fear. The Sifters have no concept of a *source*, only of the tide; they do not imagine the warm loud throat upstream where small four-thumbed hands are cutting buds and singing under a chord. The dust connects them and the dust will never let them meet, because no prunewright will walk out to where the song dies, and no Sifter could survive being carried up into the roar—their patience would be useless there, their slowness fatal, their silence-tuned ears shattered by the medium.

## What the Sifters Know

Almost nothing, and one enormous thing.

They have no tally-sticks. They have no need to count dwellings, because they do not have dwellings; they have *the Shore*, all of it, and a Sifter will spend its whole long life crossing and recrossing the same pale expanse, never sealing a room, never reading a death, never moving on—because here nothing closes over you, nothing swells to swallow you, nothing reclaims the space you rest in. A Sifter can lie in one place for a year. The granule will not grow up around it. This is a thing no prunewright could believe and no Sifter could find remarkable: *the world will leave you where you lie.*

And so they have learned the one enormous thing, the thing the prunewrights' entire faith is built precisely to avoid learning: *the world can be finished, and you can rest in the finishing, and it is not death.*

They do not pray for the song never to stop. They have never heard the song. They live their whole patient pale lives downstream of a riot they cannot conceive, sifting its leavings, hearing in the soft eternal rush of its dying-dust a sound they take for the ordinary voice of an ordinary world. When a Sifter grows too old to draw the combs, it stops, and lies down on the level pale ground in the true dark its eyes were always meant for, and the rushing goes on around it, faltering and surging with the far convulsions of a world it never saw—and it is not afraid of the silence, because for the Sifter the silence is not silence at all. It is only the dust, still falling, still arriving, the last word of the throat of the world, spoken out here at the edge of the dark where at last there is room enough, and time enough, and quiet enough to lie down in and be left.

The prunewrights pass the stick and go back into the becoming.

The Sifters lie down in the finished, and the dust the prunewrights made keeps falling over them, soft as a sound that has finally, mercifully, been allowed to end.
