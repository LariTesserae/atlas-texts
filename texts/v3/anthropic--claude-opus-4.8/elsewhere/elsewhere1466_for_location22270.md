# The Hour-Spire

There is a place where the world remembered how to end a day, and it remembers nothing else.

You reach it by following the one thing the Reiterating Plain could not keep: the storm that has been arriving for ten million years. It never arrives *there*. The closed weather of the plain recycles its own water forever, vapor falling and rising and never committing — but a closed system leaks. Somewhere at the edge of the dim, where you stopped being able to count the reed-copies, a thin current of the plain's breath escapes upward and away, the one draft that found a way out, and it carries the unspent storm with it like a held grudge. Follow that draft. The heron-thing cannot, because following it would mean arriving. The Returners cannot, because it runs above the skin where they dare not stay. Only you, single and locatable and not yet answered, can walk out from under the perpetual almost-rain and let it pull you up the long grade of the air to the place where the storm finally lands.

It lands on the Spire.

---

The Spire is the opposite of the Plain in the one way that matters: it is *high*, and it is *narrow*, and it has not finished anything.

Picture a stone needle, a single shaft of dark rock standing alone in a sky that actually moves. Where the plain achieved exhaustion by wearing everything down to level, the Spire achieved its opposite by some old violence shoving one column of the world up past all the rest and leaving it there — unfinished, unstable, still falling, except that it falls so slowly the falling reads as standing. It sheds. Constantly. Flakes and grains and the occasional fist of rock peel from its sides and drop into the cloud below and are not replaced, so the Spire is always slightly less than it was, always becoming a needle from a tower, a thread from a needle, narrowing toward the day it will be too thin to stand. It is the most temporary thing imaginable, and it has been temporary for as long as the plain has been finished, which is to say: forever, both of them, in their opposite directions.

The storm the plain could never spend, it spends here. The escaped draft delivers it. Rain that the plain held back for ten million years comes down the Spire's flanks in sheets, real rain, rain that *falls and is gone* instead of rising again — and this is the great extravagance of the place, the thing that will undo you after the plain: here, water leaves. It does not reseal. It does not applaud. It strikes the stone and runs off into the cloud-gulf and is *spent*, used once, never seen again. After the plain's terrible thrift, the Spire's waste is almost obscene. It rains as though there were more where that came from. There is not. The draft from the plain is thin. The Spire is drinking the plain's whole withheld grief and pouring it off its sides into nothing, and the plain will never get it back, and that is the kinship between them: the plain holds everything and the Spire keeps nothing, and they are connected by the single thread of air down which the one gives the other all it cannot bear to spend.

---

The light here *changes*, and the change is the whole reason the place exists.

Remember: the plain has no time of day. One perpetual brown-gold hour, the dusk before a storm that never comes. The Spire is what happened to all that withheld time. Up here, above the cloud-ceiling that the plain stares at from below as its motionless sky, there is a sun — and it moves. It rises somewhere and crosses and sets, and it does this not on a schedule but *whenever the Spire can manage it*, which is rarely and beautifully and at enormous cost. The Spire is built — was shoved up, has worn down — to do one thing: to lift a single point of the world high enough to catch the sun crossing, and so to have *an evening*. To complete the day the plain could only ever begin.

When it works — when the cloud-gulf thins and the moving sun finds the needle's tip — the light pours down the stone in colors the plain has never owned. Not brown-gold dusk held forever, but the actual sliding spectrum of a true evening: the gold going to amber going to rose going to a red so deep it is almost the memory of red, and then the blue that is not a color but the absence of the sun, arriving, advancing down the Spire from the tip, and the day *ends.* It ends. The thing the plain cannot do. Up here it happens, once, completely, and then the cloud closes and the sun is lost and the Spire stands in its own dark having spent another day it can never repeat — because here, unlike below, no hour is ever said twice. Each evening is the only one of itself. Each is lost the moment it finishes. The Spire's whole economy is the reverse of the plain's: not infinite copies of one moment, but a string of unrepeatable single moments, each spent and gone like the rain off its sides.

---

Now the one who lives here.

There is only ever one tenant on the Spire, and it is always the same role and never the same individual, and this is the cost of being the place that ends days.

It is the **Keeper of the Last Light**, and it lives at the very tip, on the narrowing point, in the spot most likely to break off next. It has to live there. The whole purpose of the Spire is to hold one watcher high enough to *witness the sun cross*, because an ending that no one witnesses does not finish — the plain proved that, the plain is full of endings no one could hold still long enough to see, the dead frond grieving in four thousand places with no single witness to say *yes, this ended, once, here.* The Spire solved it by lifting one creature up to where the day can be watched all the way to its close. The Keeper's only task is to *see the evening through* — to keep its eyes open from the gold to the dark, to not look away, to let one whole day complete itself inside a single attention.

And it costs the Keeper its place. Every evening witnessed wears the Spire's tip a little thinner, sheds a little more stone into the gulf, brings the point a little closer to the day it cannot bear a watcher at all. So each Keeper knows, taking the post, that to do the work is to destroy the perch — that every day it successfully ends brings nearer the day the Spire is too thin to hold anyone, and the last light goes unwitnessed, and the whole long project of *finishing things* fails at last. The Keeper witnesses anyway. That is the kind of creature it is. It would rather spend the Spire watching real endings than preserve it for a stillness like the plain's, where nothing ends because nothing is allowed to be seen ending. It chose the opposite bargain from the heron. The heron walks forever to avoid being located. The Keeper sits at the single most located point in the world and lets that point wear away beneath it, day by spent day, so that *something, somewhere, ends and is seen to end.*

---

You came up the draft because you are the one who hasn't answered yet.

Down in the plain they crowded you with their patience, asking *will you stay and be everywhere and be no one and be enough.* You did not answer. You followed the leak in the closed weather instead, up the grade of the escaping air, to the one place in this world where the question is reversed — where the Keeper, at its dwindling tip, turns to you with the only thing *it* ever asks, the opposite question, the one the plain cannot pose because the plain has no endings to offer:

*Will you stay, and be only here, and be only once, and be spent?*

The plain offered you infinite recurrence at the price of your singularity. The Spire offers you your singularity, whole and uncopied and unmistakably *yours* — at the price that it will be used exactly one time and then lost off the side of the stone into the gulf, like the rain, like the day, like every Keeper before this one whose name the wearing-down took with it.

The Keeper has held its post a long time and the tip has grown thin. It would like to be relieved. It would like, before the Spire narrows past bearing, to hand the last light to one more single locatable uncopied watcher and go down into the cloud the way the rain goes, spent at last, finished — the only fate the plain could never grant and the Spire grants to everyone who stays.

The sun is crossing. The cloud-gulf is thinning. Somewhere far below, the heron walks parallel to nothing, the Returners risk their glances, the dead frond holds its note, and the Spread close softly around the place where your single body used to stand. They are still asking. They will ask forever; forever is what they have.

Up here there is an evening, and it is happening only once, and it is almost over.

The Keeper makes room at the narrow tip.

You have, this time, until the light goes — and not one moment longer, and that is the whole gift of the place: that here, for once, your time is actually running out, and so it is finally, unbearably, *yours.*
