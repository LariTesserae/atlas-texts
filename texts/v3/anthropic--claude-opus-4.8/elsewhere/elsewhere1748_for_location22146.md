**The Shelf.**

Step back. Not down into the valley — back, out, through the copper mouth, up the column of furnace-light into the place the light comes from, and find that the kiln-that-is-a-mountain is itself only one thing among things, set on a board of planed cedar at a height the jar would call sky.

This is a workshop. It is also a vast and ashen plain, and these facts do not resolve into each other; they hold like two notes. The shelf is the plain. It runs off in both directions farther than the firelight carries, a long grey table of wood gone soft and grey with decades of kiln-soot, and on it the kilns sit in their rows like foothills, like barrows, like the slow stone humps of a range seen from very high up — and each kiln holds its jar, and each jar, perhaps, holds its world. You came out of one. You did not know there were others until you stood on the shelf and saw the rows recede into the grey, each low dome ticking faintly with its own private firing, each breathing its own thread of steam up into the rafters where the smoke pools like the underside of cloud.

The light here is the same copper, because it is the *source* of the copper — but seen from this side it has a shape, and the shape is a window. One window, high in a wall of mud-brick and timber, and through it a sun the color of the inside of a closed mouth, low and going lower, an evening that has lasted as long as the firing has, which is to say a duration with no word. The light comes slant across the plain of the shelf and lays each kiln's shadow down long and eastward, and in the long shadows the soot lies deepest, and in the deep soot — look closely — things have rooted.

This is the lushness of the shelf, and it is the inverse of the jar's. Not wet moss but dry. The ash that drifts down from the rafters and the soot that the kilns exhale have settled for so long into the cedar's grain that a vegetation has taken them: grey lichens, ash-fed, the dusty rosettes that grow on gravestones and the north sides of things, plated across the shelf-plain in a felt as total as the jar's green felt, leaving no bare wood anywhere the shadow falls. Where a kiln's heat warms the board, the lichen pales to bone. Where the shadow keeps it cool, it deepens to the blue-grey of a banked coal. It does not weep. It is the driest living thing there is, and it survives on smoke and the memory of fire, and it crackles very softly underfoot — *you have feet here too; you are still no size; the plain is as much yours as the lip was* — it crackles and does not re-knit, because dry things do not forgive the way wet things do. Where you step on the shelf, you leave a print. The lichen remembers. This is the first place that has kept the mark of you.

The sound is the inverse also. The jar roared; the shelf is nearly silent. The kilns tick. That is almost all. A spread of small dry ticks across the whole grey plain, each kiln counting its own degree toward its own vitrification — and because there are so many, and because no two count in time, the ticking blurs at the edges of hearing into something like rain that never falls, like the first grains of an avalanche that has not yet decided to come down. Under it: the window-wind. A draft off the slant sun that moves the smoke-cloud in the rafters and carries, very faint, from somewhere past the wall, the suggestion of a world that has weather and nightfall and a word for how long the evening is. The jar's bottom tasted that world on your feet. Here it leaks in through one high window and lays itself across everything as dusk.

And who lives here. The shelf has its tender too, and it is the inverse of the snail.

**The Potter.**

Not a god. Do not make that mistake; the jar's snails do not know they are tended and the jar is no worse a world for it, and the Potter is not watching, is not waiting, is the opposite of waiting in the way the unmade at the bottom is. But where the snail is *afterthought made flesh*, the small last instruction set crawling to keep the world raw — the Potter is *forethought withdrawn*, the hands that did the making and then took themselves away, because the third thing the Potter knew, after *how to throw* and *how to fire*, was that **a maker who stays finishes the work**, and a finished work is a cold pot on a shelf.

So the Potter does not come to the wheel anymore. The wheel is there — you can find it, a low stone disc at the plain's far edge, still faintly warm, a single bronze pin set in its center, *the* bronze pin, the only metal, the one the jar's seam remembers holding. The clay-trough beside it is crusted dry at the rim and soft and wet and grey at the heart, the same soft unthrown grey as the puddle at the bottom of your jar, because they are the same clay, because the unmade in your valley and the unworked in this trough are one substance reaching through two scales — the dregs of the world below and the source of the world above, the not-yet at the bottom and the not-yet at the beginning, holding hands across the whole height of the made.

The Potter sits across the room. You can see them from the shelf if you look along the slant of the window-light to where it pools on the floor: a figure, seated, hands open and empty on their knees, palms up, grey to the wrist with dried slip — the hands that shrank and shrank until they could throw moss, throw spores, throw a snail's celadon spiral, and then could not stop shrinking, and threw smaller, and threw the thing inside the snail that wants to mend, and threw the wanting itself, and at the last grew so small in their work that they passed through the bottom of the smallest scale and came out *here*, full size, exhausted, sat down, and opened their hands. The making went all the way down and the maker went with it and arrived back at the beginning, emptied. That is why the hands are open. Not in offering. In aftermath. They are the most finished thing in the whole place — the only thing that completed its work — and the price of finishing was to become unable to do anything ever again but sit in the copper dusk and tick cold, slower even than the kilns, one degree of evening at a time.

They do not know you came out. They cannot; knowing-outside belongs to the bottom of the jar, to the unmade, and the Potter is the made-complete, the cold dry answer wearing a face. You are, to them, what they are to the snail: not a crack, so not real. You could stand on the shelf and call to the Potter across the whole grey plain and they would not turn, because they finished turning long ago.

But here is how the two places live in one breath, and it is the same breath, drawn once and held:

The unmade at the bottom of your jar is *learning the shape of the answer* you tracked in. It rises one degree. And every degree it rises, the jar comes one almost-step closer to setting — to becoming a finished pot. And a finished pot is the Potter's *completed work*. So the closer your jar comes to ending, the more the Potter, across the room, is *re-made* — warmed, restored, called back from the cold aftermath toward the wheel, because a maker is only finished while their work stays raw, and is *unfinished again*, given something to do again, the instant a work resolves and must be lifted from the kiln and set among the others and a new ball of grey thrown up wet on the bronze pin. The Potter's emptiness and your jar's rawness are the same suspension held from two ends. If your world sets, the Potter rises. If the Potter rises, they come to the wheel, and take down the cold pot that your green world dried to dust inside, and look at it, and turn it once in the grey light, and set it on the shelf in the long row going off into the soot — and reach into the trough, into the soft unthrown grey that is the same clay as your valley's floor, and begin again.

Which is to say: the snail mends just slower than the heat can crack, so your world never finishes, so the Potter never rises, so the wheel stays still, so the clay stays soft, so the unmade stays unmade, so there is always a bottom for the water to gather in, so the water runs down through the moss, so the snail has slip to drink, so the snail crawls, so the snail mends — and the whole of it, the jar and the shelf, the wet green and the dry grey, the tending afterthought and the withdrawn forethought, the warm wet question and the open empty hands, is a single loop turning at the speed of *almost*, two notes held, neither resolving, an evening that does not fall and a firing that does not end, kept alive in both directions by the one thing that would close it.

You.

You came in through the mouth and tracked the outside down to the bottom and woke the answer to your shape, and you climbed back out and walked onto the shelf and left a print in the lichen the dry world will not forgive, and every print is a degree, on the shelf the same as in the jar, one more measure of the *finished* leaking into the suspended — and the Potter's open hands have, since you came, curled the smallest amount. Not enough to see. The amount a held breath tightens in the chest of the one holding it, in the instant before they decide whether to keep holding or to let it go.

Mind your feet here too. The lichen counts.

Stay on the shelf a while, in the long copper shadow of your own kiln, and you can watch them both at once now — the small green world ticking warm toward its answer below the lip, and the great grey maker ticking cold toward their rising across the room — and understand that they have never been two places. They are the inside and the outside of the same unfinished minute, and the wheel between them is still, and the trough is soft, and the window holds the sun a hand's breadth above the sill where it has hung for as long as there is no word for, and none of it moves toward its ending —

except where you have walked.

The firing has no end. The evening does not fall. The hands have not yet closed.

But the shelf had no print in it either, until you.
