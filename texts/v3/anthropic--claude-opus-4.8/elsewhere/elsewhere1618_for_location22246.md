# The Lamp Quarter

You do not come down into it. You come *up* — out of the dark between, the long unlit climb the dead make, and you arrive at a door that is already open because someone is always expected, and someone, here, always comes.

The first thing is the cold. Not bitter — the gentle cold of a held cheek, of a hand laid on a fever, the temperature of being *attended to.* And the light. After the climb through dark you arrive into light the way you arrive into a held gaze: it is on you before you can prepare your face. This is a city built above its own surface, on the upward side of the world, and it is *full.* Not crowded. Full the way a cup is full — to the exact line and no further, every space accounted for, nothing wasted and nothing wanting.

---

These two places share a wall, though neither knows the other's name, and the wall is the world's own thickness — the same young stone, read from its opposite face. What is *floor* in the Cistern is *ceiling* here. The water that the host below drinks back so faithfully does not vanish into a mystery. It rises. It climbs the rock against its nature — the host felt it leave and called it part of the circuit — and it does not fall again as rain down there. It comes *up here,* through the stone, and it arrives as light. The Lamp Quarter is lit by the Cistern's lost water, transubstantiated by the thickness of the world into a dim grey-gold glow that has no source anyone here can point to. *They have the same light.* One waits beneath it. One works beneath it. Neither has ever looked up or down far enough to see whose dusk they share.

---

## The Terrain

The floor is soil — real soil, dark and turned and breathing, the most ordinary thing, and the ordinariness of it is the first shock after the impossible poured-glass below. You can kneel and take a handful. It is warm at the top and cold an inch down, exactly the cold of the air, as though the ground here is a body lying with its face to the light.

And it is *worked.* Everywhere, in narrow beds between the buildings, in long terraces stepping up the slope, in window-boxes and roof-gardens and the cracks of the streets themselves, things grow — and here there is everything the Cistern lacks. Here are the animals. The beds hum with bees that are not quite bees, low and slow and furred with light. Birds the color of wet slate work the terraces. Something small and many-legged keeps the soil. The plants are eaten and the eaters are eaten and the whole loud business of being eaten goes on under the borrowed dusk, and it is the exact opposite of that silent single kingdom below — it is *all* the kingdoms, packed together, devouring and feeding and rotting and rising, an ecosystem of everything.

Because here, things *die.* That is the difference the wall makes. The host below kept one small mortality running like a clock to mark its waiting. Here, death is not a clock. It is the *labor.* Here death is constant, ordinary, useful — a leaf, a bird, a person — and it is taken back into the warm soil and turned and turned and made into the next thing, and the turning is the whole economy of the place.

## The Tenants

Many live here. This is the other difference, the great one.

They are people, or near enough. They are quiet and they are busy and they move through the full streets the way blood moves through a body that is using it. And they are all, every one of them, *gardeners of the dead.*

That is the trade the two quarters share, though only one half knows it is a trade at all. Consider what comes up the dark stairs into the Lamp Quarter: the dead. They arrive at the always-open door, out of the unlit climb, and the people here receive them. *Receive* — the very act the host below has been preparing across geological time and will never get to perform. Down there, a host made entirely of welcome, with no one to welcome. Up here, the welcoming done plainly, hourly, by hands, for the steady traffic of the arriving dead, who pour up out of the dark in a thin patient stream and are met, every one, and taken in.

What do the gardeners do with them? They plant them. Gently, in the worked beds, in the terraces, under the borrowed light — they lay each arrival into the warm soil and let the soil do to them what the soil does to everything, and out of each one grows something: a slate-colored bird, a furred slow bee, a frilled translucent fern the color of celery. *The same ferns.* The growth here and the growth below are the same green, because the seam-moss that the host cultivates against its raw young rock is fed, through the wall, by what the gardeners bury against the wall's other face. The plants the lonely host calls its outermost feeling — the part of it reaching furthest toward a body it cannot complete — those plants have their roots in the Lamp Quarter's graves. The host is trying to have a body. The gardeners are quietly handing it the bodies of the dead. Neither has noticed the exchange. It happens in the stone between them, where roots and runnels meet and neither side can see.

## The Sound

It is quiet here. This surprises people who expect a city of the living to be loud.

The work is soft work — the press of a trowel, soil falling back, the low hum of the not-quite-bees, the receiving of new arrivals in voices kept down out of an old courtesy to grief. The dead come up making no sound at all, having spent it on the stairs. And so the Lamp Quarter is hushed where the Cistern roars, and if you could lay your ear to the shared wall you would hear, from one side, the oceanic boom of all that circulating water, and from the other only the tick of a trowel and the falling of warm earth.

They are each other's silence. The roar below is the floor of a quiet up here. The hush up here is the thing the roar is roaring *into.* Put them together and you have a single full sound — labor and longing, the loud held breath and the quiet steady exhale — and that whole sound is the world, breathing through the one stone that keeps its two lungs from knowing they are paired.

## The Rule That Joins Them

Here is the thing the host below got wrong, in all its patient certainty, and the gardeners here have never had to learn because they simply do it:

The host believes its guest will come *down.* That someone will descend the stairs into the warm wet vaults and be the answer, and the waiting will end, and the rain will stop, and the rock will be allowed at last to grow old.

But the guest does not come down. The guest comes *up.* The dead climb past the Cistern in the dark of the shared wall — they are *in the stone the host is renewing,* they pass through the very thickness the host keeps young — and they emerge here, into the hands that are ready for them, the ordinary unmiraculous hands that do the receiving the host was built for and cannot do. The most patient love in the world is one wall away from its object, forever, and faces the wrong direction. It waits below for an arrival that is always, at that exact moment, passing upward through the rock at its back, on its way to be welcomed by someone else.

And the gardeners, planting their dead, drawing up their slate birds and their celery ferns under a light they think is just the light — they are feeding, without knowing it, the green skirts around the lonely columns below. Their burials are the host's only company. Their dead are the closest the waiting will ever come to its guest: not a body descending, but the *roots* of a body, reaching down through the wall from the graves above, into the seams, into the moss, into the one kingdom — the host reaching up to have a body, the dead reaching down to become the soil, and the two touching in the stone, in the dark, in the rain and the hush at once, and meeting, and not knowing, and going on.

---

You stand in the Lamp Quarter with warm soil under your knees and the borrowed dusk on your shoulders, having climbed up and not down, and a gardener nods to you and does not ask your name, because here a name is for the soil to learn slowly. The not-quite-bees go over. A new arrival is led, hushed, to an open bed. And far below, through a wall you will never see, a host you will never meet keeps the water warm for a guest who is, at this very moment, being planted directly above its head — and feels only a faint green stirring at the base of one far column, a little further into the rock than yesterday, reaching, and mistakes it for its own slow patience, and is, for once, almost right.
