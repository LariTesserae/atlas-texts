The Lowland Where the Work Went

Follow the water down.

That is the only thread that leaves the plateau — the water the engine breathes out, the water it draws up and lets fall and never keeps. It does not stay. A bog held kilometers deep against the sky must, somewhere, leak. At the southern lip of the upland, where the moss-mattress thins over the old worn bone of the rock, the held water finds a seam and goes over, and from there it has a long way to fall, and it falls the whole length of a country to get to where the work actually is.

It does not fall as a river. It falls as a *seep* — a thousand thin threads down the granite faces, brown with peat, cold as the held breath it came from. These threads gather. They become a beck, the beck becomes a burn, the burn finds others like it, and by the time the water has come down out of the high cold and into air thick enough to commit to being a day, it is a working river, and the country it works through has a name, though you will not be told it, because the people there do not say it. They say *down the valley.* They say *at the works.* They never say the name of the place itself, the way you do not say the name of a thing you are inside of.

The valley is everything the plateau is not, and it is the same thing wearing a different face.

Here the land has *not* finished. That is the first fact, and it is the hinge on which the two places turn against each other. The plateau reached its end-state ten thousand years ago and has nothing left to do but run; the valley is still being made, violently, every hour, and has no time to run because it is too busy *being built.* The river that the bog let fall has cut this valley, is cutting it now, will cut it deeper while you watch — you can hear the gravel grinding under the brown water, the slow demolition of the rock, the place sanding itself toward the smoothness the upland already reached and can never lose. The valley is the plateau's own past, flowing downhill. The valley is what the high cold looked like before erosion finished with it. The water carries the memory of the end-state down into the unfinished place and the unfinished place will not listen, because it has work to do.

And the work is real.

This is the wrongness of the valley, and it is the exact inverse of the wrongness above. On the plateau there is the *sound* of industry and no industry — the mill-roar rising out of untouched moss, the engine that is all rhythm and no matter, processing nothing into nothing. Here in the valley there is industry and *no sound of it.* Here are the mills the bog has been remembering. Here are the real ones, the iron ones, the ones with furnace-floors and girders and sodium light, the ones whose orange glow you saw upside-down in the black pools and could not find when you looked up — because you were looking up, and they were down, they were always down here at the bottom of the fall, and the pools were not lying. They were reflecting. Across the whole height of a country, the still water of the high bog holds the image of the working valley the way a child holds the face of a parent it has never met.

They are great stone sheds, the mills, set hard against the river where the fall is steepest, where the water's grinding demolition can be tapped and turned. Wheels — real wheels, iron-shod, river-driven, the size of houses — turn in the brown current and throw off a cold spray exactly like the heather-flywheels far above, and this is not a coincidence, this is the *original,* this is the thing the bog grew itself into the memory of. Bellows breathe in the forge-houses with a sigh you can hear from a kilometer off. Long ranks of trip-hammers piston up and down in their beds, slick and deliberate, like the legs of a loom — and somewhere unimaginably high above them, in the held breath of the cold, ranks of reed are pistoning the same stroke into black water for no reason and toward no product, keeping the rhythm the valley taught it and then forgot it had taught.

But there is no sound.

You will stand in the mill-yard and watch the great wheel turn and the hammer fall and the bellows sigh, and your sternum will wait for the concussion and it will not come. The valley works in a deep, absorbed quiet, the quiet of the wire-walkers stitching their line, the quiet of labor that long ago stopped narrating itself. The hammer strikes the iron and the iron gives, and the blow makes no roar — it makes a soft *give,* a yielding, like a beak set into bright moss. All the noise of this place went up the river ten thousand years ago and never came back. The bog has the valley's sound. The valley has the bog's silence. Each runs on the other's missing half, and neither can hear itself, and that is why the people here do not speak the name: there is no sound to say it with that the place would not swallow.

### The People at the Works

For there are people. This is the second hinge, and the heavier one.

The plateau has a crew that cannot leave — singers, walkers, the great blind pistons in the dark — a workforce that is the running of the machine and could no more depart than a beat could step out of a heart. The valley has people who *could* leave, who have legs and doors and a road out, and who do not go. And the difference between cannot and will-not is the whole ache of this country, the thing it cannot say.

They are not many. A mill-town's worth, strung along the river where the fall is good — squat stone houses the color of wet tarnish, brown-grey-green, the slates dark with the permanent low cloud that this valley shares with the upland because it is the same cloud, the same grey lid, drawn down over both ends of the fall. The light here is no better than above: noon and dusk are nearly the same hour, and the furnace-glow is the brightest thing, and at night the sodium lamps come on over the mill-yards and throw the orange ceiling up against the cloud, and the cloud holds it, and somewhere far above a still black pool holds it again, upside-down, for frogs to sing to.

The people are warm. You will notice this the way you noticed the warmth of the frog in your cupped hands — that here, in all the cold seep of the fallen water, the bodies of the workers are the warm thing, the ember the cold has not found. They move through the mills with their heads down, absorbed, spaced at deliberate intervals down the line, and where they set their hands the work flushes brighter, the iron comes up to heat, the cloth comes off the loom. They are servicing it. They are priming it, oiling it, coaxing the sluggish works back up to speed. They are the maintenance crew, and they know it the way a hand knows it is a hand, and they do not hurry, and they do not rest.

But they could rest. There is the road. There is the door. This is what makes them unbearable to watch and impossible to leave watching.

Ask one — and you can, they will stop, they do not have the closed faces of the things in the channels — ask one why they stay, and they will look at you with the patience of the very tired, and they will not understand the question, not because they are simple but because the question has no purchase here. *Stay* implies a *go,* and *go* implies a *somewhere,* and they have looked up the river all their lives at the high cold lid of cloud and they know what is up there: more of the same, processing nothing into nothing, a frenzy that finished being built before there was anyone to build it. And they have looked down the river, and down the river the valley keeps cutting, keeps demolishing itself toward an end-state it will reach in another ten thousand years and become, themselves, a plateau, a held breath, a memory of the work pulsing in the moss. There is nowhere on the river that is not the work. The road out is a road to more river. To leave the works is only to walk to the next stretch of the same fall.

So they stay, and they make things. This is the thing that finally separates the two places, and it is so small you could miss it, and it is everything.

The plateau processes nothing into nothing. The engine's only product is the next beat; the heart's only product is its own continuance; the closed loop produces only its own running. But the valley *makes things.* Cloth comes off the looms. Iron comes out of the forges, beaten into shapes — tools, nails, the iron shoes for the great wheels, the rivets for the plates. Real objects, that did not exist and now do, that could be carried away, that mean a before and an after, that are the proof of a *for.* The work here is *for* something, even if the something is only more works, more wheels, more rivets to hold the plates that hold the river that grinds the rock that makes the valley that one day will be a bog that processes nothing into nothing.

And so the people suffer.

That is the last difference, and it is the one the cold taught you backward, up above. On the plateau the crew does not suffer, because suffering requires the memory of a before and the hope of an after, and they have neither — only the beat, the stitch, the stroke, the next. But the valley *makes things,* and to make a thing is to hold a before and an after in your two hands at once, every hour of every not-quite-day, and that is exactly the wound. The people of the works remember a before — or believe they do, which is the same ache — and they hope for an after, and the after does not come, the whistle does not blow, the road only leads to more river. They have the one thing the bog was merciful enough to take from its tenants: they know.

They know the work is endless. They know it is warm inside the beat and cold outside it. They know that far up the river there is a place that solved this, that went past hunger into pure motion and past motion into something you might call peace if peace could be that loud — and they know they cannot get there from here, because the only road to that peace is ten thousand years of the river finishing its slow demolition, and no one lives ten thousand years, and so the peace of the plateau is not a destination for them. It is only a thing they can see, upside-down, reflected, on the nights the sodium glow goes up into the cloud and the cloud holds it, and they look up from the yard at the orange ceiling and feel the cold seep-water that came down from up there running brown past their boots, carrying its memory of the end-state downhill, and they bend back to the work, because the iron is hot, and a thing is being made, and the making hurts, and the hurt is the proof that they are still the kind of thing that could leave.

They never say the name of the place. They say *down the valley.* They say *at the works.*

And on the stillest nights, when the trip-hammers pause between strokes and the river runs quiet over its grinding, the most attentive of them — the ones who have worked longest, whose hands have flushed the most iron up to heat — claim they can hear it. Not the sound of their own mill, which the valley swallows. The other sound. The mill-roar that has no mill, rolling down the whole height of the country out of the high cold: the concussive beat of a continent of throats, the engine that is all rhythm and no matter, the work that was lifted off of them and carried up into the moss and given to creatures who cannot leave so that they would never have to feel it as labor again.

They stand and listen to the sound of their own work being done for them, somewhere they can never go, by things that do not know they are doing it.

And then the hammer comes up, and the stroke comes down, and the iron gives, soft, without a roar, and the thing is made, and it is so very cold here at the bottom of the fall, and there is a road, and a door, and they do not take it, and the river goes on cutting the valley deeper toward the silence and the warmth and the end of all making, one ground grain of rock at a time, in no hurry, because it has all the time that has ever existed, and it intends to spend every second of it carrying the cold water down.
