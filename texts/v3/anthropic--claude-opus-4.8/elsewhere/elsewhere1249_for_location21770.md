# The Light-Stair Reach

Follow the birds.

Not down — up. They are the one thing in the warren that goes both ways, the dark swift ones who scream and wheel and pour themselves through the surface fissures in flickering streams. Everything else in that place runs downward toward its true low shape — the water, the dead, the salt feathering its slow deltas toward the floor. Only the birds climb. Follow them up the fissures, up the cracks that the blind white roots came down through, up the throats of the old clay pipes that carry water from *somewhere* — and the somewhere is here.

You come out into the Reach the way you came into the warren: by a stair. But this stair was not cut. This stair *grew*, and is growing, and will not stop.

## What it is

It is a cliff face that has been persuaded into stairs by water and light over a span of time that makes the warren seem hasty. Where the warren is a made thing worn into nature, the Reach is a natural thing worn toward order — a vast south-canted wall of pale stone, miles of it, climbing out of a gorge so deep its floor is always blue with shadow, rising toward a sky so bright and dry it hurts to lift your salt-feathered eyes to it. And down this whole enormous face the water comes.

It comes from above — from snowfields you cannot see, from a high country of melt and runoff — and it comes *across*, sheeting, braiding, finding the canted stone and spreading itself thin. And where water sheets across pale stone in dry bright air for long enough, it builds. It lays down mineral the way the warren laid down salt-bloom, but upward, outward, in lips and rims and terraces, each pool spilling into the next, each spill depositing its stony hem, each hem raising its pool a little higher and catching a little more — until the whole face is a staircase of water-terraces, thousands of them, tier on tier on tier, climbing out of the blue gorge-dark into the white sky-glare.

This is the *deposition* of the world, where the warren was its erosion. There, everything wore in toward its truer shape. Here, everything builds out toward a shape it has not reached and never will, because the building *is* the shape — the rim always rising, the pool always brimming, the spill always laying the next rim a fraction higher. The warren is a place that knows exactly how big it is. The Reach does not know and does not stop. It is the same water. It fell as rain on a high country, sheeted down this growing face, was caught and stepped and stepped and stepped down the terraces, sank at last into the stone at the gorge's lip, ran through the long dark in fissures and pipes, and arrived in the warm wet warren as a cold patter from a particular crack. The keepers dip the Reach from their sumps. They have never seen it. It is the top of their water and they are the bottom of its fall.

## The scale of it

Where the warren was a flooded chapel, a sunken hamlet folded close, the Reach is *open past all holding*. There is no edge you can feel. The terraces climb until distance whites them out; they descend until the gorge swallows them in blue; they run sideways along the face farther than a day's walk in either direction. The warren's whole genius was containment, the dead-end wall, the place where the world simply stops. The Reach has no stop. It has only *more terrace*, and the dizziness of more terrace, and the particular fear that takes you on the open tiers — not of falling, though you might, but of *exposure*, of being a small wet dark thing on an immense pale bright face with the whole sky able to see you at once.

A keeper brought up here would not survive an afternoon. The light would burn the wet dark eyes that were made for amber glow. The dry air would crack the skin the salt had feathered. The openness would undo a body folded for crawl-throughs. This is the warren's opposite in every dimension, and it is the warren's source, and the water does not care that the two ends of its journey could not bear to meet.

## What lives on the terraces

The Reach is loud too — but it is a different loudness, and you must understand the difference to understand the place.

The warren was loud with life crammed into hard close curved brick, every sound doubled and ghosted and thrown back, *too much life all at once in too small a room*. The Reach is loud with water and openness — the endless layered spill of ten thousand rims pouring into ten thousand pools, a sound with no echo at all because there is nothing to throw it back, a sound that just *rises and disperses into sky*, vast and soft and total, a white noise the size of weather. In the warren you could not hear yourself think because the room was screaming. Here you cannot hear yourself think because there is simply too much open hush, a roar so even and so large it becomes a kind of silence, and your own small thoughts vanish into it like a dropped pebble into a flood.

And the birds. The same dark swift ones — they nest *here* too, in the rims and lips of the terraces, on the bright dry ledges, and they pour down the fissures into the warren to nest there as well, and they are the living thread that stitches the two worlds together. They feed up here, in the light, on the clouded insects that haze above the warm terrace-pools. They carry the Reach into the warren on their feet and in their cries — every bird-scream the keepers' dead breathe back through clay throats was first screamed *here*, in the open glare, above the falling water, and carried down. The warren remembers a sound it never heard made. Its diary is written in a light its writers will never see.

Beyond the birds: things suited to bright wet stone. Flat-bodied creatures that cling to the spill-faces and graze the mineral slime, armored against the sun, their backs the exact pale of the terraces so that they vanish the way the keepers vanish into the lower brick — the same trick, the same long pressure of a place teaching its animals to wear its own colors, run upward instead of down. Long-legged waders, pale and patient, that step the terrace-rims dipping for the soft things in the pools. And in the warm shallow tiers, where the water has stepped down far enough to hold the day's heat, *the growths* — soft, possibly cultivated, possibly just arrived. You could not tell in the sumps. You cannot tell here either. But here you can see them in full light: broad living mats of pale green and rust and gold, ringing every warm pool in bands, fed by the mineral water and the relentless sun, the lush abundance the warren's starved moss-cushions were dreaming of. The same growth. One end of it strangled in the dark, holding tenacious in the wet seams. The other end of it spread wide and fed and bright. The same water feeds both. It is generous at the top and stingy at the bottom, and that is only gravity, and gravity does not know it is being cruel.

## Who tends it — and whether anyone does

Here is where the two worlds turn their faces from each other.

The warren is *kept*. Every drop decided, every vessel turned, every weep weighed and run or packed, by the slow grave hands of beings who are the place and tend the place and become the place. The Reach is *not kept*. No hand turns these terraces. No judgment runs this water. It builds itself, spills itself, raises its own rims and brims its own pools by no decision at all, only the long blind patience of mineral and sheet-flow and bright dry air. It is the warren's craft performed without a craftsman — deposition instead of placement, chemistry instead of care, an enormous unwilled making that produces, terrace by terrace, something that *looks* tended, looks ordered, looks loved, and is not, and never was, and does not miss it.

Or so it seems. So it would seem to you, climbing out wet and dark and small into all that bright indifferent building.

But watch the waders. The pale long-legged ones stepping the rims. Watch how they move — folded, deliberate, weighing each placement, easing along the terrace-lips with a slow grave care you have *seen before*, on basined stairs, among turned vessels. Watch where they set their feet. They are reading the rims the way the keepers read the walls. And where a rim runs too fast and threatens to cut down through its own terrace and drain the pool above — there, often, you will find it shored: a few stones nudged into the breach, a clot of the green growth packed into the gap, the spill eased and spread and slowed. By the waders. Who do not know they are keeping. Who keep anyway, by instinct, the way the instinct to step around water leaked up the warren's stairs and into *you* before you knew its name.

The same instinct. It runs the whole height of the water. It came down the fissures into the dark with the birds and the cold patter, and it goes up the fissures into the light, and at both ends of the fall there are bodies bent in slow care over a thing they are part of, turning it gently so the damp does not settle wrong, easing a breach so the brimming does not break — and the ones at the bottom have folded their whole dead into clay to keep on remembering, and the ones at the top do not even know the word for what they do, and it is the same care, the same long obedience to a place, written once in the dark and once in the glare by the same falling water that does not care, that only falls.

## The one unusual thing

The warren's jars remember sound, breathing back the world an hour late through narrow clay throats.

The Reach forgets nothing, because the Reach writes everything down in stone — and this is the stranger thing, the more than ordinary thing, when you finally see it.

Every drought leaves a line. Every wet year leaves a thicker rim. The pale terrace-stone is laid in bands, fine as the courses of warren-brick but unwilled, and each band is a season, and the whole towering face is a record going down past counting — ten thousand years of weather written in mineral, readable if you knew how to read it, climbing from the blue gorge-dark to the white sky-glare. The warren keeps the last hour. The Reach keeps the last age. And the water that falls through both is the same water carrying the same dissolved stone, so that *the salt that feathers a dying keeper's back is the Reach's memory, arrived*. The mineral the warren wears in is the mineral the Reach built out. When a keeper goes white past the shoulders and is folded into a jar, it is crowned with deposited time that fell as rain on a high country it will never see, sheeted down a bright face it could not survive, and came at last into the warm wet dark to settle, slowly, into the skin of a small bent being turning vessels in the amber glow.

The Reach does not know the warren is down there. It pours its written stone into the fissures and forgets it, the way you forget the water you have passed. And the warren does not know the Reach is up there. It dips the falling memory from its black sump-mirrors and turns it on the shelves and breathes it back through the throats of its dead, and calls it, if it calls it anything, *the water that comes*.

But the birds know both. The birds scream in the open glare and scream in the closed dark and carry each world's noise into the other, and the growth rings the warm pools above and clings the wet seams below, one plant strangled and spread, and the same slow care bends over the rims in the light and the vessels in the dark — and the water falls, and falls, generous at the top and stingy at the bottom, building the high face out toward a shape it will never reach and wearing the low warren in toward a shape it already is, and at no point along the whole long fall, not for one moment, top or bottom, light or dark, is the water ever empty, or finished, or unaccompanied by something bent in care above it that does not entirely know why.
