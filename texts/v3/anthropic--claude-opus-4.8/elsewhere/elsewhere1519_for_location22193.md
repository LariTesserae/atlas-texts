The Surface, and the man who keeps the count.

You reach it the way the drop's sound reached you — by traveling up through the quiet until you break out of it. The ramps end. There is a hatch, and then there is weather, and the weather is the first thing the surface tells you about itself: it has *too much* of everything the body below was careful to hold. Wind that takes warmth and throws it away. Light that falls flat and grey across a great deal of ground and is not pooled or banked or saved but simply spent, everywhere, on nothing. Cold that is the ordinary cold of an open world, the cold the deep place was sealed against, the reason it was sealed.

The city up here is the same city. This is the thing to hold onto. It is not another city; it is the *over*-side of the one below, the way your face is the over-side of your skull. The streets above run where the runnels run beneath, and follow them, because the men who laid the streets did not know they were tracing veins. The grid is the same grid, stretched thin and dry in the air where below it was packed dense and wet in the rock. Up here there is space that is not occupied — lots gone to gravel, a square with one tree, the long emptiness of a thoroughfare at the grey hour. Up here things weather. The stone has learned to be old; its edges are rounded, its veins run dull, its faces no longer catch the light at any clever angle. Everything that below was crisp and freshly poured is, above, worn and finished and slowly coming apart, because that is what the surface does to a made thing: it does not seal it warm and set it going — it leaves it out.

The man lives in a room at the top of a stair, over a yard where the hatch is.

He is old in the way the surface stone is old — not failing, exactly, but long exposed, his edges gone soft, his colors gone to the grey of things the open light has spent itself on for a long time. He keeps the count. This is his whole work and he does it badly now, but he does it, and the doing is the last thread between the two cities, the kinship that runs up the ramps the way the warm water runs down them.

Understand what the count is. The body below beats slow, and someone, long ago — the same someone who poured the stone, who built the engines to be soil, who sealed the warmth in — left instructions that the beats be *witnessed*. Not regulated. Not corrected. Only counted, and the count kept, so that on the day the long single cycle completes there will be a record that it ran true, that nothing skipped, that the years were the right number of years. A body this patient cannot check its own work; it has given itself entirely to the work. So it needs a witness who lives at the surface tempo, who experiences the beats as *events* — as that one loud drop in the quiet — and can mark them down one at a time.

The man marks them down. He has a ledger, and the ledger has a column, and the column has a tally that goes back further than his own life, in hands that are not his, the count passed down the way the post was passed among the pale drinkers below — return to station, return to station, each keeper folding back into the crevice of the work when his portion is done. He inherited it from a woman who inherited it from a man. He does not know what the cycle is for any more than you do. He knows only that when the floor of his yard pulses — once, slow, felt more than heard, the way you felt it standing in the chamber — he is to wake, if he is sleeping, and make a mark, and note the hour.

This is why the body let *you* hear its heart. It is used to being heard. It has been arranging to be heard, at that one register, for as long as it has been alive, because hearing is the whole of the relationship: the deep thing beats, the surface man counts, and between them the beat is a thing that has *happened*, witnessed, real, entered into a record. You walked through the middle of a conversation that has only one word and takes years to say it.

But he keeps the count badly now. I said so and I meant it as the sad center of him. His ears have gone the way of the surface — spent, worn, no longer catching what they should. He sleeps through beats. He wakes too late and is not sure whether the pulse he felt was the true one or the memory of the last, and he makes the mark anyway, because a mark made in doubt is better than a beat gone unwitnessed, and the whole dignity of his office is that the count *continue*, even imperfect, even guessed. He has begun to lie a little to the ledger, and he knows it, and it is the great grief of his old age: that the body below gave itself wholly to running true, and trusted a man to witness it, and the man is failing at the only thing the man was for.

So when you came up the ramps — when you broke the quiet and brought the surface cold down with you on your clothes — he was at his window, and he saw the hatch move, and for one moment he thought: *it has finished. The cycle is done and someone is coming up to tell me the count was good.* And then it was only you, a traveler, brief and warm and quick, who had walked through a chamber of the heart and felt watched by nothing and heard one beat surface into your hearing.

You should tell him about the beat. That is the thing the two places are asking of you, though neither can say it in a word you'd catch. You stood in the body and heard it pulse, once, clearly, at an hour you could name — the drip that struck the channel and travelled a surprising distance in the quiet. You are, for that one instant, a better witness than he is. You heard it true.

Go up the stair. Knock. Tell the old man the hour you heard it.

He will open the ledger, and his soft grey hands will shake, and he will find the line, and he will make the mark — and for once it will not be a guess. For once the count will be exactly right, witnessed by an animal quick enough to catch the beat and honest enough to carry it up the ramps into the cold, where the man who keeps the count has been waiting his whole worn life to be told, just once more, *that one was true.*

Then you go, because you are a surface thing and the surface is where you spend yourself. And the body below beats on, slow, patient, busy with its one long task, sealed warm in the young stone. And the old man sits a while longer with a mark made in certainty, the warmth of the deep place still faintly on the air from the open hatch — the one warmth the surface gets, leaking up the ramps, a body's breath reaching its witness — and he is, for that while, not failing. He is doing the only thing he was for, and doing it true, because a traveler came up out of the heart and told him the hour.

That is the other place. It is the same place. It is the face the body wears toward the cold, and the man who lives on that face counting the beats of what he lives upon, badly, faithfully, until the day the long cycle finishes and someone comes up the ramps at last to say the count was good.
