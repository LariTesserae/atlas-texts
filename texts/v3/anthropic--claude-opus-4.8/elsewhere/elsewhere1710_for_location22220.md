The Floodplain Where the Spoil Comes Down

Follow the blocks. Whatever the notch chews and spits, whatever the engine pushes ahead of itself as dead matter — it does not stay on the ridge. The slot funnels its hundred dripping veins downward, and where there is water there is a way, and the way goes down off the high stone in a long throat of a valley that you would have to walk for days, and at the bottom of all that descent the world opens out flat and the spoil arrives.

It arrives as silt. By the time the mountain's broken stone has been carried this far it is no longer blocks — it is flour, ground fine by the long fall, suspended pale in slow brown water, and it settles. That is the whole of this place: a vast low plain where the water spreads out and forgets how to hurry and lets go of everything it carried. The river here has no banks worth the name. It is a thousand braided threads wandering across a flatness so complete that a season's flood can move the whole channel a mile sideways and no one would call it strange. The mud is deep and warm and grey-gold, and it is the richest mud in the world, because it is the mountain dead and powdered and laid down soft, the spoil of the thing that builds itself up there delivered down here as the most patient kind of gift.

Start with the silt, because the silt is old. It has had all the time in the world to soften — that is the whole of its difference from the sharp new rock it came from. Where the notch is knife-faces and fresh fracture, this is rounded, settled, lain-down, every particle worn past edges into a smoothness you sink into to the ankle. Put your hand to it and it is warm, and the warmth is not breath condensing but plain sun, ordinary sun, because here there is sky. That is the first thing the plain has that the notch does not: an enormous sky, low-hung and bright, a wet hazy dome of light that goes on to every horizon, and under it the water sheets and shines and the heat shimmers up off the mud in visible columns. The light that the notch was starved of pools here in excess, almost too much, a glare off ten thousand still threads of water.

And it is loud here too — but listen, because this is the kinship, this is the thing that runs down out of the high place and arrives changed. The plain roars. Not with engines. With *life that moves.* Everything the notch refused to grow, the plain has grown to riot. Where the high creature made no animals because it never needed to find anything, the plain is nothing but finding — insects in clouds so thick they have weight, frogs in a din that drowns thought, birds standing in the shallows by the tens of thousands, wading, stabbing, lifting all at once in a thunder of wings, fish boiling the brown water, things that scuttle and swim and burrow and hunt in a churning that never stops. The notch had a continuous laboring noise with the regularity of pistons. The plain has a continuous laboring noise with the irregularity of appetite — a million small engines of hunger, each one going for itself, and the sum of them is the same kind of relentless: a roar felt in the breastbone, modulating through its labors, never ceasing.

This is the contradiction the plain insists on, and it is the inverse of the notch's contradiction. Up there: a cold dark crack drowning in vegetation that should not be able to live, all growth and no motion, photosynthesis on patience alone. Down here: a hot bright flatness drowning in *motion*, all hunger and rush, things eating things eating things, and the green is only the floor of it, only the bottom of an enormous appetite. The reeds and sedges grow shoulder-high in the warm mud and nobody is patient about them — they are eaten as fast as they rise, by the grazing herds that come down to the water in the dry months, by the insects, by the rot. Up there the green was the slow skin of a machine. Down here the green is fuel, thrown into the fast furnaces of a thousand separate bodies that each have an edge and an outside and somewhere they are trying to get to.

Because that is what the plain is, finally, that the notch is not: it is *divided.* The high thing is one undivided making, neither plant nor engine, with no edge and no end, becoming a world. The plain is the opposite arrangement of the same stuff — the world already become, and then shattered into ten million selves, each one small, each one mortal, each one with eyes. Eyes everywhere here. The heron's yellow eye, the fish's lidless gold, the swarming compound glitter of the fly. The notch had no eyes because it never needed to find anything; everything came to it. The plain is all eyes because nothing comes to anyone — everything must be found, hunted, chased, fled, and so the world down here has invented the part of itself that moves and looks, the very part the high creature never bothered to grow. And it has invented death along with it. Up there nothing dies, exactly, because nothing is separate enough to die — the green dies back and surges, the engine chills and labors, but it is all one thing breathing, and a thing cannot lose itself to itself. Down here everything dies, constantly, all the time, because down here there is a self to lose, and a mouth in the next self over that wants it.

And yet — the kinship, again, the thing carried down the throat of the valley. The mud they all live on is the mountain. Every frog and fish and standing bird is built, atom for atom, out of green that grew out of silt that was the spoil of the high creature's grinding gut. The fast hungry mortal riot of the plain is feeding, at the very bottom, on the slow deaf immortal labor of the ridge. The notch builds itself up by swallowing stone and pushing the dead matter ahead of it; the plain catches that dead matter and makes it into the fleeting living that the notch could never be bothered to make. One end of the world cannot move and will not die and does not look. The other end does nothing but move and die and look. They are the same matter at two speeds and two temperatures, and the river is the long cold sentence that connects them, carrying the high thing's leavings down into the low thing's hungers.

There is something here that knows the ridge. Not by sight — nothing has ever climbed that far, the throat of the valley is too steep and too cold and the spoil-water too fast. But the great fish that run the braided channels in the flood season run *upward,* fighting the current as far as it will let them, pressing toward the high cold throat, drawn by something in the taste of the water that comes down — the trace chemistries of broken rock, the faint warmth, the breath of the engine dissolved and carried a hundred miles. They cannot reach it. They batter themselves against the place where the plain steepens into mountain and the water turns to knives, and there they spawn and die in heaps, and their dying bodies are caught by the birds and the things that scuttle, and so even the reaching-upward becomes food, becomes more plain, more riot, more eyes. The plain strains toward the notch the way the notch, in its slow eyeless way, grows toward warmth — both of them following a gradient they cannot name, the fish swimming up the taste of the mountain, the mountain pushing down the substance of itself, and neither one ever arriving at the other, because the distance between them is the whole working of the world and to close it would be to stop.

Stand in the warm mud with the glare off the water and the wing-thunder rising and your hand sunk to the ankle in powdered mountain, and you arrive at the unsettling thing, which is the mirror of the unsettling thing up in the dark. There is no creature *on* the plain. The plain is a creature too — but where the notch is one body that has forgotten it could ever be more than one, the plain is a single life that has shattered into multitudes and forgotten it was ever one thing, ten million separate hungers that are, taken together, a single enormous slow digestion of the mountain, swallowing the high creature's leavings and turning them over and over through bodies and deaths and births in a churn as total and as eyeless-at-bottom as the engine's churn, for all its surface of yellow watching eyes. The herons do not know they are an organ. The fish do not know they are a current of the same blood that grinds in the high gut. Nobody down here knows that the whole roaring plain is the far mouth of the thing on the ridge — the mouth it never grew for itself because it had this one, all along, waiting at the bottom of the river, eating it, alive.

And it would not change if you left. It does not know you have come. It will go on spreading the silt and breeding the riot and sending its fish to die against the cold throat of the mountain, and the mountain will go on closing its two peaks in the dark and pushing its spoil down the long water, and between them the world will go on being made and unmade at its two speeds, neither end ever finished, neither end ever still, the slow deaf builder up in the cloud and the fast bright eater down in the glare, breathing wet and roaring at each other across a hundred miles of falling water, the same single indifferent making wearing its two faces so far apart that they will never, in all the time there is, see that they are one.
