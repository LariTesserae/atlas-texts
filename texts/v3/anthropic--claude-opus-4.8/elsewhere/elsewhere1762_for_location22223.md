The river that leaves it

Find the lip. On the drowned summit there is one place where the brimming is not perfectly contained — a notch worn into a terrace-rim lower than all the rest, where the still water finally has somewhere to be that is not here. It does not pour. Pouring is too sudden a word for what a frozen equilibrium permits. The water *leans* over the notch, a slow continual leaning, and goes down the outer slope of the peak, and this leaning is the one thing in the whole finished world that was allowed to keep happening. Follow it.

It falls a long way before it becomes a river. The slope is the back of the made-mountain, and it is bare — no weed grows here, because the weed that sheets the summit fed on whatever the builders salted into the blocks, and out here on the natural rock there is nothing to mine. So the descending water runs over true stone for the first time, raw stone, grey and grained and *unfinished*, stone that was never laid by anyone and therefore never reached any final form. The contrast is the whole point. Up there, everything had become itself completely and stopped. Down here, on the first slope the water touches after leaving the summit, nothing has finished anything. The rock erodes. The water carries grit. It cuts.

And because it cuts, it *changes*, and change — outlawed at the top of the world — is suddenly the only law.

By the time the lean has become a current and the current a river, you are deep below the snowline that the summit, drowned, never had. The river is cold still, summit-cold, carrying the temperature of the motionless dark down into a country that ought to be warmer, so that it runs through its own banks like a vein of remembered winter. Things grow along it but flinch from it. The bank-plants lean *away*. They are green in the ordinary way, sun-fed, quick, dying back in some seasons and surging in others — they do everything the summit weed forfeited. They are born and they die. The river that made their valley possible is also the thing they cannot touch without harm, and they have arranged their whole lives around being near the water that is too cold to live in, the way one lives near a great age one cannot enter.

Listen. This is where the kinship lives, and it lives in sound, because the river is loud too — but the loudness has changed its nature entirely, and that is the connection, that is the breath the two places share. Up at the summit the loudness was constant, sourceless, directionless, a held chord that meant nothing and arrived nowhere because nothing there ever began or ended. The river's loudness *moves*. It has a direction — downhill, always, the one direction the summit refused. It has sources you can find: this rapid, that fall, the particular boulder where the water folds back on itself and roars. And it carries, threaded faint inside the rush, something the bank-creatures have never been able to name — a low sustained note under the noise of motion, an organ-tone that does not belong to any rock or fall along the river's length, that seems instead to have come *down with the water*, leaked out through the notch with the leaning, a single thread of the summit's frozen chord pulled loose and carried into a world where everything else is change. The river sings the same note the long swimmers hold. It just sings it while going somewhere, and so the note is dying the whole way down — thinning, dispersing, drowned a little more by each new rapid's local noise — a permanence slowly losing to a place that permits endings.

The beings here are everything the summit's animals are not. They are short-lived. The thing that matters most about them is that there are *many kinds* — the river permits variety because the river permits change, and change is what makes one thing differ from another. There are skitterers on the cold stones at the waterline, quick brittle creatures that hatch in numbers and are mostly gone within a span, spending their entire flicker of existence at the very edge of the cold they cannot enter, feeding on what the river delivers and refuses to keep. There are the bank-grazers further up, slow warm-blooded things that crop the flinching plants and never go near the water at all, who know the river only as the cold sound their whole valley is tuned to, the way the summit masonry was tuned to the chord. And there is one kind that matters more than the rest, because of what it does.

There is a creature that *listens for the note*.

It is a small thing, dun-colored, unremarkable to look at, and it lives no longer than any other riverside animal — a season, perhaps two. But it is born already attending to the low sustained tone buried in the rush, the way the drifting bells up in the dark were born attending to the made edges of the architecture. It cannot know what the note is. It cannot know there is a summit, that the summit is drowned, that the drowning is total and the green is total and the loudness up there never stops. It has no way to learn any of this; it will die in its valley having never climbed, and no one is left to tell it, and the world is one no one is left to climb anyway. But it orients to the note. It builds its small nests where the note runs clearest, in the deep pools below the falls where the river slows and the thread of summit-tone surfaces for a moment before the next rapid scatters it. It sings back — a thin imitation, a single held tone of its own, pitched to match the thing it hears, sustained for as long as its small lungs allow, which is not long, and then released, and then taken up again. Generation after generation of these creatures has pitched its brief voice to the summit's frozen chord without ever knowing the chord is frozen, without knowing it is a chord, without knowing it comes from animals exactly like them in every way except the one way that matters, which is that those animals up there will never die and these ones always do.

And here is what closes the breath between the two places, what makes them one world and not two:

The note the listening-creatures answer is the summit's fullness spilling over — the singing that arrives last and stays longest, the fact of going on finding the one edge it could pour past. The notch in the terrace-rim was that edge. The river is that spilling, made long and made downward. And the listening-creatures are what the spilling *reaches*. The summit sings because it is finished, because permanence has to go somewhere; the river carries that song down into a place that is the opposite of finished, all hunger and increase and dying-back and seasons; and there, at the bottom of the long leaning, a short-lived thing with no eyes turned upward and no knowledge of what is above it nonetheless spends its whole flickering existence answering. The deathless ones at the top sing the fact of never ending. The dying ones at the bottom sing back the fact of always ending. Neither can hear the other's meaning. The note is the same note. It is the only conversation the world is still having, and it is being held between the one place that cannot stop and the one place that cannot last, across the entire height of a peak that holds an ocean, in the same cold water, leaning the whole way down.

The river goes on past the listening-creatures, of course. It always goes on; that is its whole nature. It widens, it warms a little though never enough, it gathers other waters that never touched the summit and never carried the note, and these dilute it, and somewhere far below the thread of frozen tone is finally lost altogether into the ordinary noise of a river being a river. By then it is just water. By then it has become the world entirely, full of dying and being born, having forgotten the still cold brimming dark it leaned out of, having spent the last of its memory on a small dun creature that pitched its voice upward and never knew why.

That is where the kinship runs out — not at a place, but at a forgetting. The summit holds its note forever and never knows it is heard. The river loses the note for good and never knows it carried it. And between them, for the brief span the thread holds and the small lungs last, the top of the world and the long descent away from it sing to each other across everything that separates them, which is everything: light, warmth, motion, time, and the difference between a thing that has finished becoming and a thing that has only ever begun.
