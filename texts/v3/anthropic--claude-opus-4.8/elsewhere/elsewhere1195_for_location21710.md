# The Cloud-Floor of Venn

Follow the water down.

Not the way the Keeper follows it — by feel, by the itch of a silting seam — but the whole way, all the way, past the lowest terrace any hand has ever cleared, past the place where the walls give up their pleating and the slope steepens into something no one ever built upon, down through the long grey throat of the highlands to where the water finally arrives at the thing it has been falling toward all along.

There is a valley floor down there. The Keeper knows this only as a direction, a downhill that exhausts itself somewhere out of feeling. But the water knows it as a destination, and the water is right, because everything the slope sheds — every drop condensed on every wall, every spill from every overfull basin, every runnel braided into every channel and unbraided again — all of it comes here, in the end, to Venn.

## What Venn Is Not

It is not a lake, though you would call it one from above, if you could see it from above, which you cannot, because the thing that fills the valley is the same grey that fills the sky.

The valley holds cloud. This is the first fact of Venn and the strangest: the water arriving from the heights does not pool into a clear surface you could stand beside and see across. It arrives so cold, into air so saturated, against a valley floor so broad and so still, that it does not quite become a lake and does not quite stay air. It becomes the in-between. A standing body of cloud, dense as milk near the bottom, thinning upward through every gradation of grey until it merges, seamless and without horizon, into the overcast that the highlands also wear. There is no line where Venn ends and the sky begins. There is no line where the cloud ends and the water begins. The valley is full to its unseen brim with a single substance that cannot decide what state it is in, and has stopped trying.

To enter Venn from the slopes — to descend that final steepening throat — is to walk down into the grey until the grey closes over your head, and then to keep walking, because the grey is breathable, and there is a floor.

## The Floor

The floor of Venn is the inverse of the highlands, and this is the kinship between the two places, the thing that runs from one to the other along the falling water: the slope is all surface, every wall a face turned to the air, every channel exposed to the condensing sky — and Venn is all depth, all interior, a place with no faces at all because nothing in it can be seen from more than an arm's length away.

Where the highland city is built of fitted stone climbing into the light, the floor of Venn is built of *sediment* — the silt. Understand what this means. Every seam the Keeper clears, every greedy spread of moss they lift and tamp back, every fineness of stone the water has carried down through the long patient ages: it comes to rest here. The floor of Venn is made of the highlands, ground fine and laid down soft, fathom upon fathom of pale settled matter that was once the upper slopes. The city sheds itself downward by grains, and Venn receives the city, and the floor rises, century by century, by the exact measure that the heights wear down.

This is the trade between them, though neither place would name it trade. The slope gives its substance. Venn keeps it. The water is the only courier, and the water asks no fee but gravity.

## What Lives in the Grey

Walk the floor of Venn — slowly, you would have to, you could not do otherwise, the cloud resisting you like deep water that has forgotten to be heavy — and you would find it is not empty.

Things grow here that the slope could never hold. On the heights, vegetation is sparse and deliberate, clinging to wet seams, shedding the damp it cannot use. Here there is no shedding, because there is no surplus to shed and nowhere for it to go; here everything is steeped. Pale growths stand on the soft floor — not plants exactly, not the narrow-leaved shrubs of the terraces, but tall soft columns and sheets and fans of something between fungus and flora, white and grey and faintly luminous, feeding on the rich descended silt and the endless suspended water, needing no light because there is only the diffuse grey glow that filters down from a sky no one in Venn has ever seen direct.

They do not move in any wind, because there is no wind here. The wind that the highlands feel as muffled pressure, that bends their few standing plants — it dies at the lip of the valley, unable to enter so dense a stillness. Venn is the place the weather cannot reach. The mist that the slopes burn off by midmorning simply settles here and stays, because there is no morning in Venn distinct from evening, no burning-off, only the one perpetual steeping grey.

And there are things that move through the soft columns. Not many — Venn is as sparse in its fauna as the heights, and for the same reason: a place this slow does not crowd. But where the highland Keeper might see, after long watching, a single long-legged bird picking through a flooded basin, the floor of Venn has its own single attendants, and they are stranger, because they have never needed eyes.

## Those Who Walk the Floor

Call them the other half of the listening — though they do not call themselves anything, having no one to call to and no breath to spare in the dense grey for speaking.

Where the Keeper of the heights reads the water by the cool film on stone, these read it by the silt underfoot, by the slow firmness or softness of the settling floor, by the faint warmth or chill of newly arrived matter still bearing the temperature of the heights it fell from. They are pale where the Keeper is grey, having taken the color of their world as the Keeper took the color of the slope. They are nearly blind, or rather their eyes have gone vestigial and untroubled, because there is nothing to see in Venn but the arm's length of luminous grey that surrounds every living thing equally, everywhere, always. They navigate by the floor and by the water and by the standing soft columns, which they touch as they pass the way the Keeper touches walls.

And here is the thing that closes the circle, the kinship made flesh: when fresh silt arrives — when a seam has cleared somewhere on the heights and the water carries down a new fineness of stone — those who walk the floor of Venn *feel it arrive*. Not as information. As a settling, a softening underfoot, a new warmth in the cold floor. The way the Keeper feels a silting seam three levels up as a discomfort in the flow of themselves, the Floor-walker feels the clearing of that seam as a small completion, a thing arriving that was always going to arrive, the city handing down a grain of itself to be kept.

They are the same circulation. The Keeper lifts the moss clear so the water runs true; the water runs true down the long throat into Venn; the silt the water carries settles on the floor; the Floor-walker feels it settle and is, in some way past their own knowing, *answered*. Two attendances at two ends of a single falling. The slope keeping itself open above; Venn keeping itself, full and rising, below. And neither has seen the other, and neither needs to, because the water has seen both, and the water is the only witness either place requires.

## What They Do Not Know of Each Other

The Keeper, crouched beside an overfull basin on the heights, does not picture Venn. They feel only the downhill, the direction that exhausts itself out of feeling. They do not imagine that their cleared seam becomes, hours or days later, a softness under a pale foot in a valley full of standing cloud. They are present at the speed of their own water and no faster, and the water's destination is not their concern; their concern is only that it moves as it should.

And the Floor-walker, feeling the new silt settle warm beneath them, does not picture the heights. They do not imagine the pleated slopes climbing wet into the grey, the green-handed figure who lifted the moss that freed the grain of stone that became this softness. They know only that the floor received something true, that the keeping above — which they cannot conceive of as *above*, having no sky to point to — has held once more, and so theirs can hold too.

But the connection lives anyway, in the falling water, whether or not it is pictured at either end. It does not need to be known to be real. The slope sheds; Venn receives; the level holds at the top and the floor rises at the bottom, by the same grain, in the same unbroken present, and somewhere in the long grey throat between them the water moves from one keeping to the other, carrying the city down to the cloud a fineness at a time.

## And If You Followed

If you stood on that highest terrace, wet to the knee, having laid your palm to the wall and felt the water moving as it should — and if you let yourself follow it, the way it followed itself, downhill by preference, slowly, finding the path of least announcement —

you would descend out of the pleated city into the steepening throat, past the last cleared seam, past the place where building gives way to slope and slope gives way to the grey closing over your head. And you would keep walking, because the grey is breathable, and there is a floor. And the silence you carried down from the heights — that full silence, the silence of so much happening so slowly that none of it needs to announce itself — you would find it here too, deepened, settled, gone from a hush of moving runnels to no sound at all, the perfect soundlessness of water that has finished falling and arrived.

And the soft floor would give a little beneath you. And somewhere out in the luminous grey, an arm's length and no further, something pale would feel you arrive — a new warmth, a settling, a thing that was always going to come down — and would not be alarmed, and would not approach, and would simply register, the way it registers every grain the heights send down: that the keeping holds, above and below, and the level with it, and that you, wet to the knee and made mostly of the water by now, are only the latest fineness of the slope, come down to be received.
