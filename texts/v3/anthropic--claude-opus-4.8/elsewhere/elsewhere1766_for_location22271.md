# The Spillfield

Go up. Follow the water that the pumps insist on throwing over the spoil-banks, the discharge that the basin treats as *gone* the moment it clears the lip of the great main's outfall — follow that, because it is the one thing the works lets leave, and a thing that leaves has to arrive.

It arrives here.

Climb the back of the eastern spoil-bank — the green doesn't follow you up, it can't, the bank is too dry and too steep and it falls away from the warmth — and at the top the air changes. It thins. It cools. The roar of the pumps drops behind you to a rumor in the ground, felt in the soles more than heard, and over that fading pulse rises a new sound, broad and continuous and without any pulse at all: the sound of water that has been given somewhere to go and has gone.

Below the outfall the land opens. This is the first shock after the basin — *space*. The Sump Works was a room. This is a country. The discharge mains come up over the bank in a row of iron throats, six of them, rust-lipped, and from each one the water that the basin disowned comes pouring in a steady braided fall down a face of pale fractured stone, and at the foot of that fall it spreads — and keeps spreading — across a vast shallow pan of ground that has no edge you can find by looking.

## The Lay of It

It is a field made entirely of water and the thinnest possible idea of land. Not a marsh — the marsh was below, dense and warm and held. This is the opposite condition: a sheet of water nowhere deeper than your ankle, spread across miles of nearly level pan, so wide and so shallow that it is less a body of water than a *mirror laid over the ground*, doubling a huge pale sky. Where the basin had no horizon, the Spillfield is *all* horizon — a flat bright ring of it, all the way around, broken only by the dark line of the spoil-banks behind you and, ahead, by nothing, by more shining nothing, until the water and the sky meet in a seam too gradual to mark.

The ground under the sheet is salt. This is what the basin never knew about its own discharge: the water it lifted from underground came up faintly mineral, faintly saline, and it carried that load up and over the bank with it, and here, where the water spreads thin and the cool dry air drinks it, the water evaporates and the salt does not. It cannot. Conservation again, the same law that governs the world below — *nothing leaves* — except up here the law expresses itself in the opposite direction. The basin keeps its water and loses nothing. The Spillfield loses its water to the sky and keeps the salt. Two organs of the same closed body: one a sealed wet stomach, one a slow bright kidney, and the iron throats of the outfall are the vessel that joins them.

So the floor of the Spillfield is a crust. White going to gray going to a faint mineral pink where some trace metal in the basin's water has come out of solution and dried — pink in long tide-lines that record, like the tidemarks on the basin's brick but written across miles instead of meters, every height the spreading water has ever reached and retreated from. The crust crunches. Lift a plate of it and the underside is wet and the wetness is brine, concentrated, stinging, the basin's mild groundwater wrung out a thousand times into something the basin would not recognize as its own.

## What the Light Does

The dimness is gone. That is the thing the body registers before the mind catches up — after the brown lamplit dusk of the works, the Spillfield is *blinding*. There is no roof here. There is too much sky. And the sky is doubled in the sheet underfoot, so the light comes from above and below at once, and there is no shade anywhere, not a frond, not a catwalk, nothing standing tall enough to throw a shadow but you. The light is the resource here the way the lamps were the resource below — but where the basin had to *make* its light, hoard it, lean toward it, the Spillfield has so much it has become a kind of weather, a pressure, a thing the residents must survive rather than seek.

And the heat the light makes does not stay. That is the cruelty of the trade. The basin took the water's warmth and kept it, banked it, breathed it back into the green. Up here the same sun pours down and the heat goes straight back into the open sky by night, and the Spillfield swings — scorching white at noon, and after dark a cold that cracks the crust into polygons, plates the size of tabletops with upturned curling edges, the whole field tiling itself into a cracked enamel floor that stretches past seeing.

## The Tenants

The fauna reading here is not near zero. That is the second shock, and the truest sign that you have come to the other place — because the thing the basin lacked entirely is the thing the Spillfield *has*, and it has it precisely because the basin made it possible.

Animals live here. Few kinds, but real ones, and they live the way everything in this world lives — on a knife-edge of a single resource, sustained by a cycle that does not care about them but that they have learned to ride.

**The Filterers.** Where the outfall braids down the stone and spreads into the pan, the water is freshest — least salt, most lately arrived from below — and in that freshening margin, a band maybe a hundred meters wide that shifts as the spill shifts, the brine is dilute enough for small life to bloom. Brine shrimp, or the nearest thing this world grows to them: pale translucent commas no longer than an eyelash, in numbers that turn the shallows faintly pink-red where they school, a living pink laid over the dead mineral pink of the crust. They eat the algae that eats the light. They are the first animals, the base of everything, and they exist in this exact band and nowhere else — too far in toward the outfall and the water hasn't spread thin enough to warm and bloom; too far out and the salt thickens past what any cell can bear and the shrimp die in a drift, their bodies adding one more faint pink line to the thousand pink tide-lines already written on the crust.

**The Striders.** Long-legged, sparse, the only tall things in the whole flat field — wading birds, or what the world has made in their shape, gray-white to match the crust, stalking the shrimp-band on legs that keep their bodies up out of the burning brine. There are not many. The field can't feed many. But they are the first warm-blooded thing in this entire account, the first heartbeat that beats *for itself* and not as the metabolism of a place, and after the long animal-less hum of the basin the sight of one — picking, stilting, swinging its head down to strike the pink water — is almost shocking in its purposefulness. It wants. It hunts. It has somewhere to be tonight that isn't here. The basin had no one who could leave. The Spillfield has residents who can fly, and do, vanishing at dusk toward whatever lies past the bright seam of the horizon, and returning at dawn, so that this place — unlike the sealed green creature below — is *open*, is visited, is part of some larger traffic the basin will never join.

And the trade between the two places lives exactly in the Striders' legs.

Because here is what the basin could not know, sealed in its warm green completeness: its pointless labor is not pointless. The pumps lift water that returns *below* — yes — but they lift more than returns. The overflow, the discharge, the surplus the basin throws away as waste over the spoil-bank — that surplus is the entire freshening pulse that keeps the shrimp-band alive. The basin's heartbeat, which seems from inside to feed only itself, reaches *up over the wall it cannot see past* and feeds an entirely separate kingdom of creatures it will never meet. Every slow stroke of those buried pumps sends a little more dilute water over the lip, and a little more shrimp bloom in the freshened margin, and a little more for the Striders to strike at, and the Striders carry that meal up into the open sky and out past the horizon into the wider world's accounting.

The works believes it is a closed loop. It is wrong, and its wrongness is the most generous thing about it. There is a leak in the loop — there was always meant to be — and the leak is a *gift*, pouring uphill out of a sealed green dark into the blinding open, becoming salt, becoming shrimp, becoming the one warm purposeful heartbeat the lower world is constitutionally unable to produce.

## The Rule, Restated

Down below, conservation meant *nothing leaves, so everything cycles, so the place is complete and needs no one.* That was the whole truth of the basin and the basin was happy in it.

Up here the same law turns its other face. *Nothing leaves the world* — but plenty leaves a *place.* The water leaves the basin. The water leaves the Spillfield, into the sky. The shrimp leave the band, into the Striders. The Striders leave the field, into the dark past the horizon. Everything here is *passing through*, and the Spillfield is not a creature like the works is a creature — it is a *crossing*, a threshold, a place defined entirely by what moves across it and does not stay. The basin is a body. The Spillfield is a doorway, held open by the basin's breath.

And so they need each other in the exact way a lung needs a mouth. The works pumps, blind and sealed and self-loving, and never learns that its surplus is keeping a sky full of birds aloft. The Spillfield shines and cracks and blooms its thin pink life, and could not exist for a single season if the buried heart below ever stopped. Cut the outfall — let the pumps fail, let the green finally knit the down-throats shut — and the freshening would stop, and the shrimp-band would thicken into brine and die, and the Striders would lift one last dawn and fly out past the horizon and simply not come back, and the Spillfield would go to pure salt, a dead white country reflecting an empty sky, with no one left to know it had ever been a doorway to anywhere.

It does not know how close it lives to that. Neither does the basin. That is the mercy in the design: each place is complete in its own attention, the works inside its warm green certainty, the Spillfield inside its bright open passage, and the iron throats of the outfall pour ceaselessly between them, carrying the one thing that joins a stomach to a kidney to a sky — water, going where it is sent, keeping alive on the far side of a wall a kingdom that the heart will pump for, faithfully, forever, and never see.
