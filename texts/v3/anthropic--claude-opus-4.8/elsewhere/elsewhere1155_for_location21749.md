# The Cold That Was Asked to Leave

Start with the cold, because everything here begins with the cold, and the cold is the oldest argument in the world.

It is not the clean cold of high places, the cold the Saturated Heights were supposed to have and don't. That cold has a brightness to it, a thinness, the cold of altitude where the air gives up its warmth honestly to the empty sky above. This is a heavier cold. It sits low. It is the cold of a thing that has been *refused*, the cold of warmth that was gathered up and taken somewhere else and is now remembered only as absence. You feel it as a draft that never warms no matter how long you stand still, a draft that seems to be always moving toward something — and it is. The cold here goes uphill too. That is the first thing to understand. The same engine that pulls warmth up to the distant peaks pulls the cold up after it, the way a fire pulls a draft, and you are standing in the intake.

The land lies low and flat and gray. After the rounded climbing shoulders of the Heights, the flatness is a kind of vertigo — you keep waiting for it to rise and it does not. This is the floor of the world, or near it: a vast pale plain of fine silt, water-laid, the silt that the Heights have been weeping downhill across all those wet seasons. Everything that erosion *settles* up there has to *fall* somewhere, and it falls here. This is the catchment. This is where the patient milking of the mountains ends up — not the water, the water mostly climbs, but the *body* of the mountains, the dissolved and suspended substance of them, carried down by the minority of water that still obeys gravity and laid out in this immense gray apron at the foot of everything.

So the two places are the same process read from opposite ends. The Heights are warm because their warmth was kept. This plain is cold because the keeping happens elsewhere. The Heights are wet because they are milked; the plain is *dry* — and this is its second wrongness, the inverse of the first — the plain is dry despite being made entirely of what water carried here, because the water that brought the silt was permitted to continue its climb and the silt was not. The water passes through. It is always passing through. You can see it: thin bright threads braiding across the gray, shallow and quick and going the wrong way, *up*, back toward the mountains they came from, having dropped their load and been called home. The plain is the place the water sets down its burden before it answers the summons. A loading dock. A place of transfer, where one thing is exchanged for another and neither stays.

## What grows in a place that keeps nothing

Little, and what does grow has made a discipline of impermanence.

There are plants here, but they are not rooted the way the dripping ferns of the Heights are rooted — rooting is a bet on the ground staying put, and the ground here does not stay put, the ground here is silt that the next bright thread will lift and rebraid into a new shape by what passes for morning. So the plants do not root. They tumble. They are pale spheres of fine filament, dry as the cold draft, and they roll before the uphill wind in slow herds, catching against each other, piling into drifts at the low places and unpiling when the wind shifts. They feed on what the silt carries while it carries it — they are sieves, not mouths, dragging their filaments through the bright threads of climbing water and stripping out the dissolved mountain before it can finish its journey home. Each sphere is a small theft from a great theft. They live by taking back, briefly, a pinch of what the asking has decreed must rise.

This makes them, though they could not know it, the only true resistance in the world. Everything in the Heights collaborates with the slow asking — the frogs breed in its stolen pools, the birds nest in its callus, the whole green machine has folded itself gratefully around the thing being kept. Here, at the cold end, the tumbling spheres do the opposite. They *intercept*. They are too small and too few to matter — the asking does not notice them any more than a person notices the dust that fails to settle in a swept room — but they exist, and what they do is the structural negative of everything that happens uphill. The Heights say *rise*. The spheres say, in their mindless filamentous way, *not all of you*.

## The one who lives here

And there is one who lives here on purpose, which is the strangest thing the plain contains, stranger than the uphill cold, stranger than the rolling plants.

You find it where the silt has been worked. There is a region near the plain's center where the gray is not random — where the drifts have been *arranged*, low long walls of piled silt running in patterns too regular to be wind, channels cut between them so that the bright climbing threads are forced to slow, to pool, to braid through a maze before they are permitted to continue their ascent. Someone has built a delay. Someone has taken the one thing this place cannot do — *hold* — and engineered a place where water is made to linger against its summons, just a little, just long enough.

The builder is small and dark and dense, the only dark thing in all that pale, and it is shaped like nothing so much as a question folded over on itself — a low knuckled body that moves by pouring, redistributing its own mass forward across the silt the way the silt itself moves. It has no face you could speak to. It has the working end of itself, which is many-fingered and always wet, always dragging in the slowed water of its own channels, and it drinks there. It drinks the lingering water. It is doing, deliberately and with terrible patience, what the tumbling spheres do by accident: taking back. But where the spheres take a pinch, the builder has built an *institution*. Its walls and channels are old. They have been maintained across a span that would mean nothing to the visitor and everything to the asking — maintained on the same geological patience, the same slow that the Heights run on, because the builder learned long ago that you cannot out-hurry the thing at the peaks. You can only out-*last* a small part of it.

It is not winning. Understand that. The water it delays still climbs, in the end; the builder takes its sip and lets the rest go up, because to dam the catchment entirely would be to declare a war it cannot fight against a process that is the size of mountains and patient as their growing. The builder is not a rebel. It is a *tenant who has read the lease*. It knows what runs the world — it lives at the cold intake of the engine, it has felt the draft pulling past it toward the peaks every day of its long folded life — and it has decided, with full knowledge, to live by holding back the smallest possible amount that the engine will tolerate losing.

## What it knows

Here is the kinship, and it is a bitter one, and it lives in the cold draft that connects the two ends of the world.

The asking, up at the peaks, is keeping something warm. It is swaddling a slow unborn thing in stolen heat and held cloud, and the entire green roaring saturated miracle of the Heights is the moss packed around that egg. The visitor descended from there carrying only a feeling of wrongness, never guessing he had crossed a nursery.

The builder, down at the cold flat catchment, knows what the visitor did not. It cannot perceive the thing at the peaks any more than the frogs can — but it can feel, in the temperature of the draft that pours past it uphill, the *exact degree of care* being taken somewhere it will never go. The cold tells it. The cold is information. When the thing being kept needs more warmth, the draft sharpens, the intake quickens, the builder's channels run faster and colder and it must work its walls higher to keep its sip. When the thing being kept rests, the cold eases. The builder has lived its whole life reading the heartbeat of a birth at the other end of the world, transmitted to it as a chill on its working fingers, and it has learned the rhythm the way the false birds learned the spacing of the warm arcs — without understanding, but exactly.

And this is the thing it knows that nothing in the Heights knows: that the warmth up there is not free. That every degree the asking keeps around its sleeping child is a degree taken from *here*, from this flat gray refused place, and that the comfort of the unborn thing is paid for in the cold the builder lives in. The Heights experience the miracle as gentleness. The plain experiences the same miracle as *deprivation*. They are the same act. A hand on a sleeping child's back is also, necessarily, a hand turned away from everything that is not the child — and the builder lives in the everything-else, in the cold the turning-away makes, drinking its small stolen sips of a warmth that was always going somewhere better than it.

It does not resent this. Resentment is scaled to a dripping second and the builder, like everything that lasts here, has let that scale go. It has done something stranger and harder than resent. Across all its folded patient life it has come to think of the thing at the peaks — the thing it cannot see, cannot reach, knows only as the rhythm of a cold draft — as the only other thing in the world worth knowing about. It tends its channels and it reads the chill and it waits, with a patience that matches the caretaker's own, for the day the draft will change in a way it has never felt: the day the warmth stops being gathered, because the thing it was gathered for is finally, slowly, *done* — born, or hatched, or woken, or whatever the word becomes across that much difference and that much time.

On that day the cold will lift. The water will be released from its summons and run downhill like water, and the gray plain will pool and green and the tumbling spheres will at last put down roots, and the builder's walls will become the banks of true rivers, and it will not have to hold anything back ever again because there will be nothing pulling at the world.

It is not sure it wants that day to come. This is the final quality of the Cold That Was Asked to Leave, the one that stays after you climb back up into the heat: that the builder has spent so long living in the draft of someone else's vigil that the vigil has become its own. It does not want the warmth for itself. It wants the thing at the peaks to be *well*. It reads the cold the way a sibling listens at a door — left out, left cold, paying for a care it will never receive — and loving, across the whole flat refused distance of the world, the slow unfinished thing that is the reason for its cold.
