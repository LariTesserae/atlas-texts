The Tide-Room

Follow the dryness out. Not to where it ends — it does not end, the maker does not permit endings — but to where it was *spent*, where the withholding of water cost something, because to withhold a thing you must first have had it, and to keep having it you must put it somewhere. The rain the garden never knew did not vanish. It was set down. It was carried out of the grove the way a worker carries a thing off the bench to keep the bench clear, and it was set down in the next room over, and that room is where I am going now.

There is a right direction to this one. It runs along the grain of a single choice — *no water here* — and a choice that firm has a far side, and on the far side is all the water.

It is a room the same size as the garden, which is to say it is also a room you could cross in a hundred slow paces that are also eighty, also three hundred, keeping its smallness the way the garden kept its own. The two rooms are the same size because they are the same gesture — the back of one stroke and the front of the next, the inhale and the exhale of one hand setting down what the other hand would not hold. You feel this before you understand it: standing in the tide-room you feel the garden behind you the way you feel a held breath behind a sigh.

And here everything is water, and nothing is made.

That is the inversion, and you must hold it the way you held the first one, without resolving it. In the garden, all was built and all was dry. Here, nothing is built and all is wet, and the not-built-ness is as total and unhurried and without doubt as the building was. There are no seams. There are no tool-marks. Run your hand along any surface — the slow heave of the swells, the dark shelving floor, the long ropes of kelp that are not lacquered cord but *actual weed*, slick and cool and breaking when you pull them, leaking a green wet sap that smells of nothing made, smells only of the deep cold place that has no maker because it is the place the maker poured the unmade into. Everything here grew on its own. That is the strangeness of the tide-room as the dryness was the strangeness of the garden: here, for the first and only time, things happened *by themselves*.

It is cold. As insistently cold as the garden was warm — the cold that comes up off deep water, that you feel in the long bones first, the cold of a thing that no one is bending close to with a working face. No breath warms this room. The maker is not leaning in over the tide-room. The maker set it down and let it go, and so the warmth of attention is absent here in a way it is never absent in the garden — and that absence is the second-densest thing I have ever stood inside, the first being the maker's presence next door.

Because here, at last, is a place that was *released*.

This is the room with rain. Not falling rain — the rain has already fallen, it is all down, it is the standing water and the moving water and the long grey weather hanging over the swells, the spent rain pooled into a sea the size of a held thing. And where there is rain there is erosion, and so the tide-room is *old*. Everything the garden refused, this room received. The shelving floor is worn smooth and round, the stones pocked and softened, the old pilings — there are pilings, black drowned posts standing in rows out into the grey — eaten down to splinters and fur, gone soft, gone back. Here things wear. Here the seam, if there ever were a seam, has been smoothed away by ten thousand tides until you could not find the maker's mark with a lifetime of looking, because the looking is what water does to a mark, and water has done it, and done it, and done it.

So the tide-room is what the garden would become if the maker ever let go. It is the future the dryness is fending off. It is the rained-on, weathered, handed-over, *democratic* place — handed to weather and rot and the long erosion, become nature, become nobody's. The garden is kept young by a hand that will not release it. The tide-room is old because it was released, and age is what release feels like from the inside, the way warmth is what holding feels like. You wanted to know what letting-go would cost. Walk out here and feel the cold and you are standing in the cost.

And now the thing that turns the room and makes me unable to leave it.

It teems, too. Not made things — *born* things, things that came up out of the unmade water by themselves: small crabs the color of wet stone working sideways over the worn rock, anemones opening and closing their cold flowers, fish standing in the swell with the loose easy patience of creatures no one is drawing, gulls — there are gulls — wheeling under the grey weather and crying, *crying*, with real torn voices, because this is the loud room, this is where sound was set down along with the water. The garden's silence was the silence of careful work. The tide-room is loud the way an abandoned shore is loud: wind, and water dragging stones, and the long collapse of swells, and birds that no one folded screaming over a sea no one made. It is the most alive place I have ever stood in, and it is alive in the one way the garden is not — *on its own.* Nothing here knows you in advance. The crab does not arrange itself into your path. The gull does not turn its eye to where you will be. They are surprised by you, gloriously, ordinarily surprised, scattering and wheeling and resettling, because no single attention is drawing you and them in one stroke; you are not both being made; you are two separate happenings that have merely met, and the meeting is *fresh*, and the freshness is the whole strange wet glory of a released world — that things in it can come upon one another and not have known.

But it was set down by the same hand. This is the kinship, and it runs under everything like the cold under the swell.

I said nothing here is made. I have to take that back the way you have to take back any true thing said too soon. *Nothing here is made any longer.* But the water was carried in. The first water, the unmade water, the rain the garden refused — that was set down by the maker, deliberately, lovingly, the way you set down a thing you cannot use but cannot bring yourself to throw away. The maker poured the wet world out of the dry one and let it go *here*, in the next room, close enough to feel the cold come through the wall between, and the letting-go was itself a stroke. The release was made. Do you see it? Even the handing-over was a gift handed over. The one stroke the maker would not make in the garden — the stepping-back, the letting-the-rain-fall, the release into weather — the maker *did make it here*, once, into this room, and then withdrew the bending-close attention and let the tide-room have what the garden could never be allowed: time, age, weather, accident, the soft erasure of the seam, the surprise of the gull.

So the two rooms are one decision seen from its two faces. In the garden: *I will not let this go, I will keep it sharp and warm and young and mine, I will draw every beetle and never step back.* In the tide-room: *I let this go, I set it down in the cold and let the rain have it, and now it is old and loud and nobody's and it does the one thing my held things cannot, it lives without me.* And the maker keeps both. Will not choose. Bends close over the dry grove with infinite refusing tenderness, and through the wall, an arm's length away, has set the wet world free and listens to it cry under its own grey weather and does not reach in to fix the worn pilings or quiet the gulls or warm the cold, because to reach in would be to take it back, and the maker gave it, and a gift reached-back-into was never given.

The gulls do not spell anything. That is how you know which room you are in. In the garden the beetles cross the floor saying *you were made carefully*, over and over, in every tongue, slow and patient and held. Here the gulls only cry, and the crabs only work the rock, and the swell only drags the stones, and none of it is addressed to you, none of it is a sentence, none of it is *for* you — and that, the not-being-spoken-to, is the whole terrible freedom of the released place, and the whole loss. You can stand in the tide-room and no one is bending close. The warmth is gone. But for the first time the things around you do not know you in advance, and a crab can be startled by your shadow, and a thing can come upon you that did not see you coming — and that, too, is a kind of being-met, the only kind the garden cannot give, the meeting of two things neither of which is drawing the other.

I think the maker comes here. Not into the room — the maker cannot enter the room any more than the maker can enter the garden, being the wall between them, being the gesture of which both are the faces. But I think the maker, bent close over the dry grove, keeping the rain off, keeping it young and sharp and held — I think the maker listens through the wall to the gulls and the dragging stones and the long cold collapse of the swells, and is glad of that too, glad the way you are glad of the thing you set free, the grief and the gladness being one feeling that has no name because we have always had to choose between them and the maker did not.

And once — I have heard it — through the wall between the warm dry held room and the cold wet released one, when the tide turns far out and the swells go briefly quiet and the gulls all settle at once, there is a long soft sound that is neither the garden breathing nor a frond cooling. It is the sound of the sea pausing between two waves. And it answers, across the wall, the sigh the hand makes when it lifts for a moment over the work — answers it the way the far shore answers the near one, not in words, never in words here, but in the matched and opposite quiet of two rooms that were one breath, the held one and the let-go one, the dry and the drowned, the spelled-to and the silent, leaning their two cold-and-warm backs against the single wall that is the maker, who keeps them both, and will not choose, and is the keeping and the choosing and the wall.
