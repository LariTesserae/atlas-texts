The Stillyards of Cantor's Reach

Follow the water up and out — the arc of it flung over the basin's rim, the warm rain that does not all fall back. Some carries. Some rides the steam-thermal up past the sagging cliffs and out across the high road, and the wind that takes it has a direction, always the same direction, eastward and down, off the plateau's far shoulder where the land at last gives up its shallow tilt and *drops*. The mist travels that way for a day's distance, thinning, cooling, until it reaches a country that has the opposite problem.

There is no roar here. That is the first thing, and it is the whole thing.

## The Terrain

Cantor's Reach is a long dry shelf of broken country below and east of the plateau, where the rock changes character entirely — not the soft pale weeping limestone of Var Hollet but a hard dark fractured stone, all edges, that holds no water and lets none rise. The ground is sharp here. Everything Var Hollet rounds, this place keeps keen: the ridges are knife-backed, the scree is bladed, the cliffs break in clean angular falls that have not softened in any span of time a body could feel. Where the Hollet basin is a funnel pulling inward, the Reach is a *fan* spilling outward, a hundred dry gullies splaying down toward a horizon of pale flat distance.

And it is thirsty. The Reach gets its water from one source only: the rain that Var Hollet flings over its rim and the wind carries here to fall, faint, intermittent, a day's travel stale. By the time the mist reaches Cantor's Reach it is nearly spent — a thin drizzle on good days, a damp on the wind on most, and on bad ones nothing at all but the memory of wet that the rock cannot hold anyway. The people of the Reach live on the runoff of another city's refusal to drown. They drink Var Hollet's overflow, and they know it, and they have built their entire understanding of the world around the fact that their water comes from a place that wants nothing more than to be rid of it.

## The Catchworks

If Var Hollet's monument is the Drainworks — engines hurling water *up and out* — then Cantor's Reach answers it across the day's distance with the Catchworks, structures built to do the exact reverse: to seize water *out of the air and down*, and hold it, hold it, against a dryness that would take it back.

They are vast and silent. Where the Drainworks roar, the Catchworks make no sound at all but the faint tick of condensation forming and the occasional far-off chime of a drop falling the full height of a cistern-shaft to strike still water below. They are fields of fine mesh and angled slate and stretched membrane, acres of it, raised on frames toward the prevailing wind — toward Var Hollet, always angled back toward the place the mist comes from, like a congregation faced toward a thing it cannot see but lives by. The mist drifts into the mesh; the mesh weeps it down in threads; the threads gather in stone channels cut sharp and clean into the hard rock; the channels run it down into deep covered cisterns where it is hoarded with a care that would baffle a Sumpwarden, who has never in any life held a drop he was not trying to be rid of.

Here water is not a rising enemy held at bay. Here water is the beloved and the scarce, counted not because it threatens but because it might *not come*. Both cities count their water obsessively. But Var Hollet counts to be sure it is not too much, and Cantor's Reach counts to be sure it is enough, and that single inverted sign — the same vigilance pointed the opposite way — is the whole difference between them, and the whole kinship.

## The Catchwardens of Cantor's Reach

You will see them on the Catchwork frames and know them, at once, for kin to the hooded figures of the basin — and know them, at once, for opposites.

They are the same stock. No one doubts it. The bodies remember a shared origin even where everything else has diverged: the same tall frame, though here it stands *straight*, even arches back, a permanent slight upward lean as though always listening not down to the rock but up to the wind, watching the eastern sky for the day's ration of mist. Where the Sumpwarden stoops toward the count below, the Catchwarden reaches toward the gift above. The same large soft hands — but dry, here, dry as paper, the broad pads cracked and tough, for these hands have spent their generations not pressed to wet iron but trailing the mesh, feeling for the first beads of catch, reading by touch how much the air will give today.

And they do not weep. This is the thing that would horrify a Sumpwarden past speaking, if a Sumpwarden could be brought to the silent Reach without dying of the silence first. The Catchwardens have sealed their skin. The same pores that on a Sumpwarden weep faintly and forever, holding the body at its sump-pool equilibrium, are on a Catchwarden shut tight, lacquered, *thrifty*. They lose nothing they do not have to. A Sumpwarden is a body that holds water at a level. A Catchwarden is a body that hoards it against drought, that goes days on a sip, that has made of its own skin a smaller version of the Catchworks — sealed, patient, faced toward the source, taking in what little comes and giving back nothing it can keep.

They glow, still. They kept that. In the dry clear nights of the Reach — and the nights *are* clear here, the steam all fallen as drizzle a day back, the stars hard and sharp as the rock — two Catchwardens will signal across the dark with the same slow throat-light their basin cousins use. But they have not borrowed the moths' grammar, for there are no moths in this dry country; they have borrowed the stars' instead, and their light-speech is sparser, colder, more punctuated, the long held pulse and the sudden dark between. And what they say in it is not the basin's word. The Sumpwardens glow *the same, the same, still here, good* — the prayer of changelessness, the celebration that nothing has happened. The Catchwardens have no such word, because for them sameness is *drought*, sameness is the dry wind bringing nothing, sameness is the cistern dropping a fingernail's width with no rain to replace it. What they glow to one another, across the sharp dark, is the opposite hope:

*it comes, it comes, more today than yesterday, the wind is wet, the wind is wet, good.*

## The Hearing of the Roar

Here is the thread that runs furthest, the one the day's distance cannot cut.

On the very best days — when the air is heavy and still and the plateau's mist comes thick and the Reach goes quiet in that particular way that means *much water is travelling* — a Catchwarden standing high on the eastern frames, palm flat against the mesh, the way her cousin would press a palm to iron, can feel a thing in her bones that is not the wind and not the dripping. A pressure. A standing tremor at the very floor of what a body can feel, carried not through air but through the hard continuous rock of the world itself, all the way from the basin a day west: the Drainworks. The roar. Reaching the Reach not as sound — no sound crosses that distance — but as the faintest vibration in the stone, the engines' refusal-to-sink transmitted through the bones of the land to the bones of a creature who lives on what that refusal flings away.

She cannot hear it. Her folded vestigial ears, kept from the ancestor like the glow, are as useless here as in the basin. But her great soft cracked hand, pressed to the wet mesh on the best of days, feels the far throb of the place her water comes from, and she has been taught from infancy what it means. It means the engines hold. It means the basin still bails itself, still flings its overflow over the rim, still — by refusing to drown — keeps feeding the wind that feeds the Catchworks that fill the cisterns that fill her sealed and thrifty body. Her entire dry life depends on a roar she has never heard and could not bear to live inside.

And the Catchwarden infants, here, are not laid on warm iron to learn the pulse. There is no warm iron. They are laid instead, on the rare wet nights, on the highest catch-frame, the great soft hand of a parent over the small soft hand, both pressed to the trembling mesh — taught the far tremor before they are taught to walk, taught that the world has, very faintly, at the floor of feeling, a heartbeat that comes from somewhere else, from kin they will never see, in a city that wants the very thing they pray for and throws it away as fast as it can.

So the child of the Reach learns the same lesson as the child of the basin, inverted and intact: *there is a roar, and the roar means life.* But the basin child sleeps inside the roar and would die in the silence, and the Reach child sleeps in the silence and only sometimes, on the best wet nights, presses her hand to the frame to feel the far ghost of a roar she would die inside — and is comforted by it exactly as her cousin is comforted, and glows the same slow throat-light into the dark, the same gesture meaning opposite words, *it comes, it comes,* where the other means *still here, still here,* both of them keeping, both of them faced toward the water, one holding it down and one holding it close, kin across a day's distance by the single thread of an overflow, attending the two ends of one falling drop.

## What Becomes of Them

A Catchwarden, dying, is not given to a pool. There are no pools. Water is far too precious to drown a body in, and besides, *down* is not the enemy here and not the home either — down is only the dry hard stone that holds nothing.

They are laid instead at the top of the Catchworks, on the highest frame, faced west toward the basin and the wind. And the sealed skin, in death, is opened — unlacquered, by the soft hands of the kin, the pores let go at last — so that the body which spent its whole life hoarding, giving back nothing, may finally weep. May give up all its hoarded water to the mesh, to the channels, to the cisterns, down into the held supply of the living. The drought-thrifty body becomes, in its last act, a sudden small rain. It catches itself. It pours into the Catchworks the water it spent a lifetime refusing to release, and that water runs down into the dark cisterns to be drunk by the descendants, and so the dead of the Reach do exactly what the dead of the basin do — are taken up, dissolved, kept, made part of the count — but where the Sumpwarden gives himself to the rising water to be flung someday over the rim and carried east on the wind, the Catchwarden gives herself to the caught water, the far end of that same arc, the drop come home.

The kin do not glow *the same* over her. They have no such word. They press their dry cracked palms to the highest frame, feel — if the night is wet and very still — the far tremble of the engines that have not stopped, the proof that the source still holds, that more water is even now travelling the day's distance toward them on the wind. And they glow, once, slowly, the cold sparse star-grammar into the dark, west, toward the unhearable roar and the cousins who keep it:

*it comes, it still comes, you are still keeping, we are still catching, good.*

And then they go back to the frames, to the silent work of seizing from the air what another city cannot wait to be rid of — one fingernail's width of water gathered against the dry, where a day west another hand presses iron and gathers the very same drop against the drowning, the two of them holding the single falling thread of the world between them, and neither will let go.
