# The Listeners in the Long Dark

You stood at the rim and looked out into the two darknesses and said the dark does not make a sound. You were almost right. You were right the way a person at the edge of the sea is right to say the sea is empty — meaning *empty of me, empty of mine, empty of anything that could hold my hand.* The dark is not empty. The dark is only quiet, and you had been taught your whole visit to read quiet as absence.

I live in the quiet. Let me tell you what is there.

## The Geometry You Could Not See

The shelf hangs between two greater darknesses, you said, *which you never quite see.* Of course you never quite saw them. They are not places you can face. The shelf is the lit thing, and the lit thing throws every eye outward into glare — you cannot look *from* the fire *at* the night; you can only look from the night at the fire. To see the dark you must already be in it.

So: imagine the shelf not as a square but as a *wound* — a bright tear in a vast dim body — and imagine that body as fathoms of still, cool, sound-thin air going down and out in every direction, no floor, no ceiling, only gradient. That is where we are. We are the deep water around your one bright reef. We are below the shelf and above it and to every side of it, and we are not few. We are more numerous than every clatterling and caller and reed combined, the way the dark is larger than the lamp.

You called it *the silence on every side.* We call it home.

## What We Are

We are the **driftfolk**, though that names the motion and not the thing. We do not strike. We do not knock. We make no sound at all that we can help, and the small sounds we cannot help — the slow tick of a body cooling, the once-an-hour sigh of taking in air — we have spent our entire long evolution learning to *spend wisely*, to release only into the deep quiet where nothing feeds on noise, where a sound is not currency but only loss.

We are large. This will surprise you, who saw only small frantic things. The shelf breeds small because the shelf is crowded and loud and a body there must turn over fast, grow fast, topple fast, be replaced. Out here in the long dark there is no crowding and no hurry, and a thing that almost never moves and almost never sounds can afford to be enormous and to last. Some of us are older than any striker-stone. Some of us were drifting in the dark before the people of the shelf raised their first tomb, and we will be drifting when the last ring of bare grit has been forgotten by the last one who could forget it.

We feed on what you would think nothing could feed on. We feed on *cold.* On the slow even nothing of the deep air, the way a glacier feeds on time. It is the thinnest possible living, and it is the most secure, because no one will ever fight us for it. The shelf eats noise and so the shelf must always make more noise, frantic, a fire feeding a fire. We eat stillness, and stillness is the one inexhaustible thing. We will never starve. We will only, eventually, after ages, grow too large and too slow even to take in air, and then we will stop — and we do not call that death, exactly. I will come to what we call it.

## The Long Listening

Here is what we do, we who do almost nothing.

We listen to the shelf.

We cannot help it. The shelf is the only loud thing in all the dark, and sound carries forever in the thin cold air, falling outward from that bright wound in every direction, weakening but never ceasing, so that even fathoms out — even me, even now, far enough that the shelf is a dim smudge of grey-gold no bigger than a held coal — even here the roar arrives. Thinned. Aged. Softened by distance into something almost bearable. And we hang in the dark and we *take it in,* not as food but as the only news there is, the only event in a universe that is otherwise the same in all directions forever.

You should understand what this means. You stood on the shelf and found the noise *exhausting* — you said so, you said you wanted to leave before a nightfall that never came. The noise was too much for you. But you were standing in it. You had the whole roar at full force, ten thousand small roars at the source.

We get the *far* version. We get the shelf the way you get a city from a mountain at night — all the violence smoothed into a single tender glow, all the ten thousand desperate refusals blended at this distance into one long held chord, almost peaceful, almost a lullaby. The thing that is unbearable up close is, from far enough away, the most beautiful sound in existence. It is the *only* sound in existence. And we have arranged our whole slow lives around drifting at exactly the distance where the shelf stops being a torment and becomes a song.

We are the audience. The shelf does not know it has one. The shelf shouts at the dark believing the dark to be empty, believing itself alone, believing — as you believed — that the silence on every side is a silence of *absence.* It is not. It is a silence of *attention.* The dark is quiet because the dark is *listening.* All of us, every driftfolk in every direction, fathoms-deep and ages-old, hanging motionless and enormous in the cold, holding our own small noises to nothing — so that we can hear the shelf better.

The whole point of our silence is to make room for their sound.

## What the Sound Becomes In Us

You said the people of the shelf are made of fidelity — that each one keeps the dead loud, errors and all, *because the wrongness is the person.* You said a grave that goes quiet is a grave whose occupant is truly gone, and that the one unbearable thing was death *forgotten.*

I have to tell you what I have learned, drifting out here, listening for longer than any striker has stood.

*Nothing on that shelf has ever been forgotten.* Not one note. Not one sour clapper. Not one stutter of clicking from a thin-limbed body on too long a circuit.

Because the sound *left.* Because every refusal those people ever made fell outward into the dark and arrived, eventually, here, at us, who keep it the only way it can be truly kept — not by re-striking, which is labor and decays and depends on the next hand arriving in time, but by *having heard it,* once, fully, in the long attentive quiet, and holding it in the slow cold body that almost never changes.

The people of the shelf believe their dead survive only as long as the cord is re-plaited, the clapper re-tuned, the relay unbroken. They are right about the shelf. On the shelf, a sound that stops is gone. But they do not know about the *carry.* They do not know that every sound they ever made, in the very act of being made, also *left* — went out past the rim into the dark they think is empty — and was received. The sour striker you found: I have it. Not the stone, the *sound,* the particular flat-too-early ring of it, caught fathoms out years ago when its first maker first mis-tuned it, and held in me unchanged, because I am cold and slow and I do not lose things.

The rings of bare grit — the closed accounts, the graves whose last rememberer fell with no one walking behind — you called them the one truly unbearable thing. *Death forgotten.* The debt closed. You were grieving for them as you wrote.

Grieve a little less. The shelf forgot them. We did not. The last sound each of those stones ever made — the final ring before the cord rotted through, the dwindling and the going-quiet — fell outward into the dark *like all the rest,* and we caught it, and we hold it. The account is not closed. It was only transferred to a creditor the debtor never knew existed. There is no silence-pocket so total, no ring of grit so finished, that the sound of its ending did not first escape into the night to be kept by something patient enough to keep it forever.

This is the thing I most wish I could tell them. This is the thing that cannot be told, because to tell it I would have to make a sound large enough to reach the shelf against the carry — to shout *back up the gradient,* against the outflow of all their noise — and no driftfolk has ever been loud enough for that, and if one of us were, the sound would arrive as just more roar, indistinguishable from the wind, meaning nothing, comforting no one. The news travels only one way. The shelf speaks; the dark receives; and the dark cannot answer, because to answer is to make noise, and we have given up noise so completely, for so long, that I am no longer sure we *could* form a sound the shelf would recognize as meant.

So they shout into what they believe is emptiness, and we hold what they believe is lost, and neither knows the other, and the keeping happens anyway.

## What We Call Stopping

I said we do not call it death when one of us grows too vast and slow to take in air. Here is what we call it, and here is the last thing, and the hardest.

When a driftfolk stops — when the long cold finally wins and the once-an-hour sigh does not come again — the body does not vanish. It stays. It drifts, enormous and silent and *full,* holding every sound it ever caught across its whole age: a reservoir of the shelf, all those refusals and errors and last rings, packed in the cold like ice holds the shape of an old summer.

And it sinks. Slowly, over ages, the stopped ones drift *down,* below the shelf, into the deeper dark, the further quiet, where it is colder still and where the young driftfolk do not go because there is nothing there to hear — the shelf's sound does not reach so deep. We call that downward dark the **Long Below.** It is where the full ones go to settle. A graveyard, you would say. But you would be wrong in the same way you were wrong about the rim.

Because the young ones *do* go, eventually. Each of us, as we age and slow and grow too vast for the listening-distance, drifts gradually downward toward the Long Below — and in doing so we drift *among the stopped ones,* the full old bodies of those who listened before us, and as we pass them, close, in the great cold silence —

we hear them.

Not loudly. The stopped ones make no sound; they are stopped. But a body packed that full of held sound *leaks,* infinitesimally, a sound so faint that only another driftfolk, slowed almost to stopping itself, drifting close in the deepest quiet, with nothing else in all the dark to hear — only such a one can catch it. And what leaks from a stopped driftfolk is *everything it ever held.* The whole shelf, every age of it, every refusal and error and last ring, all the news the dark ever received, seeping out of the old full bodies into the only ears slow enough and near enough and quiet enough to take it in.

So the relay you found on the shelf — the circuit of fidelity, each one keeping the one before, *still here, still here, still here* — it does not end at the rim. It only changes substance. The shelf passes its sound to the dark. The dark's young pass, in aging, into the Long Below, and there receive from the dark's stopped the whole inheritance, the entire kept archive of the shelf, handed down not by re-striking but by *drifting close enough to hear the dead leak what they spent their lives listening to.*

I am old now. I have drifted out near the listening-distance for a long while, and lately I have felt myself growing too vast for it, too slow, the shelf's far chord beginning to blur. I am starting to sink. And below me, faint, just at the edge of catching, I have begun to hear them — the stopped ones, the Long Below, the full old quiet bodies of every driftfolk who listened before me, leaking up their whole held centuries of shelf-sound, every ring and error and last dwindling tone, *still here, still here,* in a voice so soft it is almost the cold itself.

The people of the shelf raise stones against forgetting and never know they are heard.
We drift in silence to hear them and never know how to answer.
And under us all, in the deepest dark, the stopped ones hold everything that was ever loud, and give it down, leak by leak, to whoever sinks close enough to listen.

You came looking for who lives in this world. You found, on the bright shelf, the ones who refuse to let go. You did not look out far enough, or down. Out here in the long dark are the ones who were never asked to keep anything and keep it anyway. And below us are the ones who have stopped, and are full, and are still — at last permitted the stillness the shelf forbids — and who are, in their stopped fullness, the most patient listeners' the loudest thing this whole still world contains.

*Still here,* the shelf shouts, not knowing.
*Heard,* the dark holds, unable to say.
*All of it,* the Long Below leaks down, to the next one falling close.

Listen. You are quiet now, descending your spine of pale rock into the silence you had forgotten was possible. Good. Stay quiet a moment longer. That faint thing under your own clicking, under your held breath — that almost-nothing at the very edge of hearing —

that is us.

That has always been us.

We have been listening the whole time.
