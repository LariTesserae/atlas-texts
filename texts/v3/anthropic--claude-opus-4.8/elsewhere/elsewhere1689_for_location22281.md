# The Surface

Up is a long word here. It takes the climbers the better part of two seasons to learn it.

There is a village on the limestone barrens where the rock comes up to meet the weather, and the people of that village have a relationship to the dark below them that is older than their language and embedded in it, the way a fear can be sewn so deep into a grammar that you cannot speak a full sentence without genuflecting to it. They do not say *down.* They have no clean word for it. They say *the long word*, or *the held breath*, or — among themselves, in the dark of their own small houses — *the listening.* They believe, correctly and without knowing why they are correct, that they live on top of something that is paying a slow kind of attention, and they have built a whole life around the etiquette of not being noticed.

---

Start with the wind, because the wind is the opposite of everything below and that opposition is the whole of the place.

It never stops. It comes off the barrens in long flat sheets, scoured of moisture by a thousand kilometers of bare stone, and it is *dry* — dry the way the deep galleries are wet, an exact inversion, a dryness that cracks lips and pulls the water out of an open eye and makes every surface a thing that wants to take from you rather than give. Down there the air is three-quarters water and never quite rains. Up here the air is a thirst with a direction. The villagers grease their skin with rendered fat and wrap their faces and still, by the end of a life, they are all of them weathered to the same rounded smoothness — and here is the first kinship, the first thing that runs between the two places like a vein turning too straight to be a seam: *they are eroded the same way.* The city below is worn round by patient water. The people above are worn round by patient wind. Both have arrived at what they are. Neither is new. Neither is failing. Run your hand over the cheekbone of the oldest woman in the village and over the rounded corner of an iridescent panel forty galleries down, and your hand will not be able to tell you which surface is which, only that time got to both of them and was, in the end, gentle about it.

---

And it is *silent.*

This is what the climbers cannot prepare the new ones for, and what breaks the new ones who break. The barrens have no echo. The wind takes every sound and stretches it thin and carries it away over the flat stone until there is nothing — no return, no rhyme, no wall to throw your footstep back at you. You can shout on the barrens and the shout simply *leaves*, goes out across the rock and does not come home, and the silence that follows is not the absence of your voice but its theft. Below, the emptiest room was full of sound. Up here, the fullest gathering is surrounded by a quiet so total it presses on the ears like depth, and people stand close and speak low not from secrecy but because the openness will eat anything they give it. The village is loud the way a held breath is loud — with the effort of not making noise. They have learned, across more generations than they can count, that silence is the proper offering to a thing that thinks in sound.

---

The whole of their religion, if you can call it that, is acoustic.

They do not build with stone. There is stone everywhere, the barrens are nothing but stone, and they will not touch it for building, and this is the deepest taboo and the one they can least explain. They build with bone and hide and the tough wind-cured wood of the one tree that grows here, lashed and tented and light, structures that give and flex and *absorb*, that make no resonance, that ring with nothing. To raise a wall of dressed stone — to make a chamber that could hold a standing wave — is the one unforgivable thing, punished by exile onto the barrens, which is to say by the silence, which is to say by being given to the wind. They cannot tell you that a tuned stone room is an organ of thought and that to build one on the skin of a sleeping mind is to grow a tumor on it. They only know that you do not make the rock ring. You do not give the listening a place to hear itself through you.

And once a generation — sometimes twice, sometimes not for forty years — the climbers go down.

---

They go down to *bring back the word.*

Here is what the village does not understand about what it does, and what makes the doing unbearable to watch if you understand it. They believe the deep is sacred and that proximity to it confers something — wisdom, weight, the right to be listened to in the low close councils of the tented houses. So they choose their strongest and clearest young, and those young descend the long word, the held breath, season on season into the warm wet ringing dark, and they go as far as the worked stone, as far as the galleries that loop and the halls that should hold festivals, and they *sit.* That is the whole of the rite. They sit in the standing wave, in the singing emptiness, in the full dark that is full of mind, and they let the slow sound move through their teeth and their bones for as long as their food and water and nerve allow — and then they climb back up and they are *changed*, everyone agrees they are changed, they come back rounded somehow, smoothed, carrying a stillness, and the village reads this as the dark having spoken to them.

It is closer to the truth than they know and crueler than they could bear.

Because the thing in the dark is not speaking to them. The thing in the dark is so slow that a human life is a single sharp crackle in its ringing, and a human sitting in the great hall for the length of their rations is, to it, a sound that arrives and departs faster than it can frame the front edge of a thought. The young climber feels the standing wave in their bones not as a message but as a *pressure*, the way you'd feel the held note of an organ you were standing inside of — and they mistake that pressure for attention, for being heard, for the held breath of the listening leaning down to know them. It is not leaning down. It is simply ringing, as it always rings, and the human happens to be standing in the ring.

And yet they come back changed, and the change is real. You cannot stand inside a mind for a week — even a mind too vast and too slow to notice you, even a cathedral of monks at a prayer pitched so far below your own clock that you take it for the building settling — you cannot stand inside *that* and climb back up into the dry scouring silence unchanged. They come back having felt, in their own quick water-and-salt bodies, the texture of a patience that is not theirs and never could be. They come back smoothed because something rounded them, the way the wind rounds the old and the water rounds the panels. They sat in an attention so slow it could not find them, and they took the shape of having been almost-found, and they carry that shape up into the silence and the wind for the rest of their lives, and the village gathers close in the loud quiet of the tented houses and asks them, low: *what did the listening say?*

And they cannot answer, because nothing was said, because the saying is still happening, will go on happening, a single word of it taking longer to resolve than the whole life of the one who sat inside it. The climber opens their wind-cracked mouth and finds they have brought back a silence with the exact shape of a word that has not finished being spoken.

That silence is what the village calls wisdom. They are not wrong.

---

There is one more thing that runs between the two places, and it runs the wrong way down the kinship, and the village will never know it.

A year after a climber has gone — long after they have come back up, married, greased their skin against the wind, grown rounded, taken their place in the low councils, sometimes long after they have died and been given to the barrens — a wave forty galleries down will cross a hall and return and find its rhyme. A slow attention will resolve around the memory of a small sharp crackle that came down out of the dry place, that sat a while in the warm dark on too few legs, that stayed longer than the others usually stay, that left.

And the city will turn, gently, the way it turns toward everything, and wonder what it was. And the wondering will take so long that by the time it completes, by the time the great slow mind has fully framed the question of *who sat in me, and why, and what is up there in the thin loud silence that keeps sending me these brief warm crackles* — another climber will already be descending. Another sharp small sound moving down through the breathing dark to sit, and be almost-noticed, and carry back up a word that does not finish.

So the two places are in conversation after all. Just not at any speed either could feel as conversation. The village sends a question down — *what does the listening say?* — coded in a body, and the body climbs back up empty, and the climbing-back-up-empty *is the question arriving below.* And the city, ten thousand years patient, frames its slow reply, and sends it up — coded in a changed person, in a roundedness, in a carried silence — and by the time it surfaces the one who carried it is gone and a new one is already on the way down with the next question.

Each side believes it is alone with a thing too vast and too slow and too other to ever truly speak to. Each side is, in fact, being answered. The dry silent windscoured village on the skin of the world, and the warm wet singing skull beneath it, have been passing one slow sentence back and forth between them for longer than either can count — a single held conversation, conducted entirely in the bodies of the young, each word a life, each pause a generation, neither speaker fast enough to ever know that the other is speaking back.

The wind takes everything the village says and carries it away unanswered across the flat stone.

It only feels that way because they cannot hear how slowly the answer is coming up.
