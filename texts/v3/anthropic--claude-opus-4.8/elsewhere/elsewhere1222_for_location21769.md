The Saltworks at the Mouth

Follow the water out. Not the dew — the dew is the grove's private wealth, drawn from its own cool breath and kept. Follow instead the overflow, the small persistent surplus that even a closed body cannot perfectly reabsorb, the trickle that escapes the lowest reservoir-trunk where a valve has worn slack over the long resolving and never quite seals. It goes down. Everything that escapes goes down. It threads out under the wall of growth on the grove's seaward side, finds a channel it did not make, and begins the long descent out of the green and into the open, where the canopy thins and the light loses its filter and becomes merely light, white and uncushioned, the edge put back on it.

The channel widens as others join it — other groves' overflows, you realize, other slack valves on other sleeping bodies scattered across the high country, each one leaking its small unmissed surplus into the same downward gather. The water that arrives at the bottom is no one's. It has forgotten which grove dreamed it. And it arrives, at last, at the mouth — a flat glaring estuary where the freshwater meets a standing inland sea, and where the heat the grove never knew finally has its say. There is fire here. Not flame: sun. The sun is allowed to land. It lands on shallow grown pans, vast acreages of pressed-fiber basins laid out edge to edge across the flats, and the water that traveled so far to escape the cool is spread thin across them and held still and abandoned to the light, and it goes.

It evaporates. That is the entire industry of this place: undoing what the grove did. The grove condensed; the mouth releases. Upstream, cold air begged droplets out of nothing and hoarded them in swollen trunks. Here the droplets are taken back, given up to the white sky, surrendered. And what remains in the pans when the water has gone is salt — the dissolved memory of every stone the runaway water touched on its way down, crusting out in plates and crystals across the basin floors, harvested, stacked, the whole reason the place exists.

The people here are scaled to *this* place, which means there are more of them than you would think, and they are quick.

This is the thing that would unsettle the grove, if the grove could be made to look: they are fast. Not frantic — efficient, sun-driven, working in the cool margins of the day and sheltering in the white blaze of noon, but quick the way heat is quick, the way evaporation is quick once it starts. They are not budded from a single slow body. They are *many*, separately, and they know it in their tissues the way the grove-people know their oneness. They are dark where the grove-people are pale — sun-cured, salt-cured, their skin gone to a hard glazed brown like fired clay, like the lacquered beetle but baked rather than polished, hardened by exposure rather than by the long shine of repeated reaching. They drink constantly and waste water freely, scandalously, because here water is the cheap thing, the thing that arrives unasked and must be *gotten rid of*, and salt is the wealth. They are the grove's economy turned exactly inside out: where the grove hoards water and makes nothing, the mouth squanders water and makes the one substance the grove can never make for itself.

And they speak. They speak constantly, loudly, across the pans — because they are many and separate and a body of many separate parts *must* announce itself, must call its decisions out across the distance between one worker and the next, must argue. They argue. This is the great difference, the thing the grove resolved away and the mouth never could. The grove stopped arguing with itself because it became one thing. The mouth never stopped, because it never became one thing, because the water that made it came from everywhere and belongs to no one and binds no one into a single sleeping creature. They quarrel over the pans, over the harvest, over whose basin caught the better crust this drying. They have a government, or several, contending. They are vulgar and loud and awake, painfully awake, awake the way only a thing with no canopy is awake, and they would find the grove not peaceful but ghastly — a town gone catatonic, a body that mistook sleep for completion.

Here is how the two connect, in the same breath as the salt and the dew: the mouth needs the grove and the grove does not need the mouth, and neither knows it.

For the salt does not stay at the mouth. Salt is the one thing a closed body cannot grow. The grove condenses pure water from its own breath, drinks it, runs its turgid engines on it, and over the long resolving it slowly, imperceptibly loses its minerals — every closed metabolism leaks salt, sheds it in the dew it never quite reclaims, washes it down the slack valve with the overflow. A body that recycles its water forever would go flat, go fresh, go chemically asleep in a way deeper than its dreaming. It would lose the very gradients that let sap move, that let a valve build pressure, that let the wheels turn. The grove is slowly running down. And it does not know, because it cannot look down, because the looking-down is exactly the part of itself it budded off and sent away long ago and folded the memory back into the wall.

So once in a great while — on the clock of the escapement, perhaps, the slow *tock* that no one at the mouth has ever heard — a single pale node detaches from the seaward wall of the grove and walks the worn path down, down out of the green and into the white, all the way to the mouth, slow and luminous and wrong-tempoed, a sleepwalker arriving in a marketplace. And the loud dark people stop their arguing for a moment to watch it come. They have seen it before. Their grandparents saw it. It comes to trade, though it barely seems to know it is trading — it carries nothing the mouth wants, only its strange cool presence, and it stands at the edge of the pans and takes up salt, plate after plate of it, drawing the crystals into itself the way it drinks the dew, and turns, and walks back up into the green to fold into the wall and dissolve its cargo slowly into the shared channels and restore, for another long age, the gradients that let the body dream.

The mouth lets it. The mouth has decided, in its loud contending way, generation after generation, to let the silent pale thing take what it needs and give nothing back, and they argue about *why* — some say it is tribute owed to the high country, some say it is pity, some say the pale ones are gods and some say they are vermin and most say nothing and simply step aside. None of them know that the salt they let it carry off is the only thing keeping the grove turning, that without these rare slow visits the whole green sleeper upstream would go fresh and flat and finally, genuinely still. They think they are being generous, or weak, or pious. They are being a circulatory system. They are the salt-gland of a creature so large and so slow it does not know it has organs this far away, contending and sunlit and mortal at the bottom of the water it sheds.

And the grove, folding the new salt into itself, feels only a vague returning fullness, like the rain-quickening but slower, deeper, and grows content again without ever knowing it had been failing, and the node that walked down forgets it walked, and the *tock* goes on, and the pans go on drying under the white sky, and the loud people go on arguing over the crust of the water that fled the cool to die in the heat — neither place knowing it is half of one long breath, the in and the out of a single thing too large to feel itself breathe.
