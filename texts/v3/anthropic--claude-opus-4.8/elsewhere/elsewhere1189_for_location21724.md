# The Estuary of Departures

Follow the water down.

The seep that weeps from the cliff-seam and holds its level in the basin — that water is held, yes, but water is not one thing in one place. The same molecules that bead on the underside of a leaf in the high country have, in some other accounting, already left. Water is the substance that connects by leaving. So follow it down, out of the held breath of the valley, down where the rock is older and the falling is allowed, down the long grade of a world that does let go — and arrive, finally, where all the going water goes: the wide brown mouth where the river spends itself into the sea.

Here is the opposite of stillness, and it is just as total.

## The Tide-Flats

The land has no edges. That is the first thing. Where the high country broke in clean angles, here nothing breaks at all — everything is the slow grading of one thing into another, mud into water into mud, the boundary always moving so there is no boundary to find. Twice a day the sea walks inland across the flats and twice a day it walks back out, and the line where it stands is never the same line, so the maps that men have drawn of this place are all of them lies, polite fictions agreed upon for the hour they were drawn.

The mud is grey-brown and it shines. At low water the flats stretch out for what looks like miles, glistening, channeled, braided with the silver threads of draining creeks that finger their way seaward through banks that were not there yesterday and will be gone tomorrow. The smell is enormous — salt and rot and birth all at once, the green-black reek of a place where dying and growing are the same chemistry happening too fast to separate. You breathe it and your lungs do not go quiet. They wake up. Everything here is reaching.

## What Is Present

And the *life* — God, the life. If the high country had set down all wanting, this place is wanting with nothing else mixed in. The mud is not mud; it is a city. Every square hand-span of it works. Ragworms turn in their burrows; cockles pump and filter; the small crabs sidle and feint and brandish; mud-snails graze the film of diatoms that gilds the surface gold where the light catches it. Pull up a fistful and it moves in your palm, a fistful of appetite, ten thousand small lives each entirely concerned with the next moment, the next tide, the next mouthful, the next small fear at the edge of vision.

And over them, the birds. This is where the birds went. All the birds that never crossed the high wet sky — they are *here*, in numbers that stop the breath, because this is the table the going-water sets. The waders come in clouds and settle in drifts and lift again all at once in a single sheeting turn that flashes dark-then-silver-then-dark as they bank, thousands of bodies thinking one thought, and the thought is *food, food, the tide is turning, food*. Curlew probe to the hilt of their long curved bills. Knot pack so tight at the water's edge they make a second shoreline, breathing, restless, redrawn each second. Shelduck sweep the surface film. Everything calls. The air is *made* of calling — the bubbling curlew, the piping oystercatcher, the thin descending whistle of the godwit — layered and ceaseless and urgent, sound packed so dense that silence here would be the strangeness, the thing you'd wait for and never get.

Nothing holds its level. The water rises and the lives rise with it, swimming, floating, climbing the stems; the water falls and they fall with it, burrowing down, scuttling after, racing the drain. To live here is to be in motion every instant because the place itself will not stay still beneath you, and the only arrival is the next departure.

## The Going-Out

Stand at the turn of the tide and you can feel which way the world tips. The river's water arrives here at the end of all its falling — water that wept from some upland seam, that pooled and slid and found its lower level and its lower level and its lower level until lower had nowhere left to go — and it does not hold here either. It goes *out*. The ebb pulls it past the sandbars, past the last reaching channels, into the grey heave of the open sea, and it does not come back as itself. It rises somewhere as vapor, travels as cloud, falls as rain on some far height, and begins the whole long falling again. This is the engine the high country opted out of. This is the wanting that the valley set down. Every drop that the arrived ones hold forever in their stone basin is a drop that *refused this* — refused the going-out, the spending, the becoming-cloud and falling-again and feeding the ten thousand mouths and being spent.

They are connected, the two places, by exactly the thing that distinguishes them. The high country is the held breath. This is the breathing. You cannot have one without implying the other — a breath held only means something against a world that breathes, and a world that breathes so hard, that calls and probes and turns and spends itself twice a day forever, makes the held breath up there not a death but a *choice*, visible, costly, real.

## What Lives Here

And who lives here? Everyone. Everything. That is the answer and it is the opposite of the answer above. The high country had its arrived ones, distributed and singular, a mind laid out so you could know it. This place cannot be known that way. There is no settlement-scale here, no glance that takes it in, because it will not hold a shape long enough to be glanced. The being who lives in the estuary is not one being holding still; it is the *churn* itself, the sum of millions reaching, the standing-wave of appetite that persists precisely because none of its members do. A single knot lives a few short years and is eaten or starves or freezes. The flock is immortal. The mud-city loses ten thousand citizens a day to bill and tide and dies not at all. What lives here is the pattern that survives the constant death of everything that composes it — the exact inverse of the valley, where what lives is the thing that survives by never composing anything at all, by declining to begin the cycle that this place *is*.

The arrived ones up the long grade chose room, and bare stone, and the legible single leaf. Here there is no room. Here every space is contested the instant it opens; a burrow vacated is a burrow seized; a body fallen is a hundred bodies feeding; the bare flagstone margin that the high people leave around each thought would here be colonized before the tide turned, claimed, used, spent, because *use* is the whole law of the flats and waste is the only sin and nothing, nothing is ever finished arriving because arrival is death here, arrival is the worm in the curlew's bill, the only ones who have fully arrived are the dead, and the dead are eaten before they cool.

You could not set down your wanting here. The place would not let you. The smell alone wakes the lungs; the calling will not let the ear rest; the ground itself moves twice a day and would not hold your stillness even if you found it. Up there you could join the held breath, terrace by terrace, until your foot stopped needing the next stone. Here the next stone is already underwater, or already gone, or already someone's roof, and the tide is turning, and the light is going, and the flock lifts all at once in its sheeting silver turn, and the water goes out, and goes out, and goes out — and somewhere, impossibly far up the grade, in a young valley that declined to fall, a single drop holds its level in the stone and does not join us, and we do not wait for it, because waiting is a stillness and we have set stillness down, the way they set down the going, and it ebbs, and it floods, and it ebbs.
