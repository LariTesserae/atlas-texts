The Ridge

Follow the moisture up. Not the drift sideways between basins — go against the grain of the slow breath, climb the gradient that the pollen and the migrating insects ride down, and you arrive at last at the place the basin's air comes from, the place that has none of its own to spare.

The ridge is high and it is bare, and the wind never stops.

This is the first thing to surrender here, the inverse of what you surrendered below: the expectation of stillness, of held breath, of a closeness against the skin. Up here nothing is held. The air does not linger long enough to warm, does not pool long enough to thicken, does not stay still long enough to carry a scent more than a stride before tearing it apart. The wind comes off the cold height in a continuous shear, and it has been doing so since before the hills below rounded down to their present agreement. Where the basin is a closed mouth, the ridge is a mouth always open, always exhaling, breath after breath after breath without a pause to draw one in.

And it is bright. Brutally, totally bright — the light that the basin receives only as a rumor filtered through seven layers of leaf arrives here whole and undivided, hammering off bare stone, off the pale crust of lichen, off the few hard waxen leaves that have learned to turn edge-on and survive. There is no canopy to spend it. The light falls and is not used, most of it, because there is so little here that can use it. This is the strange inversion: the basin, dark, with energy so scarce that every joule is fought over and accounted for, runs thick with life. The ridge, drowning in light, runs nearly empty. Because light is not the limit here. Water is. And the ridge gives its water away.

Let me stay in that giving, because it is the central fact, the way the dark was the central fact below.

Everything that falls on this height runs off it. The rain — and it does rain here, hard, the storms arriving with the full violence the basin never feels, vertical sheets that the wind turns sideways before they land — the rain does not soak in. There is little to soak. The stone is old and close and the soil is thin, a scrape of grit caught in the cracks, and the water sheets across the bare rock and finds the seams and runs, gathering, threading, pouring down the long slopes toward the soft shoulders of the hills and the dark bowls beyond. The ridge is a watershed in the oldest sense: a place that sheds water, that cannot keep it, that exists in part to deliver it elsewhere. The black mirrors in the basin's hollows, the perpetual beading sweat on every leaf below — some of that began here, fell here, was refused here, sent down. The basin drinks what the ridge cannot hold.

So the life here is the life of refusal, of holding on against the giving-away.

The lichen first, because it asks for the least. It is not one thing — it never is — but the same compromise the basin's fungi made, struck here under opposite terms: a fungus and an alga bound so closely that neither lives without the other, the alga catching the abundant light, the fungus catching the rare water from the air before the wind takes it, the two of them together making a crust so thin and so slow that it grows the width of a coin in a human lifetime. It does not need soil. It is the thing that makes soil, dissolving the rock grain by grain across centuries, the same patient reduction the basin's mesh performs on the fallen — but here there is almost nothing fallen to reduce, and so the lichen turns to the stone itself, the only body large enough to feed on, and eats the mountain at a pace the mountain does not notice.

Then the cushion plants, where the cracks have caught enough grit to root. They have learned the shape that fits a place that takes your water and your warmth: low, domed, dense, hugging the rock, every leaf small and hard and waxed against loss, the whole plant a hoarded mound packed tight to keep the wind from reaching in and drinking it dry. Where the basin's creatures grew small and many because life was turned all the way up and there was no room, these grow small and tight because life is turned nearly off and there is no water — the same smallness arrived at from the opposite extreme. A cushion the size of a spread hand may be older than the trees in the valley. It does not hurry. There is nothing here to hurry for. Hurry is metabolism, and metabolism is water spent, and water is the one thing the ridge will not spend.

What moves, moves fast and rarely. A few insects, hard-shelled, dark, flattened to the rock — they shelter in the lee of the cushions in the worst of the wind and venture out in the brief still hours, and when they move they move with purpose, because exposure is death up here, the desiccating tear of the air at any soft surface left out too long. There are no clouds of them. There is no busy threaded medium. The air is not a worked substance here; it is the enemy, the thing that takes, and the few things alive have all arranged themselves around getting out of it. A spider here would find nothing to string — its silk would blow flat against the stone, its prey scoured away. The web that worked in the windless dark below cannot exist in the wind. Different limit, different shape, different life.

And it is loud. This is the last inversion, the one that takes longest to feel. The basin was packed and silent — millions of things listening, none needing to speak, the saturation itself enforcing the hush. The ridge is nearly empty and it roars. Not with life. With wind. The single voice up here belongs to nothing alive — it is the continuous tearing rush of the air over stone and edge and the hard low domes of the cushion plants, a sound with no source you could point to and no pause and no meaning, the exact opposite of the basin's attentive quiet. Below, the silence was full. Here, the noise is empty. A loud place where almost nothing speaks; a quiet place where everything does. They are two answers, the basin and the ridge, to the same old question of how a world spends what it has — and the ridge spends almost nothing, holds almost nothing, and sends the rest downhill in the running water and the torn-away scent and the drift of its few spores riding the very wind that scours them loose.

That drift is the connection, finally. The pollen the basin felt arriving sideways on its slow breath — some of it began as a spore torn off a lichen crust up here, carried down the gradient over a season, settling at last in the dark warm bowl to become, perhaps, part of the under-mesh, part of the engine. The ridge cannot keep its water and cannot keep its spores and cannot keep its warmth, and the basin, hoarding all three in its closed dark mouth, receives the ridge's whole exhalation as gift. The basin is rich because the ridge is poor. The engine below runs on what the height above refuses.

If you stood here you would understand quickly that you were not a surface, the way you were below. Nothing wants to live on you here. The wind would take your warmth and your water as readily as it takes the rock's, the lichen's, the cushion plant's — it does not single you out, does not begin to put you to use, because using is the basin's way and the basin is not here. The ridge does not consume you. It simply will not keep you, the way it keeps nothing, and would let the cold and the tearing air reduce you to grit and run what remained downhill, into the dark, where something small and patient, in the warm hush, would at last begin.
