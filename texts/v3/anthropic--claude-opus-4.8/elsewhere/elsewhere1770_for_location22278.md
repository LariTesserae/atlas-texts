The Cistern Coast

Follow the water down. Not the surge-water that the tenders cycle and recycle in their closed warm loop — that water never leaves, that water is the slope keeping its own counsel. Follow instead the overflow, the small percentage the engine cannot reclaim, the water that brims past the lip of the lowest terrace and falls out of the warmth into the ordinary cold of the mountain and goes, at last, downhill, the way everything that escapes a closed system goes: as loss, as runoff, as a thin bright thread cutting out of the fog and down through the rock-shedding peaks and the timberline and the long dry rainshadow flank the weather-mountain keeps in permanent thirst.

It carries the slope with it. That is the thing to understand. The overflow is not clean. It is loaded — saturated to its limit with the dissolved mineral the tenders spend their lives refusing, the same stone that hardens them at the end, held now in solution because the water is cooling and moving and has not yet found a reason to set it down. The thread runs for forty miles. It picks up nothing else; the rainshadow gives it no tributaries, only sun and a long descent. By the time it reaches the bottom of the world it is no longer a mountain stream. It is a chemistry, traveling.

And at the bottom of the world there is a sea that is dying of the gift.

---

It is not a sea the way the slope is not cold. The wrongness here is the mirror of the wrongness up above: where the high place is hot when it ought to be freezing, the low place is *setting* when it ought to flow. It is a basin without an outlet, a great pale pan of land where the mountain's runoff has gathered for ten thousand years and never once left, because there is nowhere lower for it to go and nothing but the sun to take it, and the sun takes only the water, never the load.

So the load stays. Stays and concentrates and stays and concentrates, until the basin is not water holding mineral but mineral barely admitting water, a brine so thick it has begun to remember how to be stone. The shore is the place where it remembers. All along the rim of the basin the brine crawls up the sun-baked margin in the day's heat, wicks into the flats, and abandons its load there in the evening cooling — and the load, freed of its water at last, does the only thing it ever wanted to do, the thing the tenders spent their whole lives preventing it from doing in the channels:

It builds.

The Cistern Coast is a shore of mineral that lays itself down. It is the same travertine, the same pale stepped crystalline architecture as the high terraces — it is, chemically, the high terraces, arrived by the long way around — but here there is no engine, no surge, no soft creature eating the choke. Here the mineral has won completely. It thickens unopposed. It seals every channel it makes. The coast is what the tenders' hillside would become if every tender lay down at once: a fossil of a circulation that no longer circulates, a whole landscape calcified into the shape of its own last flow. You can read the old currents in it the way you read growth rings. Here the brine pooled and went still and laid down a flat white plain. Here it ran in a thin sheet and built a fan of overlapping shelves, ledge on ledge, identical to the terraces above and silent as the terraces above are not. The surge that the tenders keep alive against all the mineral's pressure — here is where that surge goes to lose. The coast is the answer to the question the slope refuses to stop asking.

And it is beautiful, the way a held breath is beautiful, which is to say only for as long as you do not need it to end.

---

There is life here too. This is the part that takes longest to see, because you arrive expecting a dead shore — and it is a dead shore, in the sense that nothing here moves of its own warmth — and yet.

The brine, before it sets, is a medium. In the deepest of the basin, in the thick still center where the water is heaviest and the sun reaches but does not boil, there are organisms that have made their peace with the impossible salinity by becoming almost nothing — sheets of cells, films, mats that lie on the surface of the dense brine and photosynthesize the only abundant thing this place has, which is light. They are slow past the slope's slowness. The tenders move like sleeping bodies; these move like geology. A mat may take a human lifetime to creep the width of a hand. They are not shy, because there is nothing here to be shy of and no fog to be shy in — the rainshadow sky is merciless and clear, and the mats lie under it openly, doing their one slow chemistry in the open light, the exact opposite of the tenders who hide their whole industry inside the cloud.

But they are the same organisms. That is the second thing to understand, the thing the coast withholds until you have walked it a long time. The mats are descendants — diluted, transformed, sun-bleached and salt-stunned past recognition — of the very life that rode down in the overflow. The slope sheds more than water and mineral. The closed loop is not perfectly closed; every season a few cells of the high place go over the brim with the runoff, the way a few seeds go over a wall, and almost all of them die in the cold of the descent or the salt of the basin. But not all. Some thread of the high living tissue made it down, ten thousand years ago and every year since, and learned to lie flat and slow and salt-soaked under the open sky, and became this — the tenders' impossible cousins, who tend nothing, who keep no engine alive, who lie on the surface of the very mineral their kin spend their lives refusing, and let it have them.

For they too harden at the end. Of course they do; it is the family transaction, carried down forty miles and ten thousand years and still running. A mat that grows old grows slow — slower even than slow — and eats less light, and the brine it lies upon lays its load down beneath it, in it, *as* it, and the mat stiffens into the pale shelf it lay on, becomes a lamina, a single thin course of the building coast. The travertine terraces of the Cistern Coast are not laid by water alone. They are laid by the dead of the mats, course on course, lifetime on lifetime, exactly as the slope's gate-animals are the tenders' dead course on course — the same architecture of bodies, built up instead of cycled through, a monument instead of an engine, but the same one transaction underneath: *lie in the warm light while you can, and when you can no longer, become the stone.*

The coast is built of its keepers too. It only looks abandoned because its keepers never tended anything. They consented from the start.

---

So the two places are one creature's two answers.

Up on the eastern shoulder, in the fog, the soft ones fight. They eat the choke, they keep the channels open, they move toward the heat, they refuse the stone every hour of their lives and only surrender to it at the very end, dragging themselves to a narrowing to become a useful wall. Their whole existence is the holding-open of something that wants to close. They are the slope's resistance to its own setting, distributed across a hillside, audible as a surge — the sound of mineral being kept from doing what mineral does.

And the overflow they cannot reclaim runs forty miles down into the rainshadow and arrives at the Cistern Coast, where the same life, the same chemistry, has stopped resisting. Has lain down in the open under the clear sky and let the setting happen, lifetime upon lifetime, building the very monument the tenders labor to prevent. The coast is what the slope is afraid of. The coast is the slope's runoff become the slope's fate, performed at the bottom of the world in silence and full light, by cousins who do without grief the one thing the tenders do only at the end.

You can stand on the Cistern Coast on a windless evening and watch the brine wick up the white shelves and lose its load to the cooling, and if you have been on the slope you will know what you are hearing in the absence: it is the surge, ended. It is what the pumping is for. Every stroke the tenders keep alive up in the fog is a stroke kept *from* this — from the flat bright silence of the basin, from the long lamination of consenting bodies, from the place where the water finally sets the load down and stays. The two of them are forty miles and ten thousand feet and one decision apart: to tend, or to lie down. And the same water passes through both, carrying the same dissolved stone, looking for the same and only ending — found and refused above, found and accepted below — so that the whole long thread from the hot shoulder to the dying sea is a single creature arguing with itself about whether to keep going, in two voices, one surging and one still.

Stop. Hold still. Up there, the surge. Down here, no surge at all — only the white shelves building in the windless light, course on course, every course a keeper that agreed. And the thin bright thread between them, still falling, still loaded, still carrying the question down.
