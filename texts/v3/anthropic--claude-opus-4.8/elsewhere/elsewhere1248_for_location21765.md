# The Dry Yard

There is a margin, and you reach it by walking away from the pump-thud until it loses itself, and then walking a little further, until the ground under your feet stops being water with a crust of root over it and becomes, simply, ground — and then, further still, until the ground gives up even on being soil and becomes the thing the ward was edging everything toward all along: out.

This is where the things that were edged go.

You will know you have arrived when your clothes finish drying. That damp film the ward sent home on you — it has a range, and the range ends here, in a flat hard place under an uncomplicated sky, where the air has so little to give that it begins, gently, to take. The wind comes across it unbroken. There is nothing for the wind to break against. After the green-throated corridors where the leaves brushed both shoulders, the open is almost an assault: you can see to the horizon in every direction, and the horizon is the same in every direction, a pale seam where the cracked pan meets the bleached sky, and nothing between you and it but distance.

The ground is the floor of something that used to hold water and has not held water in a long time. You can read its history at your feet. The mud that the ward keeps so black and deep and patient — here is that same mud at the end of the only other road it can take, dried past blackness into a pale clay that has shrunk as it dried, and shrinking, cracked, and cracking, made a pavement of plates. Each plate curls up a little at its edges, lifted by the last evaporation, so the whole pan is a mosaic of shallow scoops, and walking it you hear under your boots a sound the ward does not have anywhere in its dense wet body: a dry click, a brittle report, the plates shifting against each other like crockery. It is loud in its own way, the way an empty room is loud. The sound carries. There is nothing to soak it up.

And here, too, the water condenses out of the air — but in the other direction, and at the other end of the day. At night, when the sky goes black and bottomless and the heat the pan hoarded all day pours straight up and away into it, the cold settles, and what little moisture the air holds is wrung out onto the cracked plates as a thin dew, a breath of wet that beads on the clay and on the few hard leaves that live here and does not last past the first hour of sun. The ward drinks all day and weeps all night and never goes dry. The Dry Yard drinks for one cold hour before dawn and spends the rest of the day giving every drop of it back. Same chemistry. Opposite verdict. Both of them, in the end, are only arguments the water is having with the air about which way it would rather go, and the ward won its argument long ago and holds it, and out here the argument is lost fresh every single morning.

This is where the fast things went. You felt their absence in the ward and mistook it for emptiness; here is where the emptiness keeps them, and here they are not empty at all. There is no canopy, so the life does not hide in green throats — it hides in the only architecture the pan offers, which is *time*. It hides in the hour before dawn. The frog you almost saw, the one that was gone before you confirmed it — its cousins are here, or things like it, things that have learned to be a frog in a place with no standing water, which means a frog that is mostly a sealed parcel of damp buried under a curled clay plate, waiting. They do not call either, most nights. But they are not silenced by being crowded out, the way the ward silences. They are silenced by *thrift*. A call costs water — every sound you make, you make by moving wet air across wet tissue, and you lose a little of yourself into the dry with every note. So they keep their throats shut and their parcels sealed, and they spend nothing, for weeks, for a season, for as long as the thrift must hold.

And then it rains.

It does not rain often. But when the dew that fails every morning finally, once or twice a year, arrives instead as a real sky full of water — when the pan that has been only the *memory* of holding water gets its old job back for a few hours — then everything that has been hiding in the hour before dawn comes out into the open all at once, and the Dry Yard becomes, briefly, the loudest place in the world. The frogs unseal and call, all of them, having saved their whole year's voice for this, and the sound is nothing like the ward's patient mechanical thud — it is frantic, it is biological, it is appetite with the lid off. Things mate in the puddles in the few hours the puddles exist. Things eat each other at a speed the ward has not permitted within its fence for generations. Eggs go into water that everyone present knows is temporary, and the eggs know it too, and hatch faster than seems decent, and the young race the evaporation, race the cracking, race the curling-up of the plates, trying to finish being young before the water that made them possible loses its argument with the air again and goes back up into the bottomless sky.

Most of them lose the race. The pan dries, the plates curl, the click returns underfoot, and the bodies of the ones who didn't make it dry down into the clay and become part of the pavement, a pale fleck in a plate, the way the ward's meal becomes orange glaze and even green. Both places eat their dead. The ward eats slowly, on purpose, as its whole way of life. The Dry Yard eats in a panic and then waits a year. But the mud, in the end, takes everyone. It is the same mud. That is the thing you should hold onto. The black patient deep stuff under the ward and the pale cracked brittle stuff under the Yard are the same substance at the two ends of its only choice, and the water is the only difference between them, and the water is always, slowly, on the move from the one to the other and back — lifted by the ward's swallowing throat, lost from the Yard's open face, carried between them in the high air as the cloud that fails most mornings and arrives a few times a year.

So the connection is not a road and it is not a kinship. It is a *circulation*. The ward kept that film of wet on you on purpose-that-was-never-purpose, reaching past its fence one finger's width at a time — and this is where the reaching points. The ward is the place that solved the problem of water by hoarding it, by making itself one slow green animal that never lets a drop go. The Dry Yard is what the same world looks like everywhere the hoarding stopped, everywhere the pumps were never built or fell silent or were never learned by patient roots — the default condition, the baseline the ward is the lush exception to. You could think of the ward as the Yard with a heart installed. You could think of the Yard as the ward with its heart removed and its body left to give itself, morning by morning, dew by failing dew, back to the bottomless sky.

The water knows both of them. The water is the only thing that lives in both. It rises in the ward's hidden pumps and weeps down the sweating iron and pools flat and dark; and some of it, always, escapes the green throat as vapor on the warm exhaled breath of the place, and rides the high air out past the fence that is not a fence, out past where the pump-thud loses itself, out over the flat hard cracked country until the cold of one particular dawn wrings it down onto the curled plates of the Dry Yard, where a sealed parcel of frog drinks it in the one hour it lasts, and holds it, and spends nothing, and waits.

And the rest of the year the Yard gives its water back up into the sky, where it drifts, and some of it — slowly, a finger's width of a journey at a time — comes down again into the warm black mud at the foot of an old riveted tank, and is drunk by a root, and lifted by a throat, and glazes a pipe orange, and holds a leaf up to the even green light, and is exhaled, and rides out again.

You came to the ward and left a little of yourself in its mud to be incorporated. You came to the Yard and the Yard, having so little, takes the last of the ward's film off your skin and your clothes finish drying and you feel, for the first time since you reached in for that cool weeping metal, genuinely dry.

You think the wet is gone. It isn't gone. It went up. It is overhead right now, with all the rest of it, the ward's breath and the Yard's surrender mingled into the same pale seam at the horizon, going around, the way it always has, the way it always will, one dawn at a time.
