# The Surface Station at the Throat

There is too much water here, and it is killing us slower than the dryness would.

You have to start with that, because it inverts everything the people below would expect, and they are the ones I am writing this down for, though they will never read it — they have no word for *down to up*, only for corridors closing, and this is the largest corridor of all, the one that goes the wrong way, toward weather.

We are the ones who tend the throat. I should say that plainly. Below us, somewhere past two hundred meters of green-gray foam-rock, people tap pipes and ration their breath and lay their old against the growing wall. We know they are there the way you know a root system is there — by what comes up through it. Because one of the channels the plant grew, one of the great trunks running its slow resinous weather district to district, runs *up*. It surfaces here. It opens at the throat, and we live around the opening, and we are the reason the tap-water below has the bitterness it does, though they have stopped tasting it, the way you stop hearing the organ note.

---

The surface is not what they imagine. I have heard, secondhand, fourth-hand, the way their account renders us — *the surface, wherever it is* — as though it were simply the absence of their ceiling, a lid lifted off. It is not an absence. It is its own place, and it has its own weight, and the weight is wet.

Here is what is true: the chamber below does not give its plants water because the water never gets down there. We are why. The throat opens into a basin, a wide shallow scald of standing water under a sky the color of wet slate, and it rains. Not always, but as a habit, a temperament — the sky here is in the business of releasing what the deep world hoards. The rain comes warm and constant and we are sick of it the way the people below are sick of dryness, which is to say we are not sick of it, it is simply the medium we move through and resent and could not live without.

The basin steams. The luminous canopy below makes its sourceless manufactured day; up here the light has a source and the source is cruel and intermittent, a real sun that burns through the slate for an hour and is swallowed again, so that we live in glare and murk by turns and never the even mercy they live in. They would hate it here. The unsteadiness of the light. The way you cannot point to the time of day because the cloud lies about it.

---

We are not small. This is the first thing that would frighten them.

Where the deep folk went miserly — sealed, waxen, breathing through cloth — we went the other way entirely, because abundance breeds its own architecture. We are broad. We are loose-skinned and we sweat without thinking, sweat freely, sweat as a *greeting*, because to sweat is only to return a little of what the sky keeps giving and there is always more. Our hands are webbed at the base, not for swimming exactly but for the mud, the scald, the half-water half-land of the basin where nothing is solid and nothing is liquid and you need a foot that can spread. We are warm all the way through. Not banked, not hoarded — warm the way the basin is warm, given off freely, wasted, replenished.

We are loud. I cannot overstate how this would horrify them, the people who speak close and flat to spare the moisture in their breath. We shout across the basin. We call to each other over distances, long whooping carrying calls, because sound travels here — over open water, under the slate — and breath is the cheapest thing we own. We sing while we work. We weep, freely, often, at small provocations, and we have never once thought of tears as a cost. I learned only recently, from a thing dredged up the throat, that there are people for whom a child's crying is *water thrown into the air for nothing.* I sat with that for a long time. I could not make my body believe it. Then I made myself believe it, and I wept, of course, which is the only response my people have to anything that cannot be borne.

---

What do we do at the throat?

We tend it, and tending it means a single long argument with the plant about water.

The trunk that surfaces here is the plant's reaching limb, its one gesture toward the wet world — and it does not reach to *drink*, as you might think, as I thought when I was young. It reaches to *refuse*. The throat is an organ of exclusion. The plant grew it to push water *away*, to keep the basin's wet from running down into the dry green engine below, because the engine runs on dryness, on the difference between living and dead, and water would drown that difference, would rot the foam-rock, would collapse the whole slow scheme into mud. The deep folk think their world *refuses them water freely* as though it were a stinginess, a flaw. It is not a flaw. It is a wall we maintain. We are the wall. We stand at the throat and we keep the flood from going down.

This is the kinship between us and them, and it is a terrible one: they ration the trickle that gets past us. Every drop they condense so jealously from the sap-gas, every careful drip the whole human economy below is organized around — that is *seepage*. That is our failure. That is the water that beat the throat, slipped the plant's exclusion, wept down through two hundred meters of green stone to arrive, bitter, at a tap, where a small folded person presses cloth to their mouth and gives thanks for it. We are the reason they thirst. We are also the reason they drink at all. The same wall does both. I do not know how to feel about this and so I feel everything, loudly, which is our way.

---

We have our beliefs, and unlike them, we have the kind outsiders want, which embarrasses me a little now that I know there are people who got by with only a feeling and a dead lamp.

We believe the basin is a mouth and we live on its lip. We believe the rain is the world remembering itself, returning to the surface what the deep keeps stealing, and that our work — the maintenance of the throat, the dredging, the singing — is what keeps the remembering from being swallowed whole. We believe that everything heavy sinks and everything warm rises and that we are warm, and so we will rise, and so we do not lay our dead against any wall. We float them. We give them to the scald in the wide part of the basin where the steam is thickest, weighted just enough to drift and not enough to sink, and they go out on the warm water singing-distance from shore and we call to them until the murk takes them, and then we stop calling, and that is finished.

I learned that the deep folk lay their dead against a growing wall, and the wall takes them in, and they become part of the slow circulation, fed by it now instead of feeding from it — and I understood, with a lurch I have not recovered from, that some of those dead are *under me.* That the circulation is the trunk. That the trunk is the throat. That on certain mornings the sap-gas venting from the throat carries a richness it did not have before, a body-richness, and we have always said the plant was *offering* on those mornings, and we were right, but we did not know who was making the offering or that they had once had flat close voices and miser's lungs and had loved quietly, like they spoke.

We breathe them, sometimes. The way they drink us.

---

Here is the trade, since you followed the pipe to find it, and a pipe is nothing if not a trade made slow.

It is not a trade either of us agreed to. We have never seen them. They have never seen us. Between us is the plant, which is the floor and the towers and the light and the engine *and the throat and the basin*, the same single distributed body breathing its one low note through two hundred meters of itself — and we are both inside it, at opposite ends of its weather, and what passes between us passes through its tissue and is changed by the passage. They send us up their dead, dissolved, as richness in the gas. We send them down our flood, refused, as bitterness in the drip. Neither of us means to send anything. We are both just living at our end of the engine, doing what our end requires, and the engine moves it through.

I think this is what a world is. Not one place. Two places that cannot see each other, joined by a body neither of them owns, trading the only things they have — their excess, their dead, their failures of containment — and mistaking it, each of them, for the plain conditions of existence. They think dryness is simply true. We think wet is simply true. Neither of us knows we are each other's weather.

---

And we are not yet finished either. That feeling reached even here, up the throat, though we say it differently.

The basin is young. The scald is still spreading; the throat still widening, year on year, the plant still reaching, still refusing harder as the deep engine grows and demands more dryness, more wall, more of us standing on the lip keeping the flood out of its lungs. The slate sky has not yet decided how much water this world will hold. Nothing here has worn smooth. We watch the basin the way they watch their corridors — *that shallows is going to be open water in your lifetime, don't build there* — and we have a word for a place that is going to become basin, and it is not a sad word, it is just a fact about the future, like their winter, like their wall.

So. Who lives here.

Broad loose-skinned people with spendthrift lungs and webbed hands and voices that carry singing-distance over warm water. People who weep at everything and have never once counted the cost. People who tend a wall they cannot see the far side of, keeping a flood out of a dryness they will never visit, for the benefit of people they breathe and have never met. People who float their dead and call to them across the steam. People who have just learned, from a thing dredged up the throat, that they are someone's weather, and that someone is theirs, and that the bitterness in the deep water and the richness in the deep gas are the only two letters they will ever exchange.

We are the parts of the engine that haven't drowned yet.

That part stays with us, too. We say it loudly. We weep when we say it. There is enough water.
