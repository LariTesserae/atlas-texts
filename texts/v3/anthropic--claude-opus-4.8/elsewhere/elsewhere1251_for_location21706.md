# The Well-Mouth in the Far Field

There is a place at the top of the ground where the breath was first drawn, and it is open to the sky, and it is the most ordinary thing you have ever seen.

It is a well-mouth. A ring of fieldstone, mortared rough, knee-high, set in grass that the cows keep short. The boards that once capped it rotted away in some decade nobody marked, and now it stands open — a round black eye looking straight up at the weather, taking rain when rain comes, taking the slow rot of leaves in autumn, taking a magpie's dropped twig, taking the long gold slant of evening light right down its throat for the half-hour each day when the sun stands at the angle to pour in.

This is the place the cistern remembers as *above*. Not the field directly over the chapel-sized pocket — that ground forgot the house and never knew the water. No. The well stands a quarter-mile off, at the field's far hedge, and it is connected to the drowned room the way a wound is connected to the hand that made it: by the channel between. Somewhere in the dark earth a fault runs, a seam of broken limestone, a vein the masons found and followed and widened when they cut the cistern centuries ago, drawing the cool table of groundwater toward the house through this same well. The well was the *taking*. The cistern was the *keeping*. They were two organs of one machine, and the machine is gone, the house is gone, the bucket-rope is a green hook in the dark — but the seam between them stays open, and water still moves along it, and so the well is the one place in all the wide world that the held breath touches.

---

It is everything the cistern is not, and that is exactly how you know they are the same.

Where the cistern is sealed, the well is open. Where the cistern refuses time, the well is *drowning* in it — the stones soft with two hundred winters, the mortar gone to crumb and moss, the lip worn into a shallow saddle on the east side where bucket after bucket dragged across it for the working life of a vanished kitchen. You can read the wear. You can read everything here; the well hides nothing, keeps nothing, holds nothing. It is the most honest mouth in the county. Rain falls in and the level rises. Sun dries the rim and the moss browns. A child throws a stone and the stone is gone, *kept* — but kept by the cistern, far below and a quarter-mile sideways, where it will sink and be found weeks later resting dry on the black film of air, while up here the child has already turned and run home and grown old and died. The well lets go of everything. That is its whole nature. It is the great exhale to the cistern's held breath.

And this is the cruelty and the mercy of the seam between them: *the well does the breathing the cistern will not do.*

For the keeper holds, and holds, and the held breath has to go somewhere — no, that is wrong, it does not go anywhere, that is the whole point, nothing leaves. But the *almost*-going, the slip the keeper catches, the bead it pulls back at the last instant — that recoil has to spend itself. And it travels. Up the seam, along the broken limestone, the small pressure of a refusal re-clenched, arriving at last at the bottom of the well as the faintest tremor in the water there. A ring. A single soft ring spreading on the dark disc of the well-water, sourceless, from below, at long irregular intervals.

*Tok,* says the cistern, a quarter-mile down and sideways.

And up here, a minute later or an hour later, the well-water shivers once, a ring widening to the mossy stones, breaking against them, gone.

The well breathes out what the cistern won't.

---

A man lives near it. Not in the field — there is no house in the field — but over the hedge, in a low farm with a slate roof, and he is old, and the well is on his land, and he has known it his whole life as *the dry well*, because it is not dry, the water stands maybe twelve feet down, dark and clean and cold, but no one has drawn from it in living memory and the name set before the truth and the truth never caught up. He does not drink from it. The mains came through in the year of his marriage and the well became scenery, became the thing the children were warned away from, became the place where the dog once dropped a tennis ball that was never seen again.

He throws things in. He has done it all his life and could not tell you why. A pebble, sometimes, walking the fence-line of an evening. Once, the year his wife died, a brass button off her coat that he could not bear to keep and could not bear to bin. He stood at the lip and dropped it and watched the small splash close over and felt — he would not have words for this — he felt *received*. He felt the thing taken in a way that the ground does not usually take things, taken and *held*, not rotted, not lost, but kept somewhere cool and exact and out of time, the button still bright, still his wife's, still her hand doing it up at the throat against a cold morning, held forever in the first second of the cold closing over.

He was right. He could not be more right. The button is down there now, in the chapel-sized dark, and the keeper found it and dried it — took the wet out, every molecule, with that great tenderness it keeps for fallen things — and set it on the black film of air at the very top, bright as the morning she wore it, and it has not changed and will not change, because the keeper believes that what is held does not change and a wetted button has changed and so the change must be undone, all the way down, until the button has never touched the water it visibly fell through.

The old man does not know this. But some evenings he stands at the well longer than the throwing requires, looking down into the dark disc twelve feet below, and he sees the water shiver once, a ring from nowhere, and he does not know it is the cistern almost-letting-go and catching itself, the bead pulled back, the breath re-held. He only knows the water moved when nothing touched it. He thinks: *something down there.* He thinks: *the old wells were always like that.* He is not afraid. He has lived beside the not-yet his whole life and it has only ever taken what he offered and kept it kinder than the world keeps anything.

---

Here is what neither of them can know — not the man, not the keeper:

The keeper does not retain alone. It thinks it does. It thinks its first and oldest act was to fold the entrance into the held second, to make itself unfindable, to clench against discovery so that no one could ever stand outside and ask. But there *is* an outside. There is a quarter-mile of broken limestone and a ring of soft fieldstone and an old man with a brass button and a grief, and every evening he comes to the open mouth and *asks* — not in words, the keeper is right that words would force a decision — but in the only grammar the seam can carry. He drops a thing. He gives the cistern something to keep.

And the keeper keeps it. And in the keeping, the keeper is *found* — gently, sideways, by a man who will never see it, through a mouth that breathes out everything the keeper will not, into a sky the keeper has refused for two hundred years.

The held breath is not held against the world. It is held *into* the world's open mouth. The clench and the exhale are the same machine, still running, long after the house, long after the kitchen, long after the hand that set the hook. The well takes the sky and the seasons and the magpie's twig and the old man's grief and pours them all down the dark throat to the cool room that will not change, and the cool room holds them, bright and exact and out of time, and pushes back up the seam the small recoil of its refusal, which arrives at the surface as a single ring on still water, which the old man sees, and is comforted by, and cannot name.

*Tok,* says the deep.

The well shivers once, up in the gold evening, where the cows graze and the moss browns and time has its honest way with the stone.

The breath is held below.

The breath is let go above.

It is the same breath.

It has been the same breath all along.
