The Cloud Floor

Follow the water down. That is the only honest way to leave the high shelf — not the path you climbed, but the path the water takes, because the water is the one thing in that city moving at a speed you can keep pace with, and it is going somewhere. It pours off the raw stone, runs through the channels, fills the cisterns, overspills the lowest catchbasins at the city's downhill lip, and then it falls. Off the edge of the shelf. Into the cloud.

Go with it.

You drop through the white. For a long time there is nothing but the white and the cold and the falling, and the sound of the city thins above you until it is gone, until even the loudest of the thousand small falls is only a memory of a hum, and then that too is lost. You are inside the cloud now, the same cloud you came up through, and you understand for the first time that it is not weather. It does not move like weather. It does not thin or thicken with any wind. It simply *holds*, a permanent white stratum wrapped around the mountain's waist, and the water you are following disappears into it and does not come out the other side as rain.

Because the cloud is where the rain *lives*. The cloud is the place.

Come down out of the falling into it slowly, the way you came up onto the shelf — let it resolve. It is not white when you are inside it. White is what it shows to things that pass through quickly. From within, it is a vast soft-lit room with no walls and no floor you can find, lit evenly from everywhere, the light of an overcast that has forgotten where its sun is. And it is full. Not empty the way the city seemed empty — full the way the city actually was, packed with a population you mistook for absence, except that here the population is made of water, and water is fast, and so you can see it.

They drift. The whole cloud is full of drifting bodies of water, and they are not droplets. A droplet is a small thing in a hurry to fall. These have stopped hurrying. They have done with the cloud what the city's builders did with their own quickness — they have learned to belong to a place that is mostly suspension, mostly waiting, and so they have grown slow and large and deliberate, and they have stopped falling. They hang. Some are the size of a fist, some the size of a room, some the size of the city above — great soft lenses of held water sailing through the white at the pace of a sigh, neither rising nor sinking, finding their level and keeping it. The light passes through them and bends and they glow from inside, each one a slow lamp, and there are thousands of them, tens of thousands, fading off into the white in every direction including down, including up, a whole sky's worth of patient luminous water keeping itself aloft.

This is where the city's water goes. This is the population the high shelf feeds. Every overspilling catchbasin, every thread that leaps off the downhill lip, falls into the cloud and is *gathered* — drawn into one of the drifting lenses, taken in, made part of a slow bright body that no longer falls. The aqueducts up there, the cisterns, the careful hand-cut channels: you thought they were managing water for a city that wasn't drinking it. They were. They were feeding it down here. The whole hydraulic genius of the empty town is a delivery system, and the cloud is the recipient, and the recipient is alive in exactly the way the green tenders are alive — slow past your noticing, but not past your seeing, because water at least is something the eye can hold.

Watch one of the great lenses for a while. It is doing something. They are all doing something, and it is the slow work of the place, the cloud's equivalent of the moss tuning its valves. They are *deciding what to fall on*.

Because the cloud is the only thing here that touches both worlds. The mountain above has slowed to the pace of bedrock; the residents have sunk down through the gears of time until a year is a held note. But below the cloud — and there is a below, you can feel the pull of it, the down that the water is always tempted by — there is a fast world. A low world. The world you came from before you ever climbed. Valleys that erode. Rivers that carve. Seasons that turn at a speed a body can live by. Things that are born and grow and die inside a single one of the city's slow seconds. The cloud hangs between the two, a membrane, a held breath, and the water that has gathered into these patient lenses is choosing — slowly, over what would be ages to the world below — when and where to let itself fall again, and become rain, and rejoin the quick.

That is the trade. That is the thing that runs between the two places, threaded through the cloud that joins them. The slow city gathers water at the top of time and hands it down. The cloud holds it, ages it, ripens it in suspension. And then, drop by enormous deliberate drop, it gives that water to the fast world below as rain — but rain that has been *somewhere*, rain that has hung in the held white for an age, rain that carries down into the quick valleys some trace of the patience it learned up here. The rivers that carve the low canyons are carving with water that was once a lens in the cloud, that was once a thread off the city's lip, that was once condensed from the mist on the raw embryonic stone of a mountain still deciding to be a mountain. Erosion has not arrived on the high shelf. But it has to come from somewhere, and it is being *made* here, slowly, in the cloud, and shipped downward — the whole future weathering of the whole low world, prepared in advance in this bright suspended room, one patient lens at a time.

And there is something that tends this, the way the green tends the city. You will not see it any faster here than you saw the moss-keepers there, but you can find its trace. The lenses do not drift at random. There are currents in the white, slow rivers of held water moving in patterns too large to read from inside, and the patterns are *kept*, the way the channels above are kept. Something organizes the cloud. Something decides which lens ripens and which one waits, which falls now and which holds for another age, where the rain goes when it finally goes — over which valley, into which river, to carve which canyon that will not exist for ten thousand years. The cloud is administered. It has a civic life as dense and slow as the city's, and the two civic lives are the same life, really, the same patient people, because the cloud is only the city's lower district — the part of the work that happens below the shelf, in the suspension, at the membrane between fast and slow.

Here is how you know. The birds.

You saw one cross between the towers and called it a messenger, the quick hand of a slow people, an instruction delivered at the only speed you could perceive. You were right. But you assumed the message stayed in the city. It does not. The pale birds are large and silent and they sail off the downhill lip and *down into the cloud*, and here, in the white, you find them again — gliding between the drifting lenses, banking through the slow bright water, carrying their thoughts from the high tenders down to wherever the cloud's decisions are made. They are the line of communication between the two districts. When the city, at the top of time, resolves after an age of slow conversation that more water must be sent down, that the lenses are ripe, that the low world has gone too long dry and the rivers need their weathering — the thought is given to a bird, and the bird crosses the only quick gap in the whole arrangement, off the lip and down through the white, and delivers it. And the cloud, in its own slowness, begins to let the rain fall.

So the single bird you saw was not crossing an enormous quiet. It was crossing the threshold between worlds. It was the city speaking to its cloud. You came up through the middle of that sentence too — the long instruction passing from the high stone to the held water, the order to begin, at last, after an age, the weathering of the world below.

And now you understand the rain in the low world differently. You who came from down there, where rain is ordinary, where it falls in a season and fills a river and is gone — you never knew that each drop had hung for an age in a bright room above the cloud, learning patience, waiting to be chosen, sent down at last by a thought a bird carried off a high stone lip. You felt it on your skin and called it weather. It was a delivery. It was the slow city reaching all the way down through the cloud to touch the fast world with one patient finger, and the touch came as rain, and the rain carved the valley you were born in, grain by grain, with water that remembered being still.

You will go down now. The down has you; it always did. You will fall out the bottom of the cloud into the quick green world and forget, the way fast things forget, that anything slow was ever above you. But the next time it rains — the next time the sky goes white and lets its water down onto your hurrying life — stand in it a moment. That water hung in a bright room for an age. It was chosen. It was sent. Somewhere above the cloud a vine is mid-gesture and a word of welcome is still being spoken, and the rain on your face is the only part of all that slowness fast enough for you to feel.

Let it fall on you. It came a very long way, and it took its time.
