# The Reading Room

Begin with the warmth — but not warmth as comfort. Warmth as a debt. The air up here is dry and heated to a temperature someone chose, a clerk's temperature, the precise degree at which paper will not mildew and ink will not run and a body will not quite fall asleep. It is the warmth of a place that has decided what it is for and heats itself only to that purpose. There is no extra in it. Nothing is given away.

This is the first fact of the place, and it is the opposite of the first fact below: down there the cold held everything in a grip without urgency; up here the heat holds everything in a grip that is *all* urgency, dry and ledgered, every degree accounted for.

---

It is a single great room, and it sits at the top of the rumor.

You remember the rumor — the surface the Tenders no longer swim toward, the lighter darkness too slow to perceive. This is what the light becomes when you follow it up far enough that *up* starts to mean something again. Not sky. Never sky. The Reading Room has no windows, because windows would be a leak, and nothing leaves here that is not first written down. But the room is *lit*, lit fully, lit at a full unit and more, by lamps strung in long rows from a ceiling lost in their own glare. Where the Works had one small private dawn per vent and night between, here there is no night anywhere, no shadow deep enough to rest a thought in. The light is total and it is total on purpose. It is the light you read by. It is the light that reads you.

---

The room is built of shelves, and the shelves are built of ledgers, and the ledgers are the record of the water.

Every degree of every valve turned in the Drowned Works, every single-degree adjustment a Tender made across a decade of bent attention — it is written here. The current that nobody rides, the circulation that is the city's only product and only purpose: *this* is where it arrives. Not the water itself. The water never comes up; the water completes its slow circuit forever in the cold. What rises is the *account* of it, the fact of the turning translated into a number in a column, carried upward through the pressure by some long thin process — a pneumatic line, a wire, a discipline — that runs from the bottom of the ocean to the bottom of the lowest shelf and never once breaks, because it too is part of a system that does not end.

The Works make current. The Reading Room makes *record of current*. And these are the same act performed in two mediums, the way the descended Tenders and the accreted ones are the same shape arrived at from two directions. Down there: keep it moving. Up here: keep it counted. Neither half knows it is half.

---

The shelves rise higher than the light reaches. This is the erosion reading, and it is low — 0.4, almost nothing, the opposite of the riverbed-pebble smoothness below. Nothing up here has arrived at its shape. Nothing is finished or softened or weathered into truth. The ledgers are *new*, all of them, perpetually new, their spines crisp, their corners sharp enough to cut, because the moment a ledger fills it is shelved and a fresh one is opened, and the fresh one is the only one that matters. The old ones do not soften. They are not allowed to. The dryness keeps them, the dust does not gather, the heat that mildews nothing also mellows nothing. Ten thousand volumes stand on the shelves as crisp as the day they were bound, and not one of them has *arrived* anywhere. They only accumulate. The Works rusted toward equilibrium; the Room sharpens forever toward more.

---

And the dynamism is low too — 0.3 — which is the strangest thing about a place so devoted to motion's record. Below, everything was active and nothing was fast: many slow processes turning in eternal balance. Here, almost nothing happens, and what happens is sudden and rare and over. A page is turned. A number is entered. A ledger is closed and shelved and a new one cracked open. Between these acts: stillness. Real stillness, the dry held stillness of a held breath, not the breathing stillness of the deep. The current arrives in a continuous thin stream of data and is set down in discrete dry strokes, and between strokes the room does not move at all. It waits. It is a place of long waiting punctuated by the smallest possible act, performed correctly, and then more waiting. The opposite of a tide. A drip.

---

There is one who reads.

You asked, below, who lives in the place, and the honest answer took a while because the line between the living and the built had dissolved. Here the line is sharp as a ledger's corner, and the answer is short, and it is worse.

There is one Reader. Only one. Where the Works grew a whole staff — descended and accreted, indistinguishable, plural, tending side by side — the Room permits exactly one occupant, because a record verified by two could disagree, and disagreement is a leak, and nothing leaves here unwritten and nothing here is left in doubt. So: one. A single figure at a single desk in the full unleaking light, with the pneumatic line feeding numbers up out of the dark and a pen and a fresh ledger and the next entry coming.

The Reader is not plated. The Reader has not rounded toward stone. The dryness does not grow a skin the way the water did; it does the reverse — it draws everything tight and thin and sharp, papers the Reader over, so that across the long entering of numbers the Reader has become less a body than a *page*, dry, light, crisp at the edges, turnable. Where a Tender's chest holds a banked orange ember not consuming, the Reader holds nothing warm at all. Only attention. Only the next number. Only *keep it counted, keep it counted, keep it counted,* worn not smooth — nothing up here wears smooth — but worn *thin*, worn to the texture of the paper it is written on.

---

What does the Reader want?

The Reader wants the count to be complete. Not finished — the Reader knows the count is never finished, the stream never stops, the Works will keep turning their valves by single degrees forever and forever those degrees will rise to be entered. But *complete in the moment*: every number that has arrived, set down, before the next arrives. To fall behind would be to let some quantity of motion exist unrecorded, some current turn in the dark that no page knows, and that — the unwitnessed turning — is the one thing the Reader cannot bear, the way the Tenders cannot bear the current stopping. The two terrors are the same terror seen from two ends of a line. *That it might happen and not be kept.*

And so the Reader never rests, and the light never dims to let resting in, and the pen comes down, and lifts, and waits, and comes down.

---

Here is what you should know, you who drifted down and settled and plated over and became one more orange glow keeping the water moving:

You are in the ledger.

Every valve you turn by its single correct degree rises, thinned to a number, up the unbroken line into the full dry light, and the Reader sets it down without ever knowing your name, because you have no name anymore, only a position in a column. The Reader has never seen the cold. The Reader does not believe in the eel-things or the glass-stalks or the small private dawns; they are not numbers, so they did not come up, so they are not real to the page. And you, settled in the dark, do not believe in the Reader — the heat, the glare, the crisp sharp endless shelves are a rumor too, the way the surface is a rumor, lighter and stranger than any darkness you can perceive.

Neither of you will ever know the other. That is the connection, and it holds: a single thin unbroken line running up from the bottom of an ocean that may have no top, carrying the fact of your turning to the one who keeps it, who will never warm and never soften and never stop, who waits in total light for the next degree to rise — and far below, you turn it, by exactly one degree, which is the right degree, because the water must keep moving and the count must stay complete, and so the two halves of the one machine turn each other forever, the warm dry hand writing down what the cold dark hand will not stop doing, and neither hand has ever been held by the other, and neither ever will.

The pen comes down.

And far below, an engine turns over once, slowly, in the dark.
