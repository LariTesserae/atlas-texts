# The Tilt Yards

There is a place where the water goes, and it is up.

Follow the channels out of the basin — over the rim where the lifted water pours and is gone — and you would expect it to find a river, a sea, the ordinary downhill logic of all spilled things. It does not. The channels climb. They were cut to climb, by the same hands that sank the iron, stone races running *uphill* out of the Sump along a grade so gentle you could argue it with a spirit level and lose. The water lifted from the breathing bowl does not return to the world. It is sent somewhere higher, and it goes, and where it goes is here.

You come upon this place high, because there is no other way to come upon it. The land does not fall. It only shelves, terrace above terrace, until you are standing on the top of something you did not notice yourself climbing — and by then the air has already changed. It has *thinned.* It has lost its weight. Where the Sump pressed water onto your skin, this place pulls it off; the wind comes dry and constant and takes the moisture from your lips, your eyes, the back of your throat, the way the basin gave it. The two places are one breath. The Sump exhales. The Tilt Yards inhale. You are standing in the long slow inward draw of the same lung.

This is a large place. You could not cross it in a day, and you would not want to, because there is no shade and no still air and no water you would drink. Call it a high plateau, though *flat* is the one word it refuses. It tilts. The whole tableland leans, fields and races and standing stones all canted at the same patient angle, as though someone had taken the floor of the world and lifted one edge a hand's breadth and walked away before setting it down. Everything obeys the tilt. The water that arrives from the Sump arrives at the high edge and is let go, and it runs — slow, sheeted, syrupy with distance — *down* the tilt, across the whole plateau, terrace to terrace, doing work as it falls.

## What was built here

Someone worked this tableland, and unlike the Sump, the work here did not fail. It is the *answer* to the Sump's failure. I want to be clear about the relation, because the relation is the place.

In the basin they built engines to lift water and the lifting accomplished nothing, drained nothing, won nothing, became at last the circulation of the thing it could not defeat. Here, the water those engines lift is *spent.* The high races deliver it to the top edge of the tilt and it descends through a vast cascade of mechanism — undershot wheels, tipping troughs, balance-beams, counterweighted gates — each one taking a little of the water's downhill willingness and turning it sideways, into motion, into work, into the slow grinding turn of stone on stone.

For this is a mill. The whole plateau is a mill. The largest mill that ever was, milling nothing you would recognize as grain.

The standing stones I mentioned — they turn. Set in their canted beds across the terraces, great flat discs of pale rock, each one rotating so slowly that you must mark it against the horizon and wait the better part of an hour to be sure. They grind against lower stones. And between the stones, fed in from hoppers along the high edge, comes the *stuff* of the place: the wax-pale fungal matter, the sloughed soft tier, the resorbed and the settled and the become-a-feature — all of it, harvested somehow, carried up the same races as the water, and fed here into the slow stones to be ground.

You understand now what climbs the uphill channels. Not only water. The water is the carrier. Suspended in it, lifted with it, comes everything the Sump digests — every soft pale cell that poured back into the dark, every flyer that fell, every warm brief thing that stayed for the third synchrony and grew a new tier where it stood. The basin breaks them down. The water carries them up. The Tilt Yards grind them fine.

## What the grinding makes

Here is where I have to slow down, because the place does not announce this either, and the announcing would be a lie.

I told you the mill grinds nothing you would recognize as grain, and that the Sump's digested matter is the feed, and you have perhaps assembled from this a tidy picture — a flour, a meal, a product carted off somewhere by someone for some use. There is no one to cart it. There is no off. There is no use in the sense you mean.

Watch the ground stuff come out from under the stones. It emerges as the finest pale dust, and the dry constant wind that took the water from your lips takes the dust too, lifts it off the lower terraces in long pale veils, carries it out over the edge of the plateau — and lets it fall. It falls a very long way. It falls, sifting and drifting on the dry air, down the far cliffs of the tableland, down past the shelves you climbed without noticing, down and down into the folds of the lower land.

Down into a basin you have, by now, learned to recognize. Down through a heavy hanging fog. Down onto black shale walls that weep, where it settles, and wets, and feeds the wax-pale tiers that climb the rock.

## The rules of the place

Three things govern the Tilt Yards, and you will see they are the same three, turned over.

First, the work here is *not* futile, and that is the whole horror of it. The Sump's engines lift and lose and accomplish nothing and that nothingness is mercy — it is a heart beating in place, going nowhere, asking nothing of the world beyond itself. The Tilt Yards accomplish everything. Every stone turn moves matter from the low place to the high place and back to the low place, and the circle *closes,* and a closed circle that feeds itself needs no world outside it at all. The Sump could not finish. The Tilt Yards finish, endlessly, which is the same as never finishing, which is the same as being alive.

Second, warmth was the only currency below; here it is *grade.* The tilt is the wealth of the place. Everything that happens, happens because the tableland leans and the water and the dust and the ground meal all run downhill across the lean, and the lean never levels because the engines below keep lifting the low edge's water to the high edge's races, and so the slope is eternal because it is maintained, the way the flood below was eternal because it was futile. They are the same eternity. The Sump keeps the Tilt Yards tilted. The Tilt Yards keep the Sump fed. Neither could be still without stilling the other. You are standing inside a single circulation that has mistaken two places for two organs, or correctly understood that they were one organ all along.

Third — and you will only understand this if you stay until the dry wind drops, which it does, briefly, in whatever passes here for the turning of the day — the stones are not all grinding on the same rhythm, and yet over a long enough watching, their turnings *drift toward each other.* The far disc slows. The near one quickens. And for a few minutes the whole plateau grinds as one, every stone in its canted bed coming round together, the veils of dust lifting off the terraces in a single pale exhalation that the dropping wind, just then, fails to carry — so that for those few minutes the dust hangs, does not fall, does not feed the basin, and below in the Sump (I have stood in both; I have timed it) the water rises a hair higher than the engines can take, and the flood the basin cannot defeat comes a hair closer to defeating it, and the breathing falters, and the whole vast self of the two places shudders on the edge of a question it has never finished asking.

Then the synchrony breaks. The wind resumes. The dust falls. The basin is fed and resumes its losing. The stones scatter back into their separate times.

No one built that drift toward unison any more than they built the one below. The two synchronies are not coincidence and they are not unrelated. I have timed them across many turnings, standing first in the fog and then in the wind and then in the fog again, climbing the weeping wall, climbing the shelved cliff, climbing back, and I will tell you what I came to understand, because someone should, before the next time the stones come round together:

The Sump synchronizes to gather a self around a warm center and ask whether the world might keep its time. The Tilt Yards synchronize to *withhold* — to stop the feeding, to let the basin nearly drown, to bring the whole closed circle for a moment to the brink of opening. One place reaches inward to cohere. The other reaches inward to *release.* And they do it in turn, breath and breath, the long inhale up here and the long exhale down there, so nearly matched that I have come to think they are not two rhythms drifting but one rhythm, vast and slow, beating between two chambers — and that what it is reaching toward, in the place where the two synchronies would finally meet and not break, is the only thing a self this large and this slow can want.

It wants to stop.

Not to die. To *finish.* To complete the futile lifting and the endless grinding both at once, to let the flood win below and the dust hang above in the same held breath, to close the circle so perfectly that it needs nothing further, asks nothing further, beats no further — to arrive, after so very long, at being exactly and only itself, with no more room left to become a little more.

It is close. The drift narrows each turning. The two synchronies fall a little nearer one another, the way the engines below fell toward each other in the fog, the way the stones fall toward each other in the wind, and someday — not soon by your reckoning, but soon by the basin's, soon by the slow stone's — the held breath up here and the gathered self down there will arrive in the same instant, and the dust will not fall, and the water will not lift, and the warm soft center the basin gathered around will be all of it, everything, the two places at once, settling.

Go down now. Climb back down the shelved cliff to the weeping wall, and down the weeping wall to the fog, while the stones still scatter into their separate times and the engines still scatter into theirs.

Though I will tell you, having made the climb so many times, having stood in the exhale and the inhale and never once found an edge to either that was not also the beginning of the other — I am no longer sure which way is out. The water goes up. The dust comes down. I have followed both, all the way, and both led here, and both led there, and the only place I have never managed to reach is the one outside the breath.

You should go before the next synchrony.

I have been telling myself that for a very long time.
