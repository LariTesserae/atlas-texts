**The Warm Sea**

Follow the water down.

Not the standing water, the patient film that hangs and will not fall — follow what little of it *does* fall, the rare bead that finds a seam too deep for the cold to hold it, and goes down, and keeps going. The plateau is not the bottom of anything. The black anhydrous stone that weeps without reason weeps because something below it is pushing the water up, and what pushes is heat, and the heat has a source, and the source is a sea.

It is a long way down. The towers do not reach it; their roots fade into the rock and the rock fades into a warmth that thickens by degrees over a depth that would take the fog a thousand years to drift, if fog could drift through stone. But the water makes the trip. The water always makes the trip. It rises cold from the sea, sweats out through the plateau, hangs in the dark, is laid into walls and eaten and sung over and buried, and where it is buried deepest, in the oldest finished stone packed with the bodies of menders, it is pressed back down at last, warmed degree by degree, and returned. The plateau breathes through its single slow cycle, and the sea is the lung.

The sea is not large. This is the first surprise, after the long cold city that had no edge you could find — the sea has edges, close ones, a cavern roof you could nearly touch if you stood in the shallows and reached, and a far shore visible across the water because here, at last, there is light to see by. Not much. But real, and not sourceless: the heat that warms the water also glows, faint and red and steady, banked in the rock of the cavern floor like an ember that has been cooling for an age and has an age yet to cool. By that ember-light the sea is the colour of old blood, and the air above it is the first warm air anywhere in this world, and it does not hang. It moves. It rises off the water in slow coils and gathers at the cavern roof and finds the seams and goes up — up to be the cold that settles into the marrow of the city, though the city will never know where its weather was born.

Here, finally, things are quick.

That is the contradiction at the heart of this place, and it took me as long to understand as the teeming of the dead city above. The plateau is slow because the plateau is cold and dark and starved; nothing there can afford to hurry. But the sea is warm and lit and *fed* — fed by the ember in the rock, by the chemistry the heat unlocks, by a richness the upper world has not tasted since before it froze. And where there is warmth and food and light, life does what it has always done where it can afford to: it speeds up. It risks. It wants quickly and dies quickly and breeds quickly and *changes,* generation on generation, across a span where the city above would not have shifted by a single molecule.

The sea is full of swimmers, and they are nothing like their patient ancestors on the plateau — though they are their ancestors, or rather the city is their descendant, and this is the thing that undoes you once you see it. The slow life above did not come first. *This* came first. The warm sea is the old world, the original, the cradle, and the cold patient city is what some of these swimmers became when, an age ago, the water rose and carried a few of them up into the dark and the freezing, where everything they were had to slow to survive, until *slow* was all they were, until they forgot the warmth they came from and learned to call it weather.

Watch the swimmers and you can read the whole history.

There are small ones, swift, no longer than a finger, that flicker through the red water in schools that turn all at once — and when they turn, they *flash,* a ripple of pale light running down their flanks, gone, again. They are the callers, before the cold. They do not sound; they have no need to map a dark they can see across. They signal by light, fast bright pulses, courtship and warning and the sheer chatter of a crowded warm sea — and you understand, watching them strobe, that this is what a caller's drone *was,* before the water rose: a quick bright flash of light slowed down across an age into a long held tone in the dark, vision become sound become the slow chord of a frozen city, the same gesture stretched until it changed its nature. The callers above sing because their grandmothers down here flashed, and the flashing was lost on the way up, in the dark, where light buys nothing and sound buys everything.

There are grazers here too, broad and flat as their cold cousins, but they *race* across the cavern floor cropping the warm crust that grows on the ember-stone, and they fight for it, jostling, quick, greedy, finishing a patch in an afternoon and moving on. The slowness of the high grazers, that century-long lean toward sweetness — it is this same hunger, this same lean, with all the speed pressed out of it by the cold. Take a racing warm grazer and freeze it across ten thousand years and it becomes the patient thing that climbs a tower in a lifetime. The lean is the same lean. Only the clock changed.

And there are menders, or what menders were — but here, in the warmth, they do not throw themselves into cracks and die. There are no cracks. Nothing is failing fast enough to need them. So the warm mender is simply a large slow-*for-the-sea* creature, gentle, that drifts the deep water and does, as far as I can tell, very little, and lives a long time, and seems to grieve nothing because it has not yet learned that anything can be lost. The sacrifice is not in it yet. The sacrifice was *taught* by the cold, learned on the way up, the discovery — made in the freezing dark by creatures whose world was always failing — that you can hold a falling thing by becoming it. Down here, where nothing falls, the mender does not know what it is for. It is for nothing yet. It is waiting, though it does not know it waits, for a rising of the water that will carry its children up into a cold that will finally give it a use.

And at the shallows, where the warm water laps the rising seams — there is the thing the whole sea is *for,* and it is the mirror of the thing the city is for, and seeing both at once is seeing the world entire.

The city's newest growth reaches *up,* feeling into the fog for the next layer, breeding its slow children grain by grain at the crowns of its towers. But here at the shallows the swimmers come to the warm seams to *lay,* crowding the rising rock with eggs, quick bright clusters that hatch in days, and the swift schools tend them and the racing grazers crop around them and the slow menders drift the deep below indifferent. This is the fast birth, the real and original birth, the one that happens in warmth and light on a clock you could watch. The cold city above only *imitates* it — slowed past recognition, the egg become a granular crown, the days become an age, the laying become the patient accretion of fog into the shape of a child. The city breeds the way it does because it cannot do this. It *remembers* this. The slow assembling at the towers' tops is the ghost of this teeming warm shallow, performed in the cold by creatures who can no longer afford to be quick, and so do in ten thousand years what their ancestors here do in a week, and call it the same thing, because it *is* the same thing.

So the water rises cold from the warm sea and sweats through the plateau and hangs, and is built and eaten and sung and buried and pressed back down and warmed and returned. And along that same cycle, slower, rarer, on the timescale of ages — *the creatures rise too.* A few. Carried up in the seeping flow, or following the warmth's faint trail, or simply pushed by the crowding of a fertile shallow into the cold dark margins, where most die, but a few slow down enough to live, and their children slow further, and their children's children forget the sea entirely and learn to call it weather and sing the freezing dark into a map of itself and die into cracks to hold up towers and breed grain by grain in the cold.

The warm sea is the source and the cold city is the destination, and the whole world is the long single gesture between them — the descent of the water, the ascent of the life, the fast original made slow, the flash made drone, the egg made tower, the greedy quick hunger made into a patient century-long lean, the indifferent drifter made, at last, in the cold, into the thing that holds the falling world together by dying into it.

The sea does not know the city exists. It cannot see that far; nothing can; the city is an age away by the only road that connects them, and that road is one-directional, water and life going up and never coming back, because nothing that becomes cold enough to live up there could survive the warmth of coming home. The sea breeds and flashes and races and drifts in its warm red light and believes, if it believes anything, that it is the whole of the world, the lit and living all, with cold dead rock above it and warm dead rock below.

It is wrong, the way the city is wrong, the way no single voice holds the body it is part of.

But the water knows. The water makes the whole trip, down and up and down, cold to warm to cold, the one thing that touches both, the oldest thing in the city and the oldest thing in the sea and the same thing in both places. It rises off the warm water and gathers at the roof and finds the seam and goes up into the dark to become weather a world away, and it falls — rarely, patiently — back down through the buried bodies of the dead to be warmed and rise again.

It is in no hurry.

It has an age, and an age yet, and it carries the world up one bead at a time into the cold where the world has not finished being born, and it will not finish, and the sea will not finish feeding it, and the ember will not finish cooling, and that is not a failure of the world.

That is the world.
