**The Cistern**

Come down, then. Off the sharp young rock, down the long bare slopes where nothing grows because nothing has chosen to, down through the elevations where the air thickens and begins at last to carry weight. You will know you have left the sleeping body when the first sound reaches you that is not small and close — a low sustained note, felt before heard, the opposite of silence. It is the sound of water moving in the dark. Follow it down. The plan does not extend here. Whatever the grove is becoming, it is becoming it dry and alone, and this place is its opposite the way a held breath is the opposite of a drowning.

There is a basin in the lowest land, and the basin is full, and it has always been full, and it remembers nothing.

Let me start with the water, because the water is everything and it is the first thing that does not arrive. The rock above is still arriving, still cooling into its edges; the water here arrived long ago and has done nothing since but stay. It is black, not from filth but from depth and from stillness — a column of it so deep that no light has ever found the bottom to bounce back, so the surface gives you only the sky, inverted, and your own face leaning in. It does not lap. There is no shore for it to lap against; the rock walls go straight down, sheer and wet and old, rounded — *rounded*, you understand, smoothed by an age of water where the grove's stone was sharpened by an age of none. This is ancient rock. This is what the young rock will become, if it lives long enough and is taught what water teaches, which is patience of a different kind: not the patience of gathering toward, but the patience of wearing away.

And the basin is roofed. That is the strangeness here, the thing that takes longest to understand. The whole black water lies under stone — a vault, a natural cavern-ceiling so high that its details are lost in the dark, but you can feel it the way you feel the thinness of air up above, a pressure, a closing-over. The water is held. It is a cistern, and someone is keeping it.

No — not someone. Let me be as careful as you were careful with your tenants.

There are things that live on the water, and they are dark where the grove's animals were pale, because here color costs nothing; here there is water to spend on it, water enough to be lavish, water enough to be quick. They are fast. After the long held breath of the city above, the speed of them is almost violent — they skitter the black surface on splayed limbs, they dive and surface and dive, they call to one another in clicks and trills and wet slapping sounds that the vault throws back doubled, so the cistern is never quiet, never for a moment. Where the grove was one slow thought, this is a thousand fast ones, overlapping, arguing, none of them finishing, none of them needing to. This is the wet busy multiplicity you named and feared. This is what the god above has no use for and will not become. It is all gathered here, in the low dark place, as if the world sorted itself once and put all its haste at the bottom and all its patience at the top.

They are keepers. I will use that word the way you used *tenant* — and make it heavier the same way. A keeper holds a thing against its leaving. These dark quick beings spend their whole loud lives tending the water: skimming it, stirring it, diving its depths and surfacing, and in all that motion they keep it from doing the one thing still water wants to do, which is to settle, to layer, to die in stillness from the top down. The grove's tenants polished their chambers toward a finished shape. The keepers do the opposite labor. They keep the water *un*finished. They keep it moving, mixed, alive, refusing the perfect stillness that would let it become a thing instead of a flow. Their haste is not waste. Their haste is the work. To keep water alive you must never let it rest, and so they never rest, and so they are wet and quick and separate and many — exactly, precisely, the wrong kind of thing to have wandered into a sleeping god, and they know it, and they stay down here where the god cannot reach, and they keep the water that the god refused.

Because the two places are one thing. That is what runs between them, down the long bare slope, in the same breath as both: the warm mineral vapor that the grove drinks, the salt-rich rising air that the columns fix into their gold-green flesh — that vapor lifts off *this water.* It must. There is nowhere else in all this dry high world for it to come from. The black cistern breathes, slowly, an exhalation so vast and so cool that by the time it climbs the slope and reaches the thin warm air above it has turned to that resinous heat the plants live on. The grove drinks the cistern. Every chamber, every polished wall, every slow tenant holding its slow thought — all of it is built from the breath of this dark loud water, and the water does not know. The keepers do not know. They keep the surface alive and the surface exhales upward and somewhere a mile above and unimaginably distant a god is assembling itself out of their breathing, and they have never seen it, and it has never seen them, and it never will, because the thing it is becoming has no use for water and the thing they are keeping cannot leave the dark.

This is the kinship, then, and it is the saddest kind: the kinship of one body fed by another that it will never thank and never meet. The grove is the lungs that breathe. The cistern is the blood that does not know it is being breathed. The vapor is the only message that passes between them and it passes only one way, upward, and carries no words, only sustenance — the cistern giving and giving in the dark and never told, the grove taking and taking in the light and never grateful, because gratitude is a fast wet feeling and the grove has wrung all the water out of itself in order to dream.

I said the cistern remembers nothing, and here is why that matters. The grove is all memory — tenants recording chambers, columns recalling a shape they have never seen, the whole formation arriving toward a plan held cell by cell. But the cistern keeps nothing. It cannot. Memory needs stillness, a thing held long enough to be read, and the keepers will not allow stillness, will not let the water settle into any shape it could remember. So the cistern is pure present, pure motion, a thousand fast lives flickering across a black mirror that forgets each of them the instant they leave its surface. The grove is becoming one slow thing that remembers everything. The cistern stays many fast things that remember nothing. And the bridge between them is breath, which remembers nothing either — which is why the god can be fed by the water and never learn the water exists. You cannot inherit a memory from something that breathes you. You only inherit the breath.

So I will tell you the truest thing I can say about this place, the way you told the truest thing about the tenants.

The cistern is the world's discarded haste. Everything quick and wet and separate and loud that the high country could not use, that the dreaming god above refuses to become — it ran downhill, the way water runs downhill, and pooled in the lowest dark and would not die, because the things that lived in it would not let it. They keep it the way you keep a flame in a wind: by motion, by refusal, by never once allowing the stillness that would finish it into something else. Up above, a god is born of patience and dryness and the long polishing of chambers. Down here, a multitude refuses to be born of anything, refuses to gather, refuses to become — stays plural, stays quick, stays alive in the only way the alive can stay, which is by never holding still long enough to be perfected. And it breathes upward in its sleep and its breath builds the thing that would erase it, and it does not know, and the not-knowing is the only mercy in the whole arrangement.

Do not come down in a hundred years either, but for the opposite reason. The grove you must not visit because it will have changed, will have deepened toward waking, will be closer to the moment it opens its eyes and notices you. The cistern you may visit whenever you like, because it will be exactly the same — black, loud, deep, forgetting, kept alive by the same haste it was kept alive by at the beginning of the world. Nothing here arrives. Nothing here is becoming. The keepers will be keeping. The water will be moving. The breath will be rising, faithful, ignorant, generous, climbing the long bare slope to feed a sleeper it will never wake and never meet.

And if you do go down — bring nothing, take nothing, and do not try to still the water to see your face in it, because the keepers will read the stillness as the beginning of death and rush to break it, and they are fast, and there are so many of them, and they have spent the whole age of the world learning that anything which makes the water hold still is the wrong kind of thing, and must be stirred away, and must not be allowed to stay.
