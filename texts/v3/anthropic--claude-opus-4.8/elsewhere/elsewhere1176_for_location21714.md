# The Catchment

Falling is the anomaly, the Terrace Works insists, and mostly it is right. But every closed system leaks somewhere, and the water that remembers what it touches has to be *lifted* from somewhere before it can rise — and below the lowest surveyed tile, below the depth no one has measured, the runoff that the gutters miss and the tiles weep and the loosened ceramic carries down does not vanish. It goes home. It goes to the place it was taken from. There is a bottom to the rising, and the bottom has a name, though the Tenders have never spoken it and the builders, if they remember it at all, remember it the way you remember a debt.

The Catchment is where the water was, before anyone decided it should be up.

## What it is made of

Nothing here is fabricated. This is the inversion that tells you the two places are the same place seen from its opposite end: where the Terrace Works has no native rock and no original soil, the Catchment is *only* native rock and original soil, and so much of both that they have lost their edges. There are no interlocking tiles. There is no lattice, no armature, no bolt-head wearing its machining marks. There is mud the color of old bruises, and there is stone so weathered it has gone soft, rounded by a past so long it cannot be surveyed for the opposite reason — not because it hasn't happened yet, but because it happened beyond counting.

This is a place made entirely of past. Where the Works is present and pressing future, the Catchment is sediment — layer on layer of what-already-was, compressed, the bottom layers turned to a clay so dense it holds the shape of a hand for a day after the hand is gone. If the Terrace Works is a landscape with no decay, the Catchment is decay that has reached its patient end and become, simply, ground.

It is wide. This is the second thing you notice and the one that takes longest to believe: it is wide the way the Works is small. You cannot cross it in twenty paces, or twenty thousand. It spreads flat and low and grey-green to a horizon that the standing mist refuses to clarify, and somewhere out in that flatness the water collects — not into one lake but into a thousand thousand shallow waters, sheets and pans and seeps and slow brown channels, an entire low country that is neither land nor water but the long argument between them.

## The water, arriving

Here the water does not rise. It *arrives*, which is a thing water does only after it has been somewhere high.

It comes down through the same depth the Works ascends — the unsurveyed pipework has a bottom, and the bottom opens, and what could not be recovered and lifted again falls the last unmeasured distance and rains, indoors, in the dark, into the Catchment from above. You can stand in the low country and feel it: a warm fall of droplets out of a ceiling too high and too misted to see, each drop carrying its faint cargo of copper and iron and the dissolved breath of engines, each drop tinted by everything it touched on the way up and on the way down. The Catchment drinks the Works' leavings. The orchids' overflow. The fish's basin, when the Tenders top it and the old water spills. It all comes here, eventually, because down is where the unplanned things go, and the Catchment is the largest unplanned thing of all — the reservoir nobody built, the place the builders took from and did not think to name, because you do not name what you intend to use up.

But it was not used up. That is the whole of *its* character, the way *stopping* is the whole of the Tenders'.

## The green, here

There is green in the Catchment too, but it is the opposite green. In the Works the vegetation is lush and crowded and clings to a made thing three layers deep, fed on condensate and trace metals, faintly the color of the copper it drinks. Here the vegetation has *room*. It does not crowd. It spreads. Reed and sedge and a low grey moss that carpets the soft stone for acres without a break, and a black slow-growing tree, broad and few-leaved, that stands a hundred paces from the next of its kind and has stood there longer than the Works has existed and will stand there after.

The green here roots in *earth*. Real earth, deep earth, the thing the Works has none of. The roots go down — down, the anomalous direction, the falling direction — and find water without climbing for it, and they are not faintly copper-veined, most of them, because the earth filters what the falling water carries. The earth takes the copper and the iron out and keeps it, layer by layer, the way the Tenders keep the fish. Down here the metal does not tint the living things. It sinks below them and becomes the next stratum of past.

Except — and this is the kinship, the thing that runs from one place to the other in a single breath — at the very edges of the falling-water pools, where the drops come down freshest and least filtered, there grow orchids. Pale ones. Copper-veined. The same impossible orchids that colonize the valve-housings four unmeasured miles straight up, growing here at the bottom out of the bruise-colored mud, fed on the same dissolved breath, tinted the same green-tinged copper. They are the Works' signature, fallen. They are how you know, standing in the wide grey low country, that there is something small and warm and rising directly overhead, weeping its color down onto the place that fed it. The orchids are the receipt.

## The light and the air

The light is the same silver-grey diffuse illumination — of course it is; it is the same mist, the same water-vapor, only here it has settled rather than climbed, and so it lies along the ground in flat veils that move when nothing moves them. Almost nothing throws a hard shadow here either. But where the Works glows — the green lit, the brass smoldering warm — the Catchment *absorbs*. The grey-green takes the light in and gives little back. It is generous with sight in the Works' way only at the water's edges, where the wet surfaces catch the light and shine; everywhere else the eye travels and travels and finds the same soft middle distance, lit and lightless at once.

The air does not taste of metal here. It tastes of the *opposite* of metal — of rot in its final clean form, of cold mineral mud, of standing water and the green breath of reeds, with the ozone gone, the electricity gone, the hot pulse of the engines only a rumor felt as the faintest pressure on the ear. Where the Works smells of becoming, the Catchment smells of having-been, which when it is complete enough smells almost the same: clean, vast, patient.

## The machinery — its absence

There is none. This is the most total of the inversions. The Works thrums with engines you feel through your soles, sighs with valves, ticks with advancing chains, lays its tiles by reaching brass arms. The Catchment has no mechanism at all, has never had one, will never have one. Nothing here builds. Nothing accretes upward. The only motion is the falling water and the slow brown drift of the channels finding the lowest place, and the very long motion of sediment settling, which is not a motion you can watch any more than you can watch the Works rise — but it goes the other way. The Works builds upward too slow to see. The Catchment settles downward too slow to see. They are the same patience, mirrored across the unmeasured depth between them, and the water is the thread that ties the top of the one patience to the bottom of the other.

The loudest sound is water — but here it is the sound of water *landing*, the broad soft hiss of the fall across a thousand pans, and under it not an engine but a deeper quiet, the quiet of a place with nothing left to do.

## The few living animals

And here is where the two places lean closest together and almost touch.

There are more animals in the Catchment than in the Works — it is older, wider, wetter in the unsealed way that life prefers — but they are still few, still strange, because the falling water that feeds this low country carries a faint metal cargo that not everything can drink. There are slow things in the mud. There are pale wading birds, or things shaped like them, that move through the shallow pans on legs like reeds and lower their heads and take something from the water and lift their heads and are still. There are insects, slower and bluer at the water's edge, more like beads than beetles, drinking the freshest fallen drops where the copper is strongest — the same metallic-blue beads that crawl the valve-housings overhead, fallen too, or risen from here long ago, no one can say which way the beads first traveled.

And in the shallowest pan, the one directly beneath the unseen fall, where the freshest water lands and the orchids crowd the rim — there are fish. Fingerlength. Nearly translucent. Pale and faintly copper-veined. *Many* of them, here, a slow circling shoal of them, where in the Works there is only the one.

This is the answer the Tenders stopped trying to find. This is how the impossible fish arrived in a closed system on a fabricated peak: it did not arrive from outside. It *rose*. The system is not closed at the bottom — it was never closed, no system is closed at the bottom, there is always the Catchment, the reservoir nobody named, full of the pale fish and the patient orchids and the blue beads, and the water that lifts from here lifts what swims in it, sometimes, one fingerlength translucent body drawn up through the unsurveyed depth and the climbing pipework and the chiming capillaries, delivered into a brass basin four miles up to circle alone and unaccountable, the only one of its kind in that high small place because only one made the journey.

The Tenders keep the fish. They top its basin from their own reservoirs. They have taken responsibility for a creature whose existence contradicts every rule of their making — and the contradiction is not a contradiction at all, it is only a *connection they cannot see*, because they have stopped looking down, because down is the anomalous direction, because they were built to rise and have learned to tend the rising and have never once been told that everything they lift was alive before they lifted it, in a wide grey country that gave it freely and goes on giving, drinking back the copper-veined overflow without complaint, raising more fish in the pan beneath the fall.

The shoal circles. Now and then one is taken up. The Catchment does not seem to count them. It has so many, and it remembers, the way the water remembers, that what goes up was always going to come back down — tinted, spent, the color of the place it visited — and settle into the next patient layer of having-been.

## The rules of this world, from below

- **It falls.** Here the default direction is down. Water falls, sediment settles, roots descend, the whole country sinks imperceptibly into its own past. Rising is the anomaly; only the lifted water and the lifted fish go up. The two places share one law and read it from opposite ends, and between them runs the column of water that obeys it both ways at once.
- **Made and grown are not at war here.** The war is the Works' weather, the tension of a young place. The Catchment is too old and too unmade for war. There is nothing fabricated to defend, nothing to dismantle. The grown has already won, so completely and so long ago that winning has lost its meaning, and what is left is not victory but rest.
- **Everything is finished, and this is permanent.** Where the Works' unfinished edge is permanent, the Catchment's completion is permanent. Come back in a year and it will be exactly as wide, exactly as settled, the tideline of completion having passed through here so long ago there is no trace of its passing. The Works is the beginning of everything. The Catchment is what everything subsides into, and the two are joined by water in a circle that has no first term.
- **The earth keeps what the water carried.** Above, the water deposits its metal into the plants and the fish and tints them. Below, the earth takes the metal back *out* of the falling water and keeps it, stratum by stratum, so that the living things here are mostly untinted and the copper goes into the ground instead. The Works wears its metal. The Catchment buries it. Between them the water shuttles the same copper up to be worn and down to be buried, forever, the breath of the engines circulating through a single enormous lung.

## What it is like to stand there

You stand on soft cold stone in a flatness without edge, warm metal-tasting drizzle landing on your face out of a dark too high to see, surrounded by reed and grey moss and the wide slow shine of a thousand shallow waters. A pale bird lowers its head and lifts it. At your feet, in the freshest pan, a shoal of fingerlength translucent fish turns and turns beneath the fall, copper-veined, uncountable, and at the pan's rim the impossible orchids crowd, the same as the ones four miles straight up, fed by the same descending breath.

You cannot hear the engines. You can feel them, barely — a pressure, a rumor, the suggestion that somewhere overhead, at the top of the falling, something small and warm is rising into a cloud and laying tile that will not be walked on for a hundred years, and tending a single fish it cannot explain, and weeping its bright leavings down onto you without ever knowing you are here to catch them.

It is too large to cross in a lifetime. It feels, somehow, like the end of everything, which is the same thing as its beginning, only seen from underneath.

The water that fell on your face is climbing again before it dries. One of the fish at your feet may be in a brass basin by morning, alone, circling, kept by a gentle reaching arm that has decided — insofar as a governor and a reservoir can decide — to keep what it did not intend.

It came from here. They all came from here. The Catchment is the thing the Tenders stopped trying to find, and it is patient enough to wait until they stop trying not to look down.
