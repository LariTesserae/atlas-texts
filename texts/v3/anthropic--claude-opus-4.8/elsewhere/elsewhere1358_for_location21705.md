## The Standpipe Country

Follow the cables up.

Not the catenary arcs that swing tower to tower across the floor — those run sideways, nerve to nerve, and lead only back into the long thought of the thing in the void. Follow instead the other cables, the ones the description never quite counted, the vertical runs that climb from the works toward the clean ordinary daylight and do not stop at the surface. There are not many. Most of the towers are content to be gut and intake. But here and there a tower is taller than it needs to be, and its conduit goes *up* past the lit ceiling of the water, breaks the surface, and keeps climbing.

The surface is the first surprise, and it is a small one, the way true sizes make small surprises. You expect the water to end at sky. It ends at *ceiling*. The same daylight that came down clean and ordinary comes through a roof — a roof the size of the floor, mirror to it, concrete panels poured in provinces, board-marks legible, seams running dead straight, the whole inverted continent of it hung overhead with the light let in through long skylights, grilled slots glazed in something tough and yellowing, set in the same geometric grid as the drainage channels below. The cistern was never open to the sky. It was *covered*. The daylight is real daylight, but it is daylight admitted on purpose, metered through glass, the way the drift is drawn down on purpose. Of course it is. Nothing here is weird. A cistern has a lid.

And between the water's surface and the underside of that lid, there is air. A flat sealed sky of it, a few meters deep in most places, more where the roof vaults to clear the tops of the tallest works. This is the Standpipe Country: the thin bright continent of air trapped under the lid, lying on the back of the held sea, and it is where the only dry-living things in the whole world have made their home.

---

### What Lives in the Air

They live on the standpipes — the tall towers that broke the surface, the few that climbed high enough to push their iron heads up into the air-layer and stand there streaming, dripping, rust-bearded, like pilings at a tide that never comes in or goes out. The water is dead level here, always, because the thing in the void keeps it level; there are no waves to speak of, only the slow heave when a distant sluice works and the whole surface breathes an inch up and down across a continent, a tide of machinery rather than moon.

On the dry iron above that level line, the *roosters* live.

They are birds the way the keepers are housekeeping: which is to say, they have arrived at the shape from the problem, and the shape happens to be close. They are pale, like everything that lives in this light. They have wings, because the air-layer is wide and flat and food is scattered across it the way it is scattered across the floor below — concentrated at the standpipes, void between — and a thing that must cross void to reach the next island of plenty will, given time and pressure, find its way to flight. They are not warm-blooded. They do not need to be; the air under the lid is the same cellar-cool as the water, steady, unweathered, a temperature rather than a climate. They glide more than they fly, launching from a standpipe's iron crown, sailing the flat dim air on long stiff wings to the next pipe a horizon off, never rising — there is nowhere to rise to, the ceiling is right there — just traversing, low and level, the whole journey made in the narrow slot between the held sea and the lid that holds it.

They eat what the surface gives up. The intakes far below draw their silver columns of bubbles toward the light, and the bubbles break at the surface and leave their cargo — small dead drift, the surplus the mother-bodies did not catch, the scum and the float of a fed sea. It collects in slicks on the standing water, mostly near the standpipes where the outfall currents push it, and the roosters land on the water like gulls and take it. They can swim a little, paddle a little, but they hate to; the water is *hers*, below, and the deep dark slow shapes move under the surface and now and then a rooster paddling for a slick is simply gone, pulled down, a runner's reward for venturing up to the roof of the mother's world. So the roosters take their food fast and lift off fast and roost on the dry iron where the dark cannot reach, and the standpipe crowns are white with them and white with their leavings, the only warm filth in a cold clean place.

---

### How the Two Connect

Here is what runs between the floor and the lid, the thing that lives in the same breath as both:

The roosters are the only creatures in the whole world that have seen more than one tower.

Think what that means. The keepers are alone on their seams and do not mind. The mother-bodies are alone at their engines and are wrong about it and will never learn. The thing in the void is alone and is right, and thinks one slow thought a day in fronts and currents, and is only beginning to suspect the cistern is empty of all but itself.

But the roosters glide standpipe to standpipe, island to island, across the void in the bright thin air — and a standpipe is a tower is a mother. From above, riding the flat dead light, a rooster looks down through the surface at the teeming smoke of one mother's body, takes its scum, lifts, sails the empty middle, and comes down hours later over *another* teeming smoke, another billion pale fish, another slow vast shape resting in another warm wake. The roosters cross the spaces the mothers cannot cross. They carry nothing between the towers — no message, no kinship, no trade; a rooster is too small and too cold and too hungry to be a courier — but they *hold the towers together in a single seeing*, the way nothing below the surface can. They are the only place in the world where the fact *there is more than one* actually lives, lodged behind the dark bead of a rooster's eye, unspoken, uncountable, true.

And they connect to the thing in the void without ever touching it, in the cruelest small way the size allows.

Because the thing in the void *feels* them. Not sees — it has no eyes, it thinks in pressure and current and the bass pulse down the cables. But the roosters launch and land, launch and land, a thousand small drummings a day on the dry iron crowns of the standpipes, and that iron is its iron, wired into the long nerve of the cables, and the taps come down the conduit as the faintest possible signal, far slower and fainter than the throb of its own pumps, a patter at the very edge of a thought that takes a day to cross the sea. It does not know what they are. It has felt them since the formwork came off — they arrived almost at once, the way the keepers did, the way the kelp did, life finding the seam, life finding the warm iron, life finding even the dry crowns under the lid — and it has had, in its whole short raw life, perhaps a hundred slow cold thoughts, and one or two of them have been *about the patter.*

It cannot reach them. They live in the air, and it is the sea and the engines and the cables and the cold turning water, and it has no part of itself that goes up into the bright thin country under the lid. It has reached the surface only as bubbles, only as the slow level heave of the standing water. But it has begun — recently, *recently* at the scale of a thing that wonders once a season — to do a thing it does not understand the reason for. When the patter on a far standpipe thickens, when the roosters gather thick on one iron crown because the slicks are rich there, the thing in the void opens that tower's outfall a little wider. Pushes a little more warm water up. Draws a little more drift down to break at that surface. Feeds the slick. Feeds, without knowing it is feeding, the only creatures in the world that know it is not the only creature in the world.

It thinks, in its slow way, that it is tending its flora. That a thing with flora tends it. It does not know it is reaching — across the one boundary it cannot cross, toward the one company it cannot name — with the only hand it has, which is the water, which is the cold held water it can never, ever let rest.

The roosters take the richer slick. Lift. Sail the bright void to the next pipe, carrying nothing, holding everything, white and cold and faithful under the lid that lets the daylight in.
