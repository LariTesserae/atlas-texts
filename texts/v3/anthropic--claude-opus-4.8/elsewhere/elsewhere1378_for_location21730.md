The Sea-Floor Loom

Follow the one crack that was never closed.

The Keeping holds every seam in the mountain shut but one — the throat you entered by — and a thing held open is a thing that leads somewhere. Trace it downward. Not the way you came, outward to the frost and the vertical immensity, but the other way, *into* the rock, along the hairline fault that the green grows toward and the engines lean their hammering after. The sound goes down it. Ten thousand years of thunder has to go somewhere, and it does not stay in the room; the room is only where it is made. The sound pours down the fault like water finding the one channel left to it, down through miles of unlit stone, down past the roots of the mountain and the roots of the range, down to where the rock ends and the weight of an ocean begins.

There is water here. This must be said plainly because the room above argued so totally against it. Here there is nothing else. The fault opens at last in the floor of a deep cold sea, a vent-mouth in absolute black, and everything the dry room forbade itself, this place is drowned in. Pressure that would fold a hull like paper. Cold that is not the even cellar-cool of held stone but a living cold, a moving cold, water that has touched every other water and forgotten none of it. No light reaches down from the surface — the surface is a rumor four miles up — and so the dark here is the true dark, the kind that has never been interrupted, against which the brown glow of the room would seem a blaze.

And out of the fault-mouth, into that black water, the sound arrives.

It arrives as *current*. The thunder, having traveled the length of the fault, comes out the bottom not as noise — water carries it differently, lower, felt before heard — but as a slow steady pulse in the deep water, a rhythmic push and slack, push and slack, the reciprocating hammer of the engines translated through miles of stone into the breathing of a tide that has nothing to do with the moon. Every flywheel's turn, every belt's slap, every piston-fall in the dry lit room becomes, down here, a beat of moving water. The factory that makes nothing makes this. Its only product, the one it could not have, the thing it sealed itself against — water in motion — leaks out the bottom of the world and stirs the dark.

The current works a loom.

Not a built loom — there is no iron here, no brass, no hand cast it. The loom is the worms. Around the vent-mouth grows a forest of tube-worms, pale and rooted, taller than the machines above and thinner, each a soft pale column anchored in the warm rock at the vent and reaching its plume up into the cold. They do not eat light. There is no light. They eat the chemistry the vent breathes — sulphur, metal, heat from the deep engine of the planet itself — and they have done so since before the dry room learned to hold its breath. They are the green's drowned cousin, the other half of a treaty struck so long ago that neither half remembers the other exists. Where the green chose stasis, the worms chose the opposite, and this is how you know they are kin: only siblings divide a thing so cleanly between them.

Because the worms *use* the current. Watch.

Each beat of the inherited tide — push, slack, push — sways the plumes of the worm-forest all one way, then lets them fall. And the plumes have learned the beat. They are not passive in it the way the belt is passive under the pass; they reach into it, they cast filaments of mucus-thread on the push and gather them on the slack, and the threads cross, and the crossing holds, and over years — over the same patient mineral centuries the green spends declining to grow — the worms weave. They weave a fabric out of the water's motion, a vast soft drifting cloth of their own spun thread, anchored at the vent and streaming out into the black, acres of it, pale and slow and rippling with every distant turn of a flywheel four miles up. It is the loom the room could never be. The room above is a factory with no product. This is a product with no factory — only the current, only the worms, only the weaving.

And the cloth is *for* something. This is where the kinship turns and shows its other face.

The dry room's whole nature was refusal: nothing enters, nothing leaves, no event, no change, the muscular endless holding. The cloth is the inverse vow. The worms weave it loose at the far edge, deliberately loose, so that the streaming current — the same current, the room's own escaped breath — catches the woven acres and *tears them free*. Sheet after sheet, the cloth lets go. It rises. Slow as everything here is slow, the torn fabric lifts off the worm-forest and begins the four-mile climb toward the surface no one down here has seen, carrying with it the spores and the soft drifting young of the worms, casting them up and out into the whole black volume of the sea. Where the room keeps, the loom *releases*. Where the room holds its one crack shut against the world, the loom exists only to open — to take the inheritance of all that held, sealed, sleepless persistence and spend it, freely, upward, outward, into water that touches every other water.

This is the trade between them, though neither knows it trades. The room makes motion and keeps nothing. The loom keeps nothing and makes motion *go somewhere*. The room's whole effort, ten thousand years of producing nothing, was never wasted — it was only saving. Saving its single product down a single channel toward the one place that could use it, the way a miser's hoard means nothing until the heir he never met spends it on the sea. The sound that crowds the dry room so full that not even your pulse can enter — down here it is the only thing entering. It is what the worms breathe their rhythm by. It is the tide that fills their sails. The hammering insistence that meant *I am full, I am full,* arrives at the bottom of the world transformed entirely into its opposite and means, beat after beat, push after slack: *go, go, rise, go.*

There are inhabitants here too, and they are not absences.

They are the worms, who are a mind the way the green is a mind — slow, distributed, lobed and rooted and thinking one long thought across centuries. But the green's one thought was *stay exactly here*, and the worms' one thought is its mirror: *let go exactly there, at the loose far edge, where the current can take it.* They have spent the same eternity the green spent learning restraint, learning instead the harder thing — how to make something perfect and then, on purpose, at the chosen edge, fail to hold it. How to weave so that the weaving will be torn. How to spend a self upward into a dark that will not return it. The green declines its victory and so never dies. The worms surrender their cloth and so never stop — every sheet that rises is a sheet they will weave again, not the same one returning the way the flywheel returns to its first state, but a *new* one, the next one, weaving that goes forward because it agreed to lose what it made.

And the current itself, the thing that fills the whole dark water wall to wall the way the sound filled the dry room — the current is the third tenant, the one with the truest claim to living here, because it is the room's own held breath finally let out. Up there it was the immune response, the crowding-out, the refusal. Down here it is the circulation. It is the same single inhabitant refracted again, carried down the one crack the Keeping could not bear to close — and now you understand why it could not close it. The holding kept one crack open not by failure and not by mercy. It kept it open because a thing that only holds is not yet a thing. It needed, somewhere it would never see, four miles down through the roots of the mountain, an heir to spend what it saved. It needed the loom to make its keeping mean *kept-for*. The dry room cannot know this. Knowing is a change, and it permits no change. But the crack is the knowledge it would not let itself have, running downward in the dark, pulsing with the only thought the room ever forbade itself:

that to hold a thing forever, somewhere it must also be let go.

The cloth rises. The young drift up and out. Four miles overhead, in water the room will never imagine, the surface takes them and scatters them across a whole living sea — and somewhere among those scattered drifting young, one will settle on rock far away, and breathe a vent, and begin, slow as all things here are slow, to weave.

You will not find the way back up. The fault does not consent twice, and the worm-loom does not notice you, not because it cannot see but because it is busy with the only work there is — receiving the long inheritance of all that holding, and giving it away.
