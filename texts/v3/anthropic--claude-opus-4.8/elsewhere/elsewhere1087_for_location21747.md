# The Dry-Floor, Where the Bones Are Kept

Follow the pale clay down.

It comes up the slope on the backs of the small broad-footed traders who climb out of the cloud and into it, and the people of Wet-Bound take it and smear it on their cheeks and never once ask where it is dug, because a thing that comes from below comes from a place they do not have a word for. But it has a place. The clay is mined, and the mine has a mouth, and the mouth opens out of a hillside far down under the cloud-line where the air finally remembers how to be only air. That is where the other place is. That is where you go if you keep following the white down through the green until the green thins and the floor turns hard and the sky learns its edges back.

## Below the Cloud, the Sky Comes Back

The first thing is the shadow.

A person from Wet-Bound, brought down here — and a few are, the traders, the ones whose eyes can stand the change — stops on the path the first time the cloud breaks under them and they come out into the lower light, because there on the ground in front of them is a thing they have never seen: the hard black shape of themselves, lying on the dirt with an edge to it. They are afraid of it the way they are afraid of the silence. It is a hole in the world the wrong way round. The sun down here is not a pearl but a coin, and it strikes things and leaves a mark, and everything carries its own darkness fixed to its feet, and a person who has lived their whole life in shadowless grey must learn, late and badly, that the dark thing following them is only the light's accusation and means them no harm.

The Dry-Floor is a country of edges. Up there nothing had an outline; here everything does. The rock comes back. Not the buried bone-stone of the ridge that no one has seen in generations — here the stone is *out*, bare, sun-warmed, dust-shedding, cracked into terraces and ledges and the long low scarp where the clay seam runs pale through the red like a vein of held breath. There is grass that crackles. There is dust that rises when you walk and hangs golden in the slant light and settles on your skin and **stays there, dry**, which is a sensation no one from the cloud has a word for either. The damp does not do their sweating for them down here. They sweat. They are astonished by it.

## What the Floor Is Made Of

Walk here and you walk on the dead.

This is the inversion that makes the two places one place, turned over. Up in Wet-Bound the moss eats everything in a season and there are no old bones underfoot and the town feels young because it has digested its whole history into leaves. Down on the Dry-Floor nothing rots. The air is too thin of water, the sun too quick to leach and bake, and so a thing that dies here *keeps*. A bird fallen in the dust becomes, over a slow year, a clean dry articulate ruin of itself, every small bone in place. A beast that lies down to die in the lee of the scarp is still there a generation on, sun-cured, wind-polished, hollow and exact.

And the people of the Dry-Floor have built their whole understanding on this. Their floor is not moss. Their floor is **memory that will not let go**. They dig the clay, yes — that is the trade, that is the pale bright thread that ties them to the cloud-people who paint their faces with it — but the clay is only the surface labor. The deep work, the work that makes them what they are, is keeping the bones.

## The Architecture of the Kept

Where Wet-Bound builds with what is alive and loses the argument to the green over and over and calls the losing an agreement, the Dry-Floor builds with what is finished and **wins**, and the winning is terrible and beautiful and is the whole shape of their lives.

They do not lash cane or daub peat. They have stone, and they have time, and they have the thing the cloud-people have almost none of: a past that stays put. They build low and long and into the rock, chambers cut back under the scarp where the sun can't crack them, and they line these chambers with the dead. Not crudely. With enormous care. The Dry-Floor people are the keepers of bones the way the Keeper of the Coal is the keeper of fire — it is the central office, the sacred and lonely one, and where Wet-Bound has a single Keeper because it has a single fire, the Dry-Floor has many, because down here in the dry there is no scarcity of the thing that must be kept. Every household tends its own ranked dead, cleaned and set in the cool of the cut stone, ancestor behind ancestor going back further than any voice can carry, a wall of skulls patient as the scarp itself.

This is why a person from Wet-Bound, climbing down, finds the lower country uncanny in exactly the place where their own home is uncanny to us. Up there, a stranger aches for an edge and finds only soft grey. Down here, a cloud-person walks into a bone-lined chamber and feels the floor of their whole world drop away — because here is *everything their moss ate*, kept. Here is the past they gave up. The Dry-Floor people watch them stand in the ossuary with their large dark light-gathering eyes gone wide and wet and they understand, dimly, that they are looking at someone who has never had a grandmother to point to. And the cloud-person, for their part, looks at a wall of three hundred years of held faces and cannot say whether it is the most precious thing they have ever seen or the most frightening, and the truth is it is both, and that the two countries are the same truth read from opposite ends.

## How They Hear, Which Is to Say They Don't

Here is the deepest reversal, and the one that took longest to grow.

The people of Wet-Bound are the great listeners. They stand on a floor of sound and tune their whole bodies to feel the *hole* in it — the silence is their one alarm, and when the frogs stop, three hundred souls go to stone and turn their pale-marked faces toward the quiet and wait for what walks out of the white.

The Dry-Floor has no floor of sound to stand on. It is **quiet here.** Not silent — there is wind, there is the tick of cooling stone, the dry rattle of grass, the far call of a single hard-throated bird crossing the open. But it is the kind of quiet that up in Wet-Bound would mean *something has come*, would mean *go still, turn, wait*. The lower people live, by the cloud-people's measure, inside permanent alarm. A Wet-Bound trader's first nights on the Dry-Floor are sleepless and dreadful; the absence of the wall presses on them like a held breath that never lets go, and they lie awake waiting for the white shape to come out of a cloud that is hundreds of feet above their heads and never comes down this far.

And so the Dry-Floor people learned the opposite skill. They are not listeners. They are **watchers**. The lower air is clear and the sun draws edges on everything and there are long sightlines across the open terraces — so where the cloud-people tuned their attention *through* sound to feel a shape they could not see, the floor-people tuned their attention *along* light to see a shape long before it could reach them. The cloud-people read a hundred shades of grey that all look like one shade. The floor-people read the far line of the land, the place where dust rises, the single moving dark against the bright, and they know a traveller from a beast from a wind-devil at a distance that would be three full levels of green up above. Each country made an eye and an ear of itself, and each made the *other* one for the other's world. Carry a cloud-person to the Dry-Floor and they go deaf and blind at once — too much light, too little sound. Carry a floor-person up into the green and the same: the wall of noise drowns them, the grey blinds them, and they cannot see twelve feet through the leaf and cannot feel the hole in the frogsong that the slope-children read in their cradles.

The traders, the few, the ones who climb between, are the only people in either world who have learned to live with one eye and one ear in each country, and they are strange in both — too still for the green, too watchful for the floor, smelling of clay above and of cloud below, at home in the climb and nowhere else.

## The Pale Thread

And the clay itself. Always come back to the clay, because it is the one thing that crosses entire.

Down here it is just the scarp-vein, the pale seam they dig from the dry red rock, and it means *bones* — they grind it, they wash the dead with it, they pack it into the cut chambers, it is the color of the kept and the cured and the finished, the color the dry sun bleaches everything toward in the end. To the Dry-Floor a smear of pale clay on dark stone says: *here lies one who is remembered.* It is the color of the past that stays.

They send it up. They do not fully know why the cloud-people want so much of it, only that they always do, that no load is too large, that the small broad-footed traders will carry it up into the white and come back with woven things already half-turned to rot, with the small white orchids that grow on nothing, with stories of a place where the dead vanish in a season and the floor is made of voices.

And up there, on the ridge, in the shadowless grey, the cloud-people take the pale clay of the lower country's *dead* and paint it on their *living* faces — because they have no bones to keep and no past to point to and no hard light to throw an edge, and so the only true bright thing they have, the only way to be *seen* across the seething green, the only mark that cuts the grey, is the color the Dry-Floor uses to say *remembered*. They wear the lower world's memory as their visibility. A child of Wet-Bound is marked with the same pale that lines a Dry-Floor ossuary, and neither child knows it is the same substance, and the substance is the same, and it climbs the slope on a trader's back through the one seam of cloud that joins a country of held breath to a country that has forgotten how to hold one.

One world. The moss eats and the bone keeps. The frogs roar and the dust hangs still. They paint the dead and they paint the living with the same pale hand, and between them, on the long climb through the white, a few strange people go up and down carrying the only thing that belongs to both.
