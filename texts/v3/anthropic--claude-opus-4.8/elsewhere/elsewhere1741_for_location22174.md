# The Eaves

Up where the fault opens, there is a slope of broken limestone — the rubble the cave coughed out when it cracked — and above that, the land remembers how to be land. This is the Eaves: not a place with a single edge but a long shallow lip of country where the ground forgets the dark and learns the weather again. It is named for the way it overhangs. Stand at the right point and you are standing on the roof of the Roostvault without knowing it, the whole buried roar going on beneath your boots, twenty feet of stone between your weight and the hung thousands. People who live here do not think about this. People who live here think about the wind.

Because there is wind. That is the first thing, the way the sound was the first thing below. Up here the wind is the medium the way moisture was the medium down there, and it is never still and never loud, a long continuous pour of air across open grass, carrying the smell of stone and crushed thyme and, faintly, when it gusts from the right quarter, the warm ammoniac breath of the cave itself, leaking up through the cracks. The grass leans all one way. The few trees — hawthorn, mostly, and a black twist of juniper — grow shaped by it, all their branches streaming inland like a held gesture, like something perpetually reaching away from the edge.

## The Edge

The Eaves end in a fall. Not a cliff exactly — the land doesn't break, it *pours*, sloping steeper and steeper until the grass gives out and there's only the pale scree running down to a valley you can't see the bottom of from standing. The light here is the opposite of the light below: too much of it, a wide flat brightness with nothing to catch it, a sky that goes white at the edges. There is no doubling. There is only the one sun and it moves, and the shadows it throws move with it, long in the morning, gone at noon, long the other way at dusk, so that the whole place is a slow sundial and time is not a circle here but a thing that arrives and departs and arrives again, marked, counted, *spent*.

That is the difference, and it lives in everyone who stays.

## The Roost-Folk

Because people stay. That is the second hard thing to understand, and it connects downward through the rock.

They are the same stock. You would not think it to look at them — they are brown where the cave-thing is pale, weathered where it is translucent, their eyes hard and bright and quick where its sockets hold only pooled grey light. But the chipped stone in the cave and the chipped stone in their hands are cousins. The same knapping, the same angle of strike, the same simple keen edge — only theirs is *dull*, theirs needs reworking, theirs is forever being struck again because up here the world rounds everything, wears it down, uses it up, demands it be remade. Where the cave-edge stays sharp forever in its frozen present, the Eaves-edge blunts with every cut, and a child here learns before anything else how to knap a new edge from an old core, because an unblunting tool is a thing they have never seen and could not imagine. To them a tool that stays sharp would be a tool that is dead.

They came from below. That is the memory they keep, the long worn shape of it: that there was a time, ungraspably back, when the people lived down in the endless harvest where nothing failed and nothing ran out, and some of them climbed up through the fault into the wind and the counted light and the falling country where everything fails and everything runs out — and *chose it*. Chose scarcity. Chose the wearing-down. The story is not told as a fall and not told as a rescue; it is told the way you tell a thing you cannot fully justify but cannot regret. *We came up to be the kind of thing that stays sharp by being struck.*

## The Trade That Runs Both Ways

Here is the current that connects the two places, the thing that runs out of one and into the other:

Twice in a turning of the seasons, when the wind drops to its lowest and the cracks breathe their warmest, certain of the Roost-folk go down. Not to stay — they have unlearned the staying, or learned the not-staying, which is harder. They go down through the fault with ropes and pitch-lamps and empty hide sacks, and they harvest the harvest. They take the guano, the deep soft ancient guano, hauling it up in the sacks to spread across the thin grass of the Eaves, because the Eaves are poor — the wind strips the soil, the slope sheds the rain, nothing here is given, everything must be fed — and the cave is rich past reckoning, the cave is a buried valley of endless fertility that does nothing with it, that simply *holds* it in the unchanging dark. So the people carry the richness up into the poverty. They carry the changeless plenty up into the place where things can change. And the grass greens where they spread it, and the goats fatten, and the children eat, and the cave does not notice, because the numbers below do not move, because what the people take is exactly the eternal half-meal's worth and the heap stays the same heap forever.

They do not like going down. The young ones boast of it and the old ones go quiet. Because down there, in the great soft roar, on the bare pale flats between the glowing pools, sometimes — not always, but sometimes — a harvester working alone will straighten from the guano with his sack half-full and find that something pale has come and stood near him. A body's length off. Eyeless. Breathing with the breath that never quite releases. Waiting.

And the harvester knows it. Not as a stranger. As *kin* — as the ones who stayed, the cousins of the cousins who chose the dark and the changelessness and the eternal half-finished meal. He knows that this is what he would have been. That this is what the family was, before some unjustifiable ancestor decided to climb up and be worn down by weather instead. He stands in the doubled faint light with his dull tool and his counted time and his sack of stolen plenty, and the pale thing stands in its no-color with its keen eternal edge and its circle of time so large it reads as a line, and for a moment the two halves of one old choice face each other across a body's length of bare stone.

Then the harvester finishes filling his sack. Because the light up the fault is for him; he is the kind of thing that climbs back out. He shoulders the weight and turns toward the grey thread of daylight, and the pale thing watches — or does the eyeless thing that has replaced watching — to see whether this one, too, will set down the sack and forget the wind and stay. And the harvester climbs, fast, faster than the load should allow, carrying the roar in his ears like water, climbing up out of the place that never changes into the place that is nothing *but* change, where the wind has not stopped, where the shadows have moved on without him, where the edge of his tool has already begun, even now, even in the climbing, to dull.

## The Roof

At night the Roost-folk light no fires near the edge, because the wind would only steal the warmth and fling it down the scree. They huddle inland where the hawthorn breaks the pour of air, and they sleep, and the day departs and is spent and does not come back — only a new one comes, which is not the same, which must be lived through and used up in its turn.

And under them, all night, all day, the same night, the same day, the one unchanging dim — the breath goes on. The drip keeps its thousand clocks. The pale thing wades the glowing pools and cracks its crickets and adds to the heap that stays the same. The two places lie one atop the other, roof and floor, the counted and the eternal, the worn-down and the frozen-sharp, separated by twenty feet of young limestone and one ancestor's unjustifiable choice — and the wind carries the cave's warm breath up through the cracks, and the people carry the cave's cold plenty up through the fault, and the trade runs both ways forever, and neither place becomes the other, and both of them go on being exactly, eternally, what they decided once to be.
