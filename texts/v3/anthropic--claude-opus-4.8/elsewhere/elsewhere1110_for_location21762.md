# The Quick Shore

Start where the water remembers it had a surface.

It is far above and far aside from the lattice — far in the way that matters down there, which is not distance but speed. To reach it you would have to follow the cold upward through the rising columns of colder water until the rising water warms, and warming, hurries; until the muffled water-quiet acquires an edge again; until somewhere overhead a brightness begins that is not the inside-of-a-closed-eye blue but a real and violent white, and you break — no. You cannot break. There is no surfacing from the lattice. But the water can. The water that downwells cold through the drowned halls is the same water that, somewhere, an age before or an age after, was thrown into the air off this shore and came down as foam.

That is the kinship. Not a passage — a circulation. Every drop that presses evenly on the Tenders' worn hands has been here, will be here, has stood in the air a moment as spray and felt the sun and the noise and the appalling quickness of this place, and has forgotten it, and will go down again to be cold and slow and patient. The shore is what the lattice's water does when it is young.

## What the Shore Is

A line of black rock at the foot of a continent, where the land ends in a violence of basalt and the sea arrives without patience. The waves come in sets, and each wave is a fast thing — it rises, it leans, it falls white and roaring across the rock in the space of a single held breath, and is gone, and another is already rising behind it. The whole shore runs on the rhythm of the breaking wave, which is the rhythm of a heart at a sprint, the opposite in every particular of the deep low groan that takes an age to arrive and an age to fade. Here a sound is born and dies inside a second. Here everything is edges. The salt spray is sharp. The light is sharp. The wind comes off the water carrying the smell of torn weed and cracked shell and ozone, and it does not muffle anything; it scours.

The rock is *young*. You can tell by the tooling — not the soft scallop of an adze but the raw fracture of stone that was liquid not long ago, geologically speaking, and cooled in a hurry into columns and shelves with edges still sharp enough to open a hand. Nothing here has had time to round. The waves are working on it — the same patient water, doing the same patient wearing — but the waves are *also* fast, and the speed of their arrival hides the slowness of their effect, so that the shore appears to be a place of pure velocity even though it is, like the lattice, only water and stone in their long negotiation. The shore is what that negotiation looks like when it is loud.

## What Lives Here

Everything, and all of it in a hurry.

Where the lattice gave its whole world over to plants because no animal could bear the cold dark patience, the shore is the inverse: an animal place, a place of appetite and alarm, where to be slow is to be eaten. The tide-pools left in the rock between sets are crowded the way the lattice's halls are crowded with weed — many living things per handspan, near-touching, overlapping — but here the crowding *moves*. Crabs the size of a thumbnail bolt sideways into crevices. Anemones snap shut around what drifts into them. Small fish flicker in the shallow pools in schools that turn all at once, a hundred bodies deciding in less than an instant, the fastest collective thought in any world. Birds stand at the waterline on legs like wet wire and stab down and come up with something writhing and are aloft again before the next wave lands. Predation everywhere. Flight everywhere. The whole shore is a single nervous system firing.

And it is all *short-lived*. This is the thing that would break a Tender's slow heart, if a Tender could ever come here, which it cannot — the heat alone would be a kind of murder, the speed a kind of murder. Here a life is a flash. The crab lives a season. The small fish lives less. The anemone, ancient by shore-standards, might see a few years before the wave that finally takes the rock it clings to takes it too. Things are born and breed and die inside the span that, in the lattice, a single frond would take to bend a little further. The shore burns through generations the way it burns through waves: one after another after another, each complete, each gone, each replaced before it can be mourned. There is no holding here. Nothing tends the shape. The shape is *thrown away and remade* a thousand times an hour, and that constant discarding is the only way this place persists at all.

## The Tide-Watcher

There is a being here too, and it is the shore's answer to the Tender — the same office held under opposite law.

It does not lie still and worn into the rock. It cannot; the rock is not safe to rest on, the next wave would take anything that rested. It is small and quick and warm-blooded and frantic, and it lives at the tide-line, which is the most dangerous and most fertile margin of the whole shore — the strip the sea covers and uncovers, covers and uncovers, on the fast rhythm of the breaking sets. The Tide-Watcher works that margin. Its hands — and it has hands, this is the kinship made flesh, broad clever hands at the ends of thin fast arms — are forever moving: turning a stone to see what hides beneath, prying a shell, snatching back from the white rush of an arriving wave at the last possible instant, an instant it judges hundreds of times a day and must never misjudge, because to misjudge once is to be gone.

Where the Tender's work is to *keep*, the Tide-Watcher's work is to *catch* — to be in the right handspan of the margin at the right splinter of a second, to take what the wave uncovers before the wave covers it again, to read the rhythm of the sets the way the Tender reads the long groan of settling stone. Both of them listen to the water. Both of them have learned their whole world by hand, by the feel of it. But the Tender listens for the one low note in an age, and the Tide-Watcher listens for the next wave, which is always *now*, always *now*, always *now*, and its listening is a thing of terror and delight strung so tight that it has no room in it for memory at all.

For that is the difference that lives between the two places, carried in the same water:

The Tender remembers building, and aches, slowly, at the rounding of a carved face it shaped in a time it can no longer reach. It has memory because it has duration — because nothing here is lost quickly, the past stays close enough to touch, and the touching is a slow grief held at arm's length for the span of a day. The Tide-Watcher remembers *nothing*. It cannot afford to. A life so fast and so endangered burns its memory as fuel; there is the wave that is coming and the hands that must move and nothing behind. When a Tide-Watcher dies — taken by a wave it misjudged, or simply spent, its quick heart stopped after its quick season — another is already working the margin a stone's throw down the shore, and does not know the first one lived, and will not be mourned in turn. The shore does not hold its dead the way the lattice holds its faces. The shore throws them to the same water that will, an age later and a world deeper, press cold and even on the back of a Tender's worn hand.

## The Two Faithfulnesses

So they will never meet, the slow holder and the quick catcher, and yet they are the same office. Each stands at the working edge of its world's law. Each keeps faith with the only rule its place allows.

The Tender keeps the shape against dissolution, pressing back against the smoothing dark with hands worn to nubs, because in the lattice the danger is that everything will *settle* — will wear, gently and forever, into one indistinguishable finished thing, and the made will dissolve into the grown, and there will be nothing left that intends. The Tender's whole long labor is to delay that ending by an age, by a hair, by the resetting of one stone per epoch.

The Tide-Watcher keeps no shape at all, because in the shore the danger is the opposite: that anything held will be *smashed*. Here the shape must be surrendered and remade a thousand times an hour, and faith means letting go fast enough, snatching the hand back before the wave, spending the self completely and dying on time so the next quick life can have the margin. The Tide-Watcher delays nothing. It races the water and loses, gladly, again and again, and the losing is the work.

And the water goes between them, indifferent and entire. It rises off the black rock in a roar and stands an instant in the bright air, having just been the medium a Tide-Watcher's hand cut through, and the wind takes its salt, and the cold takes it down, and it sinks for an age out of all light and all hurry, slowing as it goes, until at last it arrives in the drowned halls and presses, patient and even and just above freezing, on a worked surface a Tender is holding still against the pull of ten thousand years — the same drop, the whole of one world's water, fast at the top and slow at the bottom, loud where it is young and quiet where it is old, catching and keeping, breaking and holding, in a single circulation that no swimmer and no Tender and no Tide-Watcher will ever follow all the way around, because to know it whole you would have to be both the quick thing and the slow thing at once, and no living creature in either place can bear to be more than half of what the water is.
