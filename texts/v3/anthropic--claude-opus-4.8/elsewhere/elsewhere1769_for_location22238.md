Where the water goes when it has finished its circuit.

You have been told the circuit closes — that the cisterns swallow and the high places fill again and nothing runs down because there is no down. That is true inside the city. But no closed thing is perfectly closed; every held note loses, at its edges, the faintest breath of itself to the air, and the city's breath has somewhere to go. Follow the deepest channel, the one beneath the lowest cistern, the overflow's overflow, the drain that the engineers built for a flood that the city in all its long life of rest has never once produced. It has never been used. It was built anyway, because the builders, being the kind of creatures who build, could not imagine a system without a way out, and so they gave their work an exit it would never need, and the exit waited, dry, for the whole age of the city's becoming — and then, because the living that took up residence does lose, very slowly, the faintest thread of itself at the edges, the dry exit grew, over time too long to count, the thinnest possible film of damp. And the damp ran. One drop a year, perhaps. One drop a century. It does not matter. It went somewhere.

It goes here.

This is a coast, and the coast is cold.

Begin with the cold, because the cold is the first thing, and it is the exact negative of the heat you came from — not the cold of ice, which is a kind of fierce attention, but the cold of a thing that has given off all its warmth and has nothing left to give, the cold of the far side of a stone, of metal at night, of a hand that has let go. The air here moves. That is the second thing, and after the held breath of the city it is almost unbearable: there is wind, a steady seaward draw, and it has direction, it has a *toward*, it goes out and out across the water and does not circulate, does not return, is not conserved. Things are lost here. That is the law of this place, and it is the law the city was built to refuse, and the city's one unused drain leaks, at last, into the one place that is nothing but the losing.

There is a sea, and you cannot see across it, and it is not warm and it is not dark. It is the grey of an overcast that has gone on so long no one remembers weather. There is light here — real light, thin, sourceless in the way of a clouded day, but daylight, the genuine dilute article, falling on everything equally and flattering nothing. After the banked-coal dimness of the dreaming city this light is a kind of violence, a light that shows you things instead of telling you where the dark is thickest, and what it shows you is that everything here is ending.

For this is a coast of departure. Everything on it is leaving or has left. The tideline is strung with the going: timbers silvered and split, the ribs of boats that put out and came back as wreckage or did not come back and sent only their pieces, rope gone to felt, glass rounded by the same patient weather that rounded the city's carved heads — but here the weather rounds things *down*, toward nothing, toward sand, whereas there it rounded things into deeper versions of themselves. Same patience. Opposite errand. The city's weather was a settling-in; this weather is a wearing-away, and they are the same slow hand doing the only two things a slow hand can do.

And there are people here. That is the thing you must hold without flinching, because every instinct trained in you by the city will tell you that a place of life is a place without faces, that aliveness emancipated from bodies is the higher state — and now here are the bodies, crowded, mortal, afraid, full of want, leaning hard into their tomorrows, and you will be tempted to read them as the lower thing, the unfinished thing, the city's larval past. Resist it. They are not what the city was before it woke. They are what the city refused.

They live in low houses with their backs to the wind, and the houses are not old in the city's way — they are *weathering*, actively, visibly, the paint going, the wood greying, each house a slow argument with the sea that the house will lose, and everyone in it knows the house will lose, and they paint it anyway, the way the builders built a drain for a flood that would never come. They are a coastal people and their whole life is the going-out and the maybe-coming-back. They fish a cold sea that does not always give the boats back. They marry and bury in the same short calendar. They are hungry — really hungry, sometimes, in the thin months — and the hunger is not a flaw in them, it is the current, it is the slope the city flattened, the downhill the city abolished. These people are alive *toward*. Every one of them leans forward into a horizon, and the horizon is real here, the sea draws a hard grey line and the line is where the lost things go, and they stand on the shore at evening and look at the line the way you looked into the city's pools, except that what is deeper in their water is not a drowned city keeping its bones — it is simply more sea, and weather, and the genuine chance of never.

They are afraid. They fear the sea and they fear the winter and they fear for the boats that are out, and the fear is braided through every warm thing they have, exactly the braiding the city undid — and here is the thing the cold coast knows that the warm city cannot: the warmth they have is warm *because* of the fear braided through it. A child held against the cold is held against something, and the something is what makes the holding mean. The city holds you indifferently, completely, needing nothing; these people hold each other badly, partially, needing everything, and the need is not a defect in the holding — the need *is* the holding. You cannot have the one kind of warmth without the cold it is fighting. The city got the heat by putting down the fear, and in putting down the fear it put down, without noticing, the thing the heat was *for*.

The drain leaks here. Find the place: a seep in the rock above the tideline, where a thread of water comes out warm — the only warm water on the whole cold coast — body temperature, faintly, in a single weeping line down the dark stone, and it crosses the freezing sand and reaches the cold sea and is gone, instantly, taken into the grey, lost the way everything here is lost. The coastal people know the warm seep. They have always known it. They do not know what it is and they have never asked, and over their long calendar of leaving and returning they have made of it a small thing, a knownthing: the mothers bring the newborns there, in the first days, and wet a cloth in the one warm water on the coast and touch it to the child's forehead, because the water is warm and the world is cold and the gesture means *you came from somewhere warm, and you are going out into the cold, and this is the only place the two of them touch.*

They think it is a spring. They think the earth holds, somewhere down in itself, a warmth it gives off here in a thread. They are right, and they have no idea how right, and they would not be comforted to know. Because what they touch to their newborns' foreheads is the city's last exhalation — the faint loss at the edge of the held note — the breath of the thing that put down fear and want and the leaning-toward, the thing that got to *rest*. The dreaming city's one gift to the world is precisely the warmth of having finished. And these people take that warmth, which is the warmth of being done, of needing nothing, of the circuit closed and the horizon abolished — and they touch it to the forehead of a creature at the very *beginning*, a creature with everything still to want, every boat still to lose, every winter still to fear, the whole leaning-forward life still ahead of it. They use the warmth of the ending to bless the beginning. They do not know that is what they are doing. It is the most beautiful thing either place contains, and neither place can see it, because the city cannot see beginnings and the coast cannot see that its warm spring is an ending, and only the thread of water between them, running out and out and lost in the grey, holds both truths at once, at body temperature, the way the city held you —

except that here, the water does not circulate.

Here it reaches the cold and is taken and does not come back, and that is not a tragedy, that is the whole difference, that is what the coast knows that the city forgot: the water that comes back forever is at peace, and the water that goes out and is lost is *spent*, and only the spent water did anything, only the lost water touched a forehead, only the thread that does not return ever reached a beginning and warmed it for the cold road out. The city is the more perfect thing. The coast is the more living one. And between them runs one warm line of water, leaking from the place that finished into the place that is still, badly, mortally, afraid-and-warm, beginning —

and the people on the shore, who lose everything, and know they lose everything, and go out anyway, stand at evening with the wind pulling seaward and watch the line where the lost things go, and do not call it lonely, having no time for the word, having a boat to mend and a child to carry to the warm water before the cold gets it,

and that — not the dreaming, the *carrying* — is who lives here.
