The Long Shore

Follow the water back, not down into the seam but along it — the drop that fell and was gone, the one that did not pool because the floor drank it. It went somewhere. Water does not vanish; it is only ever moving toward the lowest patience available to it. Run with the seep through the cracks the engine has not yet closed, through the cooling dark, through rock that is older the farther you go from the held breath, rock that has finished arguing and settled into being merely old. The temperature falls a degree a mile. The sulfur sigh thins out. And somewhere very far along, where the new world's heartbeat has become so faint it is no longer a sound but only a rumor the stone tells itself, the water arrives where all water goes when it has finished being made.

It arrives at the shore.

And here is a place that has decided, fully and long ago, what it is. If the pocket was a thing being born, this is a thing being worn — the opposite labor, the other end of the only sentence the world knows how to write. Here is flat grey light over flat grey water, a beach that goes on past the curve of seeing, and the slow exhausted lapping of a sea that has nowhere left to rise to. The held breath was all interior. This is all exterior, all surface, all *out* — a horizon in every direction and a sky the color of a thing that has been rained through so many times it has forgotten how to be blue.

The sand is the secret. Stoop and look. It is black. It is basalt — *the same* basalt, the sharp-edged new floor of the chamber, except that here every edge is gone. Every grain has been carried and tumbled and ground for so long that the geometry which could have cut your hand has been polished into a smooth dark roundness, each particle a fingerprint of the same long patience that built it, run backward. The engine extrudes the floor. The shore receives the floor, eventually, in the form of dust. What is born sharp arrives here soft. The whole beach is the chamber's body, finally, after a journey of unspeakable miles and years — its rock dissolved to silt, washed down the seam, delivered grain by grain to the lowest place, and laid out flat under a sky it spent its whole gestation not knowing was there. The light the engine carried inside itself, that sealed afternoon glow — this is the sky it was a yolk-memory of. The creature never met it. Its dust does. Its dust meets nothing else.

Because someone lives here, too.

She lives by the opposite principle, which is to say the same principle seen from the far end. Where the engine accretes, she *gathers what has come apart*. She is small — human-sized, or near it, the first thing in this telling that you could look in the eye — and she is bent, always, at the waist, walking the tideline with a flat woven tray, sifting. You would think at first she is looking for something. She is not looking for something. She is sorting the black sand from the black sand, lifting a tray of it, letting the wind take the finest motes, tilting the rest, reading it with fingers that have done this so long they no longer consult her about it. She keeps what is heavy. She returns what is light to the beach. And what is heavy, when you finally see her open her hand, is iron — fine red-brown grains of it, rust-colored, ore-colored, the exact color of the staples driven into the wall of the chamber she has never seen and never will.

This is the kinship, and it runs the whole length of the world between them, and neither of them knows the other exists.

For the iron in her palm is the same iron. Not metaphorically. Some earlier visitor — the one who drove the staples, who capped the pipe going nowhere, who hung the fogged gauge to measure the labor of a thing too large to be measured — that visitor's iron has been rusting in the wet for longer than the shore-woman's whole line of mothers. The seam-water eats it, grain by grain, dissolves the staple back into the rock from which someone once dug its ore. And the water carries the dissolved iron down, down the long seep, down past the cooling and the settling, and delivers it here, where it precipitates out of the lowest patience and lies among the ground bones of the engine as a fine red dust. She walks the shore and gathers the unmade evidence of the only mistake anyone ever made in that room: the belief that the engine could be tended. The staples were a monument to a wrong idea. And the wrong idea, rusted, washed down, and reclaimed, is the entire economy of her life.

She does not know this. She knows the iron. She knows that some shores give it and some do not, that the tide brings more after the rains, that her tray must be tilted just so or the good grains go back to the sea with the light ones. She smelts it — there is a low stone furnace up the beach, the only vertical thing for miles, and at night it glows the red that the chamber's plain light pointedly refused to be. She makes, from the dust of the dissolved staples, new iron. Small things. Tray-rims and fish-hooks and the long pins she uses to fasten her coat against the wind that never stops coming off the grey water. And — this is the part that closes the circle so quietly that the world itself seems not to notice — she makes staples. Flat iron staples, for fastening, for holding, for driving into stone where something must be hung.

She does not know what they are for. There is no stone here worth driving into; the rock is all underfoot, all flat, all beach. She makes them because her mother made them and because the shape is in her hands. She keeps them in a tin. They go nowhere visible. They hold nothing. A capped pipe, a coil of staples, an instrument made and set aside — the same fossils on both ends of the world, the leavings of effort that has lost its object, except here they are being made instead of unmade, the rust running backward into shape.

If she ever followed the water the other way — upstream, against her whole life's grain, into the warming dark, into the seam, into the held breath where the rock is still sharp — she would arrive at last in the pocket and find iron staples already driven into the wall, rusting, holding nothing. And she would not know them for her own work returned to its origin. She would think, as the first visitor thought, *someone tends this place*. She would assume a worker. She would perhaps reach into her coat for the tin and drive a fresh staple into the wet wall beside the old ones, to mark that she had been there, to measure, to hold — and the engine would take no notice, and the rust would begin, and the seam would carry her iron back down to a shore she had left, where some daughter of her daughter, bent at the waist, tray in hand, would one day gather it from the black sand and call it a gift of the tide.

But she will not follow the water back. No one does, twice. The shore is the lowest place and the lowest place is the one you do not climb out of; that is the whole meaning of lowest. She works the tideline in the flat grey light. The sea laps without ambition. The wind takes the fine motes. And out past the sorting, past the furnace, past the long curve of black sand that is the dust of a thing still being born unthinkable miles and years away, the horizon holds its single grey line — the only edge in this whole place that has not been worn smooth, because it is not made of anything, because it is only the distance agreeing to stop.

She straightens, sometimes, to ease her back. She looks at the water. She thinks — when she thinks at all, which is rarely, the work being older than her thoughts — that the iron must come from somewhere, that the sea must be grinding it off some unseen country, some far cliff or buried vein, that everything on a shore is the leaving of an elsewhere. In this she is exactly right and exactly wrong, the way the visitor was right that something was being made and wrong about what. There is an elsewhere. It is not a cliff. It is alive, and it is young, and it does not know it is feeding her, and it carries inside its unfinished body the very light she is standing in, and it will go on beating long after her furnace is cold and her tin is rust and her shore has been ground down to a sand even finer and laid out flat under a sky even greyer, by the same slow hands that made it.

The drop fell in the held breath and was gone. It is here now. It is the sea. It laps at her boots, deposits its freight of dissolved iron and ground basalt, withdraws, returns. She bends. She lifts the tray. She is the part of the engine that gathers what the engine sheds, the far cool end of the warm long body, the place where becoming has turned all the way over into the patient business of being worn back down — and between the two of them, the maker and the gatherer, the held breath and the open shore, runs the whole water of the world, carrying iron in one direction and never, not once, a single word in the other.
