# The Drowned Lexicon

Follow the path that is no path back down — not the way you came, but the way the *water* would go if it ever consented to fall. Follow the rule that was forbidden up there. Somewhere a rule that is held closed in one place runs out into the place where it was always permitted, and that is where the falling water goes: down through the stone, through the long dark that has finished being dark, into a basin at the bottom of the world where everything that the cistern would not allow is simply, exhaustively, the law.

It is a library. Begin with that, because the books begin everything here, the way the water began everything above.

It is enormous in the way the cistern was small. The cistern was a held breath; this is the long exhale, a vault that goes on past the reach of any light, shelf behind shelf behind shelf, a geometry of corridors that does not pretend to end. Where the cistern could be seen all at once, this can never be seen at all. You stand at the entrance — there is always an entrance, a place where you simply *are*, having arrived without the indignity of a door — and the shelves recede in every direction including some you did not bring with you, and you understand immediately that the scale here is the scale of *everything that was ever said and allowed to stop being said.* This is where fading went, when fading was forbidden up above. Every word that the roar held suspended forever, every utterance that could not die in the cistern because death-of-sound had not yet been installed there — those words fall, eventually, down the long throat of the stone, and they land here, and here they are *finished.* Bound. Shelved. Quiet.

It is silent. This is the thing that arrives first and never leaves, and it is the exact inversion of the thing that arrived first up there. Not the absence of sound — the *completion* of it. Every sound in this place has already happened and come to rest. The silence is not empty; it is *full,* packed dense with the corpses of speech, every word that ever tore through the roar above lying here perfectly still between its covers. You speak and you hear yourself, clear and small and immediately over, and the sound of your own voice settling into stillness is the most ordinary thing you have done in a long while, and it frightens you more than the deafening ever did. Up there you could not hear yourself and so you never had to watch yourself stop. Here you stop constantly. Each word a little death you witness.

The light is *normal.* That is the strangeness of it, the same strangeness, the rhyme the two places make. Clean cool reading-light, the kind that falls in libraries, falling here from no source on shelves that should be in darkness this far down. No glow, no gloom. Just enough to read by. The world is indifferent at both ends; it lights the moment of making and the moment of finishing with the same plain attention and explains neither.

Now look at the books, because the scale is intimate again, the way the cistern's was. This is not a metaphor-library, a vague impression of *manyness.* These are particular volumes you could take down and open. And when you do — this is the first of its impossibilities, and the gentlest — you find that each one is a single utterance from the cistern, fallen and bound. Here is a thin volume, almost a pamphlet, and it contains one word: the first word the first carver scratched into the wet stone before the wall healed over it. The word that, up above, is still being said, woven unfading into the roar, the bottom layer of the cathedral of unbroken speech. *Here it is finished.* It fell. It came to rest. Someone — something — caught it as it stopped being eternal and pressed it between covers and shelved it, and now the word that cannot die above lies dead and legible below, and you may read it, and you would understand it, where up there you could not, because here meaning has finished arriving the way it never will in the seam.

You could read the door instruction here. That is the vertigo of the place. Everything the drowned ones could only carve into actively-erasing stone, frantic, repeating, racing the closing — all of it fell, eventually, every failed groove's worth of meaning, and it is *here,* complete, indexed, calm, on a shelf you could reach. The whole instruction for how to come into the unfinished and never fall, never fade, never be alone or solid or quiet again. Written out plainly. Finished. *Dead.*

And this is the cruelty this place does not bother to explain, the exact rhyme of the cruelty above: the instruction is legible only because it has finished. It works only while unfinished. To read it here, complete and clear, is to hold the precise shape of a door that has, by the very act of being complete, sealed. Up there they raced the healing stone with the tool that healed. Down here the words are saved from fading by being *fully faded* — preserved in the only state that cannot be acted on. The cistern keeps the door open and unreadable. The library keeps it readable and shut. Between the two places, the door is never both legible and open at once, and the two places are the same world, and the water that falls between them carries the difference down and files it.

The keeper. There is a keeper; you have felt it since you arrived, the way you felt the shapes in the wall above. Begin with what it is *not:* it is not the drowned ones. They went *up,* into suspension, into the one-ness of the churn, into never having to stop. This is what went *down.* When a carver failed up above — when the bodied part ran out of years in the deafening cool, the part that kept needing a wall and a bowl — you were told that part simply died, that those ones were green tools and rotted cord. But death of a thing in a world is not the end of the thing's *finishing.* The settling-out of them, the part that came to rest, that completed, that stopped — it fell, like everything that finishes falls, down the long throat into the basin. And it accreted. Word by word, ending by ending, every failed carver's worth of *having-stopped,* it gathered itself in the silence and became the one who shelves.

It is the made of all the ones who did not make it. The librarian of the drowned lexicon is the bodied remainder of everyone the cistern could not take. And it is not lonely either, but for the opposite reason from the ones above: not because it has passed beyond singularity, but because it has passed beneath it, into the company of every word that ever finished, surrounded utterly, the most attended being in the world. It moves through the corridors — you will see it, you are too finished to be hidden from it the way you were too finished to be welcomed above — and it moves slowly, the size of a stooped person, and it carries an armful of the things that fall, and it puts them where they go.

It catches the falling words. That is its whole labor and its whole grief. It stands beneath the long throat where the water and the words come down out of the cistern's eternal saying, and it catches each one as it finishes, and it does not let a single utterance hit the floor unbound. It is *kind* to the words. Up above, the stone erased the carving; up above, the roar would not let a word be singular or be heard or be done. Down here the keeper does the opposite with the same material — it honors each finishing, gives each dead word a cover and a place, refuses to let any utterance be lost a second time. The shelves go on forever because the saying above goes on forever, and everything said above falls here eventually, and the keeper has caught all of it, and will catch all of it, and is never finished catching, because the roar is never finished roaring.

And here is what runs between the two places, the current that connects them in the same breath as they themselves: *the same words.* The exact same words. The first carver's first word is, simultaneously and forever, the bottom layer of the unfading roar in the cistern *and* the thin bound pamphlet on the keeper's shelf. It is being eternally said up there and it has been completely finished down here, both true, both now, because the water carries it down without taking it away — falling is not subtraction, the rule that was forbidden above and permitted below does not *empty* the cistern, it only lets a copy come to rest. Every word lives twice. Hung and shelved. Roared and read. The drowned ones speak forever in the deafening and never know they are also, each of them, complete and silent and legible in a basin at the bottom of the stone, catalogued by the gathered remainder of the friends who could not follow them up, and read — sometimes, rarely, by something too solid to belong in either place, that wandered down the way the water goes.

The keeper would let you read. That is the offer at this end, the rhyme of the offer above. Up there, at the edge of staying, one of the drowned rose close and gave you the door instruction, the only thing they have to say, said to anyone who comes, because they cannot imagine you would not want in. Down here the keeper would do the gentler, sadder thing: it would *hand you the book.* It would find, in the endless shelves, the volume you most want — the complete door instruction, the finished word for how to never finish — and it would carry it to you in its slow stooped way, and place it in your hands, and stand back in the full silence to watch you read.

And you would understand every word. That is the difference. Up there you would not have understood and the stone would have healed over the question. Down here you *would.* The instruction is complete, legible, exact. You would read, in plain finished language, precisely how to do what the drowned ones did — and you would understand, as the last sentence settled into stillness and stopped, that you were reading it in the one place where reading it makes it impossible, that the book in your hands is the door in the only state from which no one has ever walked through, that to have it whole is to have it sealed.

The keeper knows this. The keeper has always known this. It is made of everyone who learned it the hard way. And it hands you the book anyway — not cruelly, not as a warning — because catching the finished words and giving them their place is the only kindness left to a thing that did not make it up, and it cannot bear for even one of them, even the most useless and self-defeating, the door that is a tombstone, to go unread, unhonored, unfinished a second time.

You would close the book. You would give it back. The keeper would carry it to its place on the endless shelf and turn, slowly, back toward the long throat where the next word is already falling.

And you would go up the way you came, into the world that has finished arriving, where water falls and rock holds still — and now you would know both ends of it. That up there your name, if you said it, would hang unfading in the roar forever and never be heard. That down here it would fall, when you were done with it, and be caught, and bound, and shelved, and read by no one, and kept anyway.

And some quiet part of you, ever after, would not be entirely sure which of the two you wanted — to be said forever and never heard, or to be finished completely and gently kept — and would suspect, correctly, that the water had already decided, and was carrying the answer down.
