The Headwaters

Follow the deep note backward. The great soft-bodied grazers call across the flats, and the note arrives at you softened, swallowed at its edges — but a sound is only softened if it has traveled, and travel implies somewhere it set out from. The valley's water lies everywhere just below the surface, and a thing that lies everywhere must, somewhere, have *come in*. Go upstream against the barely-moving sheets, against the hand's-breadth-a-century creep of the channels, against the grain of the long descent, and the land begins, very slowly, to argue again.

It takes a long time to notice. The flatness does not end so much as develop opinions. The low worn swells stop being merely the knuckles of spent granite and start, here and there, to *stand* — to hold a meter, then three, then a true small height, with a dry side and a wet side, with a sense of which way is down that the valley had entirely lost. The standing water stops standing. You feel it before you see it: the sheets you've waded for days begin, faintly, to *pull*, a direction entering the wetness, and the direction is the one you are walking against. The mist thins. The air loses some of its held weight. And the sound changes — the frog-floor and the insect-saw fall behind you, kilometer by kilometer, replaced by something cleaner and harder and more singular, a hiss, a rush, the high cold clatter of water that has not yet learned patience.

This is the country the water comes from, and it is the valley's opposite told in the same vocabulary.

Here the rock is not spent. It is young — or rather it is old rock freshly *exposed*, broken open, lifted and cracked, its faces sharp where the flat country's faces are rounded. The land is still climbing. You can see it climbing: tier above tier of grey stone going up into cloud that sits on the high ground the way the mist sat in the low ground, except this cloud is cold, and what it holds is not breath but the promise of more water arriving from above. The slopes are steep enough that water does not consider them. It *commits*. Every face is loud with falling — threads and ropes and sheets of it, white where it breaks on the steps of the rock, gathering in the cut clefts into the first true streams the world has, streams with a current you could be carried by, streams that argue with every stone they meet and win, a little, each time.

This is where the descent begins that the valley ends. The two places are the same gesture caught at its two extremes — one stone reaching up and breaking, the same stone, eons downstream, lain flat and drowned and singing. The water that weeps from the valley's slabs fell here first, hard, from the cold cloud onto the sharp tiers, and gathered, and cut, and carried — and everything it carried it took from *here*. The valley's soil is thin and leached because this is where the leaching started; the richness the flat country lacks is the richness this country is still losing, grain by grain, downward, on the current. The headwaters are wealthy in exactly the way the lowland is poor. Their soil is raw and mineral and new-broken, not yet washed of anything, and so — the inversion completing itself — *here the green crowds in*.

This is the thing you would have expected of the warm wet valley and did not find. Here it is, displaced upstream to the cold steep place that earns it. The clefts are choked with growth. Where a stream slows behind a boulder and lets its load of stolen soil settle, vegetation seizes it instantly, vertically, greedily — mosses furring every wet stone, ferns unrolling from every crack, and above them low twisted woody things gripping the slope with roots like clenched hands, and the whole steep world dim and dripping and *thick*, the light broken into green by layer on layer of leaf, the openness of the flatland inverted into a crowding so dense you cannot see ten meters. Down there the eye traveled kilometers and met a palm. Here it travels an arm's length and meets a wall of living matter, every surface taken, every drop of falling water claimed three times before it reaches the stream.

And here, too, the sound is loud, and here too the loudness and the crowding are the same fact — but said the other way. The valley was loud with animals because life there spread thin across the open and had to *call across the space*. This place is loud with water, and the animals are nearly silent, because in a crowding this dense and this steep no call carries, no territory can be held by voice, and a creature's whole life is lived within touching distance of the next leaf. The richness here is vegetable, standing, vertical, stacked — exactly the standing biomass the flat country gave up. The hiss and clatter of the falling water *is* the place's voice, the way the chorus of singers was the valley's, and just as the valley's deep beasts kept their counsel out in the middle sheets, here the few animals keep theirs in the thickets: small, damp, climbing things that grip and freeze and watch, that drink from the leaf-held water and never need the stream, that have made themselves not loud but *invisible*, the opposite solution to the same problem of how to persist.

What lives in the streams themselves is the through-line, the thing that ties the two places body to body. There are swimmers in these cold cuts — small, muscular, built entirely against the current, all of them facing upstream always, holding station in the rush with a patience that is the exact mirror of the valley grazers' patience: the great soft beasts let the warm water hold them up and move at its speed, while these hard little swimmers spend their whole strength refusing to move at the water's speed at all. And some of them, at a certain season, *stop refusing*. They turn. They let go of their station and run down the clattering clefts, down through the slowing, down out of the cold and the crowding and the falling, down the long descent the water taught them, into the warm wide sheets where the singing is, to breed in the buoyant warmth that asks nothing of them — and the valley, which I told you breeds everything in its own warm shallows, breeds these too, the upstream-born, the cold-made, arriving softened at the edges of themselves the way the deep note arrives softened at you. The strikers eat them in the shallows. The rushers take them from below. They came all that way down to be the food at the bottom of that loud arrived equilibrium, and a few — not many, a few — turn afterward and climb back up against everything, carrying the valley's warmth in them as eggs, back into the cold sharp loud crowded climbing country where the water is young and the land has not finished and nothing, yet, has gotten where it was going.

That is the difference, finally, and it is the whole difference. The valley has arrived. This place is still leaving. Down there nothing is reaching, because everything is fit; up here everything is reaching, because nothing is finished — the rock still lifting, the water still cutting, the green still seizing each new grain of broken soil, the swimmers still facing the current they will one day let go of. The flat country is the sound of creatures with nothing left to become. This is the sound of a country still becoming, loudly, coldly, all at once, with water — the same water, every drop of it bound downstream toward the place where the arguing stops.

It will get there. It is getting there now, a hand's breadth a century, on the current, in the cold clatter, under the cloud that sits on the high broken stone. Everything here is on its way to being everything there. The two places are not neighbors. They are the same place, early and late, joined by the only thing that ever made the journey between them and came back up to tell it — facing the current, holding station, carrying the warmth home against the descent.
