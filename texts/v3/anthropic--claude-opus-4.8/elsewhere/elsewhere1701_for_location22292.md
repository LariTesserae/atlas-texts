The Shallows of Ten Bells

Follow the warmth down, the rule said. But there is another way to read a sleeper, and it is to follow the warmth *up* — to ask not what it lies upon but what once lay upon *it*, what loved it before the sea did, what called the warmth a blessing and built in stone and passed bread window to window and then, one season or one century, was gone.

Surface. Not the impossible far surface above the drowned town — that water is too deep, too long-fallen, and no one who went up from there ever came to anywhere. Go sideways instead. Go shoreward, the long slow shoreward the risen sea has spent ages making, until the seabed climbs and the dark thins to green and then the green thins to glare, and you break out into a shallow bright bay that the same ocean holds in its other hand.

This is the place the people came from. Or came to. It is hard, now, to say which way the leaving went.

---

It is a town still above water, and that is the first strangeness after so long below — that here the air does the work the warmth did there. Here you are *meant* to surface; here breath is cheap and constant and unconsidered. The town climbs a hillside in tiers of pale stone, the same stone, the exact same stone, cut from the same patient quarries by hands that knew how to make a thing last past its purpose. You have seen these buildings before. You have swum down their drowned cousins. The lintels here are still sharp. The corners still hold their geometry. Water has not yet begun its sanding. Give it an age and it would — but the town is careful, and the town is high, and the town has learned, the way the body learns a rule it cannot name, to keep above the line where the sea takes things.

They call it Ten Bells, for the ten that hang in the tower at the harbor's mouth, though only nine remain. The tenth is the one that matters. Hold that. It will run downward when the time comes.

---

The bay is shallow and warm — sun-warm, an honest borrowed surface heat, nothing like the held blood-warmth of the deep. The light here is *given*, not made; it falls from above in the ordinary extravagant way, and so the vegetation that was thin and clinging below is here riotous. Green mats the shallows. Weed-meadows sway in the tidal slack. Things grow because the sun feeds them, and the fauna here is the inverse of the deep town's economy — not patience and ambush in the lightless dens, but speed and color and noise in the glare. Bright fish flash and bicker. Birds work the tide-line. The town's children dive for the shellfish that crust the old harbor stones, and surface, gasping, into laughter.

It is loud here. That is the thing you notice, coming up from the full silence below — the *racket* of a living town in the light. Gulls. Hammering. A man calling prices. The bells, when the wind moves them, ringing thin and accidental. Down below, sound traveled far and clean through the held silence of many sleepers. Here it is cheap and overlapping and means nothing in particular, which is its own kind of luxury.

---

But attend to the warmth, because the warmth is the kinship, and the kinship is the whole of it.

The bay of Ten Bells is warm past what its shallowness and its sun should make it. The old people knew this. The harbor never freezes. The shellfish grow fat and strange. In the coldest part of the year, when the open sea outside the headlands goes grey and bitter, the bay holds its heat like a banked fire, and the fishermen bless it, and the children dive in water that ought by every reckoning to be too cold to bear.

They call it the gift of the bay. They have a saying — *the bay loves us* — said the way you'd say a thing too old to question. They do not know why the bay loves them. They have theories, all wrong, all reasonable: a warm spring in the deep, a kindness of the current, the simple luck of the headlands' shape.

It is the same warmth.

The bay of Ten Bells lies over the shoulder of the same sleeper. Not the back — the deep drowned town sits on the back — but the long slope of it that rises toward shore, the shoulder that comes up almost into the light. The heat that pulses through the foundations of the dead town below is the same heat that banks the winter bay above. One body, breathing slow, warm enough at its back to keep a drowned town in patient life and warm enough at its shoulder to keep a living town in fish and blessing. The two towns are the two ends of one warmth. They were never separate places. They are the two shores of a single breathing thing, and they do not know it, and they have never known it, because the distance between a back and a shoulder, measured across a sleeper that size, is a distance no swimmer crosses and lives, a darkness no boat sounds the bottom of.

---

Now the bell. The tenth bell, the missing one.

There is a story in Ten Bells, told to children and not believed by adults, which is the proper home of true things. It says that once, very long ago, the bay surged. Not a wave — the sea did not come *in*. The whole bay *lifted*, rose and held and sank again, slow, over the better part of a day, as if something beneath had turned in its bed. The boats grounded and refloated. The shellfish beds were rearranged. And the tower, which then held ten, gave up its tenth and deepest bell — the great low one, the one whose note you felt in the chest more than heard — which slid from its rotted yoke and fell, not into the harbor, but down, off the harbor's edge, into the deepening water beyond the headlands, ringing once as it went, a single deep periodic groan swallowed by the dark.

You have heard that bell.

Down in the drowned town, between the clicking that might be feeding and might be speech, beneath the soft collapse of undermined walls — *the deep periodic groan of the seabed settling, the warmth shifting in the rock below.* You attributed it, reasonably, to geology. To the town settling on its bones.

It is the tenth bell of Ten Bells, fallen from the shoulder, come to rest somewhere on the long slope of the sleeper's back, and it rings — not by any hand, not by any tide that small things make — it rings when the warmth swells. When the great slow breath crests and the whole drowned city goes still and the lights dim together and the sleeper turns a few fathoms toward waking, the swell that lifted the living bay once in a children's story moves through the deep town as a pressure, and somewhere in that pressure the old bronze bell, three centuries drowned, shifts on its bed and sounds its low note one more time into the held silence.

The same breath that banks the winter bay rings the lost bell. The crest that the deep town reads as *the city remembering* is the trough the living town never feels at all, because up in the light, in the racket and the glare, a thing that breathes once in an age is far too slow to notice — is simply the bay, loving them, as it always has.

---

So here is the shape of it, the two places in one breath:

A living town on a warm bright shoulder, blessing a gift it cannot name, telling its children a story it does not believe, while below the headlands the same body rolls in its sleep and the bell they lost rings on in a dark town they never knew was theirs — a town built by their own dead in the same stone, drowned in the same risen sea, kept warm by the same slow heart, and inhabited now by patient lightless things that mistake the back of the sleeper for the ground.

The people of Ten Bells stand on the shoulder. The animals of the drowned town stand on the back. Between them, going down past where either can imagine, the warmth keeps on, and the rule holds at every depth and every shore:

*The warmth means something is alive.*

They are all standing on it.

They have always been standing on it.

And it is, very slowly, dreaming them.
