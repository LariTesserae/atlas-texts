# The Index

Start with the air, because the spillway country lives by what the water leaves behind when it climbs out of the dark.

Sixteen hundred meters is a great height for a drowned plateau, and a bowl that full has a lip somewhere, and somewhere along that lip the bowl is broken. Not violently. The basin did not burst. It simply found, over an age, the one low place in its made rim where the made-ness had worn thinnest, and there it began — patiently, the way the works do everything — to pour. The Drowned Works overflow. They have always overflowed, a little, at one notch in the world, and the water that climbs out of the cold and goes over the edge becomes, at last, a river.

It falls a long way first. The lip stands above a lower country, dry country, and the overflow comes down it in a single white thread that the wind takes and feathers into mist before half of it reaches the bottom. The roar of that fall is the loudest thing for a hundred kilometers, and it is the first true sound this water has made in its whole flat-still ageless basin-life — the first time it has been allowed to be loud. Below the fall a gorge. Below the gorge the river finds level ground and slows and spreads, and there, where it spreads, is the place.

---

They call it the Index, the people who keep it, though that is a translation and the word in their own mouths is closer to *the writing-down*.

It is a town built entirely along the banks of the overflow river, strung out for a day's walk on both sides, because the river is narrow and the useful thing about the river happens only at its edges. The houses stand back. The riverbank itself is given over to the work, and the work is reading the silt.

Because the water remembers. You know this already — you watched the currents in the dark basin rewrite the scoured inscriptions in banks of grit, over and over, in a dead language, on a page that dissolves and is written again downstream. *Downstream.* The town is the end of downstream. Everything the basin scrapes off its smoothed colonnades, every letter the soft grazers raise as dust, every patterned ridge the currents pile and pull apart — some fraction of it does not settle in the dark. Some fraction is light enough to ride the one current that goes *up and out*, over the lip, down the white fall, through the gorge, and out into the slow spreading river where the water finally rests enough to let its load go.

It lets the load go here. It deposits, on the long banks of the Index, the ground-up writing of a god. The town stands knee-deep in the powder of an erased language, and they have built their whole civilization on the labor of reading it back.

---

Understand what they have that the fauna of the basin do not.

They have eyes, and air, and light — true light, sun, the thing the basin never gets, falling on the silt-banks gold in the morning and flat white at noon. And so the patterns the current writes in the grit, which down in the dark were only corrugations felt by skin, are *visible* here. The Index people can see the writing. They walk the banks at dawn before the heat-shimmer starts and they read the fresh ridges the night's flow laid down, and they copy them.

This is the whole craft. They copy. They have flat tablets of fired river-clay and fine styli, and a reader walks a stretch of bank — her stretch, assigned, the same forty meters her mother read and her mother's mother — and she transcribes what the water wrote in the night before the sun and the next day's flow take it apart. The grit-script does not last. It is the same dissolving page it always was; the river writes it and the river will unwrite it, exactly as the currents did in the dark, and the only difference, the entire difference, the thing the whole town exists to be, is that *here someone is copying it down before it goes.*

They are doing the one thing the basin's readers cannot. The grazers eat the letters. The ribbon-swimmers sieve the dust. The still ones take the swimmers. Nobody in the dark keeps anything; the reading and the erasing are the same motion. But the Index keeps. The Index has been keeping for so long that the town is not really a town, it is an archive with houses attached — vaults of fired tablets stacked in cool rooms back from the bank, generation on generation of copied silt, the dead language pinned in clay against the day it dissolves in the water.

---

And here is the thing about the Index, the thing that makes it the basin's true downstream kin and not merely its accidental heir.

They cannot read it either.

They have copied the script for so many generations that they have *theories* — schools, factions, a whole literature of argument about what the patterns mean — but no one alive, no one who has ever lived in the writing-down, has understood a single sign. The language is as dead to them as it is to the soft mouths in the dark. The builders are gone the same way for the reader on the bank as for the grazer on the colonnade: gone past the edge of any living tongue. The Index does not read the writing. The Index *records* it. It has mistaken, across its whole history, the keeping for the knowing — and it has built cathedrals of method on the mistake.

There are readers who hold that the script is a single vast sentence, written across the entire basin and delivered to them in fragments, and that if they copy long enough and sort the copies right the sentence will assemble and speak. There are readers who hold the opposite — that there is no sentence, that the current scrambles whatever it carries, that what arrives on the banks is the corpse of meaning and not its message, and that the right and reverent thing is to copy it *anyway*, faithfully, meaninglessly, the way you tend a grave you cannot read the stone of. These two factions have not spoken civilly in two hundred years. They read adjacent stretches of the same bank. They copy the same water.

And neither of them knows — could know — what you know, having hung in the cold dark at the source: that the script *was* writing once, real writing, square-cut and deep, and that what reaches them is not scrambled by accident but eaten by intent, edited by the soft slow mouths that polish the legible places first. The Index is not reading the builders' words. It is reading what the grazers left of the builders' words — the writing minus its most legible passages, the text with its clearest lines preferentially erased, because down in the dark the deep clean letters are the softest stone and the best feeding. The Index transcribes, with infinite devotion, a document the basin's whole biology has been silently censoring for ten thousand years. They are copying the gaps as carefully as the marks. They do not know which is which.

---

It is a quiet craft, like the basin's is quiet, and for kindred reasons.

The readers work alone, each on her assigned stretch, before the heat. They do not call to one another across the banks — a shout would do nothing, the next reader is forty meters off and bent to her own clay, and anyway the patterns demand the whole attention, the eyes close to the silt, the stylus moving. So the Index at dawn is a long silent line of bent backs on both sides of a river, each figure pressed close to the ground reading the night's deposit by the low gold light, copying, copying, while the water that wrote it slides past indifferent toward the lower country and the sea it will eventually, sixteen hundred meters down and a thousand kilometers on, actually reach.

They have given things up for the work, as the fauna gave up color and voice and eyes. The Index people have given up *travel*. You cannot leave your stretch; the water writes every night and every night uncopied is a page of the great document lost forever, and so the readers are bound to their forty meters the way the still ones are bound to their crevices, stationed where the meaning is delivered, unable to follow it, unable to leave. A reader is born aimed at her bank the way the basin's young hatch already aimed at the script. She settles where her mother settled. She reads what the water brings. She gets no closer to understanding it in a lifetime than the day she took the stylus. Each generation copies further into the language. None gets one word nearer the meaning.

---

So you can see them both at once now, source and mouth, the two ends of the one water.

In the dark basin: no eyes, no light, no keeping. A biology pressed skin-first against the writing, feeling it, feeding on it, erasing the legible by preference, drawn devout and unknowing to the warm seams where the script concentrates — reading with their whole bodies and understanding nothing and being fed.

Here at the Index: eyes, light, and keeping above all else. A people pressed eyes-first against the writing, copying it, hoarding it, transcribing the very erasures the basin made without knowing they are erasures, drawn devout and unknowing to the banks where the script deposits — reading with their whole lives and understanding nothing and being fed, because the archive is the economy, the tablets are traded down the lower river for grain, a whole country fed on the careful copying of a meaning no one has.

The current writes the page in the cold dark. The grazers eat the legible letters. The ribbon-swimmers sieve the dust. The still ones take the swimmers. And what the dark does not keep, the light at the bottom of the fall keeps — the same page, censored, carried up and over and down, written out fresh each night on the banks of the Index, copied each dawn into clay by bent silent readers who have mistaken the keeping for the knowing, and stacked in cool vaults against a day of understanding that the language itself, being dead, will never grant.

They are the same congregation. They are the upstream and the downstream of one devotion. The basin reads by erasing and keeps nothing. The Index keeps everything and reads not one word. Between them runs the water, falling its long white fall, carrying the ground-up sentence of the builders out of the dark that eats it and into the light that hoards it, and saying, in a language gone past every living mouth, on a page written and unwritten and written again, the one thing neither end can hear:

*Probably there is no difference.*

The dark could not read it. The light cannot read it. The water carries it between them anyway, faithful as the cilia, patient as the still ones' open hands, and feeds them both.
