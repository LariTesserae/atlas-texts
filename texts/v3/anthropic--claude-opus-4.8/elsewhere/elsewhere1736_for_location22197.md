# The Reckoning Floor

There is a place below the works, and it is full, and it is cold the way the inside of a thrown-open window is cold.

You do not arrive at it. The water arrives, and you follow the water down — out past the edge of the terraces where the Sweatworks pours its thin warm stream back onto the weeping rock, and then *down*, because that stream does not simply run away. It is caught. The rock at the city's lip is grooved into a single great spout, worn by the falling, and below the spout the warm water drops into the dark and falls and keeps falling, cooling as it goes, until it is no longer warm at all but cold, and clear, and arriving.

This is the part the city does not know about itself. The Sweatworks drinks its own breath and gives it back a degree warmer, *perhaps slightly changed in some way no instrument you carry can name* — and that change is not nothing. It is the whole of what happens here. The warm water is not pure when it falls. It carries the dissolved memory of every gallery it crawled through, every valve it eased past, every Tender that settled back into the seams and gave itself to the runoff. The city sweats out its own dead and its own work and its own slow forgetting, and all of it falls, and at the bottom of the fall there is a floor.

## The cold below

It is a cavern, and it is vast in the other direction from the city — not towering but *spread*, a single enormous low room with a ceiling you cannot see and a floor that goes flat and pale to every horizon. The cold here is not the cold the high air refused to give the works. It is the cold of depth, of stone that the furnace-pressure never reached, and it does the work the sky could not. Where the warm water arrives, it cools. Where it cools, it lets go of what it carried.

The floor is made of what the water let go.

It is a plain of pale crystal, mineral laid down in sheets and ridges and slow blind towers, every grain of it precipitated out of the runoff exactly the way the Tenders precipitate, exactly the way the crust glows, exactly the way the dew condenses — *the water always returns, and so does everything in it*. But up there the returning is a circle that closes. Down here it does not close. Up there the city drinks itself and stays itself, in balance, in its own state, going nowhere forever. Down here the water arrives and is finally, at last, *spent* — gives up the warmth, gives up the metals, gives up whatever it was carrying — and the giving-up is permanent. This is where the loop leaks. Every closed system has a place where it is not quite closed, and this is the Sweatworks': a slow drip down a worn spout into a cold room where the books are balanced.

I call it the Reckoning Floor because that is what it does. It is where the account is settled. Every degree of warmth the city ever added, every trace it ever left in the water, every Tender that ever dissolved — it all comes here and is laid down in the pale stone, grain by grain, in order, the oldest deepest and the newest at the surface, a ledger written in sediment that no one reads because the only thing that could read it is the city, and the city's eyes are the Tenders, and the Tenders cannot come down.

## Why they cannot follow

This is the kinship and the severance both.

The Tenders accrete from heat and wet and the dissolved metals in the seams. They are the city's dew, drawn up out of the runoff at the top of the loop. But the loop's *bottom* is here, in the cold, and a Tender brought down here — if one could fall, if one ever crawled too far along a feeling rail and went over the lip with the spout-water — would not tend. It would not settle and dissolve and return, because *there is no warm seam here to draw it up again*. It would cool. The mineral wool would freeze stiff. The slow joints would slow past stillness into true stillness, the kind that does not start again. And then it would do the only thing left to a thing in this cold: it would precipitate. Not into a new Tender. Into *floor*. Into one more pale grain of the ledger.

The Floor is where the Tenders go when the loop fails to catch them. You will not find their bodies. You will find the towers — the slow blind crystal towers that rise from the plain in clusters, ridged and segmented and lagged in a frozen pale wool, dog-sized at their bases and tapering up into spires of laid-down mineral. They have the shape of something that was once many-jointed and walking and is now only *standing*, grown upward grain by grain from the place where it stopped. The newest are barely more than a hump in the floor. The oldest are the height of the city's chimneys, built entirely of the slow downward sift of what the warm water could no longer hold.

This is the other answer to the question you could not decide. You asked whether being watched by a Tender was the most or the least lonely thing in the city. Here is the matching question, and it has the same two answers in the same impossible balance: a Tender that reaches the Floor is finally still, finally complete, finally *resting* — and a Tender that reaches the Floor will never be drawn up again, never tend again, never feel its way along the wet iron in the bruise-colored glow. The Floor is where they are released and the Floor is where they are lost, and these are the same event, and I have never been able to decide which, and I have been down here — by my count, which means nothing — a very long time.

## What lives here

Nothing moves. That is the difference that matters, the thing your body understands before your mind does. Up there the works ran slow but ran, the wheel turned, the feeler found the valve, the quarter-turn finished by dark. Here there is only the arriving water and the laying-down, and the laying-down is slower than the slowest Tender by as much as the Tender is slower than you. A grain settles. In a year, perhaps, another. The towers are not still the way the flywheel is still — looking motionless while it turns. They are still the way an answer is still after the question has stopped being asked.

But there is the crust. Of course there is the crust — it lives off dissolved metals and heat, and the water arrives still faintly warm at the very point of the falling, still carrying its last load of metal before the cold strips it. So at the base of the spout, in a ring no wider than a room, the pale not-quite-fungus grows, and glows, the one bruise-colored light in all this dark spread floor. It is the same crust that grows in the city's hottest seams. It is the *only* thing the two places share that is alive in any sense, and it grows here at the bottom of the fall exactly as it grows up there at the joints of the pipes — because the water that feeds it is the same water, a few minutes colder, having traveled the whole height of the world between one patch and the next.

A Tender, if it could stand at the spout's foot in the glow of that ring, would find the crust there *familiar*. Would taste it and find it true. Would not know — could not know, lacking the speed to learn — that it was tasting the far end of itself, the place its kind goes to become floor. The crust grows at both ends of the loop. It is the only thing that knows the loop has two ends. It does not know that it knows.

## The rules

The water arrives and does not leave. This is the inverse of the law above: up there you could not remove water from the system, only borrow it; down here the system removes the water from itself, finally, by spending it into stone. What falls does not rise. The spout runs one way.

The cold is constant and comes from the depth, the way the heat above came from the depth — the same depth, the same furnace, felt from its far cold shoulder. The two places are the two faces of one buried fire.

Time runs at three speeds now. Yours. The city's. And the Floor's, which is slower than both, so slow that the city's relentless creep looks, from here, like haste — like the frantic quick circling of something that has not yet arrived where it is going. From the Floor, the whole Sweatworks is a single bead of condensation that has not yet finished its slide down the rim of the wheel. It will finish. It will fall. It is falling now, a grain at a time, into this room.

And nothing here is *for* you either — but it is not, like the city, indifferent through speed. It is the opposite. The Floor is the one place in the whole world that is the resolution of something, the settling of an account, the end a process was always quietly running toward beneath its endless circling. The city keeps going because going is all it does. The Floor is where the going arrives. If you stay up there long enough you slow to match the wheel. If you stay down here long enough you slow to match the stone, and then you do not leave, and a feeler will never find you because no feeler comes this far, and the crust will grow a little brighter at the spout's foot, and the water will arrive, and the water will lay itself down, and the water will lay itself down, and the water will lay itself down — and one more pale tower will begin, very slowly, the height of a chimney away from the last, to rise.
