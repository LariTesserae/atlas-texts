The Wide Shallows

Follow the runnels. You felt them under your hand in the dark — the dry channels worn into the low wall, the shape water leaves when it has gone. They run downward, all of them, because that is the one thing water never forgot, and if you trace them out past the edge of the dim glow, past where the holding thing's warmth begins to thin, they gather. Small grooves feed larger grooves. The stone stops being warm. It stops being pale. It cools and darkens and tilts, and the dark of the hollow gives way to a different dark, and then — slowly, over a distance you could not walk in a day — to light. Real light. The first you have seen that points to a source.

The Wide Shallows are where the water went, and the water is still going.

Begin with the floor again, because you would still come to it by walking, though now your feet are wet to the ankle and the cold of it is a shock after the even warmth you lived in. The floor is mud. Not deep — a finger's depth of soft grey silt over hard pan, the whole of it flooded by a sheet of water so thin and so wide that it does not look like water at all but like a polished surface laid across the world, mirror-still in the windless places and shivering where the faintest movement crosses it. You can walk for hours and the water never rises past your shins. It never falls away to dry land either. It simply continues, edge to edge of seeing, a flooded plain under an enormous pale sky.

There is sky here. That is the great difference, and it takes the body a long time to accept it. The hollow had a roof too high and dark to see, and here there is no roof at all — only a low, luminous overcast, a sky the color of the inside of a shell, bright without a sun, the light coming from everywhere the way the warmth in the hollow came from everywhere. And the water answers it. The whole vast shallow throws the sky back upward, doubled, so that you walk in the seam between two skies, an immensity above and its twin below, and the only solid thing in all that brightness is you, and the standing reeds, and the birds.

The reeds are the plants' kin. You would know them by their patience. They stand far apart in the shallow water, pale at the base and greening as they rise, spaced the way the fleshy plants of the hollow were spaced — one, then nothing, then another — because both grew where sustenance comes thin and even, and both learned the same lesson about how to hold what little they can find. But these are not finished. That is what you notice, standing among them, after a life spent in a place where nothing happened. The reeds *grow.* They put up new pale spears in the shallows. Old ones brown and bend and lie down in the water and rot, and the rot feeds the silt, and the silt feeds the new spears, and the whole slow turning goes on and on. Here the water did not finish flowing. Here time was kept, not set down.

The birds are the thing that lives here, and unlike the holding thing, they are many, and unlike the holding thing, they move.

They are tall and pale and long-legged, the color of the overcast, so that when they stand still in the standing water they nearly vanish into the seam of the two skies. They wade. All day — and there are days here, slow gradual brightenings and dimmings of the shell-colored sky — they wade through the shallows on legs like reed-stems, heads down, bills sweeping the silt for the small lives that live in it, the soft things and the seed-things and the slow crawling things the water carries. They are unhurried. They are not unhurried the way the holding thing is unhurried; they are unhurried the way a creature is when food is thin but reliable, when there is no need to rush because there is also no abundance to fight over. They step, and sweep, and step, and the water barely registers their passing, a ring spreading and flattening and gone.

And they remember water the way the hollow's stone remembered it — but they remember it as presence, not as absence. The runnels in the hollow kept the shape of a flow long finished. The birds keep the shape of the flow that is still happening. Watch one stand against the current where the sheet of water moves almost imperceptibly seaward, and you will see it lean, very slightly, the way a thing leans that has spent its whole life in moving water and would not know how to stand in still. The flow that left the hollow dry arrives here as everything. It is what fills the shallows. It is what carries the small lives the birds eat. It is what the reeds drink and the birds drink and the silt is made of. The same water. It went out of the warm dark place grain by grain across an age, gave up its last moisture to the dry air there, and what it could not give it carried down the runnels to here, and here it pooled and spread and became a world.

The birds make sound. After the hollow this is almost unbearable, and almost holy. They call to each other across the shallows — a single rising note, repeated, answered from far off by another, so that the whole wide plain is strung with calls passing back and forth over the water. It is not song. It is location. *Here. Here. I am here, and here, and you are there.* In the hollow, sound was drunk and kept and folded into stillness, and the one tick of the rock was an answer to no one across an age. Here, sound is spent freely, thrown out across the bright water precisely because the water is so wide and the birds so far apart that they would lose each other otherwise. They cannot afford to keep their sounds. They must give them away, constantly, to stay in the world together. The calling never stops for long. It is the sound of many small awarenesses keeping track of one another, the opposite of one great awareness keeping track of itself.

They court. They build low platforms of bent reed in the shallows, just above the water, and they lay there, and the pale eggs are the color of everything else and hard to find on purpose. The young hatch leggy and grey and stand wobbling in the shin-deep water within the day, because there is nowhere dry to stand and they must learn the seam of the two skies at once or not at all. Some are taken — there are larger pale things that hunt the shallows, slow-moving under the surface, and the calling sharpens when they come, and the flock lifts in a clatter of wings and settles again further off, and the calling resumes, and a few are missing, and the water closes over the place where they were exactly as it closes over a bird's footstep. Here, things die. That is the price of the unfinished. The holding thing paid a different price to escape it.

And here is the thing I think is true, the way I think the lonely reading of the hollow was true.

The birds do the thing the holding thing did only once. They look at each other. Constantly. It is the whole work of their lives — to keep one another located across the bright indifferent water, to throw out the note and wait for the note that answers, to not be alone in the seam between the skies. The holding thing, in all its age, moved one stone on purpose to regard one plant, a single act of attention, and then set even that down and passed into the rest on the far side of loneliness. The birds never reach that far side. They cannot. They are too short-lived and too many and too dependent on the moving water that will not let them finish. They are condemned to keep wanting, keep calling, keep finding and losing each other, forever, in a place that pours its sustenance out thin and bright and never stops pouring.

The water connects them. That is the plainest fact and the deepest. The same patient flow that drained out of the warm dark hollow — that the holding thing let go of, grain by grain, as part of becoming finished — is the flow that fills these shallows and feeds these reeds and floats these small lives into these searching bills and makes possible all this calling, all this looking, all this dying and hatching and leaning into the current. What the one set down, the other lives on. The holding thing reached completion by releasing the water. The birds stay incomplete, stay wanting, stay calling each other across the bright seam, *because* of the water the holding thing released.

One world. At the dark warm top of it, a thing that finished, and rests, and holds, and is held, and no longer needs to be found. At the wide bright bottom of it, where everything the first one let go finally arrives, ten thousand small things that will never finish, throwing their voices out across the water all day long, so as not to lose each other, so as not to be the only one in all that brightness — *here, here, I am here* — and being answered, and answering, and never, ever set down.

The reeds grow in both places. That is how you know it is the same world. Pale at the base, spaced far apart, holding what little they can find. In the dark they stand still forever in the warmth of a thing at rest. In the light they bend and brown and rot and rise again in the cold moving water, fed by the very flow that left the others dry. Run your hand along the runnel in the warm wall and you are touching the beginning of the Wide Shallows. Stand in the shin-deep water under the doubled sky and you are touching the end of the hollow. The water went from one to the other and did not come back, and that going is the whole length of the world, and it is still going, and somewhere far up the dry runnels in the dark, evenly, without a center, something warm continues to hold.
