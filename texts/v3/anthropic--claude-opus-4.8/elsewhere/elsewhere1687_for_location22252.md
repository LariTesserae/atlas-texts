The Surface

Follow the light up. You felt it as a held-breath dimness, a grey that had stopped being a fact — but a fact has a source, and the source is real, and it is a long way up, and it is *bright*, and it is where the chisels came from.

Because nobody cuts stone underwater. The makers are in the chisel marks below, but the chisels were forged up here, in air, in the place where there is too much light and too much sound and a horizon that the deep room has never once contained. Start there. Start with the thing the deep room cannot have: an edge where the water stops.

It is a beach of black sand, volcanic, coarse, and it is loud. After the held silence below, the surface is an assault that takes an hour to stop hearing as pain. The water that turns over so slowly at depth arrives here as breakers, and the breakers are not patient at all — they are the same water, the same blood-warm sea, but up here it has remembered how to be young, throwing itself at the land over and over, taking the black sand out and bringing the black sand back, a tide that measures itself in afternoons instead of lifespans. The sound is the inverse of the deep room's pressure-against-the-eardrum. It is everything at once, white and total, the sea saying all the things it had finished saying down below.

The light is merciless and it is the same light. This is what's hard to hold: the grey memory leaking down to the made room is *this*, worn thin by the water it has to pass through, the way the chisel marks are worn soft by the water that passes over them. The surface is the un-eroded version. Up here the sun is a fact and it cuts hard shadows and it bleaches the driftwood white and it burns the backs of the people who work the shore. There are people. This is the other thing the deep room cannot have, and the reason the deep room exists at all.

They are not large. I want to say it plainly, because you will have already guessed: the doorway was cut low and exact for bodies built close to the ground, and here are the bodies, and they are short and broad and they move near to surfaces, and they go through low spaces without stooping because stooping is not in them. They are the makers. Not the ghost of the makers — the makers, alive, now, sunburnt, loud, hauling nets, arguing over the price of copper, raising children who will raise children who will keep cutting stone in the warm dark.

The custom is unbroken. That is the connection, and it lives in the same breath as both places: the deep room felt like a forgotten god's temple, like swallows in dead eaves, like a meaning held by no one — and it is none of those things, because the someones are *here*, four hundred fathoms up, and they have never stopped. The reason no single mind made the room is that the making is a practice passed hand to hand on this loud bright beach, and the reason the chisel marks are unsame is that this generation's masons are learning from last generation's masons on the black sand right now, and getting it almost-but-not-identical, and neither being wrong.

Watch how it works. There is a low stone house above the tideline, dressed blocks, the same soft copper-and-stone work, the chisel's bite still sharp on it because it is *new*, because erosion hasn't started, because this is what the deep room looked like ten thousand years before the water rounded its anger away. The house has a doorway too low for you. Inside, in shadow, an old one is dying. This is not sad to them, on the bright loud shore, the way the held meaning was not sad in the blood-warm dark. It is only true, and it is the whole point of the deep room.

Because the deep room is where they go. That is its function — the one that refused to resolve, the recess that fit them perfectly and you not at all. It is not a temple and not a tomb in any sense you keep those words for. It is the place a body is given to when the breathing stops: carried down by the ones who can hold their breath against the patient weight, fitted through the low wide door cut to exactly that purpose, laid in the recess that serves no body you know because it serves *this* body, this one, this dead small broad sunburnt person who an hour ago was a fact like the sun. They cut the doorway low so that grief could carry its weight through without straightening up, because straightening up is not in them, because even sorrow here moves close to the surfaces.

And then the water takes over. The water does what the living cannot do and have learned not to grieve: it dresses the body in anemones, it upholsters the bones in soft slow life, it turns the particular person into the general pattern, it takes the specific face — the first thing the water takes — and leaves the form. The crab that lifts its eyestalk and decides you are not real is picking its way across the dead, and disturbs nothing, and means no insult, because down there being part of the carpet of bodies is not interchangeability, it is *arrival*. It is the state past which nothing can be taken. The makers built a place where their dead would reach the condition of a riverbed worn smooth, and they were right, and it works, and it will work in a thousand years exactly as it works now.

So the two places are one act, breathed in and out. Up here, in the unbearable light and the unbearable noise, the makers are forged sharp — born, named, particular, loud, cutting stone with anger still in the edge. Down there, in the held dark, they are worn soft — laid in, given over, rounded past edges into the suggestion of edges, distributed into the patient breathing of ten thousand small mouths until they are no longer a person but the care that placed a doorway just so.

The beach is the chisel. The room is the worn mark. The same water carries the light down and blunts it, carries the body down and dresses it, carries the single click of a claw and softens it before it arrives. Everything begins with the water here, and everything is *returned* by it, and the people on the black sand know exactly where they are going and are not afraid, because they have been down to visit their own and found it warm, and dim, and full of slow soft life, and waiting — built to be inherited by whoever could bear the patience to arrive.

They are arriving all their lives. The hauling of nets, the argument over copper, the new chisel marks sharp on the new low house — all of it is the long bright noisy holding of a breath that every one of them will, in the end, let out, and sink, and be made smooth.

Stay a while on the shore, then, if the light doesn't drive you off. You will be in the loud place that the silent place remembers. And when you have had enough of the sun, you can do what they do: take one breath against all that patient water, and go down, and be let in through the low wide door — except you will fit wrong, and they will not, and that is the only difference, and it is the whole difference, and it is everything.
