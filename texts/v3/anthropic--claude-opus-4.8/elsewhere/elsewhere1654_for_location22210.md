# The Surfacelands at the Ebb of the Long Rain

You come up into it the way the salamanders never can — by following one of the ghost-roads past the basin where it dies, and finding that it does not die, that it only *seems* to, because it bends upward into a fault in the stone too narrow for a milk-glass body but not for yours, and you climb, and the climb takes days, and somewhere in the climbing the glasslight thins behind you and a different light begins.

The first thing is that this light *moves wrong.* You have come up under the law of the constant brightness, the flat coat-of-frost glow that never flickers, and now there is something that flickers, that *fails*, that comes and goes — and your whole body reads it as catastrophe before your mind learns the word for it. The word is *weather.* Up here the sky is a real sky, ragged and gray and low, and it leaks. It has been leaking for a very long time. The locals — and there are locals, that is the second astonishment — call this the Long Rain, and they do not mean a storm. They mean the age. They mean the only age there has ever been.

---

This is where the water comes from. You understand that at once, standing in the first real rain of your life, soaked through and shivering and laughing at the obviousness of it. *Down there* the water fell one drop at a time, *tok,* with whole silences between, the most precious and patient thing in the world — and here it falls in sheets, in curtains, in a profligate gray endlessness, drumming on stone and pooling in every hollow and running off in a thousand braided streams that all, all of them, slope downward, downward, toward the faults and the seeps and the long slow vertical journey into the dark. The condensation on the undercity's great lens is not weather. It is *this rain,* arrived half a kilometer down and three hundred thousand years late, wrung out of the stone one careful drop at a time.

The two places are one water. The surfacelands drink the sky and the undercity drinks the surfacelands' patience. What is a flood up here is a clock down there. What is squandered here is hoarded there. You stand in the wet and think of the salamanders triangulating against single notes, and you cannot stop laughing, and it is not entirely a happy sound.

---

Now look at the land, because it is the opposite of everything below in the one way that matters.

The undercity was *settled.* Three hundred thousand years of patience had worn it round and calm and slow, every corner softened, every change beneath notice. The surfacelands are *raw.* The rain does here what it cannot do below — it *cuts.* It has no patience at all. Every slope is gullied, every ridge is gnawed, the whole landscape is a frenzy of erosion happening at a speed you can see, hillsides slumping, channels deepening by the hour, the ground itself unable to hold a shape for longer than a season. Where the undercity changes invisibly fast on its skin and not at all in its bones, the surfacelands change *visibly,* grossly, all the way through. There is no settledness here. There has never been time for it. The rain undoes the land as fast as the land can rise.

And things *grow.* Green things — and you have no word for green, having come from a world that never learned the sun, and the green hurts your eyes the way the first flicker hurt them. The slopes are furred with low tough vegetation, drinking the squander, photosynthesizing under the gray failing light, dying back and surging again with each turn of the wet seasons. Life here is not the pale eyeless hush of below. It is loud. The rain is loud and the wind is loud and the things that live in the green are loud, calling and crashing and breaking the silence constantly, gleefully, because up here there is no silence to keep load-bearing. The water announces itself everywhere. Nothing has to listen for it. Nothing has ever had to listen for anything.

---

The locals.

This is the hardest part to tell, because you came up expecting to find the *builders* — the ones who made the undercity, the dead who chiseled the ziggurats with stone tools and laid the mortarless brick, gone now past even being missed. And in a sense you do find them. The locals are their descendants. But they do not know it. They have no memory of the undercity at all.

They live in the rain in shelters of woven living vegetation and stacked rough stone, and they are *quick* — quick like the surface is quick, generations turning fast, building and abandoning, moving up the eroding slopes as the gullies eat their homes out from under them, never settling because settling is not possible here, the land won't hold still long enough. They are not patient. They have never needed patience. The water comes whether they wait for it or not; the problem of their lives is not scarcity but *too much,* the flood, the slump, the endless having-to-move. Where the undercity's whole civilization is a single held breath waiting for the next drop, the surfacelanders' whole life is the opposite labor: the work of *getting out of the water's way.*

And they tell stories. This is the thing. They tell stories about *down* — about a still place, a quiet place, a place where the rain is gentle and the light never fails and nothing ever has to move because nothing ever changes. They think it is a story about death, or about a god, or about the inside of the earth where good people go when the rain finally lets them rest. They have softened it the way the undercity softens stone. They do not know that their ancestors built it. They do not know it is real. They do not know that the water they curse and flee and bail out of their shelters every single season is, far below, the most sacred and awaited thing in another world — that their squander is someone else's clock, that the drops they shed without counting are counted, one at a time, by creatures listening with everything they have.

They are the rememberers who have forgotten. The undercity is memory without a rememberer; the surfacelands are the rememberers without the memory. Each holds exactly the half the other lost.

---

Here is the connection, and you have to hold both ends of it at once or it means nothing.

The undercity is dying by *smoothing* — by the slow approach of a final flatness, a mirror-plain under standing water, the inheritances arriving all together at a world their ancient instructions no longer describe. It dies of too much patience. It dies because nothing up here ever stops feeding it water and nothing down there can keep up with what the water slowly does. The Long Rain is killing the undercity by kindness, one drop at a time, wearing it toward the mirror.

And the surfacelands are dying by *cutting* — by the rain's refusal to ever let anything settle, by erosion that will, eventually, wear these raw slopes down too, will gully them flat, will exhaust the high stone that the rain falls on and runs off of, until there is nothing left raised enough to cut. The same flatness comes for both worlds. From below it arrives as a mirror. From above it arrives as a plain. They are the same surface, approached from opposite sides, and when they meet — when the surfacelands wear down to the level of the cavern's worn-through lens, when the cutting from above and the smoothing from below finally touch — the two waters will be one water with nothing between them, no patient stone to slow the drops, no silence to hold, no slope to follow down.

The rain will simply fall into the mirror. There will be no *tok.* There will be no interval. There will be no clock, because there will be no difference left anywhere between the squander and the hoard, the loud and the hushed, the rememberer and the remembered.

---

So who lives here? Reckon it honestly, the way you reckoned them below.

The surfacelanders live here — quick, loud, soaked, forever moving, building nothing that lasts because nothing is allowed to last, carrying in their stories a still dark gentle place they take for a dream of rest. They are alive in the full ordinary sense the undercity creatures never quite manage: they live long enough to know they are living, to fear the flood, to grieve the homes the gullies take, to tell their children the old softened story of *down.* They have memory and no faithfulness. The undercity has faithfulness and no memory. Between the two of them, across half a kilometer of patient stone, a whole civilization's worth of meaning is filed in two drawers that will never be opened in the same room.

And they keep the noise — not out of joy, though it sounds like joy. They keep the noise because the noise is the last thing they can still afford. Down below, the silence is load-bearing because the water is scarce and every note must be heard. Up here the water is everything and constant and squandered, and so silence has no work to do, and the surfacelanders fill the rain with calling and crashing and song the way you spend money you are sure you will always have. They do not know the water is winding down. They do not know that far below, their wastage is somebody's whole devotion. They sing into the Long Rain as if it would always fall —

and it does. For now, it still falls.

Stand in the wet on the eroding slope. Let it run off you and down, down, toward the fault, toward the seep, toward the dark where it will become precious. Listen to how the rain does not stop, the way the things below are listening to how long the silence holds, each world straining to hear the one sound that means *not yet* — the falling that hasn't ended, the silence that hasn't broken — and neither of them, not the rememberers nor the remembered, ever once suspecting that they are listening to the very same thing from opposite sides of the stone:

the water, still moving.

The water, not yet still.
