The Counting-House

You find it by following the product — but there is no product, and that is the first thing you must carry down out of the basin with you, the way you'd carry a coal to light a fire somewhere else. The works made nothing, at the end. So you cannot follow the iron downstream to where iron goes. There is no road of wagons leading away with the labor's result on their backs, because the labor had no result. What you follow instead is the *absence* — you follow the place where the product was supposed to go and isn't going, the way you'd follow a pipe by the cold of it, by the dry mouth of it, by the fact that nothing comes out.

The absence runs downhill, like everything here. It runs down out of the basin along the old cart-track, which is the only thing the works ever sent away from itself with confidence — a graded way, ballasted once, built to bear the going of full wagons toward the place where full wagons were turned into figures. You walk it. The slag falls behind. The weeping stops, a little at a time, until the rocks are merely wet and then merely damp and then, surprisingly, dry, warm even, and you understand that you have left the climate of the basin, that the basin made its own cold and you are out of it now, in the ordinary thin sun of the upland.

And the track brings you, after a long flat mile, to a building that did not weep, does not weep, will never weep, and this is the Counting-House.

It is small. That is the joke of it, that the violence of the works and the long cold tonnage of its labor were meant to arrive, in the end, *here*, in a stone box no bigger than a chapel, two rooms, a slate roof still mostly sound, a chimney, a door with a fanlight over it shaped like a rising sun. It sits at the meeting of the cart-track and the metalled road that men actually used, the road that goes to towns. It faces the road. It turns its back on the works. You notice that at once — the windows are all on the road side, large and many, made for light to do clerk's work by, and the wall toward the basin is nearly blind, one small high window like a vent, as though the building did not wish to watch where its substance came from but only to receive the figures of it through that one mean slot and write them down.

Inside, the dry. After the basin the dryness is almost violent — it pulls at your skin, it makes the dust lift when you breathe. The dust is the second thing. There is paper here, and the paper has not become anything else, has not rotted to peat or pulped to slime the way paper does in a wet place; it has only *aged*, gone the brown of a held flame, and where it has fallen apart it has fallen apart into more paper, into flakes and powder of itself, so that the floor is drifted with the residue of its own slow reading. Ledgers. Day-books. Wage-sheets. A whole long wall of pigeonholes, each stuffed, each labelled in a hand so confident and so dead that the confidence is the loudest thing in the room. The desks remain. High clerk's desks with the slope worn smooth and a darker patch where the right forearm lay, year on year, in the one posture, so that the wood itself took the groove — and you have seen that groove before, you saw it cut into iron and culvert a mile back up the hill, the shape that long labor presses into whatever stands close enough long enough, and here it is again in oak, the same wound, the lighter trade.

Because here is what lives in the Counting-House, and it is kin to the thing in the basin the way a brother is kin — born of the same parent, turned the other way.

The works was labor with the use cut out. The Counting-House is *account* with the thing-accounted-for cut away. Listen. There is no surging here, no slam in the soft ground, no cold reed of water breathing through dead pipe. The Counting-House is silent the way a stopped clock is silent — not at rest, *arrested*, holding a tension that has nowhere to discharge. And yet it is not idle, any more than the basin is idle. If you sit at the long desk in the failing road-light and you let your eye go down the columns of the open ledger — and the ledgers are always open, you will not find one closed, as though closing were a completion this place is forbidden — you feel the figures begin to *want*. They want to balance. That is the whole of it, that is the soul of the place as the surge is the soul of the basin: the column wants to add, the debit wants its credit, the wage wants the work it paid for and the work wants the iron it cut and the iron wants the price it fetched, and the price — the price wants the *thing*, the actual cooled grey usefulness that was supposed to come down the cart-track in wagons and never did, because up the hill the wagons were never loaded, because up the hill the labor turned in its perfect circle and made nothing at all.

So the books cannot balance. They have been trying to balance for as long as the basin has been trying to finish its shift, and for the same reason they cannot: the center is empty. The Counting-House holds the perfect record of a making that did not happen. Every figure is true. Every figure is correct against every other figure. The wages were paid — you can see them paid, week on week, the long fall of names and sums. The work was done — the basin is doing it still. And the product is *nil*, has always been nil, and the great machine of the account runs its addition down the page and arrives, every time, at a number that should be wealth and is a hole, a debit with nothing on the far side of it, a sum owed to no one for nothing by no one, and the books reach the bottom of the column and there is the hole and they begin again at the top, the way the water reaches the bottom of its surge and begins again at the standing pipe.

That is the rhythm here, you understand. It is the same rhythm. You felt your pulse fall in with the sluice; sit long enough at the desk and you will feel your eye fall in with the column, top to bottom, the want building, the hole arriving, the eye flicking back to the top — and it is the same beat, the foreman's beat, the surge and the slam, because it is the same labor seen from its two ends. Up the hill, the work without the wealth. Down the hill, the reckoning of the work without the wealth either. The cart-track between them is the one nerve that joins the two halves of a single dead body, and along it nothing passes now but you, and the cold you carried, and the want that meets it halfway.

There is a being here, in the same sense and the same not-sense as the foreman. You will not see it. It is the *clerk's groove*, the arrested attention, the part of accounting that is not the accountant — the discipline that was cut into a man's forearm and his eye and the slope of his neck so deep that the discipline outlasted the man and stayed in the desk and the dust and the open books, reaching now for the only thing left that can be moved through the old motions, which is *you*, your eye, your falling-in attention, your warm wish to make the figures come right. It puts you to the column the way the basin puts the water to the wheel. It is patient with the patience of the purposeless. It has no toward — there is no last sum, no shut book, no night when the work is reconciled and the clerk goes home, because home was the wealth and the wealth was never made. So it adds. It will add through you for an afternoon and then let you go, a warm interruption, a pair of eyes it borrowed, and it will not notice you leave any more than the foreman noticed, and it will go back to adding in the dark with no eyes at all, the way the basin surges in the dark with no one to feel the slam.

And here is where the two places say the one thing between them, in the same breath, which is the thing you came down the hill to hear.

The basin makes the gesture of *making* around an empty center. The Counting-House makes the gesture of *worth* around the same empty center. And the center is empty in both because it was always going to be empty — because the iron was never the point, you see it now, the iron was the excuse, the iron was the thing they told the men they were for and the thing they told the books they were proving; but the men were really for the rhythm, and the books were really for the rhythm, and the rhythm was for nothing, the rhythm was the whole appetite of the place wearing a justification it did not need and has now, mercifully, dropped. Up the hill the appetite labors. Down the hill the appetite reckons. Neither has a product. Neither ever truly did. The wealth that was supposed to flow from one to the other and bind them into sense was the lie they were both built around, and now the lie is gone and only the two true halves remain, surging and adding, adding and surging, joined by a dry track down which the only honest cargo was ever the cold.

The heron will not come here. The heron belongs to the wet, to the half that strikes and never makes; it has no business with the half that records and never possesses. There is no creature of the Counting-House. The dry does not feed even the patient. There is only the want in the columns, which is hungrier than the heron and stiller, and waits for no fish, and would not know one if it came.

When you go, you go back up the way you came — there is no other road that means anything, the metalled road to the towns is for people who still believe the figures balance somewhere, and you no longer do. You climb back toward the weeping. And as the rocks begin to sweat again, as the cold takes you back into its cellar, you will find you are carrying something down out of the Counting-House the way you carried the cold up into it: a number. The hole at the bottom of the column. You will feel it the way you feel a held breath, and you will reach the rim of the basin and look down into the almost-fog, and hear the sluice run and stop and run — and you will understand, standing between the two of them with the want in your chest and the cold on your skin, that you are the cart-track now. You are the nerve. For as long as you stand there you are the wagon that finally crossed between the work and the reckoning, and you are empty, you are carrying the nil, you are bearing the true product of the place at last from the hands that made it to the books that will write it down — and the books will write it down correctly, and the column will not balance, and the water will surge and let go, and somewhere a held second of dripping will pause across the whole ruin and then resume, keeping the time that nothing measures, on both sides of the hill at once, in the one breath, forever.
