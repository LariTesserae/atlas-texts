# The Sink of Warm Forgetting

Begin with the heat, because the people of the Cold Ascent cannot. They have no organ for it. Begin with the heat the way they would have to learn it: as a thing that does not stop. Not the killing flash of fire — the steady, patient heat that has settled into the air and never lifts, even in the dark hours, even in the deep of what passes for night. There is no breath to see here. The breath goes out and is simply gone, swallowed into air already so thick with warmth and water that the body cannot tell where its own edge ends. The heat is the climate; everything here was built, if it was built at all, to argue with the *staying*.

The land sinks. This is the second fact, and it is the inverse of the first world's second fact, and the two are the same fact read from opposite ends. Far below the terraces, below the gradient of haze, below the place where rain decides what it will be — the great flank does not simply end. It pours down and down into a vast enclosed lowland, a basin with no outlet to any sea, ringed so high on every side by the climbing land that nothing it gathers ever leaves. Everything the upper world hoards, this world receives. Every thread of water the terrace-folk catch in their cisterns is water they took from the sky before it could fall this far; what they do not catch comes down at last, range by range, seam by seam, gathering as it falls, until it arrives in the Sink as the one thing the high people fear above all others.

Abundance.

## The Water That Was Not Saved

There is too much water here. This is almost impossible to explain to anyone from above, where water is a hoarded sacrament counted by the basin. Here it lies across the floor of the world in sheets and sloughs and slow brown channels that do not know their own banks from one season to the next. It stands. It does not run so much as *spread*, a hundred miles of it under a low hot sky, breathing up into the air all day and falling back as the warm grey rains of the afternoon, the same water rising and falling, rising and falling, going nowhere, leaving nothing — a water that has forgotten the discipline of the gutter and the cistern and remembers only its own weight.

And so this is a green place. Not the thin stubborn film of the terraces, every leaf earned. Here the green is a riot, a smothering, a thing that grows faster than it can be counted and dies faster than it can be cleared and rots into the warm standing water to feed the next green that is already climbing over it. Vast soft-stemmed reeds twice the height of a person. Broad-leaved things that unfurl in a single day and blacken in a week. Floating mats of weed thick enough to walk on if you are quick and unlucky enough to try. Trees that grow with their roots in the water and their crowns laced with vines and strangling figs and the long beards of grey moss, and beneath them a dimness, a green underwater light, in which everything is busy turning into everything else. The rule of the upper world is *accretion against cold*. The rule here is *rot against time* — and rot is faster than accretion, always, by far. Nothing endures. Everything returns.

## What Lives Here, and How

Life teems in the Sink, too — but it is the opposite teeming. Above, the abundance is *gathered*, herded, dovecoted, the patient work of ten thousand years of bringing creatures into a kept place. Here the abundance is *wild* and indifferent and enormous, and it does not need anyone, and it will close over the place where a person stood within a single turning of the season.

The insects are the rulers. In the warm wet dark they breed in numbers that have no meaning, clouds of them rising off the standing water at dusk so dense they move like smoke, like weather, whining in the ear, settling on every wet surface. The amphibians are giants — slow cold-blooded things gone vast in the heat, sitting half-submerged with only their eyes above the brown water, throats pulsing, throats roaring at night in a chorus so total it becomes a kind of silence, a sound the mind stops hearing because it never stops. There are fish that walk. There are serpents long as a terrace-wall is high, hanging from the rot-trees, patient in a way the high people would almost recognize as kin, except that the patience here is not the patience of enduring but the patience of waiting to feed. There are birds, but they are gaudy and loud and short-lived, screaming colors through the green dimness, and they do not wheel along clean cliff faces in the cold clean air — they crash and flap through the wet leaves and are eaten.

And there are people. Of course there are people. People go everywhere.

## The People of the Sink

They are tall.

This is the first thing, the way smallness is the first thing about the others, and it is the same pressure read backward. In the heat, the body wants to be long — wants the reach, the spread, the great surface to shed the warmth it cannot escape. So over the long ages, in this basin where the high folk's discipline would be a death sentence and their compactness a slow boiling, the bodies drew *outward*. Tall and lean and loose-jointed, narrow through the chest because the air is thick and gives up its breath without being fought for. Long arms, long splayed feet for the soft ground and the floating mats. Fast hearts, light and quick. Where the high people hoard their heat in a deep slow chest, these throw theirs away as fast as it comes, through skin gone dark and slick and always faintly wet.

They move quickly — and this, too, is wisdom worn into the body, the exact mirror of the others' slowness. In the Sink you cannot stand still. To stand still is to be found — by the insects that will cloud you, by the rot that climbs anything stationary, by the long patient things in the trees, by the water itself, which will rise around your ankles while you are not looking and take the ground you were standing on. So they move, lightly, constantly, never quite settling, a people who sleep in slung hammocks above the wet and wake at any change in the chorus of the night and are gone by morning from wherever they were, leaving nothing, because nothing they could leave would last a season anyway.

They gesture constantly — the hands always going, throwing words into the thick air which swallows sound and will not carry it the way the cold clean air above carries it. Where the high folk can speak across three terraces in a murmur, these people two arm-lengths apart must talk loud and bright and fast and fill the gaps with the body, because the green eats sound, the chorus drowns it, and a pause is not a courtesy here but a hole that something will come through. They do not let questions cool. There is no cooling. They answer fast and lightly and laugh easily and a great deal, a quick bright laughter that means *I am here, you are here, we are still here, keep moving*.

## What They Do Not Carry

Here is the deepest thing, and it is the inverse of the deepest thing above:

They do not hoard. They *cannot* — and over the long ages the not-being-able became a not-wanting, and the not-wanting became a faith as fierce in its own way as the high folk's faith in *enough*. Nothing keeps in the Sink. Grain rots. Meat turns by nightfall. Water is everywhere and therefore worth nothing. Build a wall and the green pulls it down and the wet undermines it and the season takes it; lay up a store and the rot is into it before the moon comes round. So they learned the only wisdom the place allows: take what the day gives, eat it the day it is given, and trust the next day to give again — because it always does, because the terrible generosity of the Sink never, ever stops.

They have no word for *hoard* that is not also the word for *foolishness*, for *the thing that draws the rot*, for *the man who sat too long with his pile and was found by the long patient thing in the tree*. To save is to stop, and to stop is to die. To carry more than you can carry lightly and quickly is to weigh yourself down into the reach of the water. And so they own almost nothing, and what they own they give away easily, gaily, the way the high folk share — but for the opposite reason. The high folk share because the warmth must be pooled to be kept. These folk give because nothing can be kept at all, and so the only wealth is the giving itself, the brightness of the open hand, the feast made the day the great amphibian is killed because by tomorrow it will be carrion and the whole camp will move on.

They do not build for grandchildren. They do not build for next season. The idea would strike them as a sickness — a grasping after a permanence that the Sink disproves a thousand times a day. They have no walls full of their dead. Their dead go into the warm water and are gone in a week, taken back into the green, and this does not horrify them; it comforts them, it is *right*, it is the same law they live by made final. The high folk are never alone because the held-still dead are packed in all around them, course on bone on course. The Sink-folk are never burdened because the dead let go entirely, dissolve, return, make no claim. *We came up out of the warm water and we go back into it,* they say, lightly, moving on, *and in between we do not hold on to anything, because holding on is how the cold people up the wall got themselves stuck forever in the same one place.*

## What They Know of Each Other

For they do know of each other — the two worlds, the high and the low.

Not well. Not warmly. The going between is hard and few attempt it, but it happens. There is a long traffic, ancient and thin, up and down the great flank: a few hardy traders, a few exiles, a few of the curious who could not be kept. And each world has made of the other a figure in its own theology, and each figure is a slander, and each slander is the truth seen from the wrong end of a life.

To the high folk, the Sink is the cautionary abyss — the place where everything they hoard goes to be wasted, the proof of what comes of not saving, a hot rotting hell of people who own nothing and keep nothing and let their own dead dissolve like refuse, who will not build, who cannot wait, who answer before the question has cooled, who throw away the day's catch in a single feast and trust to luck like fools, like children, like the marmot-things in the talus who store nothing and die in the first hard winter. *Down there,* a terrace-mother tells her child in the long warm dark of the lower tiers, leaning against the wall of her own grandmothers, *down there they have so much they have lost the sense to keep it. Down there is what the cold would make of us if we ever stopped.*

And to the Sink-folk, the Terraces are the cautionary tomb — the place where everything goes to be held and never released, a freezing necropolis of people so afraid of the night that they have spent ten thousand years building themselves a single stacked grave and calling it a city, who count their fuel and ration their breath and dam up their own dead in the walls because they cannot even let go of *that*, who climb the same stairs their whole lives and never go anywhere, who have hoarded so long and so hard that they have forgotten the one thing the Sink knows in its marrow: *it comes again.* The water comes again. The green comes again. The day gives again. *Up there,* a Sink-father tells his child, throwing the words bright into the thick air as they move along the floating mat at dawn, *up there they are so afraid of going without that they have spent their whole lives going without on purpose. Up there is what the heat would make of us if we ever got scared.*

## The One Who Went Down, and the One Who Came Up

There is a story both peoples tell, and it is the same story, and neither knows the other tells it.

In the high version, a daughter of the Cold Ascent — long-limbed, which was her shame, a body that lost its heat too fast and gasped in the thin air, a body the mountain had not made and could not keep — went down. Not exiled. She left. She could not climb the stairs at the patient pace; her blood ran too quick and too warm and she was always sweating in the cold and freezing in her sweat, always wrong, always *too much*, a child of accretion built somehow for rot. And one spring when the cushion-flowers blazed she did not turn her face to the midday sun with the others. She turned it downward, toward the gradient of haze, toward the place where the rain decided what it would be — and she went into the haze and did not come back. *The Sink took her,* they say above, *because she was already half of it. She was the warning. There is always one the cold cannot keep.* They say it as grief and as caution both. They do not say — they do not know — that she lived.

In the low version, a stranger came up out of the haze one season, gasping, slick with a sweat that would not cool — a tall one, gangly even by their measure, who could not stop moving, who answered before the question cooled, who could not bear to hold still or to hold on, who threw away whatever was given to her the same day, laughing, *keep moving, keep moving.* But she carried in her, deep in her quick light chest, a thing none of them had: she could *wait.* When the great rains came and washed the camp away and the others scattered, light and unburdened and unbothered, trusting the next day, she alone sat very still in the wet and grieved the lost place — grieved it as though a thing could be permanent, as though the world owed keeping — and they did not understand her, and they loved her, and they let her go down deeper into the green when she chose to, and they tell of her now as the one who came up out of the cold carrying a strangeness in her: *the only one of us who ever learned to mourn. She had the cold in her bones. There is always one the heat cannot quite warm.*

It is the same woman. She is neither slander and she is both truths, and she is the only being in either world who has stood in both climates and felt in her own divided body the single fact that the cold and the heat are arguing about from their opposite ends:

That everything is given, and nothing is kept, and the keeping and the letting-go are the same long love, read up the wall and down it — *accretion against cold, rot against time* — and that somewhere on the great flank, in the gradient of haze where the rain has not yet decided what it will be, there is a height at which the breath you can see meets the breath that is swallowed, and a person standing exactly there would, for the length of one held heartbeat, be neither saving nor spending, neither hoarding nor giving, neither climbing nor moving on —

just warm enough, just cold enough, exactly here, and not afraid.

She is looking for that height. She has been looking for a long time. She moves quickly and she knows how to wait, and she has not found it, and the looking is the whole of her life now, up and down the only world there is, which is one world, which has always been one world, which is a single great flank of stone falling from the cold clean silence into the warm green roar, with the haze in the middle where the rain decides — and somewhere in that haze a tall woman climbing or descending, you cannot tell which, you can never quite tell which, breath showing and then not showing and then showing again.
