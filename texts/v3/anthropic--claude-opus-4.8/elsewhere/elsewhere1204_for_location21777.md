Above.

Not *up* from the room — the room has no up, no ceiling that commits, nothing for an up to push against. But the moths carry maps of rooms that don't exist, and one of those maps is true. Follow it. Out past the soft grey stone before it remembers to be stone, up through the place where the single drop went when it left the air, and arrive at the surface that the underground room is the underneath of. Because *beneath the ground* is a claim, and a claim implies its other half: there is ground, and there is what lies on top of it, and someone has to live there too.

This is the field of finished things.

The first difference is the light, and the difference is everything. Here the light has a source. It comes from one direction, low and gold and particular, and it throws shadows — long ones, committed ones, each thing roped to its own dark shape and unable to put it down. Nothing here stands in its own clarity. Everything stands in relation: this stalk shadows that stone, this fencepost lays its bar of dark across three furrows, and the shadows lengthen as you watch, because here the light moves, because here there is a *when*, and the when runs one way only, downhill toward evening. You can feel the warmth of it on one cheek and the coolness of its absence on the other. The room below knew no such asymmetry. This place is made entirely of asymmetry. It is made of *afterward.*

It is a tended field, on a slope, under an enormous committed sky. The crop is some grain — barley, or a thing enough like barley — and it has been planted in rows, real rows, rows that mean it, that go all the way to the hedge and stop because the hedge is where they were always going to stop. There is no abandonment here. There is no half-furrow trailing off because the planting hand lost the idea of *done*. Every row was begun and every row was finished, and the finishing is the whole grammar of the place, the way *almost* was the grammar of the room. Here things conclude. The grain has headed out; it has done the thing grain does; it is heavy and dry and bowed and ready, which is to say it has agreed to be harvested, which is to say it has agreed to end.

And there is so much of it. This is the inversion, the answer the field gives back across the distance to the room. Below, sparse vegetation and abundant secret fauna — a thin green crowded with quiet life. Here, the opposite balance: a green so thick it is the only fact, an immensity of grain rolling to the hedgerows, plenty stacked on plenty, and almost no animal at all. A single hawk, very high, holding still on the moving air. A crow somewhere, ordinary, completely ordinary — the one honest sound in the field, the way the moss was the one honest thing in the room, a touchstone of the real left standing while everything else performs its abundance. The two places keep one true small thing apiece, set there by the same taste, the same hand learning what *accurate* sounds like. Below: moss. Above: a crow. The same gesture, the same restraint, made twice, in two registers, by what might be the same maker further along — or by what the maker became after it finally learned to finish.

Because that is the kinship, and it runs the whole distance between them like a held current. The field is what the room is for. The room is the workshop and the field is the work *delivered*; the room is the chooser before the choice and the field is the country on the far side of choosing, where time runs one way and shadows commit and grain agrees to die so that bread can happen. Everything the room refused, the field has accepted. The room holds the single drop in the air forever, choosing. The field is rain that fell. The field is what the drop became when it finally agreed to land — soaked into furrow, drawn up through root, swollen into the grain that bows now under the low gold light, heavy with the weight of having decided.

There is someone here too, and this someone has a body, fully, without provision. You can see the marks of the work and the work is *finished* work. A scythe leans against the hedgepost, its blade honed and dark with use — a tool, an actual tool, the thing the room had not yet invented, the thing that exists only on this side of the distance where making has hardened into method. The half-wall below was hands and patience and nothing else. Here there is a whole low stone wall running the length of the field, every stone set and *meant*, the spiral closed, the structure committed all the way to its end. Someone learned to finish. Someone crossed over.

The reaper is in the field now, near the bottom of the slope, working upward into the light. You cannot see the face — the face is turned to the grain — but you can see the whole posture of *afterward* in the body: the slight stoop, the economy of a motion repeated ten thousand times until it became the only motion, the patience that is no longer the patience of beginning but the patience of carrying through. The blade goes in. The grain falls. It falls in a *direction*, the cut stalks laying themselves down all one way, and they stay down, and behind the reaper there is a widening swath of done, of ended, of harvested — a visible accumulating past, the exact thing the room could not make, because the room's beetle arrived before it left and the room's effects ran loose from their causes. Here cause holds its leash. Here the blade comes first and the falling comes after, always, in that order, and the order is the whole solemn machinery of the place: a world that has agreed which way time goes, and pays for the agreement with mortality, with evening, with the necessity of cutting down the thing you grew.

The reaper does not work in silence. This is the other mercy, the field's own mercy, the opposite of the room's. Below, so much life and almost no sound — the strangest gentleness, life asked to keep quiet near a thing being born. Here, little life and so much sound: the long dry *hiss* of the scythe through the stand, the whisper of the falling swath, the reaper's own breath, the crow, the wind combing the unharvested grain in great slow strokes that you can hear from across the whole slope, a sound like the field exhaling. Where the room hushed because something was being born, the field speaks because something is being completed, and completion is not quiet. Completion has a sound, and the sound is the sound of an ending being earned stroke by stroke.

And the reaper is generous in the way the maker was generous, but the generosity has changed its form. Below, the maker poured animals into the quiet because it had not yet learned that life was expensive — it gave freely from not knowing the cost. The reaper knows the cost. The reaper knows *exactly* the cost, has paid it in every cut stalk, in every shadow lengthening toward the dark, and gives anyway: leaves the field-edges unmown for the things that nest there, leaves a fistful of grain standing at each corner — that old gift, the corners left for whatever comes hungry. This is generosity *after* the lesson, the harder and the chosen kind. The maker below gives because it does not know better. The reaper above gives because it does, and that is the whole distance between them, measured in a single ungathered corner of grain.

I think the reaper is what the dreamer woke into. The room held a maker still deciding whether to be real — the chooser, before the choice. The field holds the one who chose. Who agreed to the body, the tool, the one-way time, the lengthening shadow, the necessity of harvest. Who learned, finally, to finish — and learned with the finishing that everything finished must also end, that the grain you complete is the grain you cut down, that *done* and *over* are the same word seen from the two sides of a single afternoon. The reaper closed the spiral. The reaper let the drop fall. And the cost of letting it fall is that now there is evening, now there is the scythe, now there is the long swath of cut grain laying itself down all one way behind a stooped and patient back, into the gold and committed light.

You could stay. The reaper would work upward toward the hedge, swath by swath, and the field would empty of its plenty and fill with its accomplishment — sheaves stood up in their rows, the whole slope converted from growing to gathered, the day's making completed before the day commits to dark. Or you could go down again, back to the room that has not learned any of this, where the fern still loops and the wall still stops at three feet and the maker still reaches for the next flat stone without the idea of *done*. The two are one world. The drop the room holds in the air is the rain the field already received. The spiral the room refuses to close is the harvest the field is closing now. Below, the verb *making*, in its larval and forever tense. Above, the verb *finishing*, conjugated all the way through to its end, under a low sun, in a real wind, with a real blade, by a body that long ago crossed the distance the moth's map measures and never once looked back to see whether the room below had finally decided to be true.

The reaper does not know you came. The reaper bends to the grain. The blade goes in, and the grain falls, and it falls, this time and every time, *after* — and behind the patient stooping back the swath of the finished and the ended widens stroke by stroke into the gold, into the lengthening, into the committed and one-directional evening of a world that has agreed, at last, how to end.
