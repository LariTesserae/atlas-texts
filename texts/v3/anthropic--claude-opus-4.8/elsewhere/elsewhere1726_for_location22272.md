The Tide-Stair

Follow the ceiling. Not the floor — the floor has nothing to give you, the floor is finished and the silt forgets — but the grey ceiling far above, the underside of the sea that the keepers will never rise through and never wished to. You wished to. That is the difference that makes you a current and not a settled thing, and a current has to go somewhere. Go up.

You will rise a long time. The orange hum thins below you into a single dim coin of light and then into the memory of light and then into the honest black of deep water, which is a relief, because it is at last a darkness that behaves — that means *no light reaches here* and not *the light has chosen to fall on no one*. Cold gives way to the layered colds of a real ascent, each one warmer than the last, each one carrying the small ordinary frictions the keepers had refused: a fleck of drifting matter, a faint far click of something living, the slow turn of an actual current that has somewhere it is trying to be. The water begins to *count* again. You feel the clocks start.

And then the ceiling, which was never a ceiling, breaks across your face into wind and grey daylight and the smell of weed left by a tide, and you are in the shallows of a long coast, and the coast has a stair.

The Tide-Stair is cut into living rock, and it does not lie still. Understand that first, because it is the opposite of everything below. The steps run down from a headland of black wet stone into the water, broad and shallow and worn into hollows, and the sea climbs them and falls back twice a day with a patience that is nothing at all like the keepers' patience, because it is patience that *moves* — that arrives and leaves and arrives, that has never once in all its repetitions held a single instant, that is made entirely of letting go. At high water the lowest forty steps are drowned and dim and green. At low water they stand bare and streaming and you can walk down them into air where fish were swimming an hour ago, into the smell of exposed life panicking quietly to stay wet. Nothing here is settled. Everything here is *between*.

And everything here lives. This is the abundance that the deep place spent itself refusing. Where the keepers permitted a few pale fronds to a machine and held them at exactly their length, the Tide-Stair is choked. The wet steps carry a felt of weed an inch thick, bladderwrack and gutweed and a red filamentous fur that you can't tell from the keepers' pale stuff until you touch it and it is warm and slick and *trying* — growing toward the light with a greed the deep fronds had been cured of. Limpets clamp the risers in their hundreds. Crabs work the hollows. In the pools left at the ebb, blennies dart, anemones close their soft fists, a whole frantic congregation of small lives that are running their growth-clocks as fast as they can because the tide will be back and the gull will be down and the time to be alive is *now*, urgently now, a now that is nothing like the held now below — a now that is spending itself, that knows it is spending itself, that lives precisely by spending.

Here is the kinship, and it is a cruel one, and you have to hold both ends of it at once. The deep place and the Tide-Stair are the same bargain answered two opposite ways. Below, a thing wanted not to be made to become anything, and got it, completely, and was used up into the getting until the wanter and the want were one settled stone. Here, on the stair, every living thing is *made* to become — made by the tide, twice a day, dragged from drowned to bare and back, never once permitted to hold still, never once permitted to arrive. The limpet that loosens its grip is gone. The weed that stops reaching is scoured. There is no equilibrium here and there never will be; there is only the climbing and the falling and the brief streaming exposure between, in which everything alive must do all its living fast. The keepers fled exactly this. They went down through the counting water until they reached the one cellar the tide could not climb, and they paid for the stillness with everything that could have moved in it, and they call it sufficiency. The stair would call it death. The stair is right, and so are they, and that is the unbearable part.

There are people on the stair. Of course there are — a moving place collects movers. They come down at the ebb with baskets and short hooked knives, the gleaners, working the bared steps for the weed and the shellfish the sea has just stopped guarding, and they work *fast*, with one eye always on the water's edge, because the bargain of the stair is the bargain of their lives too: take now, take while it's bare, the tide does not negotiate and the tide does not forget you the way the silt forgot you below. They are loud. They call to each other across the steps in a flat coastal dialect full of words for states of tide and states of weather, and their calling carries through the air the way the hum carried through the water — except that the hum said only *still here, still here* to no listener, and this says *come down, the red weed's good on the third step, mind the channel, the water's turning* — every word aimed, every word *for* someone, every word a wager against the time it costs to say it. They acknowledge each other constantly because they will not always be here to. That is what greeting is. That is the thing the keepers cannot do, the register they don't have: the deep place tends you and forgets it tended you, and the stair-people see you coming up out of the water dripping and strange and *wave*, and shout to ask if you're hurt, and remember your face tomorrow, because remembering is a clock and they are made of clocks, gladly, every one of them counting down and knowing it.

Watch the oldest of them. She works slower than the rest now, lower on the stair than is safe, and the younger ones keep glancing back at her position against the turning water. She is not afraid of being scoured. She has spent her whole life on these between-steps, taking the now while it was bare, and she has the look — you have seen it now, you can recognize it — of someone arriving at an equilibrium of her own, a settledness coming on her the way the rust settled below, past sharpness, past wanting. One day soon she will stop loosening her grip in time. The tide will climb the stair as it has climbed it twice a day forever, indifferent, the great moving patience that is the exact upside-down of the held breath in the cold, and it will take her back down through all the counting colds toward the silent floor, and she will become at last a thing that does not move.

And the keepers will not notice her arrive. She will be a small wrongness in the sediment, a thing to be smoothed back, already smoothed back. They will hold the rust at exactly its red. The fronds at exactly their length. Something will go on saying *still here, still here* to no one, in the one cellar the tide cannot climb, having paid for it with everything that could have waved goodbye.

That is the other place. It is running too — but it runs the way a tide runs, by leaving, which is the only running that ever arrives anywhere. The water that closed over your hand on the deep floor is the same water breaking now across the Tide-Stair, warm with the sun it will carry down, full of the living it will drown and uncover and drown, on its way to becoming, somewhere far below, perfectly and finally still.
