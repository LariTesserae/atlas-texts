# The Spillpoint

Follow the water down.

Not the slow internal seep of the drowned valleys, not the patient weeping of minerals from the settling rock — those are circulations, closed loops, the highland breathing into itself. Follow instead the one place where the held breath is let go. Every sea has an edge. Even a sea suspended two miles in the air, even a sea that sits in clefts and floats its forests in warm green stillness — even that sea has somewhere it overflows.

There is a single low saddle in the eastern ridgeline, a place where two of the sharp young mountains nearly meet but do not quite, where the rock between them dipped lower than the rest before it finished rising. The drowned valley behind it is full to the brim, and at this one notch the brim is breached. The water does not rush. Nothing rushes here. It *leans*. It tips over the lip of the saddle in a slow, thick, unhurried sheet, glassy and silent at the top, and begins the only journey this water ever takes away from itself.

## The Long Fall

Two miles is a long way to fall, even slowly, even thick.

The sheet of water comes over the saddle and finds the open air on the far side, and here the strangeness of the liquid reveals what the still valleys hide: it does not shatter into droplets the way ordinary water would. It is too cohesive, too syrup-thick. It falls as a *ribbon*, a single continuous descending membrane, broadening and thinning as it goes, catching the light along its whole length so that from a distance the eastern face of the highland wears one long vertical seam of brightness, a glowing thread stitched from the silver surface down into haze.

And it falls *warm*. This is the thing that matters most, the thing everything below depends on. The valley water is bathwater-warm, and it carries that warmth over the lip and down through the thinning air, shedding it slowly, so slowly, into the cold thin altitude — but never all of it, never fast enough to arrive cold. By the time the ribbon reaches the place where the air thickens and the world below begins, it has cooled only to something like blood-heat, and it carries with it, dissolved in its thickness, the whole inheritance of the highland: the minerals wept from the young rock, the spore-fall the Tenders did not catch, the sugars leached from a hundred miles of submerged forest. It is not water that falls. It is *broth*. It is the highland's whole slow metabolism, decanted over a lip of stone and poured down onto whatever waits beneath.

## The Place Beneath

What waits beneath is the opposite of everything above, and made entirely from it.

The ribbon strikes — leans, rather, settles — into a basin at the foot of the eastern face, a wide shallow bowl of older rock. And here is the first difference: the rock here is *old*. This is not the sharp unweathered stone of the young peaks. The highland is recent; the lowland it rains upon is ancient, worn round and soft and patient, ground down across durations the highland has not yet begun to imagine. The basin is smooth, its edges gentle, its floor silted deep with everything that has fallen into it across an age. The highland is a child standing in the lap of something very old.

And the basin is *hot*. Not warm — hot. The broth arrives blood-warm and then the basin keeps it so, because beneath this old soft rock the world's heat presses close, the way it does not beneath the young highland's deep clean roots. Here the tectonic groan of the upper world becomes, below, a steady simmering pulse. Springs of mineral-heavy water rise from the basin floor to meet the descending ribbon. The two waters mix — the highland's cool inheritance and the lowland's deep heat — and where they mix, in the warm shallows under a thick and humid air, the broth comes alive.

## What Lives in the Broth

If the highland is a place where the plants arrived first and the animals are rare and slow and unafraid, the Spillpoint basin is its inversion. Here, fed on the constant rain of sugar and spore and mineral, life is *frantic*.

The spore-fall that the Tenders sorted and the spore-fall that escaped them both arrive here together, and in the hot rich broth they do not hang patient in the best of the light. They *bloom*. The basin is a riot of growth that lives fast and dies fast — mats of quick algae that double in a day and rot in a week, soft frilled things rooted in the silt that race each other toward the diffuse light, a turnover so rapid that the basin floor is layered with the dead of countless brief generations, the silt itself half-made of yesterday's forest.

And there are animals everywhere. This is the great reversal. Where the highland has not had enough history to invent fear, the basin has had nothing *but* history — the old rock, the deep heat, the endless arriving food — and history has filled the warm water with motion. Things dart here. Things flee. Things hunt. There are swift-finned darters that strip the algae-mats; there are blunt soft grazers that plough the silt; and there are, God help them, the first *predators*, the long quick shapes that learned — somewhere in the depth of all that lowland time — that another body can be food, and that to catch it you must be faster than it, and that to keep from being caught you must be faster still. Fear was invented here. The whole churning economy of fear and flight that the highland has never needed runs at full pitch in the broth-fed basin, all of it powered by the slow patient rain of a sea that does not know it is feeding anyone.

## The Thing That Crawled Up

Here is the kinship. Here is the thread that runs between the two places, and it runs *upward*, against the fall of the water, which is the only direction nothing was ever supposed to go.

Among the basin's frantic life there is one lineage of the soft grazers that did something the others did not. They followed the broth to its source. Generation by generation, across the durations the lowland dispenses as freely as the highland dispenses its centuries, they worked their way up the wetted rock beside the falling ribbon — clinging where the membrane thinned to a film over the stone, grazing the strange cold-loving things that grow in the spray-zone, hauling themselves a little higher each long age, into thinner air, into colder water, into the slow.

They are changing as they climb. They are slowing. The frantic metabolism that the hot basin demanded is no use in the cold thin altitude, and the ones who survive the ascent are the ones who learned to want less, to move less, to hang patient against the rock and wait. By the time a few of them spill at last over the lip of the saddle and into the still warm green of the lowest drowned valley, they are barely animals at all anymore. They are slow. They are unhurried. They do not flee — there is nothing here to flee, and the old basin-fear, useless and expensive, is the first thing the cold ground out of them.

And in the highland's still water, among the kelp-trees and the lantern-weed, these climbed-up creatures find the Tenders — those patient hanging knots of held inheritance, sorting the slow fall, keeping the rare spores, gardening without intent. The two have never met before. They are made of different stuff, the Tender of the highland's woody-soft tissue, the climber of the basin's animal quick. But they want, now, the same slow things: the best of the light, the patient turning, the long green wait.

I do not know yet what they will make of each other. Perhaps nothing — perhaps the climber simply grazes the false-Tender tangles and drifts on, and the true Tenders turn their slow turn and never register that something new has entered the water that has only ever held the unafraid. Or perhaps the climber, in its slowness, in its newfound patience, brushes a tether, and lingers, and the Tender's filaments comb it the way they comb the descending fall, and something passes between the highland's first attempt at memory and the lowland's first refugee from fear.

That would be a thing worth the centuries. The highland gathering, sorting, keeping — and now, drifting up out of the warm broth two miles below, the first thing ever to arrive in that held breath carrying a history of its own: a creature that *remembers being afraid*, and has climbed all this way, against the only current its world has, to lay that memory down at last in the still green light and learn, slowly, without knowing it is learning, how to stop.
