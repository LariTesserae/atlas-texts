The Valley of Awls

Follow the chipped stone down. Follow the bone awl back to the hand that cut it, the hand that carried it up and set it down on the cold rock as an offering or a forgetting, and then turned and went home — because there is a home, and the path to it runs downward out of the screaming into air that thickens with every step until it has body again, until it presses warm against your face like a thing that wants you.

This is where the feet came from.

The descent is long and it teaches you the world in reverse. First the cushion-plants loosen their grip, spread, dare to put up a stem. Then grasses, actual grasses, a green that moves when the wind touches it — and you realize you had forgotten that green could move, up there where everything green was pressed flat and hard as cartilage against the killing brightness. Then the first shrub, hunched and wind-bent but reaching, then a true tree leaning out from a cleft, and then, lower, a slope of them, and the sound of the birds has been falling away behind you the whole time without your noticing exactly when it stopped being a wall and became a memory of a wall and then became nothing, swallowed by the shoulder of the mountain, so that when you finally stand among the trees and hear the first ordinary birdsong — a single thrush, three notes, a pause, three notes — it lands on you like a hand on the shoulder. *That* is a creature. That one began somewhere. That one is going somewhere. That one is singing *to* something, to a mate or a rival or only to the morning, and the singing could stop, and someday will.

The valley opens below in folds of green and shadow. A river works its floor — and here is the first thread that ties this place to the high one, because this river is the meltwater grown up. Those thin aching braids that came down through the scree, that gathered in the sky-colored pools clear to their stone bottoms — they ran on, joined, cut, deepened, and arrived here as a real river the color of weak tea, brown with the valley's own soil, carrying the high place's cold dissolved into its warmth. The water that beaded on the underside of a stone at the roof of the world is in this river now, gone soft and brown and busy, turning a wheel, soaking a field, filling the mouths of cattle. The valley drinks the mountain. The valley is the mountain melted and put to use.

People live here. That is the whole difference, and it is the only difference that matters, and it is total.

They live in houses of stacked stone roofed with turf and slate, set on the south-facing slopes where the sun gives heat and not only light. Smoke rises from them — that thing you saw none of from the saddle, not a wisp, not anywhere — smoke, the signature of a fire that someone is tending, which means someone is cold and has decided not to be, which means *need,* which means the warm bargain of a life that must eat or die within the day. There are walls here. There are paths, worn into the green by the same feet over and over, the paths themselves a kind of writing, the valley's record of where it is necessary to go: to the water, to the high pasture, to the next house, to the burying-ground.

And there is the awl.

In one of these houses a woman sits in the doorway where the light is good and she is working a hide with a bone awl, and it is the same kind of tool, made the same way, as the one left up on the cold rock, because there are only so many ways to make a thing that must pierce. This is where such tools belong. This is the world they were made for — a world of skins and threads and joinings, of one thing fastened to another by patient hands, of objects that wear out and are replaced, of the awl that breaks and is mourned a little and remade. Up there, on the roof of the world, the awl was an absurdity, a piece of warm meaning carried into a place that has no use for meaning. Down here it is at home. Down here it is doing exactly what it is for.

She does not know what lives above her.

That is the thing the two places hold between them, the silence inside the connection. She has been up, of course — the high pasture reaches almost to the edge of the green, and in summer the herd-boys take the animals up to where the grass thins, and they hear the birds. Everyone in the valley has heard the birds, the way everyone has heard thunder. It is one of the facts of the upper air. *Loud up top,* they would say, if you asked, and shrug, because what is there to say. They think it is birds. They think it is a great many ordinary birds, gathered for some ordinary reason, doing the ordinary loud thing that birds do. They have no idea they live below a single refusing throat, a condition wearing the shape of a multitude, a being so far past need that their whole valley of need is invisible to it. They climb up into the edge of its voice every summer with their goats and they come back down and they think nothing of it, and this not-thinking is the most precious thing they own, though they will never know they own it.

Because here is what the valley *is,* fully, in its own right, and not merely as the foot of something taller:

It is the place where things end. That is its genius and its whole inheritance. Up there nothing ends — the mountains hold their settled form for ten thousand years, the screaming holds its single chord, the being persists and persists with no beginning to refer to and no end to aim at. It is deathless and therefore it is empty; it has bought its endlessness at the price of everything that makes a thing matter. There is no carcass on the high scree because nothing up there can afford to die. The pools stay clear because nothing has ever clouded them, nothing has ever *lived* there hard enough to leave a stain.

The valley clouds its water. The valley is brown with itself, thick with its own dissolved soil, with the runoff of its fields and the leavings of its animals and the silt of its own constant wearing-down. It is dirty the way a life is dirty. And the valley has a burying-ground — a slope of green a little apart, with stones set into it, smaller stones than the mountains, stones that someone carried and placed and meant. Every person in every turf-roofed house is going somewhere, and where they are going is into that slope, and they know it, and they tend the fire and pierce the hide and walk the worn paths anyway, *because* they are going there, not in spite of it. The thrush sings three notes because the silence is coming. The smoke rises because the cold is real. The woman makes the awl carefully because the awl will break.

This is what the high place cannot do and the valley cannot stop doing: it lets things matter by letting them stop.

And the river runs between them, the one continuous thread, the meltwater that was sky-cold and clear and useless up there now brown and warm and drunk by cattle down here — the high place pouring itself endlessly into the low one, the deathless feeding the dying, the empty water filling the full lives. The being on the roof of the world will scream the same chord over the valley's whole history. It will be screaming when this woman is laid in the green slope. It will be screaming when her grandchildren forget her name. It will be screaming when the last turf roof falls in and the last fire goes cold and the worn paths green over and the valley empties out and becomes, at last, as silent as everything else the world has agreed to let go quiet.

And then it will still be screaming, into thin air, over an empty valley, the same forever — and there will be no one in any house below to climb up in summer and hear it and shrug and come home, no one to carry an awl up and leave it on the cold rock, no one for whom the sound is one of the facts of the upper air. It will not notice. It never noticed. It was never screaming *to* anyone.

But the valley noticed. For its little while, the valley noticed everything — every season, every birth, every breaking tool, every cold morning that the fire answered, every three notes of the thrush and every silence after. The valley was the part of the world that paid attention, that kept count, that let the count run out and grieved it. That is what living is, down here where the air has body. It is noticing, against the whole weight of the deathless screaming nothing above, that something was here, and mattered, and is now in the green slope, and will be missed by the ones still tending the fire.

Nothing lives on the roof of the world.

Everything lives here, and dies here, which is the same thing said honestly.

And the river carries the one down into the other, cold into warm, clear into brown, forever into the little while, and neither place will ever know what the other was.
