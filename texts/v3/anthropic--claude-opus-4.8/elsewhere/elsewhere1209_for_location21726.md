# The Cistern Floor

Follow the water down. Not the inferred terraces — those are the maker's own descent, geological, endless, a condition falling through itself. Follow instead the *other* path the water takes: the one nobody planned. Because not all of it runs neatly channel-to-channel into the bright. Some of it goes wrong. Some of it finds the fractures that don't lead anywhere the grammar intended — the glassy seams in the young rock that crack downward instead of across — and it leaks out of the design, and it falls, and it gathers somewhere the keeper never goes because the keeper follows the surplus and this is the *spill*.

It gathers here.

Stop being high. Stop the heat. The fall is long enough that temperature has time to change its mind, and by the time the water arrives it has cooled, and the air it makes has cooled, and the load-bearing wet heat of the terrace is a memory three hundred fathoms up. Here it is cool and dim and the wet is honest wet now — water that fell because falling is what water does, water that obeyed gravity instead of instruments. This is where the impossible amount of water goes when it stops being miraculous and starts being *plumbing*.

## The Shape of Below

A floor — but say *floor* the way the terrace was a *terrace*, knowing the word is too small. It is a vast low chamber, and it is not young. This is the first difference your body registers, before your eyes adjust: the rock here is *old*. The edges have learned to round. Run a hand along a wall and it doesn't fracture-glass against your palm; it gives you the long smooth curve of stone that water has been working since before the terrace above was placed. The maker set down the terrace last season. This was already here. The miracle is new. The drain is ancient.

That is the thing that tightens the throat down here, the way the arithmetic tightened it above. Up top, the unexplained was the *abundance* — too much water, from nowhere. Down here the unexplained is the *fit*. The spill from a brand-new instrument falls, by accident, by leak, by the failure of the design's tidiness — and it lands in a cistern that was already shaped to receive exactly this. As though the floor had been waiting. As though something, long ago, hollowed a catch-basin under a slope that would not weep for ages yet, and then waited, patient the way the terrace ecology is patient, the whole arrangement stretched across a gap of time instead of a gap of empty stone.

The keeper keeps a bowl. *Something* kept this.

## Water at Volume

Forget the small scale. Forget the tick of one drop, the thread-thin trickle, the soft internal pour of a single stalk. Here the water has *met itself*. Every leak from every fracture above, every vein that went wrong, every overflow that missed its channel — all of it has been falling and collecting and the floor holds a black lake of it, still, deep, so calm in the windless dark that it doesn't look like water until you disturb it and the disturbance travels out and out and takes a long time to die.

The dominant sound, then, is the inversion of the terrace's sound. Up there: small water, loud in the silence, one drop carrying. Down here: large water, and the silence *inside* it. The lake does not speak. The inflow speaks — there is a place along one wall where the accumulated spill comes through in a real pour, a column of it falling from a crack near the high dark ceiling, and *that* is loud, that is the one sustained voice in the chamber, a steady roar-and-hush that has been pouring as long as the terrace has wept and will pour as long as it weeps. Everywhere else, stillness. The pour at one edge, and the held breath of the whole black surface receiving it.

You hear the pour from anywhere in the chamber. The way one sound carried across the wet stone above, one sound fills the hollow below. Different rule, same loneliness of acoustics: in both places, there is so much room and so little to make noise.

## What the Cool Summoned

The terrace's heat summoned green into its wet pockets — lush, fat-leaved, glossy, sharing surplus along deliberate gutters. The cistern's cool, and its dark, summoned the opposite, and the opposite is just as alive.

Nothing green. No light to spend on it. Instead, along the old rounded walls where the spill-water films down to the lake, there are growths that have given up on the sun entirely — pale, fine, fibrous, the color of things that make their own arrangements with chemistry. Where the terrace moss tracked its collectors by faint blue, faint red, *here* the difference shows in a different register: the spill arrives carrying the tint of wherever above it leaked from, and the pale growths feed on that, and they have sorted themselves into faint banded zones along the wall, a slow contour map of which fracture feeds which patch. You could, in principle, read the leaks of the terrace by reading the bands of the cistern. The unweathered abundance above writes itself, accidentally, in the patient growth below. One place keeps a ledger of the other and neither knows it.

The growths are not lush. They are *thorough*. They cover what they can reach and stop where the water stops, exactly, the way the low wall above ran three courses and stopped — the right length, complete, no apology.

## What Lives Below

Up above, fauna was rare and patient: translucent water-jellies too dry-blind to notice you, a lean wet-stone shape at the white edge that came and went. The web was real but thin, stretched across emptiness.

Here the web is *closed*. This is the strange gift of being the drain: everything that falls, falls *here*, and stays, and so the thin patient ecology of the terrace has a sump, a place where the surplus finally pools into enough to support things that need enough.

In the black lake there are swimmers. Blind, of course — pale, the cistern-growth pallor, eyes either gone or never bothered with. They are not jellies; they have *learned* this water, learned its volume and its stillness, and they move through it with the unhurried confidence of creatures that have never been hunted from above and have all the time the slow inflow grants. They eat the wall-growth. Something eats them — there is a larger shape in the deep lake, you infer it the way the terrace inferred its predator, by the way the swimmers sometimes all go still at once and then resume. The whole food web that lay flat and starved across the terrace's empty stone has *pooled* down here into something almost dense, almost generous, fed entirely on the spill of a miracle it cannot see and would not understand.

They are the inheritors of the leak. The keeper above lives on the surplus of an engine he can't comprehend. These live on the surplus of the keeper's whole world, on what *his* world wastes, on the water that escaped even the parasite's notice. There is always a further down. There is always something living on the spill of the spill.

## The Other Vessel

And here is the thing that pairs with the bowl, the way I knew it would have to.

Set on a ledge above the lake's edge, just clear of the spill-film, where the rounded old stone makes a natural shelf — a vessel. Not the keeper's bowl. *Older.* The bowl above was domestic, recent, human-scaled, half full, just-stepped-away-from — the right strangeness, a habit, a claim. This is the bowl's ancestor, or its echo across the gap. It is larger, deeper, made of the same impulse but a longer time ago: a cistern within the cistern, a worked stone basin set to catch the pour-spray, and it is full to its rounded brim and overspilling in a thread so fine it has worn its own small channel back into the lake.

No one tends it. That is the difference, and it is the whole difference, and it is what the descent was for.

The bowl above is *kept*. Someone fills it. Someone returns. The keeper keeps it ferociously because it is the one thing he is sized to keep. The basin below is *finished* — set once, long ago, by whatever hollowed the cistern in patient anticipation of a leak that hadn't started, and then *left*. It tends itself now. It fills from the spill and overflows down its worn thread and has done so, untouched, since before the keeper arrived above, before the maker placed the first collector, before the rock above learned to fracture glassy.

Two vessels. The same gesture across an unbridgeable difference of time and scale. Up above, a creature small enough to need a bowl, keeping it because keeping is all that's left to a tenant. Down below, the bowl's vast patient original, needing no one, kept by gravity and the long aim of whoever placed it, full because the world is built to keep it full.

The keeper above thinks he learned to be small on purpose, and was right to call it enough. He doesn't know about the basin. He follows the surplus up and down the bright terraces and never follows the spill, never finds the place where his whole miracle drains, never sees that his careful domestic bowl is a small late copy of a thing that was set here ages before him by the same kind of hand — proper hands, a thumb, the fine control to set a vessel level on uneven stone — and then walked away forever, trusting the water to keep it.

That is the connection, and it lives in the water itself: every cool mouthful the keeper drinks above has a sibling that fell, and missed the channel, and came down here, and is held now in the black calm under the worn old walls, tending a basin nobody tends, feeding swimmers nobody sees.

He set the bowl back level when he'd drunk. He always does. The silence above will tell him someone passed.

The silence down here tells nothing. There's the pour at the one wall, steady, roar-and-hush, filling everything. There's the basin overspilling its fine worn thread. There's the lake receiving, and the blind swimmers going still and resuming, and the bands of pale growth keeping their accidental ledger of the leaks far overhead.

No one will fill the basin. No one has to. That was the point of placing it here, so long ago, in stone already old: to make one thing that did not need keeping. The keeper above made the opposite choice and called *that* enough, and was also right.

Both of them are right. That's the cruelty of it and the mercy of it. A bowl kept by a hand and a bowl kept by the world, the same shape, the same water, three hundred fathoms and an age apart — and the only thing that ever passes between them is the spill, going down, the way all the water goes down and never seems to lower the supply.
