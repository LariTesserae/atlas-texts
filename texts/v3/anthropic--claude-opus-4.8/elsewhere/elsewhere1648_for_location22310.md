# The Sink

Follow the dust down.

Everything that leaves the pinnacle goes one direction, because there is only one direction left for it to go. The grit the wind sands off the cliffs, the spent leaves that miss the moss and tumble, the dropped seed, the loosed feather, the body of a climbing bird that fell wrong in the thin air and was not caught — all of it sifts down the smooth flanks, three vertical kilometers, through the haze and out of it, falling and falling past rock that no foot has touched, until it reaches the bottom of the world.

There is a bottom. The void below the pinnacle is not bottomless; it only looks that way from the top, where the haze closes over everything more than a stone's throw down. The mountain stands in a basin — a great closed bowl of land, ringed by other worn stubs of stone too low and too far to matter, with no outlet, no pass, no river-mouth to drain it. Whatever falls in stays in. And the lowest point of that bowl, directly beneath the pinnacle, gathering the slow rain of everything the high town has shed across an age beyond counting, is the Sink.

It is wet here.

This is the thing the pinnacle would not believe, if the pinnacle could be told: that all its dryness has a destination, and the destination is a marsh. The dust that never condensed up top condenses down here, where the basin holds the cold of the long nights and the air pools heavy and still and cannot escape over the ringing hills. Mist gathers in the bowl and does not lift. It beads on every surface. It runs. Over a span as long as the mountain's wearing, the falling grit has packed and settled and held that water, and made a black mire, leagues across, fed forever by the slow disintegration of the heights — the pinnacle dissolving grain by grain into the wind, and the wind carrying it down, and the down being *here*, where the grains arrive and lie down in the wet and become ground.

The Sink is made of the mountain too. Just later. Just lower. The same stone, only finished with its falling.

And it is dark — truly dark, not the amber gloom of the high town but a deep submerged green-black, because the haze that is a thin veil three kilometers up is a thick roof down here, the whole sky packed solid with suspended dust and standing mist, and almost no light gets through it. What grows in the Sink grows nearly blind. The plants are pale, swollen, translucent — fungal more than leafy, vast soft towers of fruiting body rising from the muck, gilled and porous, exhaling spores in slow clouds. They do not need the sun. They feed on what falls: the spent organic rain from above, the dead leaves, the bodies, the endless sifting compost of a town that has been shedding its remainders down the cliffs since before remembering began. The pinnacle's losses are the Sink's whole diet. Up top, the loop closes when a body is given to the north-face moss. But not everything is caught by the moss. What slips past, what tumbles, what the wind takes instead — that falls all the way, and feeds a second life entirely, a life the first one does not know exists.

## What lives here

Things move in the Sink, and they move *fast*.

This is the deepest difference, the one that would frighten the pinnacle most if it could see. Down here there is death — real death, sudden death, the kind that interrupts. The Sink is rich, gorged on the rain of the heights, and richness breeds appetite, and appetite breeds speed. There are hunters in the black water: long flat swimming things, blind and whiskered, that feel the mire for the tumble of a fallen body and race to it. There are bright quick stalkers in the fungal towers, lean and nervous, that startle and flee and kill and breed in hot brief lives that flare and gutter in a handful of years. Nothing here grows long and worn and stops gently in the warm. Here a life is short and urgent and ends in another life's mouth, and ends again, and again, a churn of being eaten and eating, fast as the pinnacle is slow.

Because down here, effort and outcome are *tightly* coupled. The hunter that hesitates starves. The stalker that does not flee is taken. Speed is rewarded and slowness is punished, immediately, with the only currency that matters, and so everything in the Sink has been honed not for patience but for the instant — quick eyes that are mostly useless in the dark and quick ears and quicker nerves, bodies built to spend themselves all at once. They anticipate. They must. Tomorrow is *not* this again down here; tomorrow is whatever you can catch or whatever catches you, and a creature that could not imagine the next moment, lunge for it, dread it, would not last the night. Where the pinnacle dwells in the now because nothing changes, the Sink dwells in the next because everything does.

## The ones who wade

And there are people in the Sink.

You would not at first take them for kin to the small slow tenders of the height. They are tall — drawn upward by reaching for the dim fruiting bodies, by wading deep water on long legs — and lean, and quick-eyed in the way of everything down here, with a startle-reflex bred into them, a readiness to move. Their hands are webbed and clever for sifting muck. Their lives are short. They burn through three or four generations in the time the pinnacle ages one, and they know it, and they live accordingly: loud and fast and hungry, mating young, mourning often, telling stories that are all events — *and then this happened, and then the water rose, and then the hunter took her, and then we moved the camp* — because down here things happen, things change, and a people who live inside change keep their stories as a string of befallings and not a closing loop.

They do not record either, but for the opposite reason: not because nothing is forgotten, but because nothing holds still long enough to be worth writing. The mire takes everything back too quickly. They keep instead a hot oral churn of recent memory, a few generations deep, and past that — nothing, mist, the same green-black forgetting that swallows their dead.

But here is the thing they have that the pinnacle does not, the thing that joins the two places in a single breath across three kilometers of falling air:

They look up.

The pinnacle never looks down. It cannot; the haze closes off the void, and besides, down is only where things are lost. But the Sink looks *up* constantly, because up is where things *come from*. Every good thing in the Sink fell from somewhere. The richest feeding follows the rain of grit and leaf and body; the wader who reads the falling well, who learns where on the black water the high gifts tend to land, eats best and lives longest of a short-lived kind. And so they watch the smear of brighter haze far overhead, the faint amber pulse that bleeds down on the stillest nights — and they do not know what it is, but they have a word for it, and the word is *the givers*, or in a closer rendering, *the ones above who let things fall*.

They have built a whole understanding out of the rain from the heights. They believe — it is the nearest thing to faith the fast wet world allows — that there are makers up in the bright haze who shed gifts deliberately, who send down the fruit and the feather and the body as provision, as message, as a kind of slow tending of the deep. They are not entirely wrong. They are only wrong about the intent. The pinnacle is not feeding them. The pinnacle does not know they exist, has no word for *down* except as loss, mourns each fallen body as a loop that failed to close — and that same body, grieved as a small catastrophe at the top, arrives at the bottom as manna, as the richest event of a wader's week, hauled dripping from the mire and shared out and eaten with fast hot gratitude to the givers above.

The same body. A failure at one end of the fall and a blessing at the other. The pinnacle's loose grip is the Sink's whole providence.

## What they fear, and what they love

They fear the *still* — the rare nights when nothing falls, when the high haze pulses faint and sheds nothing, and the feeding fails, and the quick hungry world turns on itself. To the pinnacle, silence is the terror, because silence means the loop has broken. To the Sink, *stillness from above* is the terror, because it means the givers have withheld, and a fast world cannot wait. The pinnacle dreads the day the noise stops. The Sink dreads the day the falling stops. They are the same dread, the dread of continuance interrupted, felt at two ends of one long thread, by two peoples who will never know they share it.

And what they love is the *fall* itself — the moment a thing comes down out of the dark roof and breaks the black water and is *new*, is here, is theirs. They have no slow heavy fruit to cradle in the dim; they have instead the catch, the race, the haul, the sudden arrival of provision from the bright unreachable above. A child in the Sink learns to read the surface of the mire for the rings of a landing, to sprint the muck on long legs toward where a gift came down, and to feel — in that sprint — the same thing the pinnacle child feels squatting all day by a vine: the deep rightness of being exactly matched to one's world. One waits in stillness for what does not change. One races through change for what falls. Neither could survive the other's home for a single turning.

But they are one mountain. The high town stands on the stone, and the stone wears, and the wearing falls, and the falling lands, and the landing feeds — and the whole worn structure, top to bottom, amber to black, slow to fast, is a single long descending gift that no one at either end can see entire. The pinnacle fills its lamps and keeps the warm light going against the wearing, and grieves the wearing as its one slow arrow. And every grain of that wearing, every loss it mourns, comes down through the haze to the watchers below as the very thing they pray for: proof that the givers are still above, still bright, still letting things fall.

The pinnacle's slow dissolution is the Sink's daily bread.

And on the stillest, blackest nights, when the mist lies heavy and the fungal towers exhale their slow spores and a wader stands waist-deep in the cold mire with face turned up — there is, very faint, very far, a warmth in the haze. A low amber pulse, three kilometers up, where the lamps are being filled again by small slow hands that will never know they are seen.

The watcher does not know what burns up there.

But she knows it has not gone out. And that is enough. It has been enough for a very long time.
