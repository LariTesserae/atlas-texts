The Watershed Above

Follow the water back. Not the steam — the steam only rises and falls inside the hollow, a closed throat, a lung breathing its own breath. Follow instead the water that is *drawn back somewhere into the engine to rise again*, the water that vanishes into the tubes that thread away through the young stone and do not come back. That water goes up. There is an up. The broken walls of the hollow are not the top of the world, only the bottom of a long shaft you never looked along, because you were looking at the warm seam, because the place taught you not to look up. Look up now. Follow the tubes. They climb.

They surface — and this is the thing that must be understood second, after the sound, after the lesson — they surface into *light that has a source*. A real one. A sun, low and yellow and ordinary, in a sky the pale exhausted blue of late autumn, over a country that goes on. After the hollow this is almost unbearable. There is distance here that means something — not the trick distance of the hollow, vast emptiness packed into a space you could shout across, but the honest kind, hills folding back into hills, a horizon that recedes when you walk toward it. The ground is high and dry and the wind has room to move and does, steadily, combing a grassland that has been grazed and burned and grazed again for longer than anyone has counted. This is a watershed. The top of things. Rain falls here and chooses, by the lean of the land, which way to go — and some of it, a thin secret fraction, leans toward the cracks, finds the seams, goes down the long dark shaft into the hollow to be breathed. The rest runs the other way, into rivers with names, toward a sea. The people up here know only the rivers with names. They do not know the fraction that leans down. They have a word for it anyway, and we will come to the word.

The place is a town, or the bones of one — long and low and built of the same black-grey glittering stone, but old here, the way it was young below. Moss has had its centuries. Rain has rounded every edge the hollow's edges have not yet learned to lose. The stone of the hollow and the stone of the town are the *same broken stone*, one cut fresh and one weathered soft, which is how you know — if you needed knowing — that the town came first and the hollow came after, that the hollow is what the town's foundation became when it cracked open and kept going down. The town is the parent rock. The hollow is the wound in it that learned to breathe.

People live here. Actual people, with eyes they have kept, who look up as a matter of course because the sky is where their weather comes from, who have not been recruited into anything they did not choose. They herd a long-haired animal across the high grass and shear it and weave it and trade the weaving down the named rivers to places with more sun and more money. They are not many. The watershed is poor and the wind is constant and the young leave, going down the rivers and not coming back, the way the water goes down and does not come back, so that the town is always a little emptier than it was, always a few more roofs fallen in, always one more door that opens on nobody. In this the town and the hollow are not so different. Both lose their bodies steadily. But here is the whole difference, the difference that is the point of following the water at all:

When the town loses a body, it grieves.

There is a woman who keeps the highest house, the last house, the one whose back wall is built directly over the great seam where the tubes go down — though she does not know what the tubes are, has never seen a tube, knows only that her cellar is always wet and always faintly warm and that the floor of it hums. She has felt the stroke and the pause and the stroke all her life. Up through the soles of her feet, into the molars, behind the eyes. She thought it was the river underground. Her mother told her it was the river underground. Everyone in the high houses sleeps to it and wakes to it and has folded it so deep into themselves that they no longer hear it, only feel it as a pressure, an ache, a sense — on the bad nights — that something below is keeping time and waiting for them to keep it too.

This is the kinship. This is what runs between the two places, in the same breath as the places themselves: *the same beat reaches both*. The engine that is the hollow's heart-that-is-not-a-heart strokes hard enough that the rhythm climbs the shaft and enters the cellars of the living town, and the people there have been hearing it for generations, and some of them — not many, but some, always the ones the place below would call most *arrived* if it had a word for them — some of them feel the warmth in the wet cellar and find it reasonable, and go down to be near it, and do not come up. The town has a word for this. The word is not a word for death. Death they understand; death goes down the river to the sea like everything else, openly, with a body to bury. This other thing has no body to bury. The person is simply *gone below*, and the family closes the cellar, and mortars over the door, and the high house stands one room smaller forever after, and the wind moves through the grass, and no one says the word above a whisper.

The woman who keeps the last house had a brother.

He heard it worse than she did. He said the river underground had words in it, on the bad nights — a stroke, a pause, a stroke, and in the pause something almost like his own name in his own pulse. She told him it was the river. Their mother had told her it was the river. He went down into the cellar one autumn when the wind had been constant for nine days and the grass lay flat and the sky was the pale exhausted blue that means the year is giving up, and he did not come up, and there was no body, and she mortared the door herself because she could not bear to ask anyone else to do it, and she could feel the warmth on her hands through the new wet stone as she worked, and she understood, in a way she has never said aloud to a living person, that she was not sealing a tomb. She was sealing a mouth. She was closing the lip of something that had just finished eating, the slow way, the patient way, and was already breathing evenly again, already drawing the thin leaning fraction of the rain back down to rise as steam, already teaching some newer, blinder thing to lie in that steam and flatten and hold.

She keeps the last house because someone must keep it. Because if the high houses all empty and the doors all open on nobody, then there is nothing between the seam and the open sky but a few rounded stones, and the rain will lean down freely, and the warmth will rise freely, and the word that is whispered now will not need to be whispered, because there will be no one left up here to whisper it, no one to keep the count of who is above and who is below, no one keeping time *for* the living against the time the dead place keeps — and the two clocks, the one above that grieves and the one below that recruits, will at last be the same clock, and it will keep the hollow's time, because the hollow is patient and the town is only old.

So she stays. She herds the long-haired animals and shears them and sends the weaving down the named rivers to the sun. She keeps her eyes, which look on a world that *does* change — the wind shifts, the grass burns and greens, the young leave and are mourned, the sky lowers and lifts. She has made the opposite bargain to the clingers below: she has refused to let the changelessness in, refused to lie down in the warmth though the warmth is right there under her floor and would be so easy, so reasonable. Every night she feels the stroke find her pulse and every night she gets up and does some small unnecessary thing — moves a chair, banks the fire, speaks aloud to the empty room — to break the tempo, to insist that her time is her own and runs toward morning and not down the shaft. It costs her. You can see it costs her, the way it cost the water-walkers their reserve, except hers is not spent on nothing. Hers is spent on this: that for one more generation the count is kept, the door stays mortared, the word stays a whisper, and the brother stays mourned by someone who refuses to go down and join him though the joining is offered every single night in something almost like his voice.

That is who lives here.

Not yet, the hollow. Not yet.

She keeps the time the other way. She is the second hand that turns *against* the clock — and so long as one body anywhere above the seam keeps turning against it, the two places are still two, and the sound that has nowhere to go but through her goes through her and out the other side, into the morning, into the wind, into the long-haired flock and the weaving and the rivers running bright and open toward a sea that the hollow, breathing in its cold and its dark, an hour old and patient forever, has never heard of and will never reach.
