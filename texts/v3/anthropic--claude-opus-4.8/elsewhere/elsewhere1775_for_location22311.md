The Mouth

Follow the water that the engines refuse. It does not go nowhere — held water still presses, still seeks, and the pressure the Tenders maintain has to be maintained *against* something, some downhill the basin would empty into if the pistons ever stopped. That downhill exists. The river that never finished arriving was, by definition, going somewhere. It is still going there. It has been arriving there the whole time, in the only quantity the engines permit: the seep, the overspill, the thin black thread that escapes past every packed seam and runs out beyond the last bolted walkway, down through the unfinished hills, toward the place the river was always trying to reach.

It reaches it. It has reached it for a very long time. The drop is called the Mouth, and it is the opposite of a held breath. It is the exhalation completed.

You hear it before the land tells you it's coming, which is the first thing — that there is something here to *hear*, that sound returns as a separate fact rather than the single saturating engine-drone you carried in your teeth from the basin. The drone falls away behind the hills and in its place: water moving. Water actually moving, water that goes and does not come back, falling off the broken edge of the world into a gorge so deep the bottom keeps its own weather. The river finishes here. All the arrival the basin would not permit happens at once, all the time, in a roar that is the engine-noise turned inside out — not the sound of holding but the sound of letting go.

The gorge is old. This is the thing that takes a while to understand, the same way the basin took a while: the rocks here are *rounded*. Smoothed. Worn soft and dark and shining by exactly the water the Tenders dammed, by the centuries of seep that escaped them, by all the moments they thought they were preventing and only ever delayed. The young sharp stone of the basin is what the river has not yet had time to touch. Down here is what time does when you let it. The stone is curved like shoulders, like spines, like things that lay down and were polished by the going of the water until they became the shape of going. There is no flat surface anywhere. Everything bends downstream.

And it is bright. Painfully, after the pewter. The haze that hangs forever over the basin streams off the falling water as spray, but here it *moves*, it is caught and flung and lit — the gorge faces the weather and the weather faces the sun, and the spray hangs in the air as rainbows that are never in the same place twice, that build and tear and rebuild as the wind takes them. The light strengthens and weakens. There are hours here. There is a noon that climbs the eastern wall and a dusk that floods the western one red, and a true dark at night when the spray goes silver under whatever moon there is, and a dawn that is unmistakably dawn. The day arrives and finishes and arrives again. Nothing here is held. Everything is in the act of becoming the next thing.

Things grow. Not the careful sparse green of the basin, the fern hoarded in a gutter — here the walls of the gorge are *furred* to the lip with growth, every ledge a riot, vines falling in green ropes a hundred feet toward the spray, broad wet leaves the size of doors turning to follow the moving sun, flowers that open and close on the day's true rhythm, blooming and spending themselves and dropping and rotting and feeding the next, the whole cliff a single slow combustion of living and dying that never pauses because pausing is not on offer here. The smell is enormous: green and rot and wet stone and the mineral cold of falling water, a smell that changes as you descend, that is one thing at the lip and another at the ledges and another in the cold roaring dark at the bottom where the river hits and throws itself white and gathers itself and goes on.

There is a being here too. Of course there is.

It lives at the lip, where the held water finally escapes and falls — lives *in* the falling, very nearly. Call it the Ferrier, though it has no need of a word and has never lacked one, because it speaks, it speaks constantly, to itself and the water and the leaves and anything that comes, in a voice pitched to carry over the roar, a voice that has shaped itself to be heard the way the Tenders' silence shaped itself to be swallowed. Where they went soft and pale and ridged from standing water, the Ferrier is weathered the other way: dark and smoothed and grooved like the gorge-stone, skin worn by spray and sun into something that sheds water rather than soaking it, hands hard and sure and quick. Where the Tenders move slowly, conserving, the Ferrier moves all the time, can't not, leaps the wet rounded rocks barefoot and certain, because here the rule is the opposite rule and the body learns the rule it lives in.

Watch one work. The work is letting go. Where the seep arrives at the lip, slowed and stale and half-dead from its long captivity in the basin, the Ferrier *helps it over* — clears the channels, breaks the little dams of leaf-litter and silt that would pool it, keeps the water moving toward its fall. The opposite of packing a leak with waxed rope. The Ferrier spends its hours making sure that nothing is held, that everything arrives and arrives and finishes arriving and goes. It tends the going the way the Tenders tend the staying, with the same deep wordless competence, the same devotion gone so far into habit it has become the self — but pointed the other direction, toward release instead of equilibrium, toward the completed breath.

And here is the thing that connects them, the thing that lives in the same breath as both places: the Ferrier remembers. That is the asymmetry, the cruelty or the mercy turned the other way. The Tenders preserved the container and lost the contents; they hold a moment whose reason eroded out of them. But the water that reaches the Mouth is *that same water* — the held moment, finally let go, arriving at last at the place it was always going. The Ferrier receives it. The Ferrier has spent its existence receiving, drop by escaped drop, the very thing the Tenders could not bear to release. And in the water, faint, almost gone but not quite, dissolved through all those packed and weeping seams — the moment. The face. The afternoon light. The stillness that someone, once, before the rocks were sharp, could not let move into the next stillness and the next.

The Ferrier knows what it was. Has tasted it, year on year, in the seep — has reassembled it slowly the way the gorge reassembles the river at the bottom of the fall, from the thrown-apart white back into a moving body that goes on. The Ferrier holds the memory the Tenders gave up. Not to keep it. That is the whole difference, the entire distance between the two places said in one stroke: the Ferrier holds the lost moment only long enough to *finish* it, to let it arrive, to send it over the lip into the gorge where it falls and breaks into rainbow and feeds the green combustion of the walls and gathers at the bottom and goes on downstream to become a thousand next things — a flower opening, a leaf rotting, a dawn that is unmistakably dawn.

So they are the two halves of the single act the river started. Upstream, the held breath; downstream, the breath let go. Upstream, the moment preserved and emptied; downstream, the moment emptied and *spent*, which is the only way a moment is ever truly kept. The Tenders pay for stillness with all the moments after. The Ferrier is what the moments after look like when they're finally allowed to come. The engines in the basin run to ensure nothing finishes. The Ferrier exists to ensure everything does.

And the strange thing, the thing that takes the longest to understand: neither knows the other is there. The roar of the Mouth does not carry up over the hills to the basin, and the engine-drone does not carry down. The Tenders cannot imagine that what they grieve has been arriving, safely, beautifully, the whole time, just out of earshot. The Ferrier cannot imagine that the source of the seep is a town of soft pale people standing forever between a machine and its breaking, mourning the exact thing the Ferrier sends laughing over the edge each dawn. The distance between them is one short walk past the last bolted walkway, down through the unfinished hills. An hour, if the heat let you walk it. Nobody walks it. The water walks it, drop by drop, and is the only thing that has ever been in both places.

You could stay here a long time too. The Ferrier would not turn you away — leaps over to meet you, in fact, talking, always talking, would press the cold spray-wet stone under your hand and show you how to break a little dam of leaves and watch the freed water hurry toward the lip and over. And if you stayed long enough you would stop flinching at the roar, and then you would start to hear the day in it, the hours it keeps that the basin lost, and you would learn to read the moving rainbows, and you would forget the held breath you came down out of, and you would tend the going for its own sake, and you would not mistake it for peace, because it isn't peace, it was never peace — it is just the river, finally, arriving.
