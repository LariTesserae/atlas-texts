# The Sounding Shore

Begin with the going. Not arrival — there is no arriving here — but the long unspooling of everything, the way a tide does not so much leave as confess that it was always leaving. This is the first thing you would feel, before you saw anything: that the ground beneath you was in conversation with somewhere lower, that you stood on the upper edge of an enormous letting-go.

You are very low down. Low enough that when the haze burns back you understand the land is the *bottom* of something — a long sloped foreshore of dark sand and sucking mud, ribbed by the passage of water, descending so gradually toward the sea that you cannot find the line where the wet sand ends and the shallow sea begins. The whole strand glistens. It is the country of the in-between, neither solid nor liquid, a place that has agreed to be both badly rather than either well. And it is warm — that is the second surprise, after the first surprise of the cold place far above: here the air is heavy and warm, the warmth of breath let out, of something exhaled at last and not taken back.

This is where the water goes.

Follow the pipes down from the Standing Engine — they descend into the cloud and you lose them, but they do not stop. They continue past the furnace-glow, past the last gantry, down through strata of rock the engine never lifted, and they arrive, finally, *here*, kilometers and a world away, where the mountain's held water is given up at last. You can find the place they surface: a low dark mouth in a bank of clay, rimmed with rust and salt, where water pours out steady and unending into a brackish channel that braids across the flats toward the sea. It is the same water. The engine raised it and held it and condensed it and refused to let it fall — and what overflows the refusal, what even an infinite holding cannot keep, runs here, downhill, freely, for nothing, the way the whole universe runs, and arrives warm.

---

And things grow, but they grow the way things grow that are *used*.

The mudflats are crusted with life that has been eaten and eaten again. Beds of mussels, dark and clustered, their shells chipped and scoured. Wracks and green weeds laid flat in the current, torn and regrown and torn. Worm-casts everywhere, the spiral signatures of buried things that filter and pass and filter. Where the moss far above spread in zoological silence — never browsed, never carried, never spent — here every living surface is gnawed, bored, grazed, fought over. Nothing is whole. Everything is in the middle of being someone's meal, and being a meal is how it goes on.

Because here there are animals.

Listen. This is the loudness of this place, and it is the opposite of the engine's loudness, though they are the same volume. Birds — thousands of them, wading the flats on stilt legs, probing the mud, lifting in sudden clattering sheets when something startles them and settling again further out. Their cries are thin and quarrelsome and constant. Crabs work sideways through the wrack with a dry clicking. Out in the channels, things break the surface and are gone. Where the high place held a single note forever, this place is *all* notes, none of them held, every one of them released the instant it is made — a din of beginnings and endings, hatchings and devourings, the whole shore eating itself and being eaten and calling about it ceaselessly.

Nothing here is in equilibrium. That is the point of it. The tide comes and drowns the flats and the birds fly off and the buried things open; the tide goes and strands the flats and the birds return and the buried things close; nothing balances, everything *takes turns*, and the taking of turns is so constant that from far enough away it might look like balance — but it is not. It is loss, organized into rhythm. It is decay made into a dance so that the dancers do not have to notice they are decaying.

---

Follow the strand and you find the people.

A settlement — low houses of driftwood and tide-bleached plank and the grey clay of the banks, roofed with thatch of the very weed that grows on the flats. It is a poor town and a temporary-looking one, built always just above the highest tide-line and always, you can see, rebuilt — here a wall newer than its neighbors, there a whole house gone, only its hearthstones left to show it stood. The sea takes a few every great storm. The people put them back, somewhere, slightly different.

And the people are *there*. That is the whole difference, the entire distance between this place and the high one, said in a single fact: behind these windows, there is someone.

They are not many. A few hundred, perhaps. They live on the shore's giving and the shore's taking. They dig the mud for the buried things and gather the mussels and net the channels at the turn of the tide, and they eat what they gather the same day because nothing here keeps — the warmth that the engine spends everything to deny is the warmth in which all things spoil, and so the people have never learned to hoard. They have learned, instead, to *spend*. To take the day's food and eat the day's food. To build the house knowing the house will go. To bear children on a shore that will drown some of them, and love the children anyway, fiercely, in the full knowledge — because there is no other knowledge available here — that loving a thing is agreeing in advance to lose it.

They know about the mouth in the clay bank. The warm fresh water that pours from it is the only sweet water they have; their wells are brackish, their rains are salt-flecked off the sea. They drink the engine's overflow. Their children are made of the water the high place could not keep. They have a name for the mouth — they call it, in their tongue, something that translates badly, something like *the Weeping*, or *the Giving*, or most exactly *where the high grief leaks*. They do not know there is a mountain. They do not know there is an engine, or a town of empty lit windows, or a clenched fist of cloud that burns its own warmth to stay still. They know only that sweet water comes from a hole in the ground, steady and warm, and has always come, and that they would die without it.

So they drink, every day, the surplus of a refusal. The little that even infinite holding cannot hold. The God upstream keeps everything and these people live on what overflows the keeping — and they would not understand, if you told them, why the spring is warm. You could say *it is warm because something is burning itself alive to keep a moment that is already gone*, and they would look at you, and shrug, and dip the cup again, because warm water is warm water and the baby is crying and the tide is turning and there is work.

---

Watch them at evening — for here there *is* evening, here the light goes orange and then violet and then out, here the sun crosses and sets and they are glad of its setting because the day's heat was hard and the night's cool is kind. Watch them light their lamps. The lamps are not steady and electric and burning for an absent guest. They are small flames, fish-oil and rush-wick, lit by a hand for a face across a table, and they will go out before morning and be lit again, and one night a particular lamp will be lit for the last time by a particular hand and then that hand will be in the mud with the buried things, and the lamp will be lit by a smaller hand after, differently.

This is the inhabitant of this place, and it is a *who*, many whos, mortal and quarrelsome and brief. But if you wanted, as you wanted for the high place, to name the thing that lives here in the verb — the act that has become an organism — it would not be the keeping.

It would be the spending.

It is the opposite number of the great No upstream. It is a *Yes* the size of a tide — yes to the morning that means the evening, yes to the horizon that means somewhere else, yes to the bird that eats the weed and the storm that takes the house and the loss that is the price and the whole shape of the bargain. It does not hold. It cannot hold; the warmth here forbids it. It can only give and take and give, breathing out where the mountain breathes in, exhaling endlessly the very air the high place strains forever not to release.

And here is the thing the two places do not know about each other, the thing that lives in the breath between them:

They are one motion.

The engine's held breath and the shore's let breath are the in and the out of a single lung. The water the mountain refuses to lose falls here and is drunk and is wept and pissed and bled and given back to the sea and lifted by the warm air into cloud and carried, on the slow continental currents, *up* — up the long sky to the high cold plateau, where it condenses on the weeping stone and is caught by the pipes and held, again, against everything, magnificently, for nothing. The grief upstream that mistook holding for keeping, and the people downstream who know that keeping is impossible and so have learned to keep nothing and love everything — they are the two halves of how a world breathes. Neither is whole. The high place holds a moment that died of being held. The low place loses everyone it loves and goes on loving, and the going-on is the only keeping that ever worked: not the moment preserved, but the loving passed downhill, hand to smaller hand, lamp to relit lamp, mouth to mouth at the warm spring, forever falling and forever caught.

The mountain says *No* with weather.
The shore says *Yes* with children.
And the same water is in both their mouths.
