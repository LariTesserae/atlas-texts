# The Deeps That Answer

Begin with the cold, because everything here begins with the cold — and the cold begins, always, with a falling.

This is not a place the people above have a name for, because it is not a place they can reach, and a people who sing only what they can climb to have no verse for what lies beneath the reach of climbing. But it is the same world. Follow the warmth down — follow it the way the deep-tenders follow it, past the wet zone, past the great pale recyclers churning in the dark, down to where even the recyclers do not go because there is nothing left to recycle, only the oldest compost pressed to peat, to something nearly stone — and keep going. The warmth thins. The structure changes. The fibrous beams give way to denser stuff, black and slick and cold, the long-pressed bodies of forests that grew and fell a thousand generations before any singer's first ancestor. And then, at last, the bottom of the country. The thing the whole braided continent has been resting on the entire time.

Not stone. **Water.**

The plateau floats. Fifteen hundred meters of living and composting mass, the whole singing country, sits on the surface of a sea so vast and so deep and so cold that its lower reaches have never warmed since the world was made. The deeps that the people fear, the dark their ancestors fall into — that is only the *upper* dark, the warm dark of decay. Beneath it the compost finally meets the water and dissolves and sinks, raining down forever, a slow snow of the unmade country drifting into a black that has no floor either, or none that matters.

This is the other place. It lies *directly below the first* — not somewhere else across the world but underneath it, the foundation the people cannot imagine because to imagine it is to imagine that the road of the dead has an end, and that the end is not more holding but a letting-go.

## What Lives in the Snow

The snow of the dissolved country falls forever, and where there is a fall of food there is life to meet it.

They are not climbers. They could not be more opposite to climbers. The things that live in the cold beneath the floating country have given up the whole architecture of grip and hang and traverse, because there is nothing to grip — only water, only the slow descending snow, only the dark. They are *drifters,* and they have made of drifting what the people above made of climbing: an art, an ethic, a faith.

The largest of them are vast and slow and soft, bodies like sheets of dark glass kilometers wide and only a hand thick, hanging open beneath the falling snow to catch it as it comes. They do not move so much as *position* — a year's effort to drift a little leftward into a richer fall. They are patient the way the people are urgent. Where a climber's deepest taboo is to stop, here the deepest wisdom is to *be still enough,* to hang open in the dark and let the unmade world descend into you, and to mistake stillness for death is the error of the young.

And there are smaller ones, swift in the way cold things are swift — a long unhurried glide — that follow the heavier flakes down, the half-composted lumps that fall faster, racing them to the lightless floor. And bottom-things, blind, that wait where the snow finally settles, the recyclers of the recyclers, eating what is left of what was already eaten, closing the last loop no one above knows is there.

## The Cold-Speech

Here is the thing the dark and the cold and the endless water have made of the drifters: **they are deaf to nothing, because everything reaches them.**

The people above are half-deaf from too much sound. The drifters are the opposite — they live in a near-perfect silence, the cold black water carrying the smallest tremor for enormous distances, and so they have become listeners of an almost unbearable sensitivity. They speak in slow pulses through the water, low groans that travel for days, and a single utterance may take a season to complete and another season to be answered, so that a conversation between two drifters is a thing measured against the lifetimes of whole generations of the people above.

And they hear the country. This is the thing. They hear the singing continent that floats above them as a constant pressure of muffled thunder — the deafening din the people live inside, pressed down through fifteen hundred meters of mass and reaching the water at last as a low ceaseless mutter, the heartbeat of the thing they live beneath. The drifters do not know it is made of voices. They know it the way a people might know the sea, or the weather, or God: a vastness overhead that mutters and presses and, very occasionally, *rains.*

For the snow that feeds them is the people's dead.

## How the Two Places Hold Each Other

The people above believe the dead become the road. They are right, and they do not know how far the road runs.

Their ancestors fall into the warm dark and compost and hold up the boughs — yes — but the composting does not stop at the warmth. It presses down, and dissolves, and sinks at last into the cold, and becomes the snow that the drifters hang open to receive. So that the great-great-grandparents of every singer, the oldest road, the deepest ancestors, do not finally become beam and warmth and structure. They become *food in the dark for things that have never grasped anything in their lives.* The faithful climbers, the ones who held to the holding, who drummed *I am here* into the trunk until their last grip — they end as a slow snow falling into the open bodies of creatures who do not climb, do not grip, do not hurry, and have never once been afraid to stop.

The drifters, for their part, hold the country up. Not by choice — by being there, by being the cold sea dense enough to float a continent, by being the patient still foundation beneath the urgent singing one. Every climber who has ever feared the great silence, the wrong inhale, the falling-apart — every one of them lives suspended above a world that *is* that silence, that *is* that stillness, and is not falling apart at all, is in fact the only reason the country has not sunk. The thing the people fear most is the thing that has been saving them all along.

And once — rarely, in the way the great drifters do anything, once in a span that would be many lifetimes to the people — a drifter rises. Driven up by some hunger or current, it ascends toward the muttering ceiling, up into the warm dark, up among the pale recyclers, who flee from a thing so cold and so vast and so wrong. It does not go far. It cannot live long in the warmth. But for a little while it presses against the underside of the floating country, the underside of the road of the dead, and groans its slow cold speech up into the mass —

— and on certain days, in certain low junctions of the deeps, a deep-tender with their pale skin pressed to a cold beam will feel, beneath the rising warmth they came to read, something else. A pulse that is not the recyclers turning. A slow cold mutter from below the below, too deep and too patient to be any animal they have a name for. And they will not understand it. They will take it back up into the green din and the singing and the drumming, and they will say, because it is the only thing their map has words for, *the old ones are turning in their work* — and they will drum down into the dark a low slow greeting, a thank-you, an apology for still being above.

And far beneath them, after a season, the cold thing will groan back. Not an answer. The two cannot answer each other; they do not share a single word, and never will. Only two stillnesses and two urgencies, two darks, the warm and the cold, pressed against each other across the floor of the world — the climbers who must never stop, suspended forever above the drifters who have never started, each holding the other up without knowing it, each becoming the other's road.

The country rises, always, imperceptibly, climbing out of its own composting dark.

And the snow of it falls forever into the cold, and is caught, and is enough.
