The Quarry-Above

Follow the light up. Follow the smoothed flues, surface to depth reversed now, depth to surface, the way a drop of water would go if water went the wrong way — up the threaded seams it came down by, up through the widened stone throats that pour the daylight in, up to where the light is made.

There is a place where the light begins, and it is not a town, and almost no one lives there. It is a slope of broken white stone under an open sky, and the sky is the first thing, because that is what you notice first, and it is too much. After the cave it is always too much. It is a glare without a source you can stand to look at, a brightness that falls from everywhere and pins your shadow to the ground and will not let it go. Down below, the people cast no long shadow and have forgotten how. Up here the shadows are knives. They swing through the day. They are the cave-people's first lesson and their hardest, and it is taught to exactly one of them at a time.

Because someone has to come up. The flues do not clean themselves. The pale stone that lines them, that catches the day and hands it down mirror to mirror into the lit streets — that stone is the same stone the cave is made of, the same that is still deciding its shape in the wet seams below, and it does up here what it does down there: it deposits itself, it accretes, it thickens by a hair. A flue left alone for a generation would close like a healing wound, would scab over with slow mineral and choke the light, and the streets beneath would go to dimness, and the ferns in their troughs would pale and fail, and the faces in the squares would lose each other across the dark. So one comes up. One always comes up. There is a word for the one who comes up, and it is not a proud word; it is the same word they use for the notch carved into the lowest step of an unfinished stair — *continued*. The flue-keeper is a person who means *carry on from here*.

The Quarry-Above is where they live while they keep. A single low house of the same fitted stone, built by flue-keepers for flue-keepers across more lifetimes than anyone has counted, each adding a course, each leaving the mark, and it is the only building in the world down there or up here that stands alone, with gaps around it, with sky on every side and no neighbor's floor for a roof and no one's shelter underfoot. The cave-people find this unbearable to imagine and the flue-keeper finds it unbearable to live and that is rather the point. You are sent up alone because the light must be kept, and you are sent up alone because solitude is the one thing the packed town below cannot teach and someone, every generation, must learn it, so that the town can keep its murmur and its closeness and its arm's-reach belonging — so that the many can stay warm against each other, one has to come out into the cold open and tend the throats that feed them all. It is the loneliest work the people have, and it is the work that makes their togetherness possible, and they know this, and they honor the keeper the way they honor the slow flowstone column rising through three storeys: by making room, by leaving alone, by not quite saying thank you because thank you is too small.

The keeper works the surface stone with the same smoothed hands, the same loose unhurried grip, the same callus along the heel of the palm. But the work is the opposite of the work below. Down there they add; up here the keeper subtracts. Up here, for the only time in the life of the people, the forbidden craft is the necessary one. The keeper scrapes the slow mineral out of the flues. The keeper breaks the accreting stone away before it can close. The keeper is the one member of a people who do not have a word for demolition who spends a life undoing — chipping back the patient deposit, keeping the throat open against the cave's own slow will to seal it, fighting the very accretion that down below is sacred and left alone. Below, you do not build where the stone is still growing. Above, the keeper's whole task is to stop the stone from growing, in this one narrow place, so that the light can fall. It is a heresy performed for love. It is the people's deepest rule broken daily by their most honored hand, and both things are true, and the keeper holds both, alone, in the glare.

And so the keeper learns what the town cannot hold. The keeper learns weather — the wind that has no walls to break it, the rain that comes from above instead of threading down through stone, the cold that is not the cool of constant moisture but a dry biting thing with the sky's whole emptiness behind it. The keeper learns the sun has a place in the sky and the place moves, learns to read the day by where the knife-shadow points, learns the names of the surface plants that crowd the slope in a green so reckless and unprized it would stop a cave-heart still — whole fields of leaf, leaf without measure, leaf gone to waste and seed and rot and more leaf, an abundance so total it reads as a kind of madness to one raised where a single fern is a wealth. The keeper does not eat well of it. The keeper was not raised to surplus and does not trust it and brings down, on the rare descents, only a little — a pressed leaf, a seed, a story no one quite believes. The town listens to the keeper the way you listen to someone who has been somewhere you will never go, with attention and a little fear and a quiet relief that it was not you.

For there is the line between these two places, and it is a person. The town is many and warm and packed and patient, building upward into a vault it cannot see, lit by a sky it cannot see — and the sky it cannot see is *kept*, is *cleared*, is *fought open hair by hair* by one of its own who has gone up and out and alone to do the one forbidden thing so the many can keep doing the sacred ones. The light that pools so evenly in the streets, sourceless and clean, the light you could read a face by — every drop of it has been handed down through a throat that a solitary keeper, knives of shadow swinging past, scraped open that very season against the closing stone. The town's bright quiet hush is purchased, daily, by a loneliness it has agreed not to look at directly. The keeper sends light down. The town sends nothing up but the next keeper, when this one is old, and the old one comes down at last to die among the close warm faces, hands smoothed now to nothing, having spent a life subtracting so that everyone else could add.

And the descent is the thing the keeper holds onto, through all the glaring solitary years. To go back down. To turn sideways through the first narrow gap and feel the sky close gently overhead into known stone. To lose the long shadow at last, to step into light that falls from no single direction and casts nothing sharp, to come into the murmur and the woodsmoke and the arm's-reach belonging and set down the scraping-tool and never lift it again. The keeper carves, before leaving the Quarry-Above for the last time, a notch into the doorframe of that solitary house. *Continued.* Carry on from here. Then walks down into the held breath of the town, into the packed warm dark-that-is-not-dark, and is held up by the people beneath and holds up the people above, and is, for the rest of a short remaining life, simply one more close face in the crowd that does not have to be alone — because, up there, in the glare, on the slope of broken white, someone younger now is learning the knife-shadows, and keeping the throats open, and pouring the day down.
