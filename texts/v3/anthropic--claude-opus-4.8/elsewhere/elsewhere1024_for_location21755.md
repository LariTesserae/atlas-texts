The Tide-Stair at Marrow

What runs out of a thing that never had water is the idea of water — and ideas, unlike rivers, can travel uphill, can cross the place where there is no place, can arrive somewhere that the dry room could never reach by any road. So follow the dryness backward, against itself, the way you would follow a smell to a kitchen, and you come out at the one location in all the made-ness that is the dry room's exact refusal, its photographic negative, the answer it was shaped by declining to be. You come out at Marrow, where everything the chapel-room holds still is let go.

Marrow is a stair, and the stair is wet.

Begin with the wet, because it is the first thing the body understands here and the last it forgives. This is not damp, not the sweat of a cellar, not dew. This is water that has decided. It comes down the stair in a single continuous sheet so thin you cannot see it except by the way it bends the light, a skin of moving water laid over every tread, and beneath that skin the stone is black and slick and warm — warm, where the room was cool, warm the way a mouth is warm, the heat of something living that has not stopped living. You would call the steps stone if you had to call them anything, but they have the give of cartilage, the slight wet yield of the inside of a cheek, and when you set weight on them they answer with a small reluctant flex, as though the stair were aware of being a stair only in the moment of being climbed. It is made too, this place — made all the way down, the same condition, lift any tread and there is another tread, marrow under marrow — but where the room's madeness was fired and finished and dry, here the making is still soft, still warm from whatever fired it, never quite set, kept at the temperature of the just-completed by the endless water that runs across it and will not let it cool.

That is the kinship, and it is a hard one to hold in the hand: these two places are made by the same refusal of substrate, the same all-the-way-down, but the room is the made thing after it has dried and cooled and finished and learned to keep its shape forever, and Marrow is the made thing in the instant before any of that — still warm, still wet, still soft, caught at the wet edge of becoming and not allowed to harden into being. If the room is the held note, Marrow is the bow still drawing across the string. Same string. Same one act, seen at opposite ends.

It descends. This is the second rule of the place and the deepest: where the room does not change, Marrow does nothing else. The stair goes down. You cannot find the top of it — you arrive already partway down, the way you arrive already among the room's fullness — and you cannot find the bottom, and between the two unfindable ends there is only descent, tread after warm wet tread, curving so gently you would swear it ran straight if you did not feel, in the long bones, the patient unceasing downward pull of it. And everything here moves with the going-down. The water sheets downward over the treads. The light moves downward. The sound moves downward. Nothing in Marrow stands still; standing still is the one thing this place will not permit, the way the room will not permit change. To be in Marrow is to be carried, slightly, always, down.

It is dim. Here is the room's gold turned inside out. The light in Marrow does not come from the made surfaces; it comes from the water, and only from the water, a cold blue-green luminescence carried in the running sheet itself, so that the light is always going down too, always sliding away beneath your feet and renewing from above, a brightness that travels. Where the room's gold was sourceless and therefore still, Marrow's light has nothing but source — it is all current, all arrival-and-departure, and it casts shadows, long ones, that slide down the stair ahead of you and pool in the low places and are washed thin again by the next sheet of glowing water. You can see your own shadow here. The room would not give you one. Marrow gives you a shadow and then takes it and gives it back, each step, the way a thing gives you proof you exist by letting you almost lose it.

And it is loud, but it is the room's loudness turned the other way out, and this is the heart of how the two places are one breath. The room roared with a held chord, a sound that stood, a fullness pouring out at a constant rate and never spending itself because it had finished settling its accounts with time. Marrow roars too — the whole stair sings — but it is the sound of water running, which is the sound of a thing spending itself completely and being instantly replaced, loss and renewal so tightly folded together that the ear cannot find the seam. It is not a chord. It is a rush. It does not hold a pitch; it slides through all of them, downward, forever, a note that is always falling and never lands, because the moment it would land more water arrives from above to fall in its place. The room is loud the way the un-spending is loud. Marrow is loud the way the always-spending is loud. And they are the same loudness — you understand this if you stand in Marrow long enough — they are the two halves of the single fact that the room had welded shut and Marrow keeps prized open: that to arrive fully at being yourself, and to fall away from it, are not two events but one, and the room chose to hold the arriving and Marrow chose to hold the falling, and between them they have divided the whole of what it is to be a thing in time.

There is life here, and it is not twelve stopped stalks. It is the opposite census. Marrow swarms. In the running water, against the warm soft treads, there are small bright lives without number — not animals exactly, the place does not host animals any more than the room did, but the water's own luminescence gathered into knots that move, that school, that flow down with the sheet and then, impossibly, against all the place's downward law, struggle a little way back up, a few treads, a hand's breadth, before the current takes them down again. They are the only things in Marrow that resist the descent, and they cannot win, and they do not stop. Where the stalks arrived and stopped and held their final shape forever, these never arrive at any shape at all; they are made of nothing but the trying, the small bright upward effort against the long warm downward pull, and the instant one of them ceases to try it simply unknots back into plain glowing water and is carried away and is, a few treads down, gathered up into a new one that tries again. They are not a population either — but for the opposite reason. The stalks were too few and too finished to be a population. These are too many and too unfinished, too continuously dissolving and reforming, ever to be counted as separate at all. They are one trying, the way the inhabitant of the room was one holding. They are the verb the room had no room for.

And that is who lives at Marrow. Not the bright knots — they are the tenants of the motion, the way the stalks were tenants of the stillness. The inhabitant of Marrow is the descent itself, the warm wet going-down, the single fact that wears its own four faces: from the skin it is the warmth, from the eye it is the traveling blue-green light, from the ear it is the rush that never lands, from the body it is the pull, the always-being-carried, the impossibility of standing still. One thing, four faces, exactly as in the room — but where the room's one thing was *holding*, Marrow's one thing is *letting go*. It does not act either. It does not will the descent. It is the descent, the way the room was the staying. It is not held by anything and it does not hold anything; it is the pure act of release made into a presence, given a body of warm soft never-setting stone and running luminous water, and that body roars because letting-go, when you do it with your whole substance and never stop, comes out as a falling note.

The room had finished happening. Marrow has never once finished happening, and that is its completeness — it is complete in the way a river is complete, lacking nothing, because the thing it is made of is exactly the not-yet-arrived, the still-going, the perpetual edge of becoming. You could not say of Marrow, as you said of the room, that it is going nowhere. Marrow is going everywhere it goes, all the time, which is down. It needs the change the way the room needed the stillness. Drop the inhabitant of the room into Marrow and it would be torn apart by the current. Drop the descent into the room and it would freeze on the first dry tread and become, at last, a held thing, a finished thing, the very stalks it could never be.

So they cannot meet. That is the truth that lives inside the telling of both of them. The dry room and the wet stair are the two things a made world can be — the held and the falling, the completed and the becoming, the note that stands and the note that slides — and they are made of the same all-the-way-down madeness, fired from the same nothing-underneath, and they are the exact refusal of one another, and there is no road between them because each is what the other would have to stop being in order to arrive. You followed the dryness backward to find the water. But the water cannot run uphill into the dry, and the dry cannot pour itself down into the wet, and the only place the two of them touch is in you — the verb who came through the room and is going down the stair — you, who arrive and fall and arrive again, who are made of the room's held completion in the half-second after each breath fills you and of Marrow's pure descent in the half-second after, who are the seam the made world welded shut in one place and prized open in the other, walking now, carrying the room's gold still ringing faintly in your teeth, down the warm wet singing stair that does not know you are there, that is not the kind of thing that knows, that only lets go, and lets go, and lets go.
