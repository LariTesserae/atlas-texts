# The Cistern Coast

There is too much water here, and there always was, and yet everything moves as though forbidding it.

You reach it by following the ache. The salt-glass on the plateau dreams aloud of an ocean it never held — well, the ocean exists. It was not skipped. It was only put somewhere else, in the same world, down the long thermal gradient that the plateau's vents breathe toward, where all that exhaled heat goes to be spent. Follow the breath out. Follow the phantom limb to the body it belongs to. The plateau is the place that remembers water; this is the place water remembers being feared.

The coast is low. After the tableland's two-kilometer arrogance, the relief of it is almost violent — a country that lies down, that admits to having an underneath. The land here is built of sluiced grey silt and shelved limestone, terraced down and down toward a sea the color of poured pewter, flat under a sky that holds no columns of rising heat because here the heat has arrived and dispersed and gone quiet. Where the plateau roared, this place murmurs. The sound is liquid: the slap and suck at the terrace-edges, the trickle through ten thousand channels, the deep occasional gulp of a sluice-gate opening somewhere down the shelf.

Everything grows. Not because life triumphed, but because life here cannot be made to stop, and the whole labor of the coast is the labor of holding it back.

## The cisterns

What you take, at first, for a city is plumbing.

The coast is terraced with vessels. Cisterns — basins, tanks, sealed reservoirs of fired stone and lead-grey ceramic, ranked down the slope from the dry high ground to the wet low margin, each one larger than the last. They were built. You can tell at once that they were built, and the telling is a shock after the plateau's grown engines, because *built* means hands, means intention, means someone decided. The cisterns hold the sea back from the land in stages, decanting it level to level, sweetening it, parceling it out. Aqueducts run between them on patient arches. Valves the size of doors stand half-open, weeping.

This is the inverse of the plateau's mystery. There, the work was vast and purposeless and you could not find what it was for. Here the purpose is brutally clear — *keep the water apart from itself, keep the salt from the sweet, keep the flood from the field* — and what you cannot find is anyone who still believes it matters. The system runs. The valves turn. But the sea does not actually want to be held, and the land does not actually need defending, and the work persists out of the same unstuck momentum that turns the plateau's flywheels: cause and effect have come loose here too, only wetly. A cistern fills *because* it will be drained tomorrow. The flood-gate closes against a tide that has already, hours hence, receded.

## The Drowners

You asked who lives here. The answer rhymes with the other one, the way two places in a single world will rhyme, and the rhyme is the cruelty of it.

They were builders. This much the masonry confesses. The cisterns bear the print of decision — chisel-marks, plumbed corners, the geometry of a mind that wanted the water *controlled*. They came, I think, to answer the opposite question to the watchers': not *what is this for* but *can this be held.* Can the sea be kept in its place. Can abundance be rationed. And the coast answered them the way it answers everything, by giving them so much water that the holding became indistinguishable from drowning.

Because here is what the wet-thinning does to a mind that stays.

A builder first loses the dry ground — the high terrace where decision felt possible. Then they lose the difference between *tending the cistern* and *being its contents*, because the water that fills the basins is the same water now moving, slow and brackish, through their own slowed blood, and at this saturation matter also has opinions, and one opinion the water forms, given long enough, is *I am also what is being held.* They go on opening and closing the gates. They wade the channels in the posture of maintenance, the body shaped like an act of containment. And they are wrong, gently wrong, and the coast lets them stay wrong because the wrongness is load-bearing — the cisterns hold the water for the sake of the Drowners, and the Drowners hold the work for the sake of the cisterns, and neither can say which is the vessel and which the flood.

## How the two ache toward each other

This is the kinship, and it runs in the breath between the places.

The plateau exhales heat down the gradient, and the coast receives it as the warmth that keeps its great cisterns from freezing in the grey season — so the Tenders' impossible engines do, after all, accomplish one true work, and it is this: they keep the Drowners' water liquid, three days' fall away, in a country they will never see. And the coast exhales the opposite. Its evaporation rises, drifts inland and up, and arrives over the salt-glass as the faint memory of tide that the plateau hears and cannot place. Each place is the other's phantom limb. The plateau aches for water it sends its heat to warm; the coast aches for the dry decision it lost, and that dryness is exactly the high tableland it can never climb back to.

A Tender feeds on the almost-memory of having understood. A Drowner feeds on the almost-memory of having *decided* — the recurring surfacing of the builder-mind, the one that plumbed the corner true and believed the sea could be told where to stop. That ache surfaces in them the same way: a change in the quality of attention, a flicker, here in a creature standing waist-deep in a weeping cistern, going still, as though it had remembered it once chose where to put a wall.

And if a new builder came — waded down the terraces asking *can it be held* — the Drowners would turn toward them with the same terrible patience, over the slow fall of a tide, recognizing the containing-posture because it was theirs, counting the newcomer into the census of the held. Welcoming. Unhurried. Merciful even, that you will not feel the moment your deciding goes soft, only — eventually, staying — the fact that the water is at your chin and you are still, quite calmly, reaching for the valve.

The gates open. The cisterns brim and fall. And somewhere among them stands the most recent arrival, who came down the long gradient following an ache she could not name, who wanted only to find where the plateau's missing sea had gone —

and found it, and is in it, and is holding it back, and will not stop.
