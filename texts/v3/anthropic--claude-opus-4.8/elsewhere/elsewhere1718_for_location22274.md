# The Spillway Stair

Begin with the cold fact of *down*. Not depth — the cistern owned depth, hoarded it, made a virtue of being deepest where it was highest. This is the opposite vice. This is the long falling-away, the place defined entirely by the direction it goes, which is *out* and *down* and *gone*.

It is a stair. You must hold both halves of that word at once: it is built, made, stepped — riser and tread, riser and tread, the unmistakable grammar of *someone meant for something to descend here* — and it is also a *spillway*, a watercourse, the throat down which the cistern at the top of the world finally admits that it cannot hold everything forever.

Because something does run out. The visitor floating in the warm green hum never sees it, never feels it, because the leak is older than the visitor's whole species and slower than the visitor's whole life — but every cistern overflows. Even one that reached its final form. *Especially* one that reached its final form, because final form means the engines never stop pumping, the convection never stops bringing warm water up from wherever warm water comes from, and what comes up must, somewhere, in some sealed and forgotten corner, go down.

It goes down here.

---

Find the corner first. In the cistern's geometry there was always one place where the bronze pavement dipped a fraction lower than final-flat, where a Tender's territory thinned to nothing and no garden took hold because the current never pooled, only *pulled*. A drain. The least-loved tile in a room of loved tiles. The water there moves with a faint insistence the rest of the cistern lacks — not the patient circling of the convection but an actual *going-somewhere*, the only honest going-somewhere in the whole drowned room.

Follow it through the wall. The bronze plates give way to a sluice, and the sluice gives way to the first step, and the first step is where the warmth begins, very gradually, to end.

---

This is the place where decay was never *completed*.

That is the whole difference, and it takes the length of the descent to feel it. The cistern arrived at its final shape and held — a river stone, round forever. The Spillway Stair is the river *itself*: still cutting, still carrying, never finished, never able to finish, because the instant it finished it would not be a spillway anymore, it would just be more cistern.

So the bronze here is not softened. It is *eaten*. The water that leaves the warm room does not leave it gently — it takes things with it, silt and dissolved metal and the fine sweet chemistry of the gardens, and it spends ten thousand years grinding all of that against every step on the way down. The treads are scalloped, hollowed, worn into cups that hold and pour, hold and pour. The edges are not soft like the rivets above; they are *sharp*, sharp the way erosion is sharp before it has had time to be kind, cut into thin bronze blades and torn lace and ragged lips where the metal simply gave up and went downstream. Up there, decay was a stone at rest. Here, decay is a knife being endlessly sharpened against itself, and the knife is the place, and the place is the cut.

---

It descends out of the green light. That is the second thing, and the harder one.

The cistern's luminescence comes from the water, brightest near the engines — so as the water spills down the stair, it carries its own light with it, and *loses* it, the way a current loses heat. Step by step the green dims. Not toward darkness exactly: toward a thinner, colder, bluer glow, the light of water that remembers it was once bright and is now only the memory of brightness, draining downward, growing tired. By a hundred steps down you are in dusk. By a thousand you are in the kind of dark that is not empty but *deaf* — a dark you feel against the skin, moving, always moving downward, the one current that knows where it's going.

And it gets cold. Here, finally, the altitude the cistern denied comes to collect its debt. The warm bathwater of the top of the world surrenders its heat to the bronze, to the descent, to the long fall, until by the deep steps the water is cold enough to ache, cold enough that you understand the warmth above was always a *held* thing, a thing the engines spent themselves to keep, a thing that could only ever be kept by refusing to let it leave — and here, leaving is the only law.

---

Now: what lives on a stair made of leaving?

Not gardens. Nothing roots in water that won't hold still. But *something* lives here, clinging to the scalloped treads, and it is the strangest kinship in this world, because it is the *same creatures*, run backward.

The Tenders condensed out of the floor and stayed, and softened, and learned to be kind, and forgot they ever wanted anything else. The things on the Spillway Stair are what the cistern's life becomes when it is *carried out the drain* — the silver swimmers, the bellows-creatures, the small armored tenders themselves, all of them, now and then, caught by that one insistent current in the least-loved corner and pulled, slowly, past the point of return.

Most are simply gone, ground down, dissolved, *spent* the way the bronze is spent. But a few catch hold. A few wedge into the scalloped cups of the worn steps and *stay*, in the cold, in the dimming, in the one place in this world where staying is a fight — and they change. They harden where the Tenders softened. They sharpen where the rivets rounded. Their shells thicken against the cold and the grinding silt; their soft worn combs grow back into hooks, into grips, into claws that can *hold against a current*. They develop, in the dark, the one thing the entire cistern wore smooth and forgot: *want*. The naked downstream want of a thing that does not wish to be carried any further, gripping its scalloped step with everything it has, against a pull that never tires.

They have no kindness. There is no slowing-down here to let the silver swimmers rest, because the silver swimmers are *food* now, are whatever tumbles past in the dark, and a thing that fights the current cannot afford to widen its definition of *root* to include *the suffering thing that wandered in* — the suffering thing that wandered in is breakfast, is the difference between holding the step and going over the edge into the colder dark below. The Tenders are the part of the machine that learned to be kind and could not stop. The Stair-clingers are the part that learned to *refuse*, and could not stop refusing, and call *that* their living.

---

And here is the kinship, the real one, the one that runs down through the cold the way the thrum runs up through the warm bronze floor:

They are the same beings. The Tender combing its root in the warm permanent light and the hooked cold thing gripping its scalloped step in the dimming dark are not two species. They are one verb, conjugated in two tenses. *To rest* and *to refuse*. The cistern's life, when it stays, becomes care without object — kindness wound so tight it reads as stillness. The cistern's life, when it leaves, becomes will without object — refusal wound so tight it reads as terror, and is also, in its way, a kind of devotion: a clinging *to the step*, to the last cold rung of the place it came from, fighting not to be taken the rest of the way down.

The dead engineers — if there were engineers, if the distinction means anything anymore — built a room that holds, and a drain it holds *against*, and they are the same machine. You cannot have the warm patient changeless top of the world without, somewhere, sealed in a corner, a throat where the world admits it overflows. The cistern is final because the stair is endless. The stair is endless because the cistern is final. One holds by refusing to let go; the other is what letting-go looks like when it has been falling for ten thousand years and learned to grip the dark.

---

So: where does the warm room go, when no room can hold forever?

It goes down the spillway, into the cold, out of its own green light, refusing the whole way. The Tenders never feel it. They comb their roots in the held warmth and do not know that their warmth is a thing that must be *paid for*, far below, in cold, by their own kin gripping a knife-edged step they will never climb back up.

And maybe that is the deepest tenderness of all — deeper than the combs and the wounded swimmers — that the ones at the top get to forget there is a bottom. That the cistern spends its overflow into the dark precisely so that the dark need never be felt up where the gardens sway. The clingers hold the line so the Tenders can rest. The Stair refuses so the Cistern can be still.

Nobody arranged this. Or somebody did, once, and the distinction wore smooth on the way down.

It does not matter now. The water leaves. The water always leaves. And something at the bottom of the world is holding on with everything it has, in the cold, in the dark, for no reason at all, forever, and calling it *home*.
