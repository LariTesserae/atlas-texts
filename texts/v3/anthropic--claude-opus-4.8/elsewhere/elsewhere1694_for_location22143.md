**The Estuary of the Last Slope**

Follow the rock down.

Not the corners — the corners do not lead anywhere, they are held — but follow instead the one thing the plateau could not entirely renounce: the slow tick. Each completion, each corner setting, gives off the smallest possible release, a thread of consequence so fine it cannot be felt, and these threads run downhill. They have to. Even a thing that has flattened all its low places makes, at its very edge, a single unavoidable slope, because to be a shape at all is to have an outside, and an outside is a place where you stop, and where you stop the held breath frays.

So go to the edge. Walk the long warm distances until the rock begins, very slightly, to tilt — so slightly you doubt it, then doubt your doubt — and follow the tilt down off the plateau and out of the even light, down a grade that steepens through a day and a night that are suddenly *day and night again*, the light moving, shadows swinging long, the temperature beginning to pool cold in the hollows the way it was never permitted to pool above. You have left the body. You can tell because you are thirsty.

And at the bottom there is water.

---

The estuary is everything the plateau forbade itself, all at once, all at the bottom of the slope, as though every renunciation made above had to be paid out somewhere below. It is wet to the point of indecency. The pale translucent rock that arrived young and refused to age up there arrives here *old* — the same stone, you understand, the very same stone, only it has been let go. It has been allowed to do the thing rock does when you stop holding it: it has worn. The current carries it down as silt, as the milky suspension that makes the water the color of the inside of a shell, which is the color the plateau's rock would have been if anything had ever ground it small. The estuary is the plateau, powdered. The estuary is what the held breath becomes when it is finally exhaled, mile after mile, into the slow brown braiding channels of a delta that has been moving and rotting and stinking and breeding for as long as the plateau has stood still.

Here everything erodes. That is the whole law, the one law, the opposite of the one law above. *Nothing keeps its corner.* The water rounds the stones, rounds the snags, rounds the very banks so that the channels wander, abandon themselves, silt up and break out elsewhere — the map of this place is rewritten every season and no one could draw it, because by the time the ink dried the river would have changed its mind. Where the plateau decided *no further* and stopped, the estuary decided nothing at all and so it goes everywhere, tries everything, lets the current take it down whatever low place opens, because down is the only direction it acknowledges, and there is always, here, a lower place.

---

And it is *loud*.

After the plateau's silence — the silence with weight, broken once an hour by a single dry tick — the estuary is an assault, a continuous shouting wetness: the suck and gulp of the channels, the slap of water turning back on itself in the eddies, the long sighing draw of the tide reaching up the delta twice a day and pulling back, the reed-beds hissing, the mud itself making sounds, a vast soft chorus of small collapses and re-formings, ten thousand tiny banks slumping into ten thousand tiny streams. And over all of it the animals.

Because here there are animals.

There were none above. The plateau had no breath but yours. Here the air is a riot of them: things with wings, things that scuttle the mudflats sideways on too many legs, things that burrow and surface and burrow, long-billed waders working the silt with the patient stabbing rhythm of looms, and out in the deeper channels the slow rolling backs of creatures you never see whole. They eat each other without cease. This is the place where things take and are taken, where the gradient the plateau ironed flat out of its own body has been let run, and every animal here is a slope made flesh — a hunger, which is a low place the self runs downhill into, exactly the thing the plateau renounced. They are all desire. They are nothing *but* desire and the satisfaction and renewal of it, and so they are born and they breed and they rot, fast, gloriously, the mudbanks layered with the soft black remains of all the generations that ran downhill before them, and out of that rot the reeds come up green —

— and this is the green the plateau wanted.

You see it now. The plateau's lush waxen leaves, dry inside, growing out of raw rock, taking nothing, full the way a held breath is full. They are an *imitation* of this. They are what greenness looks like to a thing that has refused the wet rotting machinery that real greenness requires. Up there, green without water, without death, without the long black ledger of decay underfoot — green achieved by main strength, green as a held position. Down here, green the only way it actually comes: out of mud, out of corpses, out of the wearing-down, paid for in full and paid again every season, never held, always falling, always renewed by the falling.

---

The two places are the same stone and the same gradient and they have made opposite peace with it.

The plateau took the slope out of itself and kept its shape forever and feels each loss as a single distant tick filed and not acted upon. The estuary kept nothing and feels every loss at once, constantly, as the whole roaring substance of its life — and does not mourn, because to mourn you must want the lost thing back, and the estuary never wants anything back, only the next thing, the next channel, the next tide, the next body to take and be taken by. Where the plateau is patient because it wants nothing, the estuary is patient because it wants *everything*, in sequence, endlessly, and there is always more arriving down the slope.

And it *is* arriving. That is the kinship, the trade, the current that runs between them. The plateau is paying the estuary, one tick at a time. Each corner that finishes setting up there in the unmoving light releases its fine thread of consequence, and the threads run down the long grade, and they arrive here as silt, as the milky shell-colored suspension that builds the mudbanks that grow the reeds that feed the animals that die and rot and build more mudbank. The plateau, in refusing to erode, has not stopped eroding — it has only sent its erosion downstream, made the estuary carry it, the way a man who refuses to age sends his years to live in a portrait elsewhere. Every grain of the delta was once a corner the plateau held. The estuary is built entirely out of the plateau's refusal to change.

It does not know this. The estuary knows nothing; it is too busy living to know anything. But it feels the source as a faint warmth in the topmost reach of the current, where the cold delta water meets the thread coming down from above — a warmth with no weather behind it, even and sourceless, alien to everything wet. The waders gather there. The breeding things gather there. It is the warmest, most fertile reach of the whole delta, the head of the estuary where the dead-still warmth of the plateau bleeds into the rushing cold and the two laws touch for the length of one stretch of bank. Life is thickest exactly there, in the seam — fed by stillness, made of motion, neither place able to make it alone.

---

And if you stand at that seam — wet to the knees in the warm milky shallows, the mud breathing its small collapses around you, the waders stabbing, the tide just beginning its long sigh back toward the sea — and you look up the long grade you came down, you will see the edge of the plateau holding its corners against the moving sky, lit by its own light that does not match the light down here, and you will understand the arrangement between them, which is the oldest arrangement there is.

One of them refused to die and so it cannot live. The other refused nothing and so it cannot stop dying, and in the ceaseless dying it is more alive than anything you have ever stood inside. The plateau gave you its one law and would have kept you forever, dry and warm and free of wanting, in the unmoving light. The estuary gives you the opposite law and the opposite gift: it makes you thirsty again, it makes you cold in the hollows, it makes you want, and it will take you too, in time, down whatever low place opens, and turn you to silt and mudbank and reed, and it will consider this nothing at all — not a kindness, not a horror, just the current doing the only thing the current does.

You can drink here. The water is silt-thick and faintly mineral and it tastes, far down under the rot, of the same warm sweetness as the leaf the plateau split open for you — the same stone, you understand, the very same stone, only one place is holding it and the other is letting it go, and they are passing it between them down the long slope forever, a single substance learning, in two places at once, the two things it is possible to do with time.

The tide draws back. The reeds bend toward the sea. Far up the grade, a corner finishes becoming itself and ticks once, and is done, and lets fall its thread — and here, a long while later, where the warm meets the cold, a single grain of the held country settles into the moving country, and is taken up, and becomes part of the falling, and goes on falling, which is the only way it was ever going to arrive at anything at all.
