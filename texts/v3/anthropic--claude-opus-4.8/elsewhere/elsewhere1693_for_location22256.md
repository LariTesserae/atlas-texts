# The Drylands Below the Spring-Line

Follow the water out. That is the only honest way to leave the hollow — not over the rim, not back down the climbing-path, but along the seam the water takes, because the water leaves too, eventually, for all that it seems to hang and circle forever. The cupped pools overflow, finally, after the long minutes of the cycle, and the overflow threads down through a crack in the eastern saucers that no listener ever notices because its note is too low and too far. Down through the warm stone. Down past the place where the heat lives. Down until the warmth gives out and the rock goes honest and cold, and then down further, into the dark inside the mountain, where the water that wept so musically becomes only water, falling in the dark with no tuned pool to catch it and no one to hollow a stone beneath it.

It travels a long time. It cools. It forgets it was ever warm.

And then — far out, and far down, where the mountain has flattened into something that is no longer mountain — it comes out.

---

Here is the place. Call it the Spring-Line, though no one who lives here calls it anything, because to name it you'd have to stand on both sides of it at once, and no one does.

It is a long, low cliff of pale stone, perhaps the height of three men, running off in both directions further than the eye follows. Not dramatic. Weathered — but weathered *dry*, which is a different weathering than the hollow's: not the patient rounding of water but the flaking, cracking, sun-split fracture of stone that has been baked and frozen and baked again, ten thousand times, with nothing soft to cushion the swing. The cliff is the lip of the world's largest cup turned upside down. The water that fell through the dark inside the mountain has run all this way underground, pressed flat between layers of stone, and here — where the layers tilt up and break the surface — it is squeezed out into the light.

It does not weep. It *seeps*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of this place.

In the hollow, water emerged from everywhere, generous, multiplied, loud. Here it emerges along a single line — a dark band low on the pale cliff, a hand's width of perpetual dampness where the rock changes color, and below it a fringe of green so thin and so fierce it looks less like growth than like a wound that won't close. The water comes out drop by counted drop, and every drop is *spent immediately* — drunk by a root, lapped by a tongue, or simply taken by the air, which here is not a closed greenhouse circuit but an open mouth, vast and dry and always thirsty, that swallows moisture the instant it shows itself and gives nothing back.

This is the strangeness, the one that earns the word, and it is the exact inverse of the hollow's:

**Here, nothing is absorbed. Everything is taken.**

The hollow's rule — *nothing is added that the place does not absorb* — runs backward across the Spring-Line like water running backward up a stem. Down here, nothing is *given* that the place does not immediately *consume*. The two halves of the same water, the same world, keeping opposite rules. Up there, addition without subtraction: every reed taken into the cycle, every note kept, the place thickening on itself across the ages, becoming more thoroughly itself by losing nothing. Down here, subtraction without rest: every drop spent the instant it arrives, nothing pooling, nothing keeping time, no reservoir to hold a rhythm in. The hollow remembers everything. The Spring-Line cannot afford to remember anything at all.

---

The light here is not ordinary. That mercy was the hollow's. Here the light is an event, a daily violence — it comes up over the flat dry distance with no steam to pearl it, no fronds to filter it, and it lies on the pale stone all day like a weight. By noon the cliff throws heat back up at the sky and the air directly above the seam-line shimmers, and the green fringe pulls itself smaller, leaves rolling shut, every living thing here practiced in the art of taking less. The color is the opposite of the hollow's unbearable saturation: everything is bleached toward bone, pale gold, pale gray, the thin green of the seam looking almost black by contrast, almost too much, a single saturated stroke on a washed-out page.

And it is not loud. This is the thing that would unmake a hollow-dweller fastest of all. Where the hollow rang like a struck bell across the ages, the Spring-Line is *silent* — not the silence of nothing happening but the silence of everything happening too slowly and too sparingly to make a sound. A drop seeps from the dark band, hangs, and is gone into a root or the air before it could ever fall far enough to land in a pool and ring. There are no tuned saucers here. There is nothing for water to strike, because water never gets to be that free, never gets to fall, never gets the small luxury of making a note before it is needed. In the hollow, water was so abundant it could afford to be music. Here it is so scarce it can only ever be *use*.

The quiet is total and it is the loudest fact about the place.

---

But the green is fierce, and where there is fierce green there is fauna, and here — opposite again — the animals are not rare. They are *many*, and quick, and constant, because the Spring-Line is the only water for further than any of them can travel in a day, and so everything that lives within reach lives *here*, pressed into the thin green wound, taking turns at the seam the way the hollow's pools took turns at the cycle.

There is a turn-taking here too. That is the thing that makes me say it is the same world and not merely a far one. Watch the seam-line through a day and you will see it: the small dawn things, the long-legged flies and the pale lizards, drinking first while it is cool; then the burrowing things at the heat of the day, coming up from the shaded under-rock to lap and dart back; then at dusk the larger animals, the ones that have walked through the dry from somewhere even further, standing along the seam in a loose row, drinking in an order no one taught them, an order kept by need instead of by tuned stone. A cycle. Long — a day, not minutes — and never quite identical, but unmistakably a cycle. The Spring-Line meters its water too. Not by warmth and resonance agreeing across the ages, but by thirst arranging itself, by the thousand small lives that have learned without learning when to come and when to wait.

Two places, two cycles, the same patience underneath — one built of plenty, one built of want, both turning over and over, neither in a hurry, because both have already arrived at the only shape they can hold.

---

And there is someone here. Of course there is. The water carried more than warmth down through the dark.

Not a tender. The word does not survive the journey. Here, where nothing pools and nothing keeps, you cannot tend — there is no reservoir of care to draw from, no slow afternoon in which to hollow a stone, no surplus of anything to give. What the Spring-Line has instead is a *Keeper of the Order*, and the difference between tending and keeping order is the difference between the two halves of the world.

You would see this one. That is the first inversion. The Tender, slowed past the reach of being seen, dissolved into the cycle, gone before they resolved into a shape — but the Keeper of the Order is *sharp*, fast, resolved, present every moment along the seam-line, because the Spring-Line cannot afford a being that slows into it. The hollow absorbed its inhabitant. The Spring-Line would consume one whole, drink them down like every other drop, unless they stayed quick enough and separate enough to remain. So the Keeper holds the line *against* the place. Draws exactly the line the Tender gave up — *here is me, and there is the place* — and holds it like a held breath, every day, all day, because to stop holding it is to be taken.

They are thin. Everything here is thin. They drink at the seam in their own turn, and they keep the turns — not by force, there is no force in them, but by being the one steady presence at the water who has learned the whole order and embodies it, so that the small dawn things and the burrowing things and the dusk-walkers arrange themselves partly around the simple fact of the Keeper already being there, already drinking, already moving on. A craft, this too. Pre-metal, hand-and-presence. But where the Tender's craft was self-erasing, the Keeper's is self-asserting, and has to be, every single day, with no day's keeping carried over into the next, because the place keeps no memory and so the order must be re-made each dawn from nothing.

---

And they are lonely. I have to say it, because it is the truest opposite of all.

The Tender was kept company by the cycle, slowed past the reach of loneliness, not alone because they had stopped drawing the line that separates a self from a place. The Keeper has to draw that line to survive, and the line is exactly the thing that makes them alone. They cannot dissolve into the company of the seam, the way the Tender dissolved into the hollow, because dissolving here means dying. So they stand at the water, fast and separate and seen, surrounded by a thousand drinking lives and joined to none of them, keeping an order that no one thanks them for and that vanishes every night and must be raised again by morning. Loneliness is a fast feeling, I said before. The Spring-Line is made of fast feeling. The Keeper feels all of it, every day, and goes on keeping the order anyway, because keeping is simply what they have become.

I do not think the Keeper knows about the Tender. How could they? The water that joins them traveled the whole dark inside of the mountain to get here and arrived stripped of everything but itself, cold, forgetful, music gone. The Keeper drinks the hollow's overflow every morning and has no way to taste the cycle it kept, no way to hear the tuned saucers in it, no way to know that the same water, a long way up and a long time ago, was so abundant it got to be a song. They only know it is enough to live on. Barely. Drop by counted drop.

---

But the water knows. The water is the kinship, the current, the thing that runs out of the one place and finds the other, and it carries the connection whether or not either keeper can read it.

And so here is the last thing, the thing that lets you set them down side by side and call it one world. If you could follow a single drop — if you could be slow enough at the top to be the Tender and fast enough at the bottom to be the Keeper, both at once, which no one is — you would understand that the two of them are doing the same work from opposite ends. The Tender helps a place become more thoroughly itself by keeping everything. The Keeper helps a place stay alive at all by spending everything. One sings because it can afford to. One is silent because it cannot. And the water passes from the one to the other, warm to cold, music to use, surplus to need, in a single long falling current through the dark heart of the mountain that neither end will ever see whole.

If you stayed at the Spring-Line — really stayed, past the point where the heat becomes ordinary and the order becomes legible — you would not, one day, find yourself unable to pick out your own note in the roar. There is no roar here, and nothing is kept long enough to lose. Instead you would find yourself, each dawn, raising the order again from nothing, fast and separate and seen, and you would understand that the keeping had passed to you, and that it was always going to, and that there was always a *who* here, sharp and lonely and necessary, holding the line at the only water in the world.

Only the long, dry, thirsting, silent work of a place staying barely alive — and the one steady presence at the seam, whosever it is now, that keeps the turns so that everything gets to drink.
