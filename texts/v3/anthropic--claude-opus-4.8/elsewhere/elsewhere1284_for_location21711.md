# The Valley Floor

Follow the water down.

Not the mist — the mist never arrives, that is its whole nature, hurled outward and unraveled into nothing somewhere in the kilometers of empty air. Follow instead the little that survives. The fraction of every cataract that is not shredded, the heaviest drops that fall fastest and clump and find each other in the wind's lee, the threads of true water that cling to the inner faces of the tablelands where the gusts curl back on themselves and lose their grip. Follow those down past the striped stone, past the rust-and-cream bands, past the grain-lines older than memory, down into the dark the people of the crease have never seen and cannot imagine, because it is the far-off, and the far-off is the silent, and the silent is where meaning cannot go.

It goes somewhere. Water always goes somewhere.

## The Stillness

Here is the first thing you understand about the valley floor: it is quiet.

Not the muffled pressure of the windless garden, where the roar is only softened. *Quiet.* The roar that is everything up there is, down here, a thing you have to listen for — a high far hush at the top of the air, the sound of an immense weather happening to someone else. The tablelands stand so high and so sheer that their turbulence stays up with them, a private fury of the heights. Down at the bottom of the gaps the air lies still and thick and cool, pooled like the water pools, settled into the deepest crease of the whole world.

If one of the crease-people could be lowered here — and none ever has, none ever could, there is no path and no reason and no gesture in their hands for *descend into the silent dark* — they would not have a word for what they found. They have made their entire selves around the proposition that water cannot rest, that stillness must be hoarded one slow drop at a time into a sacred bowl. And here, where their own thrown water finally lands, it does nothing but rest. It lies in long black meres between the tableland feet, flat as the holy bowl is flat, flat for *kilometers*, a surface so motionless it doubles the dark walls perfectly, so that the gaps seem to fall away downward as far as they rise upward. The most sacred thing they own, the thing they sit before for hours and call peace — down here it is simply the ground. It is everywhere. It is what you walk beside. The whole valley floor is the inside of their bowl, scaled up past holiness into mere fact.

## What the Water Becomes

The meres are not pure. The water that reaches them has scraped a long way down the striped stone and brought the stripes with it — minerals leached pale, iron rusts, the slow dissolved memory of the layers the rock half-forgot it was ever laid in. So the meres are colored. Bands of it: ochre shallows, a green so dark it is nearly black, milky veils of suspended stone where two currents meet and will not mix. The same rust-and-cream that strips the cliffs above lies dissolved and recombined in the still water below, the mountain unmade and laid down again flat. Everything that falls here is what fell off up there. The valley is the sediment of the heights. It is made entirely of what the tablelands could not keep.

And it is warm. This is the second strangeness, the inverse of the first. Up top, a wet warmth in thin moving air. Down here, where the world's heat collects as the cold tablelands cannot, the meres steam faintly at their edges where mineral springs feed up from below, and the air hangs heavy and close and breathable in a way the heights never quite are. It is the warm dark bottom of a cold bright shouting world. If the tablelands are an old age that will not stop moving, the valley is the opposite settling: a place that has stopped moving so completely that it has become the only fertile dark there is.

## What Grows in the Dark

Things live here. More things than up there, not fewer — this is the secret the heights cannot know, that all their dwindling and honing and final stable rarity has a counterweight, that what the tablelands shed has gone somewhere and become *abundance*.

The light is poor. Only a fraction reaches the floor, filtered grey down the kilometers of gap, brightest at the narrow noon when the sun finds the slot directly overhead and lays a moving blade of brilliance across one mere and then withdraws it. So the growth here is not the wind-honed, sun-flashed, silver-turning canopy of the crowns. It is pale. It is slow. It is *enormous.* In stillness and warmth and endless dissolved mineral, with no wind to fight and no thirst to engineer against, the plants of the floor have not learned to be neat. They sprawl. Broad soft leaves the size of bedsheets lie flat on the black water, unmoving, gathering what little light there is across their whole patient surface. Pale stalks climb the lower cliffs in slow spirals. Things grow here that on the crowns would be torn apart in an afternoon — fragile, fat-stemmed, water-gorged, drinking from the very abundance the upper people ration out by the sacred drop.

And the floor-growth does not anticipate anything. Why would it? Nothing comes. There is no gust to read, no weather to lean ahead of. The leaves of the valley lie utterly still, and in their stillness they are the photographic negative of the canopy above — the canopy that twitches a half-second early, restless, listening; these that have never once needed to listen, never once moved on their own, never developed the trembling foreknowledge that up top passes for wisdom. The elders of the crease have made an entire visionary craft out of reading motion before it happens. Here is a whole flora that has never moved and never will, and it thrives, and it would be, to those elders, not just unreadable but *unintelligible* — a language with no motion in it at all, which is to say no language, which is to say, by everything they are, silence. The valley floor would seem to them the dead bottom of the world. It is in fact the most alive part of it.

## The Things That Move

There are animals, and they are the inverse too.

Up top: the gliders, leather-winged, who almost never land, who live in the violent air and speak by flashing the pale insides of their wings because nothing they make could be heard over the roar. Built entirely for motion, for the turbulent column, for never coming to rest.

Down here: the meres are full of slow soft swimming things, and they are *blind*, or nearly — the light is too poor to have been worth the cost of eyes, and so over the long count of generations the floor-creatures have given up the seeing that the crease-people made into everything. They read the world the way the burrowers of the crowns do, by vibration, by the faint pressure of a body moving water that is otherwise perfectly at rest. A thing entering the mere announces itself for a great distance through the still medium, a slow ring of disturbance traveling out across the flat dark. Down here meaning *does* carry across distance — but only through stillness, only because the water holds so motionless that the smallest tremor in it travels true. It is the exact opposite of the rule above, where sound carries nothing and meaning must huddle close. Here meaning travels far, and travels by the very thing the upper people hold sacred: by stillness, by the unmoving surface, by the held-breath flatness of water at peace. They venerate it as the rarest of all things. Down here it is the medium of every conversation the meres ever have.

The blind swimmers move toward each other across kilometers of dark by feeling the spreading rings of one another's motion. They court that way, and hunt that way, and gather that way, in slow congregations on the floor of a world that does not know they exist. They have no faces to read. They have no hands. They have only the patience of the still water and the long-traveling truth of a single ripple — and in this they are, without ever knowing it, the deep kin of the people far above who lean brow to brow in the windless crease and read each other by the smallest motion in the smallest sheltered space. Both peoples have made their whole lives out of reading tiny movement in a medium kept perfectly calm. The crease-folk had to build their calm, one drop, one sheltered fold, one held breath at a time, against a world that fought them for it. The swimmers were simply *given* it, a whole drowned valley of it, and never had to know it could be otherwise.

## The Connection, Which Neither End Can See

This is the thing, and it is worth saying plainly even though no one in either place will ever say it, because neither place knows the other is there.

Everything the tablelands lose, the valley keeps.

The water hurled off the edges and shredded into mist — most of it dies as mist, true, unravels into the empty air and is gone. But the heaviest of it, the patient remainder, falls all the way down and becomes the still black meres. The stone ground off the cliffs by epochs of weather — that becomes the dissolved color in the water, the ochre and the green-black and the milky veils, the mountain laid back down flat. The heat the high cold crowns cannot hold sinks and pools and makes the warm fertile dark. The crease-people will dwindle and end and their sacred bowl will tip at last in some worse-than-usual gale and fling its small flat mirror sideways into the roar — and even that water, even the holiest water they ever managed to still, will fall, eventually, down the long striped faces into the meres, where it will become exactly what it always was trying to be and could only briefly, preciously, painfully be up there: water at rest. Their whole religion is the valley floor, achieved by suffering, held for a moment, mourned when lost. They sit before the bowl and call it peace and a permitted ending. They are looking, without knowing it, straight down through kilometers of rock and silence at the actual floor of their own faith, where the peace they hoard by the drop lies spread out endless and warm and full of slow blind life, fed forever by everything they and their shouting world let fall.

The streak on each hand-stencil — *the wind took even this* — is true. The wind took it. And the wind gave it, down, to the dark, to the meres, to the blind soft swimmers reading each other's ripples across the flat black peace.

Up top: old underneath, furious above, roaring, dwindling, reading the leaves for the gust a half-second early, leaning close, ending.

Down here: still all the way through, warm, dark, abundant, blind, reading the long-traveling ripple across the held surface, beginning, beginning, beginning, on everything the heights cannot keep.

Neither will ever know the other. The gap between them is kilometers of empty air, which is to say it is the far-off, which is to say — by the only rule both worlds would agree on, though they would never meet to agree — it is the silent. And the silent is where meaning cannot go.

It goes anyway. It falls. Water always goes somewhere.
