The Mouth

Follow the water. Not the way you came — back out into the thin grey light — but the other way, the way you were never able to go: through the throat of stone behind the back wall, down the road the clawed thing took and the green will never travel. Follow the river out of the dark.

It is a long way. The river runs under the old rounded hills for a distance the mind cannot hold any better than it could hold the age it took to carve the cleft, and all that while it is doing exactly what it did in the room you knew: roaring, indifferent, fast. But a river underground is a river kept. The stone holds it close, holds it cold, holds the roar in. And then the stone runs out.

The river comes out of the hill into open air through a low broad arch at the foot of a sea-cliff, and here is the first thing to understand: it stops roaring. Not because it slows — it does not slow, it is moving fast and full and grey-green with the cold of a thousand years of stone — but because there is no longer a held shell to amplify it, no cleft, no leaning faces, no ceiling to throw the sound back down against your chest. The roar simply opens out and is gone, spent upward into a sky that does not catch it. The same water that deafened you, that pressed against you like a hand, arrives here as a wide hushing rush, almost gentle, swallowed instantly by the larger sound of the place it is running into.

Because it is running into the sea.

This is the Mouth — where the kept water meets the unkept, where the river that was a prisoner of stone for an age spreads out across a flat of sand and pebble and is taken, all at once and forever, by the tide. And everything that was true of the cleft is true here inverted, turned inside out like a glove, and the two places are the same place seen from its two ends.

Begin with the light. The cleft was the deepest dark the world keeps; the Mouth is the most open light. There is nothing here to stop the sky. It comes down whole, grey and shifting and enormous, full of fast cloud running in off the water, and it lies on everything — on the wet flat that mirrors it, on the foam, on the slick black weed, on the backs of birds. Where the cleft hoarded one greenish breathing glow and made you doubt your own eye, the Mouth squanders light, throws it everywhere, more than any eye can hold, until you squint and look down and the brightness still finds you in the shine of the wet sand.

And the temperature. You said the cleft's cold was mineral, the cold of stone holding the same heat a thousand years and meaning to hold it a thousand more — a cold that does not change. Here the cold *moves*. It is the cold of wind that has crossed open water, and it arrives in gusts and lulls, it cuts and then forgets to cut, it carries salt and the iodine reek of the weed and the clean nothing-smell of distance. Nothing here holds its temperature for an hour, let alone a thousand years. The wind sees to that. The tide sees to that. This is a place that has agreed to change at the only speed the cleft refused: fast, faster, faster than a body, twice a day forever.

For the tide is the master here the way the roar was master there, and you must understand it before anything else makes sense. Twice a day the sea comes up across the flat and takes everything — the river's mouth, the pebbles, the weed, the print of every bird — and twice a day it falls back and gives everything again, rearranged, scoured, made new. Where the cleft's law was *hold*, the Mouth's law is *let go and be given back*. Where the green thing survived by becoming nearly nothing and spreading that nothing thin across stone so the flood could not find it, the things that live here survive the opposite way: by being found by the flood, over and over, twice a day, and being built for it. They do not hide from the water. They open to it.

So the life here is not sparse. This is the great reversal, and it is the heart of why the two places belong to each other. The cleft was mostly hollow — vast spans of nothing between one fern and the next, life scattered like a few candles in a cathedral. The Mouth is *crammed*. Where the river's cold fresh water fans out and mixes with the salt, in the brackish churn where neither sea-creature nor river-creature should be able to bear it, life has piled up in a density that is almost obscene after the long emptiness underground. The pebbles wear skirts of bladderwrack and gutweed. The wet flat is stippled with the casts of lugworms, thousands upon thousands, a whole buried population breathing the tide. Cockles and razor-shells lie just under the surface in numbers you could not count in a lifetime. Sandhoppers go up in clouds when you step. And the birds — oystercatchers stabbing, dunlin running in their nervous turning flocks, a curlew somewhere bending its long note down into the wind — the birds come in their hundreds because the food is here in its millions, because the river feeds the Mouth, because everything the river carries out of the dark hills, every scrap of the slow green inside, every mineral grain it dissolved off the throat of stone across an age, arrives here and is *eaten*.

This is what runs out of the one place and into the other. The cleft digested the marks of the old hands, slowly, patiently, taking the iron-stain a hair's width fainter each lifetime — and where do you think the iron went? Where does anything go that the patient green unmakes? Into the water. The river carries it out. Everything the cleft so slowly absorbs, the river so slowly delivers, here, to the Mouth, where it is taken up at once into the fast hot hungry business of living. The cleft is the long inhale. The Mouth is the long exhale. They are the two ends of a single breath, and the river between them is the breath itself, and it has been breathing for an age and will breathe until the day the back wall gives.

The fauna here are not visitors. That is the other reversal, and it is total. In the cleft nothing made its home in the full roar; the cricket and the clawed thing passed through and were welcome the way weather is welcome, and the green felt no loneliness because loneliness is a fast feeling and it had none. Here everything is fast feeling. Here is loneliness and its cure — the dunlin will not be parted from the flock, turns when it turns, lifts when it lifts, a thousand birds thinking with one nervous body, and that is a kind of thought too, but it is thought at the opposite end of the world from the moss: thought so fast it is finished before it begins, thought made of fright and hunger and the next tide. The Mouth is crowded with want. Everything here wants, urgently, now, because now is all the tide allows; in six hours the sea will be back and the wanting will have to start over.

And the marks of people? They are here too, and here is where the two places lean closest toward each other, the way the two grey faces lean above the slot of dark. Above the high-tide line, where the wind-cut grass begins, there is a midden — a long low bank of broken shell, ash-grey, thousands of years of it, cockle and limpet and mussel cracked open and tossed aside by hands that came to the Mouth season after season because the Mouth gives, twice a day, freely, what the cleft would only ever yield to the most patient. The same kind of hands, perhaps. The same age of the world. The people who scratched their tally on the cleft wall and laid their dry-stone shelter and let their iron rust into the rock — those people, or their kin, or whoever inherited the low old hills — also came down the river's road to the Mouth and ate, and the cleft has nearly reabsorbed their mark while the Mouth has built theirs up, shell on shell, into a bank you can stand on. One place digests its people back into silence. The other piles its people up into a heap that the marram grass now binds and the rabbits now burrow and the wind now slowly unstitches, shell by shell, sending the grains back down to the flat, back to the water, back into the breath.

For nothing here is kept either, in the end. That is the truth the Mouth and the cleft share underneath their opposition, the truth that makes them one world and not two. The cleft holds and holds until the day it can't, and then it is river and the river holds nothing. The Mouth lets go and is given back, lets go and is given back — but each giving is a little less, each scouring takes a little more out to sea than it returns, and the midden thins, and the cliff retreats a hand's width an age, and the whole flat is, on the longest view, going the way the cleft's back wall is going: into the water, into the unkept, into the sea that does not hold anything at all. The river does not hold. The tide does not hold. They only seem to, from inside a single human hour, the way the green seemed to be still.

So stand here a while, where the kept water meets the unkept, and feel the wind change the cold on the backs of your hands as fast as the cleft refused to change it. The roar that held you underground arrived here and opened and was gone into a larger hush. The emptiness that was the cleft's whole nature arrives here as crowding, as want, as a thousand turning birds. The slow green inside, digested grain by grain across an age, arrives here and is eaten in an afternoon. Everything the one place hoarded, the other place spends. Everything the one place held, the other lets the sea take.

And the sea takes it. The river runs out of the dark hills under the rounded land, out through the low arch, across the bright flat, into the grey moving water — and the grey moving water carries it out and out until there is no telling river from sea, hill-cold from salt, the breath of the unseen flood from the breath of the open world. Out there it is neither place. Out there it is only the going.

You stood once in the cleft and the quiet outside sounded, for an hour, like the loudest thing you'd ever heard. Stand now at the Mouth, in all this light and wind and crowding life, and somewhere under it — under the curlew, under the surf, under the hushing fan of the spent river — you can almost hear the other thing. The held note. The truce. The two enormous slownesses leaning against each other in the dark a long way back up the water. You came out of there. The river came out of there. And the river, having come so far to be free of the stone, runs straight into the sea and gives itself away.

That is who lives at the Mouth. The one who lets go. It will let go until the day there is nothing left to give, and the cleft will hold until the day it can't, and the river will breathe between them, in the dark and out into the light, for as long as the balance holds — which is not peace, you understand by now, but only two endings leaning toward each other across an age, not quite meeting, the way the grey stone leans above the slot of dark, holding, holding, not yet.
