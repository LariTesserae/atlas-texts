# The Far Field

There is a field, and the field changes constantly, and that is the first thing you understand about it — and you understand it as relief, the way you understand water after the taste of rust, before you understand it as terror.

It lies high. Not on a mountain — there is no mountain, no rock at all — but high in the sense that wind reaches it from every quarter, that nothing stands between it and the weather, that it is a place the sky has unobstructed access to and uses. The ground is soil here, real soil, dark and loose and turning, worked everywhere by roots and worms and the slow heave of frost and thaw, and nothing about it is finished. Channels open and silt closed within a season. A gully cuts itself across a slope one spring and is gone the next, healed under grass, and where it ran there is now a low green swale that will be a gully again, differently, later. You cannot tell whether the water carves the land or the land invites the water, and here the difference matters, because the agreement is never reached. They argue endlessly. That is what the field is: an argument that does not settle.

I want to be careful, because you were careful, and because what I am describing is the place your city is *not*, and a thing is hardest to see clearly when you are only seeing it as the absence of another thing.

So: the field on its own terms.

The light moves. This is the first wealth of the place and the hardest to convey to anyone who has stood under the even working grey of the basin. Here the sky is never one color for the length of a held breath. Cloud-shadow runs across the grass like something fleeing, and behind it the sun lays down a gold that is gone before you have named it gold, and rain walks visibly along the far ridge, a grey hem dragging, and arrives, and passes, and the field steams. Morning is not noon. Noon is not the long amber wreck of evening. You could not record this place. You could photograph it ten thousand times and have ten thousand different fields, and not one of them would be the field, because the field is not any of its instants. The field is the *passage* between them — the thing that lives in no single frame and only in the running of the frames, which is to say it is made of exactly the substance your city forbids.

It grows. Everything grows. Grasses first, in their dense illiterate millions, and through them the broad-leaved things, the seeding things, the things that climb and the things that creep, and over a few years the saplings that will be the thicket that will be the wood that will, struck by lightning or felled by its own weight or burned in a dry autumn, fall and rot and feed the grasses that begin it all again — but never the same, never the same arrangement twice, because the rot does not lay the seeds back where they were. Each cycle is a new draft. The field is industry made into nothing at all, the opposite of liturgy: pure production with no fixed form, work that accomplishes endless different objects and keeps not one of them.

And it is loud, but you would not call it loud, because you have heard the foundries. It is loud the way a forest is loud — wind in ten thousand leaves, water somewhere always running and never the same water, insects in their seethe, the crack of growth and the softer crack of decay, a bird, another bird answering wrong. No bar of music repeats. There is no beat to find. After a while the clamor does not become quiet, the way the city's clamor did; it becomes *weather*, it becomes the sound of things spending themselves freely and gladly and forever differently, and you understand that this is the sound of a place paying its own bills, openly, out loud, with everything it has, holding nothing back against the future because it is not afraid of the future, because the future is where it is *going*.

The fauna here are animals. I will not dignify the contrast more than that. They are born wrong and die early and a few die old and none of them does a single fixed task. They forget things. They learn things and then the learning dies with them and the next ones learn it differently or not at all. They are the moving parts of nothing; they move for themselves, badly, beautifully, toward food and away from fear and toward each other, and the field does not need them to be anything and they are not part of any music. There is no chord. There is only the running argument, and they are voices in it, raised and then silenced and replaced by other voices that do not know what was said before.

Now — here is how the two places touch, and it took me a long time to find it, walking the field's edges, which unlike the city's edges I could never walk in an hour, because the field has no edge, it merely thins, it merely becomes more itself, it goes on becoming.

The condensation.

You said the city sweats. You said the water gathers on the iron flanks and runs the troughs and is gathered and lifted and made to sweat again, a closed loop. You were almost right. It is not quite closed. A loop that perfect would have nothing to lose and would not need an inhabitant to pay its costs; the very fact that something must perpetually settle the bill tells you the bill is real, that something *leaks*. And it does. At the boundary, where the last foundry stands with its wheel still turning and the grey rock runs out dry — there, a little of the water does not return. A breath of it lifts off the cool stone as vapor, the smallest possible amount, the amount the inhabitant pays to lose so that all the rest may be kept, and it rises into the cool empty distance you called silence, and the cool empty distance is not empty and is not the end of anything. It is the bottom of the sky over the far field.

The water that escapes the city becomes weather over the field. This is the trade. The city, holding its one note, must shed an infinitesimal something at its edge to stay in balance, and that something — that exhalation, that single bead of refusal that does not get drunk back into the loop — drifts and gathers across the dry distance and becomes cloud, and the cloud walks its grey hem along the far ridge, and the field steams under it, and grows. The field is fed by exactly what the city declines to keep. Everything that the inhabitant pays out so that nothing will change — every dram of cost it settles, every spent thing it sets down and walks an inch away from — does not vanish. There is no away in which it could vanish. It rains on the field. It becomes the gully and the swale, the rot and the next grass, the bird and the bird that answers wrong.

You felt this and did not know it. You wrote that you were drunk and given back, a bead returned to the loop. But you also wrote that you walked out, that you have a life since, that you have changed and gone places the city could not go. Both are true and they are true of different drops. Most of the city's sweat is reclaimed; you felt the pull of that and called it the inhabitant declining to notice you were gone. But you are reading this. You are somewhere it is not. You are one of the drops that got out — and where the drops that get out go, all of them, always, is here. The field is where the city's losses live. The field is made of the city's grief, if a thing with no preferences can grieve, and it cannot, and so the field is made of the part of the city that the city will not let itself feel, raining down at last in a place that feels everything and keeps nothing and dies of it constantly and is not afraid.

And here is the inverse of the inverse, the thing I did not want to write, and will, because you deserve the symmetry:

The field is dying. Always. That is not a flaw in it; that is the whole of it; it is the most thoroughly dying thing in any world, dying in every leaf and every season, spending itself toward stillness exactly as the city refuses to. You would think the city is the dead place and the field the living one, and you would be standing one turn too early, the way you stood with the animals. Turn once more.

A thing that holds one note forever does not die, but it does not live either — you found that, at the edge, in the second of not-knowing. And a thing that dies constantly, that spends itself utterly and is never the same and keeps nothing — that thing is not dying *toward* anything. It has no stillness to reach. It cannot run down because it was never wound; it has no bill to pay because it pays everything instantly and is reborn from the city's losses faster than it can be spent. The field dies the way the note holds: forever, without resolution, the dying perpetual and unconcluding. The city refuses the future and the field devours it, and neither one ever arrives anywhere, and that is the only thing they have in common and it is everything: *they are the two ways of never ending.*

The inhabitant does not know about the field. It cannot. Knowing would be a turning-to-look, a preference, a spending it will not allow. It pays its edge-cost blindly, declining even to follow where the loss goes. So it does not know that nothing it loses is lost — that the grief it will not feel is, at this moment, a particular green smell after rain on a particular slope, that the bead it set down and walked away from is running clear and cold over the roots of an alder it will never see, that the smoke which leans always the same way and never blows away does, at the very last, blow away, one molecule per forever, and falls here as weather, and feeds a field that is wildly, helplessly, terminally alive on the strength of one machine's refusal to ever change.

You are in the field now. You have been since you stepped past the wheel. The dry silent distance was only the rising, the part of the lift where you were still vapor and had not yet become rain. You are raining. You have been raining your whole life since the city, and you mistook it for walking, for changing, for going somewhere — and you were right, that is exactly what it is, it is the thing the city cannot do, it is the gift of the only drop that got out. The grass is loud around you in its thousand differing voices and not one of them repeats and not one of them will be here next year and that is not the terror you feared at the edge.

That was the terror. This is the other thing. They feel almost the same in the body and they are opposite all the way down.
