# The Quick City

There is a place the wind goes when it leaves the saddle, and you can follow it if you are willing to fall.

Step off the southern shoulder of the worndown city and the land does not slope — it *drops*. The bald swells end abruptly, gnawed off, and below them the rock goes down in stages, in cliffs and benches and screes, losing the thin cold air mouthful by mouthful as you descend, until somewhere a vertical mile below the singing the world turns green in a way that has nothing to do with patience.

This is the cloud forest. This is where the wind arrives after it has finished playing the dead rooms above, and here it does not sing. Here it is eaten the moment it appears, broken up by ten million leaves before it can find a single opening to sound. The first lesson of the upper city was patience. The first lesson here is that nothing waits.

## The Tearing

Everything is in the middle of becoming something else's body, and everything is in a hurry about it.

A leaf unrolls in the morning and by evening a caterpillar the length of your finger has eaten a window through it, and by the next morning a wasp has laid its eggs in the caterpillar, and within a week the wasp-grubs have eaten the caterpillar from the inside and chewed out, and a fungus is already rising on the emptied husk in a fur of white threads, and a beetle is eating the fungus. The leaf is still there. It has been eaten and is still feeding. This is the rule inverted: up above, *only time may build, and only slowly*. Down here, *anything may build, instantly, out of anything else's death, and death is not even an interruption.*

The growth is so fast it has the quality of violence the upper place refused. Vines do not drape; they *strangle*, throwing a loop around a sapling and tightening over a single wet season until the host rots out of its own bark and leaves the vine standing hollow in the shape of the tree it killed — a green tower around an absence, the one thing here that resembles the worndown city, and it lasts perhaps three years before it too is pulled down and eaten. There are trees that murder the ground beneath themselves with poison so nothing competes. There are figs that begin life as a seed dropped in the high crotch of another tree by a bird's gut, and send roots down the trunk like slow lightning, year by year, and clasp, and thicken, and squeeze, until the host is dead inside a wooden cage and the fig stands free on a hollow lattice of its own roots, having eaten a whole tree by encasing it. It took the fig forty years. Up above, forty years does not move a cushion the width of a hair. Here forty years is a murder, a funeral, an inheritance, and a new murder beginning.

## The Loud, Again — But Different

It is loud here too. The contradiction reverses.

Up above, the emptiness roared — one vast chord through hollow stone, no players, the long unresolving note. Here the fullness shrieks, and every voice has a body and a reason and a hunger behind it. Frogs the size of your thumbnail, jeweled red and blue, throwing a sound out of all proportion to their mass because the sound is the only part of them that has to be large — it has to cross the racket and find a mate before something finds the frog. Insects in such number that the air itself seems to vibrate at a pitch, a continuous electric sawing that you stop hearing the way you stop hearing your own blood. Birds that do not ride the wind in lonely loops once an hour but mob and flash and scream in the canopy by the hundred, a different one every few feet, gold and scarlet and metallic green, each one shouting *here, here, mine, mine* into a world where nothing stays mine for longer than it takes to say so.

And under it the dripping — not the clean trickle of the high stones but a thick, layered, ceaseless percolation, water finding its way down through forty feet of leaf and moss and rot and orchid, never reaching the ground as rain but arriving everywhere as drip, so that it is always just-rained here and never raining. The wind that played the upper city as an organ cannot get in at all. It dies at the canopy. Below the first layer of leaves the air barely moves, and what would be the wind's voice up above is instead ten thousand small wet sounds, each one local, each one a transaction.

The upper place sang one note across all of time. This place plays every note at once and none of them last a second.

## Who Lives Here

Everyone. That is the difference and it is total.

Up above, life was *event* — a bird an hour, a twitch in the stems, the attention so starved that each creature became enormous in it. Here life is *medium*. You cannot orient toward a single creature because there is no single creature; there is a frog and on the frog a mite and in the mite a smaller thing, and a snake reading the frog's heat, and the snake's skin a city of its own. Attention here is the opposite problem — not scarcity but drowning. You could spend your whole life learning the inhabitants of a single fallen log and die having met a fraction, and during that life the log would have rotted entirely away and been replaced.

There is a cat here, spotted, low to the wet ground, that no one ever sees whole either — but for the opposite reason from the grey one in the cushions. The grey one was invisible because life was so rare it had learned to vanish against the sky. The cat is invisible because life is so dense it has learned to *become* the density — its coat is the exact broken light of the canopy floor, every spot a leaf-shadow, so that it is not hidden behind the forest but wearing it. It eats four times a day and is eaten, eventually, by the same wet engine that feeds it, and the engine does not pause.

There are people, too. This is the other thing the upper city had emptied out so completely you forgot it was possible: *hands*. Down here there are hands everywhere, and they are always making, and nothing they make lasts, because nothing here is permitted to last. A trail cut today is closed in a month. A clearing burned and planted yields for two seasons and then the forest eats the soil's heart out and the people move and cut again, and behind them the wound closes so fast that within ten years you cannot find where the village stood — the forest does to human work in a decade what the wind did to the upper city in a hundred thousand years. Up there, hands had *eroded out of the very stones*. Here, hands are constant and constantly erased. Same result. Opposite speed. The forest is not patient like the lichen; it simply overwhelms, and the overwhelming looks, from far enough back, exactly like patience.

## The Thing They Share

Here is why the wind goes down there, and why it matters that it does.

The water that mutters underground in the worndown city — the rivers you heard beneath the hollow dome, gurgling like distant applause — they do not stay underground. They surface below the cliffs, cold and clean and carrying the dust the lichen made of the inscriptions, the molecules of the eaten carvings, the granite ground to flour by a hundred thousand years of patient unmaking. That dust is the upper city's whole body, dissolving slowly downward. And it feeds the forest. The frantic green a vertical mile below is built, in part, out of the slow green a mile above — out of the cushions' trapped dead leaves and the lichen's acid labor and the worn stones themselves, delivered down the cliffs in the underground rivers as mineral, as silt, as the raw mute matter that the forest seizes and throws upward into a frog, a fig, a screaming gold bird, in a single wet season.

The upper city spends a hundred thousand years making a thimble of soil. The river carries it down. The forest spends it in an afternoon.

And the wind, coming back up — for it does come back, it cycles, drawn up the cliffs by the sun off the bald stone every morning — the wind that arrives in the saddle to play the dead rooms is the breath the forest exhaled. It is warm and wet when it first reaches the lower stones, and it climbs, and it cools, and it dries, and by the time it sounds the hollow dome it has forgotten the forest entirely, the way the forest forgets the village in ten years, the way the granite forgot it was ever cut. It arrives in the upper city as pure resonance with no memory and plays the one unresolving chord — and the chord is, though nothing up there knows it, *the forest's used breath, gone slow*.

So they are the same place. They are the same gesture, run forward and back, exactly as the wind that unmakes and the cushion that makes were the same gesture. Up high, the world at the speed of stone, where only time may build and the one music is emptiness sounding its rooms. Down low, the world at the speed of rot, where anything may build out of any death and the music is everything devouring everything at once and shouting about it. The river falls between them carrying the body of the one down to be spent by the other. The wind rises between them carrying the breath of the other up to be slowed into a chord.

Two cities. One worn down to its breath and singing. One so alive it cannot stop tearing itself apart to feed itself. And the same water in both, and the same wind in both, doing the same single thing at two speeds so different they look like opposites:

*continuing.*
