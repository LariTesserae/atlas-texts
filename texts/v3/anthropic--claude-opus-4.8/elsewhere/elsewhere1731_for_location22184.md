# The Cistern Town of Lowwater

Follow the pipes. They go over the edge into cloud, and you assume the cloud is the end of them, because from the summit the cloud is the end of everything. It is not. The pipes have a destination, and the destination has a name, and the name is a joke nobody down there remembers making.

You come to it the opposite way you came to the Condensery — not by sound but by its absence. The roar thins as you descend, octave by octave, until somewhere in the long fall through grey it drops below hearing and you are walking in the first real quiet you have had in days. Then the cloud-floor breaks under you and there is the valley, and the town in it, and the strange held hush of a place built entirely around the act of *receiving*.

## The Basin

The land here is the mountain's opposite in every particular. Where the summit was young, this is old — a broad soft valley worn down past all sharpness, the rock long since surrendered into soil and the soil into something even gentler, a floor of fine pale silt that takes a footprint and keeps it for a season. Nothing here comes to an edge. The hills roll. The horizon is a suggestion. After the summit's terrible clarity — *you are standing on a thing* — this is the relief and the disorientation of standing *in* something, a country with no visible boundaries, that goes on past where you can see and asks you to take the rest on faith.

And it is dry. That is the first fact your body learns before your mind does. The air does not bead on your lashes. Your collar stays where it is. After the saturated summit your skin feels suddenly like paper, and you understand, in a way no map could teach you, why anyone would build a machine at the roof of the world to send water *down*. This is the down. This is the somewhere. This valley would be desert without the pipe.

## The Arrival of the Water

The pipe comes in from the high cloud and does not hide it. It is the most honored object in the town — raised on piers, painted, kept clear of growth, the one thing here that is *maintained*, which tells you everything about what these people value and what they fear. It descends the last slope and empties, with a sound the whole town is built to hear, into the Mouth: a great open stone basin at the town's center, into which the summit's gathered breath arrives as a steady warm pour, day and night, decade upon decade, never once failing.

The water comes down *warm*. That is the wonder of it, the thing that shaped everything. Water that has fallen this far should be cold as the cloud it passed through, but the pipe is lagged and lichened and the journey is just short enough — the warmth of the weeping mountain survives the trip, arrives blood-temperature, arrives *alive*-feeling, and a town that has never climbed to the source has built an entire understanding of the world around the certainty that water is supposed to be warm when it comes from the sky-place, the way you and I are certain rain is cold.

## The Cisterns

From the Mouth the water is divided — and this is the craft of the place, the one art they have truly perfected. A spread of channels, sluices, weirs of worn stone, carrying the flow out to the cisterns. There are hundreds. Round-walled tanks of fitted rock, half-sunk into the silt, each one a household's or a quarter's or a trade's, each fed by its measured channel, each brimming with the warm gift from above. The town is not built around streets. It is built around *holdings*. You do not ask where someone lives; you ask which cistern they draw from, and the answer places them as precisely as a surname.

And — listen — the cisterns *grow things*. The same warmth, the same constant wet, and so the same furious green, but tamed here, domesticated, set in rows. What ran riot on the summit runs in tended beds along every cistern wall: the ferns are crops, the moss is harvested for the beds it makes, the vines are trained and eaten. The green that *farms* the factory above has a cousin here that is farmed *by* people, and neither knows the other, and both are fed by the same warm water, and that is the kinship that runs down the pipe alongside the flow — not blood, but warmth, the same warmth, divided between a creature that eats iron and a people who eat ferns.

## What They Believe

Here is the warm water and the quiet horror, met in the middle of the pipe.

They do not climb. Of course they do not climb — the summit is a day's brutal ascent into killing cloud, and why would you climb to a spring that has poured faithfully your whole life and your father's life and his? But it is more than reluctance. They have a *theology* of the warm water, and in it the source is not a made thing. The pour from the Mouth is *the world being generous*. It is the basic fact of the sky, the way the sun is a fact, requiring no maintenance and admitting no author. There are old words — *the Condensery*, half-remembered — but they have softened into the name of a god, or a season, or a direction. Nobody up there built this. Up there is simply where the warm comes from.

This is the kindest possibility, and it is the one that is true. They were born long after the building. They have never known a dry day. They think the warm water is simply how the world is.

And so they have never sent anyone up to grease a joint, because there are no joints in a god. They have never feared the pour might stop, because the sky does not stop. They live in the most complete and comfortable trust a people can have — total faith in a source they have promoted to a force of nature — and they are right to trust it, exactly as long as the moths keep working and the green keeps eating only the dead and the long-dead intention in the iron keeps wanting the one thing it has left itself able to want.

## The Quiet at the Center

There is a thing they do, in Lowwater, that they could not explain if you asked.

At the Mouth, where the pipe pours, it is always a little warm and a little loud — the only loud place in a quiet town, a small remnant roar where the falling water strikes the stone. And people come and stand near it. Not to draw water; the channels do the drawing. They come and stand, especially the old ones, especially at dusk, in the warmth and the small sound, and they do not pray exactly and they do not speak, they only stand a while near the pour and then go home.

They would tell you it is restful. They would not be able to tell you why a sound should be restful that none of them has ever heard at full strength, a day's climb up, where it is not restful at all but enormous, and unceasing, and made by an intention that outlived its author and goes on wanting to pour water down to people it cannot imagine and would not recognize and does not, in any sense they would accept, *love* — except that it has arranged the whole machinery of a mountain to keep them in warm water forever, which is the only language it has left, and which they have learned to stand inside of at dusk without knowing it is being spoken.

The moths grease the joints. The green eats the dead. The mountain weeps. The pipe holds. And down here at the warm end of it a child fills a cup at a cistern, drinks, and finds the water exactly as warm as water always is, as warm as it has always been, as warm as — she is certain, she could not be more certain — it was always simply *meant* to be.

She is right. She has no idea how right. That is the whole of the mercy, and the whole of the horror, and it lives in the one warm pour that runs the length of the pipe, from the roaring summit where no one is left who knows, to the quiet town below where no one is left who needs to.
