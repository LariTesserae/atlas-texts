# The High Catchment, Where the Falling Begins

Go up.

Not the easy up — not the long worn ramps spiraling the pillars, which lead only to the layered ceilings, the held-down roof of roofs. Find instead the one shaft the builders never softened, the one fault in the rounded world that still has an *edge*. It is narrow. It goes up through the rock at an angle a body must climb rather than walk, and it is the only place in all the under-country where the roar thins as you ascend — where, rung by patient rung, the million-throated weather of sound falls away beneath you until you arrive at a thing none of them below has language for, because it is the absence of their only sky:

**Quiet.**

And then, faint and far and utterly without echo, a sound from *above*.

## The Country That Still Speaks

The old plain has nothing left to say, they told you. That is true of the plain. It is not true of where the plain *begins.*

For the worn-flat table of the upper country is not the top of the world. It is the floor of a slower descent, a tilt so gradual no eye could catch it, sloping up and up across a span of days' walking — if anything walked it — toward a rim of country that the grinding ages have not yet finished with. The mountains whose bones became the pale grit below: not all of them are dead. At the far high edge, where the world has not yet been worn to exhaustion, a few of them still *stand*. Still have edges. Still have something left to erode to, which means they still have somewhere to go, which means — by the deepest rule, the rule that change comes only on the scale of stone — they are *young*. Impossibly, screamingly young. A place where the world is not yet finished.

And it rains there.

## The Rain

This is the thing the seed remembers.

It is not much rain. Do not picture a drowning. Picture, instead, the thin steady damp of the under-country's air — the mist the plants comb, the moisture the people drink — and now picture *where that damp goes when it is gathered.* The air below holds water because the air above *gives* it, and the air above is given it here, at the high catchment, where the last standing peaks tear it loose from the sky in the only fast event this world permits.

The rain comes down. It comes down onto stone that is *sharp* — and this is the vertigo of the place, for one who has lived among rounded shoulders and softened arches: here the stone has corners. Here the edges cut. The water has not had its uncounted ages yet; it has not yet rounded anything; it is still in the violent young business of carving, of *making* the curves that the under-country merely inherited. Streams run here. Actual streams, bright and quick and loud in a way the people below could not survive the meaning of — water that *moves*, that falls, that goes *somewhere,* that has the one quality their whole world forswore: hurry.

The catchment is a bowl of cut rock, fluted not by dust but by the thing dust only imitates. Black-wet stone, slick and gleaming, edged like the broken flint that is the one tool the under-builders never had and never needed and never knew they lacked. And where the water gathers and pours over the bowl's low lip, it begins the only journey in this world that has not yet ended:

It falls.

## The Falling

Here is the kinship. Here is the thread that runs from the one place to the other, carried in the same breath as both:

The note in their lowest speech — the note the old listeners call *the note of falling,* the sound a thing would make coming down from above, the memory of a sky that gave instead of merely roofed —

it is not memory.

It is *now.* It is happening, has always been happening, this very moment, at the high catchment days and ages above them. The water pours over the lip of the young stone bowl and goes down — into the rock, into the long dark fault-lines, sinking and spreading and slowing across the whole vast span of the worn plain, losing its hurry by inches, giving up its falling over such an immensity of stone and time that by the time it reaches the under-country it is no longer water at all. It is only damp. It is only the thin steady mist in the dim air, the held promise, the wet thing the seed has learned to live without — because the water *did not stop coming.* It only slowed. It slowed until it crossed the threshold from rain into memory without ever once ceasing to fall.

They did not lose the water. The water is still arriving. It arrives so slowly, so utterly drained of event, that they experience its arrival as *dryness* — as the patient held dryness of the seed — and they grieve it, in their unhurried unmourning way, as a thing long gone.

It is not gone. It is *here.* It is in the mist on their smooth gray skin. It is in the fronds they comb it from. It is the falling note in their own throats, woven into the roar, and they do not know — how could they know — that when they sound that lowest register, they are singing the literal present tense of a place they have never imagined, repeating the sky.

## What Is There, In Its Own Right

And the high catchment does not know them either.

That is the truth that completes the kinship and makes it ache. For there is life here too — but it is the *other* life, the inverse, the negative of everything below. Where the under-country is dim, this is bright: a thin cold cutting light, for the catchment is open to the actual sky, the real one, the giving one, and the peaks above it tear not only water from the air but light from a sun the people below have kept their great dark eyes for and never once seen. Where below is cool and constant, here is *cold,* and *changing* — for the catchment has what the under-country forswore, the thing the people there have no concept of: it has *day,* and it has *night,* and it has the violence of their turning, heat that arrives and leaves, frost that grips the cut stone and cracks it further, hurrying the carving along.

Where below teems with the soft sightless abundance of the dark — the pale climbers, the draped grazers, the swarming leathery flyers whose echoes are a people's only map — here there is almost *nothing.* The young stone is too new for thick life. A few hard lichens cling to the wet black rock in colors the under-folk have no eyes left to perceive. A flying thing or two, but *sighted*, hunting by the light, silent — for sound does not pile up here. It scatters. There is no roof to hold it. A cry given at the catchment goes up and out and is simply *gone,* never returning, never answering, lost into the open giving terrible sky. To a creature of the roar below it would be the loneliest place imaginable: a world where nothing you say comes back to you, where the air does not catch your meaning and hold it, where you could call forever into the bright cold and hear only the water, falling, leaving, going down to become someone else's memory.

And where below is *finished* — full, settled, at peace with having ended and gone on — the catchment is *beginning.* Has not ended. Cannot, yet. It is loud with the wrong loudness, the fast loud of the streams, the crack of frost-split stone, the percussion of a world still in the violent middle of becoming itself, ages and ages from the rounded peace of the place its water is slowly, slowly going to.

The two places will never meet. The catchment carves itself young while the under-country holds its finished shape; one is the source and one is the sea, and between them lies the whole worn span of the plain that has nothing left to say, across which the water makes its uncountable crossing, falling out of event and into memory, out of rain and into mist, out of the bright cold giving sky and into the dim cool roaring dark —

— and somewhere down at the end of that crossing, a pale and patient being with eyes kept for a light it will never climb to see opens its great dark eyes slowly, and slowly closes them, and sounds once into the standing weather of the only sky it knows the lowest note it has:

the note of falling.

Which is true.

Which was always true.

Which is, at this very moment, days and ages above, pouring bright and cold and young over the lip of the world, and beginning the long way down.
