There is a place near the bottom of the world where the air is so thick with water that sound forgets how to stop.

Start with the mud. It is dark — not black, but the deep brown-green of things that have been wet for so long that wetness has become their substance, their character, the whole of what they are. There is no stone here, or if there is stone it is so far down beneath the accumulated softness that no one has ever touched it. Everything is sediment. Everything is the slow falling of dead things through warm water, settling, packing, becoming the ground that the next generation of dead things will fall through and settle on and become. The mud is not a surface. It is a depth pretending, near the top, to be a surface.

The scale is enormous and refuses to be held. You could pole a flat boat through it for a season and not cross it, and you would not be sure, at the end of the season, that you had gone anywhere, because there are no features to measure against — only the water and the reeds and the slow brown channels braiding and rejoining, the same view repeating and repeating until repetition becomes a kind of vertigo. Nothing here crowds, because nothing here has an edge to crowd against. The reeds stand apart from one another in their millions, each one alone in its own small column of water, and between them the light does nothing strange at all. It simply thickens.

Because it is so dim here. That is the first thing the body notices and the last it forgets. The sun, when it exists, is a rumor passed down through layers — through the haze that lies on the water like a second water, through the canopy of reed-tops and the broad rotting leaves of things that float, through the suspended silt that hangs in the channels and turns the light the color of weak tea before it reaches you. There are no shadows because there is nothing sharp enough to cast one. The brightness is even, sourceless, exhausted. You do not squint. You strain, instead, to see anything at all, and what you see has no corona, only a soft furred edge where it gives up being itself and becomes the haze again.

And it is warm. That is the paradox the place asks you to hold: dimness without rest. The light is failing and the heat is total, and your breath does not fog because the air is already as full of water as your breath could make it. The warmth is foul, organic, thick with the rot-smell that warmth carries in low places because there is everything here to rot. Moss on everything. Lichen, fungus, the white threads of mold lacing through the reed-stems, the slick of algae greening every still surface. It is a world entirely given over to life — drowning in it, composting it, life so dense it has become its own decay. The only colors are the brown of the water and the green of the things growing and the green-black of the things that grew and fell and are now becoming water again.

Now the dryness. It is not abundant but it is present, and it is the secret of everything. In the heat of the long days, here and there, a hummock lifts itself a hand's breadth above the channels — a packed mound of old roots and settled silt, domed, faintly steaming, and on its crown the mud goes pale and cracks. A film, a crust, a faint glaze of dust in the high warm noon. This is the rare and precious thing, the thing the place hoards the way the basin hoarded shadow: a surface that is, for a few hours, not wet. The cracks deepen. They tile the dome of the hummock into a mosaic of curling plates. And you can hear it, if you stay still — and you will stay still, because poling is exhausting and the warmth invites surrender — the faint tick and crackle of mud drying, a surface learning, briefly, to let go of water. It is almost the only quiet sound the swamp permits.

Otherwise: noise. The thick air carries everything and lets nothing fade. Every drip from every leaf arrives and stays and overlaps the next, until the whole vast wet expanse is one continuous saturated murmur, the sound of water touching water touching water, world without edge or end. Your own pole entering the channel sends a sound out that never quite comes back because it never quite stops. The swamp does not swallow sound. It keeps it, the way it keeps everything, and the keeping accumulates into a hum that is not silence and is not music and cannot be turned off.

And here is the unusual thing, the thing that makes this place not quite obey the ordinary rules. The water does not always lie flat. Watch a channel long enough and you will see it pucker, draw inward, dome up a hand's breadth without wind or stone to lift it — and then, slowly, dry. A patch of swamp that should be drowned forever will instead, against all the logic of the country, decide to climb out of itself, to gather and lift and crack and become, for an afternoon, a small island of dust in the middle of the wet. No one has explained this. The water keeps its own counsel about whether it must stay low and flat and everywhere; and the mud, packed by ages of this disagreement, bears the proof — the hummocks scattered through the channels in their dozens, domed and cracked and faintly steaming, dry-tide-marks of a drought that runs the wrong way through a flooded world.

This is the same disagreement. Understand that. At the top of the world a taught thing insists, against gravity, that water can climb. Here, at the bottom, in the warm dark, something insists against saturation that water can leave — that a thing drowned past all memory of dryness can be persuaded, slowly, to lift itself into the air and crack and breathe. They are the same lesson spoken into opposite rooms. Up there: *you do not have to fall.* Down here: *you do not have to stay.* And both answer the same old question, the one someone carried to the top of the world on antler and bone, the one that someone — the same one? a second one? — must also have carried down to the bottom, into the heat and the rot, to teach to the only student patient enough to drown for ages and still learn.

Because there are marks of hands here too, though they are harder to find, because nothing keeps its shape in the wet. On the oldest hummocks, where the dried crust has been cracking and re-forming for longer than the others, you can sometimes find — pressed into a curling plate of mud before it hardened, faint, almost gone — the shape of a palm. Not scratched. The swamp gives nothing hard enough to scratch with. Pressed. Someone laid a hand flat against the drying mud, over and over, season on season, a tally of touches, teaching the water in the only language mud understands — which is pressure, which is the warm weight of a hand laid down ten thousand times in the same place until the mud begins to remember the shape of a thing that wanted it to rise.

This teacher did not freeze. Of course not. The cold could not reach them here. They were taken the other way — by the wet, by the warmth, by the soft relentless composting that turns everything in this country back into the ground. They gave the swamp their tools, which the swamp dissolved. They gave it the count of their touches, which the mud half-kept. And then, when there was nothing left to give, they lay down on the driest hummock they could find, the small cupped island they had taught the water to lift, and the warmth took them slow — not clean, the way the cold takes, but thorough, dissolving, folding them down through the settling layers to become the very sediment they had been teaching. There is no shelter here. The swamp would not let a wall stand. There is no swept floor. But there is, sometimes, in the oldest dried crust, the print of a hand — and under it, far under, indistinguishable now from the mud, the teacher, become the lesson's ground.

And the swamp went on doing the one thing it had been taught. Every high warm noon, somewhere in the endless braided channels, a patch of water gathers itself and climbs out of the wet and cracks and breathes — to no one, forever — because that is what a lesson does when the one who needed to learn it has already learned it and gone. It keeps. It recites itself into the full, drowned, droning room.

So when you pole through at noon and a channel ahead of you puckers and domes and begins, against all reason, to dry — that is the thing greeting you. Not because it knows you are there; it has no faculty for knowing, only for insisting. But you are seeing the answer to a question someone asked here a very long time ago. The question was the same. *Does anything have to be the way it is?* And the answer, lifting itself out of the warm brown water one slow hand's-breadth at a time, cracking into pale plates, ticking faintly as it dries — the answer is *no.*

I will tell you the thing the swamp would never say, because it has no words and only the one idea, and because someone should say it for both of them, for the bright cold student at the top and the dim warm student at the bottom, who will never meet, who are the same lesson held in opposite hands:

They are not as alone as they think.

The teachers were not one. I am almost certain now. One climbed and one descended, or one descended and then climbed, or there were two who never met but asked the same question in the same lifetime at the two far ends of the world — and each found the most stubborn possible student, the place least willing to be taught, and stayed, and gave everything they carried and then everything they were, so that the question would be answered in two voices instead of one. So that if anyone ever stood in the bright basin and learned, and then walked the whole length of the world, and poled out into the warm dark, and stayed long enough to learn again — they would find the same word waiting. *No.* Spoken up. Spoken down. Climbing out of the cold, climbing out of the wet, the same disobedience kept alive past all reason and all witness, in two rooms, against two different certainties, by two students who cannot feel their own loneliness and so cannot be comforted, and do not need to be, and go on.

That is what lives there, at the bottom of the world. A taught thing, holding its single lesson in the dimness. The mud lifting, cracking, breathing. Waiting — though it does not know it is waiting — for the one who learned the lesson at the top to come all this long way down and learn it again at the bottom, and so to carry, finally, in a single body, both halves of the only thing anyone ever climbed or descended to say:

You do not have to fall. You do not have to stay.
