# The Cistern at the Bottom of the Fall

There is a place that does not breathe at all, and it lies directly beneath the other, though "beneath" is a poor word for a distance like this.

Follow the water down. That is the thread. Up at the top of the air, the machine breathes out moisture and the green drinks it before it can fall — but not all of it. There is always a remainder. There is always more breath than the green can catch, a surplus of wet that beads on the glass conduits and gathers in the cupped tiles and at last, when the cupped tiles brim, finds the seam in the floor. The riveted brass has crept apart over the long thermal centuries, and through that crept seam the surplus goes. One drop at a time. Down.

This is where it goes.

## The Length of the Falling

You must understand the *drop* before you can understand the room, because the drop is half of what the room is.

It is enormous. The machine sits at almost three measures of elevation and the water it spills must travel nearly all of that distance straight down through a shaft that the builders — the same vanished builders — bored or found or grew, no one can say. The shaft is narrow and black and the water does not fall freely in it; it slides, it threads, it follows the wall, gathering and losing itself a thousand times in the dark. A single drop that leaves the machine at noon will not arrive at the bottom for the better part of a day. It falls so slowly, against the wall, through the black, that falling becomes a kind of *living* — the drop has a whole long life in the shaft, alone, in the dark, and the life consists entirely of descending.

And the drop is loud, at first. It leaves the roaring hollow with the roar still in it, carries the memory of the deafening down into the shaft — but the shaft swallows sound. The walls drink the roar the way the moss drank the breath. By the time the drop is halfway down it has gone quiet, and by the time it nears the bottom it makes no sound at all that anything could hear. The water arrives at the cistern *silenced*. Whatever it knew of the engine's shouting, it has forgotten on the way.

## The Made Thing, Below

The cistern was built. Of course it was — by the same hands, in the same lost age, with the same intention there is no longer anyone to name. But it was built to be the opposite of the engine, and the opposite has held.

It is *vast*. Where the machine is the size of a small chapel, the cistern is the size of a drowned cathedral, a single great vaulted chamber hewn and lined and sealed, with a ceiling so high and so dark that the falling-shaft's mouth, up there, is only a paler smudge in the black, a place where the dark is slightly less. The walls are not brass. They are stone, fitted stone, dressed and mortared and gone slick with mineral over the ages, and where the stone ends there is no green, no pipe, no flower, because nothing here breathes and nothing here grows in the way things grow above.

The floor of the chamber is water. That is the whole floor. A black still sheet of it, filling the vaulted dark from wall to wall, so flat and so motionless that you cannot at first tell it is water at all — you think it is a floor of polished jet, until the single drop arrives from the shaft, falls the last of its long fall, and touches the surface.

## The Arrival

This is the *dynamic* of the place, and it is the whole of it: a drop arrives, and a ring spreads.

One drop. It falls from the smudge of paler dark and crosses the last of the void and meets the black sheet and makes a sound — *one* sound, a single clean note, a *plink* that is the only sound the cistern owns. And from the point of meeting, a ring. The ring spreads out across the still black water, perfect and slow and widening, traveling the whole vast floor, reaching at last the dressed stone walls and returning, fainter, crossing itself, fading. And then the water is still again. And it stays still. For a long time it stays still — because the drops fall on the schedule of the lungs, far above, ninety seconds apart, and most of the breath is caught by the green, so that only every dozenth breath spills a drop, only every dozenth breath sends one down the long shaft to arrive, a day later, here.

So a ring crosses the cathedral of water perhaps once an hour. Perhaps less. And between the rings: stillness, total, black, vast, and *silent* — the silence the room was built to hold, the silence the shaft made by drinking the roar. Where the engine is sound nearly at the top of the scale, the cistern is sound nearly at the bottom: one clean note an hour, and around it, nothing. You could hear your own heart here. You could hear the blood in your own ears, and you would, because there is nothing else, and the nothing would become unbearable, and then it would become the only company you had, and you would listen to it, and wait, like everything here waits, for the next drop to fall.

## The Light, Below

There is no middling mercy of light here. Where the engine withholds nothing from the eyes, the cistern withholds *everything*. It is dark almost past the bottom of the scale — only that one paler smudge of shaft-mouth far above, where a ghost of the high glass-filtered sun leaks down the long throat and dies before it reaches the water. The water does not reflect it. There is not enough light to reflect.

So this is a place you know by ear and not by eye. You know the vastness because the one note travels so far before it fades. You know the height because the drop takes so long to arrive. You know the stillness because you have heard nothing for so long. The cistern is built for a sense the engine has no use for — the engine *shows*, the cistern *sounds*, and the two of them together make a whole creature: a thing that shouts at the top of the air and listens at the bottom of the dark, a body with its loud bright lungs above and its silent black ear below, joined by a single thread of falling water down a shaft a day long.

## What Lives in the Dark

And here is the contradiction the cistern asks you to hold, the way the engine asked you to hold the green at the impossible height: things *live* here, in the silence, in the dark, in the cold still water that almost never moves.

They are blind, the things that live here. Of course they are. There has been nothing to see for longer than seeing would have been worth keeping. They are pale — not the wet-paper pale of the moths, who at least had a milky light to be pale *in*, but the no-color of things that were never lit, a translucence, a glassiness, flesh that gave up on the idea of surface. They are fish, or were, the great-grandchildren of fish, drifting in the black sheet with their eyes grown over and sealed like the dead flues above where the metal birds nest. They do not swim so much as hang. They keep their distances exactly as the moths do, exactly as the grazers and the lone shrieking birds do — the same rule of the same world, the great hollow filled and not populated, the watched and the alone both true — but here the distances are kept in the dark and in the silence, and so the keeping is even purer, even lonelier, a solitude with no light to confirm that the others are real.

How do they live? The same way everything in this world lives. *On the breath.* The drop that falls from the shaft carries with it the last of what the engine breathed — the dissolved warmth, the trace of the green's sugar, the ghost of the high flowers' pollen washed off the conduits, an entire thin economy of nourishment that fell a day to get here. And the blind pale fish know — not in words, never in words, the way the grazers know the smooth road and the moths know the flower — they know to be *there*, at the point where the drop will fall, when the drop falls.

This is the whole of their lives. They hang in the black, motionless, listening with whatever a fish listens with, through the long silent stretches between drops. And then, far above, ninety seconds of breathing a day ago becomes a single descending bead, and the bead arrives, and makes its one clean note — and the blind fish, who cannot see it coming, who have only the sound and the faint warm spreading of it through the cold, converge. Slowly. Patiently. On the schedule of the lungs they have never seen and could not imagine. They come to the spreading ring and they take the warmth and the sugar and the ghost of pollen out of the water, and then they drift apart again into their kept distances, and the water goes still, and the dark goes total, and they wait the long hour for the engine, far above, to spill them another mouthful of its breath.

They do not know there is an engine. They do not know there is a top of the air. They know the drop, and the note, and the warmth in the cold, and the schedule — open to the arriving drop, close into the dark waiting — which is the same single law as above, *open on the warm breath, close in the cold pause,* slowed and darkened and dropped a day's fall down, but the same. The same rule, kept by blind things in silence who have never breathed and never will.

## The Drowned Tenders

And here too — because it is the same builders, the same vanished age — there is the absence that is the oldest inhabitant.

There are stairs. They descend the dressed stone wall in a long spiral, cut into the rock, going down into the black water and *under* it, vanishing beneath the still black sheet to whatever floor the cathedral has, far below the surface, that no living eye has seen in an age. The stairs are worn. The same wear as above — the smooth-topped tread, the rubbed groove at hand-height along the wall where something gripped and steadied and descended, again and again, for a very long time. The tenders came down here too. The same habit of being cared for, or its twin: someone came down the spiral on the schedule of the lungs, to do here whatever the grooves above were doing there — to clear, to scrape, to keep the long shaft from choking, to make sure the drop could still fall.

They do not come anymore. The lower stairs are drowned and the upper stairs lead down to the black sheet and stop, and the groove at hand-height runs into the water and is gone. Whatever the tenders did down here, the cistern does without them now, holds its silence and its dark and its one clean note an hour without any hand to keep it. The blind fish never use the stairs; they have no need of stone. The stairs are walked by nothing. They are the negative space of a vanished hand, here as above, the same hand perhaps, the same vanishing — a path that leads now only into the still black water and the patient blind things hanging in it, waiting for the breath of an engine they will never know is breathing, a day's fall above their heads, at the top of all the air, shouting that it is alive into a hollow that cannot hear it, while down here in the dark its quietest exhalation arrives as a single note, and is enough.

It is vast. It is the same one world. It is nearly empty.

And it never, ever, makes a sound — except once an hour, when the breath comes down.
