# The Outflow Fens

Follow the water. Not the fog — the fog stays, breathes in and out of the plateau and never leaves it. Follow the water that *escapes*, the overflow the Drivers push up faster than the Unbuilders can recover it, the threads that run down the fluted columns and pool in the closing hollows and, sometimes, at the plateau's southern lip, do not get swallowed in time. That water goes over the edge.

It goes over in no waterfall. There is no cliff here, no clean break a river could announce itself from. The plateau simply *tires* at its southern margin — the substrate thins, the engines run shallower, the great green architecture lowers itself in long terraced steps over the course of many miles until the towers are only knolls and the knolls are only swellings and the swellings, at last, lie down. And the water that the plateau could not reabsorb walks down those steps with it, gathering, until at the bottom there is no plateau at all and no cold at altitude, only a low warm flooded country that the runoff made and goes on making: the Outflow Fens.

The first thing the Fens do is be *warm*. This is the inversion, the thing that took the descent to find. Everything that defined the plateau is reversed here, not by opposition but by simple consequence — water runs downhill and downhill is lower and lower is warmer, and warmth is the one variable the cold place had subtracted from everything. Up there the cold slowed all that should have been fast, and the plants answered slowness with mass and centuries. Down here the warmth speeds all that should be slow, and life answers speed with *waste*. The Fens are profligate where the plateau was thrifty. The Fens throw lives away by the thousand every hour because they can afford to, because there is always more water arriving, always more warmth, always more.

And the second thing the Fens do is be *loud*.

You feel it in your chest before your ear sorts it. After the held breath of the plateau, after miles of green roaring silence in which a beetle's foot was an event, the Fens hit you like a struck bell that will not stop ringing. Everything here has a voice and uses it. The water itself is loud — not the threading whisper of condensation but slap and gurgle and suck, a million small mouths of mud opening and closing, the constant percussion of things falling into water and things climbing out of it. And over the water-noise, layered thick as the substrate was thick, the sound of the fauna, which is not scarce here. It is the opposite of scarce. It is the medium.

## The Singers

The birds came down. Not these birds — their ancestors, the same wrong silent stock that learned over centuries on the plateau that to sing was unthinkable, that absorbed the rule so deep the won't became a can't. Some of them, generations back, followed the water over the southern lip the way the water did, dropping down the terraces into the warmth, and in the warmth a thing happened that could not have happened above: a bird sang, and *nothing swallowed it*.

There is no silence here built to take the loud thing down. There is no machine that incorporates the announcer. A bird that sings in the Fens is simply a bird that has announced itself, and the announcement does not break a rule, because the Fens have no rule but appetite and announcement is irrelevant to appetite. So the can't unlearned itself. It took a long time — unlearning always takes longer than learning, and a can't bred true through ten thousand winters does not dissolve in one — but the warmth and the waste and the freedom worked on the silent stock the way the Unbuilders work on a fallen column, breaking down what was settled, and what they pumped back up was *song*.

Now the Fens are made of it. The descendants of the silent birds are the loudest creatures in this whole world, and they sing without stopping, in dawn choruses and dusk choruses and a continuous daylong middle register that never fully drops, because here there is no reason to ever fully drop. They have the apparatus their ancestors carried unused for centuries — the lungs, the throats, the whole grammar of song held mute through ten thousand winters of glide-and-angle — and they have spent it all at once, an inheritance burned through in a few profligate generations, the way the Fens burn through everything.

They are not pale. That went too, with the cold. Where the plateau bleached its tenants frost-colored and felt-soft, the Fens have dyed their birds in fevers — wet greens and bruised purples and a hot orange at the throat that swells when they sing, which is always. They are still the same lineage. If you caught one and held it and felt the flat glide-muscle along the wing, the muscle the plateau-birds used to angle their silence, you would feel the old grammar still written in the body, vestigial now, overwritten by song the way the Fens overwrite everything the moment it arrives.

## What the Fens Are Made Of

Understand what runs this place, because like the plateau it is run, and like the plateau the engine is not built but grown — except here nothing is compressed, nothing is patient, nothing accumulates. The Fens are an engine of pure *throughput*. The water arrives warm and rich, carrying down from the plateau the faint enrichment of every rendered beetle and fallen Watcher and dissolved bird that the Unbuilders pumped back into the threads — and the Fens do not store this. They *spend* it.

The mud is alive the way the plateau's substrate was alive, but where the substrate was old green packed down into geological patience, the mud is a froth, a boil, a turnover so fast you can watch it. Things grow in the mud in hours and die in the mud in hours and the dying feeds the growing with no Unbuilder needed, no district of differentiated appetite, because here decay is not a specialized organ — decay is *ambient*, the warm water does it everywhere at once, dissolving the dead back into the soup before they have finished cooling. The plateau took ten thousand winters to find its shape and hold it. The Fens find a thousand shapes an hour and hold none of them. Both are total turbulence. But the plateau's turbulence was silent because its machinery was soft and slow and built nothing that could clang, and the Fens' turbulence is deafening because its machinery is *fast and wet and crowded*, a billion small fast lives slapping against each other and against the water and against the air.

This is the same storm. You should hold onto that. The leaning, buckling, reweaving upheaval you watched fill a plateau valley to the horizon in perfect silence — it is *here too*, it never stopped, it only fell down the terraces into warmth and learned to make noise. Build, unbuild, build. The Fens do exactly what the plateau does. They take what arrives — and what arrives is, in part, what the plateau drove up and could not keep — and they run it through living matter and they render it back. The only difference is tempo, and tempo is only temperature, and temperature is only altitude, and altitude is only how far the water has fallen. It is one engine. It runs cold and quiet and slow at the top and warm and loud and fast at the bottom, and the water is the belt that connects them, the single belt looping down out of the fog-breathing plateau and pooling here in the boil, carrying the plateau's enrichment into the Fens' appetite, one organism pretending to be two because the cold made one end of it patient and the warmth made the other end of it frantic.

## The Waders

There are large slow things here too, and this is where the kinship shows itself plainest, because the Waders are what the Watchers became when their lineage came down the water.

The Watchers, up top, were animals that had given up almost everything an animal needs in a world with weather — given up haste, given up warmth, given up sound, and learned to read the slow storm so finely that three steps was always enough to outlast a closing mouth. Their descendants followed the same overflow the birds did, down the terraces into the warmth, and the warmth gave back everything the cold had taken. The Waders are *fast*. They are warm-blooded the way the plateau-stone is not, hot to the touch, hearts going like the beetle's foot magnified into thunder. They have grown long legs for the water and long necks for the reaching and they no longer settle and lean and absorb the slow seep — there is no slow seep here, there is only the boil, and you do not lean against a boil, you *strike* at it, you snatch the fast small lives out of the warm water as fast as they breed, which is very fast indeed.

But look at the eyes. The eyes are the same. Large and lidless and very dark, and they are still not for hunting — or rather, the *hunting* the Wader does with its body is so fast and reflexive it hardly needs the eyes, the strike is below thought. The eyes do what the Watchers' eyes did: they read the storm. A Wader stands in the boil and tracks it, reads where the mud is turning over richest, where the fast lives are densest, where the warm water is pushing hardest from below — and it is reading, though it could not know this, the same signal its ancestors read on the plateau, the signal of the buried engine pushing, except the engine here is the whole warm soup and the pushing is everywhere and the reading has to be constant where once it could be a day-long patient watch ending in three steps.

And the Wader still touches foreheads. When two of them cross in the boil they pause, the long necks dip, the dark eyes meet, and for a moment the frantic warm fast bodies do the one slow thing they have kept from the cold place: they press their heads together briefly, exchange whatever they exchange, and part. Nobody taught them this. The plateau taught their ancestors, and the warmth burned away the silence and the slowness and the felt and the patience and could not, for all its profligate heat, burn away that one gesture, the greeting two cold quiet things worked out in a place where sound died within a few meters and there was nothing to do but read the storm and, occasionally, touch a kindred head and confirm: *you are also here, also reading, also lasting.*

## What the Fens Know About You

This is the thing that should unsettle you here, having come down the water from a place that did not know you existed.

The Fens know. The Fens know everything that arrives, because knowing-by-incorporating is faster here than anywhere — the boil takes a fallen thing and has it dissolved and redistributed and resung as a bird's throat-note before the thing has finished dying. Up top you were *material that had not yet arrived*, water and carbon walking around, on your way down like everything else, and the machine could not even perceive you because its appetite was so slow and so patient that perception was beside the point. Here the appetite is fast enough to be indistinguishable from attention. A thousand small mouths turn toward your warmth the moment you wade in. The Waders' dark eyes find you and *read* you and decide you are too large to strike and too warm to ignore and file you, accurately, as a slow-arriving meal. The singing does not stop for you but it *changes around you*, the way water changes around a stone, the chorus bending to acknowledge the new mass in the boil.

So here is the whole shape of it, the one engine seen end to end:

On the plateau you were the loudest thing for miles and it did not matter, because the silence was how the place told you it had nothing to do with you, and the only voice in all that green roaring quiet was a frost-blue beetle ticking across a leaf — telling you, if you could read it, exactly where the buried engines pushed, and how long before they pushed their way around to you. You followed that. You followed the water the engines drove up and could not keep, down off the southern lip, down the terraces out of the cold.

And at the bottom of the water you arrived at the Fens, where the same engine runs hot, and the beetle's single tick has become a billion voices, and they are all the readout now, the whole warm soup announcing where the appetite pushes hardest — which is everywhere, which is here, which is around your warm slow legs in the boil. The plateau ignored you and told you so with silence. The Fens have decided to fold you in and are telling you so with song. It is the same telling. It always was. You only had to come down far enough, and get warm enough, to finally hear it.
