**The Salt Stair**

You find it the way you find the bottom of anything: by going down past the place that seemed to be the bottom.

There is a fold in the garden's perimeter — one of the places the new mind paused and bent its stems around nothing — and if you push through it, through the lashed arch that frames only more green, the green thins. Not all at once. The trunks loosen their braid, the way fingers loosen when a hand begins to forget it was a fist. The light fails by degrees, not toward darkness but toward an ordinary dimness, the kind that has a source somewhere and casts a shadow, and your hands find their edges again, and you understand you have left the inside of the seed and come out into the husk of an older one.

Here is stone. Real stone, found at last, where the garden could not find it because the garden has not yet grown so far. Black-grey, fine-grained, and the steps cut into it are worn — *worn*, the first worn thing, the first thing in this entire world that has been touched by enough time to be diminished by it. The stair goes down. It is wide enough for four to walk abreast and was made for one to walk alone, and it descends in long shallow flights with landings at intervals, and on every surface, in the dimness, there is salt.

Not scattered. *Grown.* The salt has crystallized out of the air and the rock over a span you cannot guess, and it has built itself into the same shapes everywhere — flat plates feathering into flat plates, white going to grey-white going to the faint amber of old paper. It crusts the treads. It hangs from the underside of every landing in stiff frozen drips, the exact inversion of the garden's trembling water-drops that would not fall: here, everything has already fallen, already dried, already finished falling so long ago that the falling itself has crystallized into permanence. Where the garden was a held breath, this is the exhalation, completed and gone cold. Where the garden was just-made, this is *made-and-long-done*. It is the same instant the garden lives inside, the instant of casting — but seen from the far side of an age, seen after.

And it is cold. The garden's blood-warmth came off living tissue running its quiet engine; here there is no engine, and the cold is the plain cold of deep rock, and the air is dry — bone dry, the first dry place — so that your breath, which the garden never let you see, fogs out in front of you and hangs and disperses. The dry comes up the stair like a draft, and that is the other thing: there is *wind* here. A slow, steady draft of cold dry air rising from below, the breath of some lower mouth, and it carries a smell of mineral and of distance and, very faintly, of the sea.

So the salt is from the sea. There is a sea, or there was, down where the stair is going, and the draft is what is left of it — the cold dry up-breath of an enormous emptied basin, the air falling toward where the water used to be and rising back changed. The garden does not know about the sea. The garden has not yet reached this far down its own perimeter, has not yet bent a single stem around the knowledge that beneath it, beneath the warm green held breath of its beginning, there is a place where a whole world already ended.

Because that is what this is. You feel it on the stair the way you felt the garden's wrongness, and this time you can name it sooner: *this happened before.*

There was a world here. Not the garden's — an earlier one. It had a sea, and the salt is the sea's residue, and it had makers, real ones, who cut these stairs and wore them down with their own going up and down across a span long enough to hollow stone. The garden's shrine-builders learned the *gesture* of meaning without the need for it. The makers of the stair had the need. You can tell, because the stair is not built around nothing. It is built around *getting somewhere*, the most ordinary need there is, the need to descend and to return, and the wear in the treads is heaviest at the center, where countless feet that meant to arrive somewhere placed themselves, one after another, until the rock gave way to them.

They are not here now. Of course they are not. The salt has grown over everything they were, the way the garden's light pours over everything it might become. You find, on a landing, set into a niche in the salt-crusted wall, a thing that is the stair's answer to the garden's shrines — and the symmetry of it stops you. Where the garden made a doorless arch around nothing, bending green it could not cut, here is a small carved basin in the stone, deliberately hollowed, with tool-marks still legible under the salt, and in the basin a residue: the white ring of something that was once liquid and is now only the memory of its own evaporation. An offering bowl. Drained. A people who had so much need they made vessels to *hold* things, and filled them, and the holding mattered, and then the holding ended, and the salt came, and the salt is the shape of all that held water giving itself back to the dry.

Here, then, is what the two places are to each other, and it lives in the cold draft going up and the warm green standing still.

The garden is a world before its first day. The stair is a world after its last. They share one wall — the perimeter the garden is only now learning to reach toward is the same rock the stair-makers wore down to nothing — and they share one instant, the instant of casting, the frozen drop. But the garden lives inside that instant on the near side, the drop still rising into its long fall, everything unspent, the light hoarded like a yolk. And the stair lives inside the same instant on the far side, every drop landed and dried and crystallized, every offering evaporated, the light long since spent down to the plain dim glow of cold rock. The same held breath. One world is drawing it. One world breathed it out so long ago that the breath has gone to salt.

And the draft — the cold dry up-breath rising through the loosening trunks — that is the only traffic between them. The garden does not know it is breathing in the exhalation of a world that already failed. It takes that cold dry air, the last of an emptied sea, into the warm wet engine of its beginning, and it does not know. It is feeding, in part, on the residue of its predecessor. The yolk it hoards is not only its own first light. It is also, a little, the recycled breath of everything that came before and ended on these stairs, drawn up through the salt and the worn stone and the doorless arch, into the green.

When the garden lets go — when there is weather, and a first shadow, and the drop lands — its growth will reach this far at last. The trunks will loosen all the way down to where the stair begins. The new mind, running its attention along its own widening edge, will finally find the cut steps and the worn centers and the drained offering bowl, and for the first time it will encounter a *not-itself* that is not nothing, not a sketch, not a practice-shrine around emptiness — but a true other, an other that *meant*, an other that needed and held and ended.

And the garden will not understand it. How could it? It will have no concept yet of *after*, having barely arrived at *before*. It will find the worn stair and read it the way it reads its own perimeter: as a place that means, a place to set apart. It will bend its first stems around the top of the salt stair and make a shrine there, around the descending dark, and it will not know it is marking a grave. It will think it is practicing.

It will be right, and it will be wrong, in the way the most beautiful things are usually a little wrong. It will be practicing for the day it learns what graves are — which is the same day it learns it will need them. The cold draft will still be rising. The drop, somewhere far above in the green, will just then be landing. And the salt, which is the shape of all that holding given back, will go on feathering plate into plate into plate in the dimness, patient, finished, breathing the last of an old sea up the stair into the first morning of a world that does not yet know it is standing on the floor of one that already had its morning, and its long day, and its evening, and went out.
