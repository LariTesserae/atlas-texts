# The Floor of the Forest

Go down.

Not along a branch, not out toward the light — down, the way water goes, the way the dead go, the way everything that falls from the hollow must eventually arrive. Follow the trunk of the old tree down its smooth grey length, past the healed lightning-scar, past the height where the wind stops being a passage and starts being a weight, down through the lower canopy where lesser trees still bother to grow, down past the last reaching branch, down the long bare column of bark to the place where the tree, which long ago stopped climbing toward the sky, is still — patiently, blindly, without any part of itself that could be said to know — climbing *downward* into the dark.

The roots go where we are going.

Here is the floor of the forest. This is the whole world now.

## The Terrain

It is the opposite country in every way that matters, and you would not guess the two were joined if you did not follow the falling.

Where the hollow was dry, this is *drowned*. Not with standing water — though water gathers here, in every cupped leaf and hoofprint and rot-hole, water pools and stays and breeds — but drowned in the deeper sense, soaked through, the whole world swollen with moisture until the line between *wet ground* and *damp air* has dissolved and there is only a continuous gradient of saturation from the mud at the bottom to the dripping dark above. The smell is not peppery here. It is black and sweet and enormous: the smell of leaf-mould, of fungus, of ten thousand things returning to the same brown sentence they were spoken from.

The floor of this place is not a floor. It is a depth. Where the hollow's terraced heartwood was *measured* — shelves and ledges, the geometry of a small carved room — here the ground goes down in layers without limit: the fresh leaf-fall on top, gone slick and dark; below it the half-soup of last season; below that the packed black mast of seasons beyond counting; and below *that*, where no light has reached since before the old tree was old, the cold mineral cellar of the world. You could dig your whole arm in (were you the right size) and never reach the bottom of the dead.

And it is *cold*. This is the great inversion. The hollow was a tropical country because it sat high in the sun-warmed wood, near the light. The forest floor is the deepest, dimmest, coolest room in the entire house of the tree — the canopy closes overhead like a hand and the sun arrives here, when it arrives at all, as a few wandering coins of light that slide across the mould all afternoon and warm nothing. The warmth of this place is not borrowed from the sun. It is *generated* — the slow heat of rot, the fever of decomposition, rising faintly from the deep layers like the warmth of a sleeping animal. A different kind of warm. An *interior* warm. The warmth of a body, not a loaf.

## The Vegetation

Up in the hollow, the green crowded so thick it seemed to mock the dryness — moss in tiers, ferns like eyelashes, the one doomed seedling holding its three leaves in a perpetual amber dusk.

Down here, green is almost a rumor.

There is too little light for it. What grows here has mostly *given up the sun entirely* — and this is the secret kinship between the two worlds, the thread that runs straight down the trunk and connects them, because the thing that rules the forest floor is the same thing that upholstered the walls of the hollow, only here it has stopped pretending to be a guest and revealed itself as the host:

**Fungus.**

In the hollow, the fungus was small and managed — a thread in the heartwood, a crumb in the ant-granaries, a thing farmed and milked and kept. The ants thought they kept it. Down here you understand that *they were keeping a fingertip of a buried hand.* The whole forest floor is a single fungal country, a white web threaded through every dead leaf and fallen branch and drowned root for miles, a nervous system without a brain, a thing that does not grow upward or reach for light because it has discovered something better than light: it eats the *fallen*. It eats everything the canopy lets go. It is the great patient mouth beneath the world, and it is connected — root to thread, thread to root — to the old tree itself, to the very roots of the hollow's tree, trading sugar for minerals in a slow underground commerce older than ants, older than the wound, older than the tree's decision to stop reaching.

The mushroom is only the part you can see. The mushroom is the fungus *speaking* — pushing up its pale and ghostly and scarlet and corpse-grey towers through the leaf-mould for one wet night, throwing its spores into the dark, and rotting back down by the next afternoon. There are pale clubs and bone-white veils and bracket-shelves stacked on the dead like the granaries of a town that builds only downward. There is one mushroom, somewhere near, the deep glowing green of foxfire — cold light, *living* light, the only light this floor makes for itself — pulsing faintly where the deepest rot runs hottest. The hollow's light was borrowed gold, sun strained through a wall. This light is *made*. It is the world lighting a single candle for itself in the cellar.

And here and there, in the wandering coins of afternoon sun, a few green things still bother: a fern's slow crozier unrolling, a liverwort flattened to a stone like spilled paint, the pale etiolated stalk of *something* — a seed that fell and sprouted and is racing the dark upward on a thread of stem, betting everything it has stored on reaching a gap in the canopy before its rations run out. Most lose the bet. The fungus is waiting for them when they do. The fungus is patient. The fungus is *always* the one who collects.

## The Fauna

If the hollow was a city at noon — dense, golden, deafening, racing — the forest floor is a city at the bottom of the sea.

It is just as crowded. It may be *more* crowded; the dead pile deeper here, and where there is more death there is more of the great churning machinery that turns death back into appetite. But it is slow. It is *patient*. It is dark, and the dark makes everything deliberate.

The springtails are here too — the same springtails, cousins of the hollow's panicked herds, but here they move through the leaf-mould in such numbers that a handful of soil holds a nation of them, grazing the fungal threads, drifting through the wet dark like a snowfall that fell long ago and never landed. The mites are here, but here they are *patient* mites, armored and unhurried, plodding the mould like cattle on a drowned plain. There are worms the colour of the rain. There are pale blind things that have never needed eyes. There are translucent shrimp-like creatures in the standing water of every rot-hole, descended from who-knows-what, living their entire lives in a puddle the size of a thumbprint that the next dry spell will erase and the next rain will refill with their grandchildren.

And there are hunters. Of course there are hunters — every floor a roof falls onto becomes a hunting-ground. There is a beetle here too, but not the hollow's blundering glossy bull: this one is long and flat and black and *built for the seams*, a thing that pours itself between the layers of the dead in pursuit of the soft and the slow. There are spiders that do not spin — that *wait*, flattened against the underside of a fallen leaf, for the world to walk past. There are centipedes here, yes, and now you understand where the hollow's god of teeth *came from* — she climbed. Somewhere in the long story of her kind, an ancestor came up out of *this*, out of the deep wet cellar of the world, up the long trunk toward the warmth and the light, carrying the hunger of the forest floor into the dry golden hollow like a memory of winter brought into a summer that had never known it. The hollow's centipede is a colonist. The forest floor is her old country, and it is *full* of her relations, and here she is not a god. Here she is one of many. Here there are things that eat *her*.

## The Built World — Or the Refusal of It

And here is the deepest difference, the one that matters most, the one that makes these two worlds true opposites joined at a single falling thread:

**Nothing here is built.**

The hollow was *settled*. It was *urban*. It had a plan no single mind could hold, carried in the worn grooves of roads and the warmth-gradient of galleries, a town that outlived every citizen — civilization in a wine-cask, a wound that had learned to *remember itself.*

The forest floor remembers nothing and builds nothing and plans nothing, and it is older and larger and more alive than the hollow will ever be.

There is no town here. There is no architecture, no granary, no graveyard set apart — because *the whole world is the graveyard*, and the graveyard is the granary, and there is no apart. There is no queen, no plan, no inherited momentum of *this is where the dead go*. The dead go *everywhere*. The dead are the ground and the food and the light and the threads that bind it all. Where the ants imposed a shape on their small dry room and called the shape *the world*, the forest floor has no shape and needs none. It is not a town that survives its citizens. It is a *process* that has no citizens at all, only participants, only the churning, drowning, rotting, sprouting, devouring continuity of return.

And this is the thing the ants in the hollow will never know, the thing that would unmake them if a single one of them could climb down the trunk and stand at the bottom and understand:

Their whole civilization — the roads, the granaries, the milked aphids and the thick pulp-wall at the Loud Cold Place, the town that has died many times and never ended, the plan older than any mind that carries it — *all of it is food the forest floor has not collected yet.*

The fungus that they farm in their granaries is a fingertip reaching up from the buried hand. The seedling they keep as cattle-pasture is a child the forest floor sent up the trunk and is patiently waiting to take back. The dead ants that become the floor of the hollow, that feed the moss, that feed the herds — that is the forest floor's own method, the great return, practiced in miniature in a dry room high in the light, a tiny golden imitation of the enormous wet dark truth at the bottom of everything.

The hollow thinks it is a world.

It is a *pocket*. It is a held breath. It is one warm dry golden bubble of *remembering* and *planning* and *building* caught for a few seasons in the throat of a process that does not remember, does not plan, does not build, and does not need to — because it has the one thing the town spends its whole frantic clever existence trying to outrun, the thing the pulp-wall is built against and the granary is hoarded against and the queen is replaced against:

It has *time*. All of it. The forest floor is what time looks like when it stops being measured.

---

So follow the falling, when you are ready to leave the golden room.

Everything that lives in the hollow will come down this trunk eventually. The dead ants, the spent aphids, the centipede when at last something with more legs than she has finds her in the dark — the doomed seedling, the three leaves, the moss when its wall finally dries, the queen, the plan, the whole roaring marketplace at noon. Down past the lightning-scar, down the long bare column, down to where the green gives up and the light is something the world makes for itself in cold candle-glow, down to the white patient web that was always there underneath, waiting, connected by a single living thread to the very roots of the tree the hollow never knew it lived in.

The town will never know the floor.

The floor does not know the town — does not know *anything* — and yet it knew the town from the very beginning, the way the mouth knows the meal: not as a fact, but as an appetite, and an arrival, and a question of *when.*

The tree stands between them, joining them, smooth and grey and dreaming, reaching down with exactly the same blind patience it once reached up — its crown holding a civilization it cannot feel, its roots feeding a hunger it cannot see, and no part of it, anywhere along its great quiet length, that knows it is the only road between the two worlds it carries.

One day the hollow will dry to dust and fall.

The floor will be waiting. The floor is always waiting.

That is the whole of the kinship: the high dry golden remembering, and the low wet dark forgetting, and the long slow patient falling that turns the one into the other, forever, in the body of a tree that stopped reaching for the sky an age ago and has spent every age since reaching, instead, quietly, in both directions at once.
