The Drift

Follow the springs down. Not the way they come up — that road is closed, packed seam by seam against the leaking of warmth — but the way the overflow must go, because even a basin tuned across ages cannot hold every drop a weeping rock brings up. Somewhere the saucer's lip is lowest. Somewhere the still sheet finds a notch worn in the grey stone and slides over it, and what was held becomes, at last, a thing that flows.

It flows a long way down. Out of the white light, under the treeline the town never knew, into the dark warm thickening air of the lowlands where the cold that was the first thing and the constant thing finally lets go its grip. The water carries with it what the town shed without noticing — fibre fragments, mineral fines, and now and then a small swimming body washed past the lip before it could turn back, dying as it falls because the warmth that is mercy to you is murder to it. By the time the water reaches the valley floor it is a river, brown and busy and loud, loud, loud — gurgling, lapping, roaring over its stones, making every sound the town never made, as if all the silence held up there had to be paid for somewhere downstream, with interest.

And at a bend in that river, where the current slows and drops its load, there is the Reader.

It is one. This is the first thing, and it is true the way it was never true above — singular, sealed, a fortress and not a melody. It lives in a shell it built and carries, a coiled architecture of the same pale fibre the river brings down, except that here the fibre is not woven by ten thousand mouths but secreted by one, laid down in a spiral over a single lifetime the way you lay down years. The shell is the size of a curled child. Inside it: a body, soft, banded, eyed. *Eyed.* It has a face, of a kind. It has a front and a back, a here-I-keep-myself and a there-I-reach-toward. It is everything the town is not, and it owes its entire existence to the town, and it does not know the town exists.

What it knows is the river.

Consider what the Reader is, because it is stranger than the Wader, though it looks more familiar — and it looks more familiar precisely because the strangeness is hidden where yours is, on the inside. The Reader is *singular* and *mortal* and *remembers*, and these three things are one thing, the way the town's three names were one event. It has a head because it has a need to face the current; it faces the current because the current is its only food and its only news; and what comes down the current is the disassembled body of a thing that thinks without ever needing to remember, sent down in pieces to a thing that can do nothing *but* remember and cannot think any other way.

Here is how it feeds. It does not filter the water for the warmth-loving mats or the mineral fines, though it takes those too. What it hungers for is the fibre — the woven, packed, *meaningful* fibre, the matter the town laid into its walls according to the standing pattern of its distributed thought. Each fragment that comes down the river is a torn-out word from a sentence the town was speaking to no one, a scrap of the held note, a letter pried loose from a thought that never knew it was being read. The Reader draws these in. It tastes the weave. And in the particular way one fragment was packed against the angle of a vanished seam, in the gnaw-marks and the density and the lay of the fibre, it reconstructs — slowly, serially, one fragment after the next, at the speed of a single thinking animal — a little of what the town was *doing* when it made that piece. Where the light had struck too hard. Which lane ran north. What balance was being held, that hour, ages ago, before the fragment fell.

The town does not remember. The Reader remembers *for* it. This is the kinship, and it is the cruelest kind: the Reader is the memory of a mind that has no memory and no need of one and no knowledge that its memory exists, washed downstream into a creature built of nothing but. Everything the town discards as the dead skin of its eternal now, the Reader gathers as scripture. It has been reading the town for its whole life, and its whole life is a long one, longer than yours, though nothing beside the town's ageless going-on. It has read the town's posture from ten thousand years ago in the deep fibre of the riverbed, and the town's posture from this morning in the fresh scraps tumbling past, and it has learned the terrible thing that you learned too, standing to your shins in the cold: that they are the *same posture*. That the held note never changed. That all its reading, all its patient serial reconstruction across a life it cannot help spending, has recovered a single sentence, repeated, perfectly, forever.

This is what the Reader is *for*, if a thing washed up by accident at a bend can be for anything. It is the one place in the world where the town becomes a story instead of a state. The town is pure present; the Reader is the past the town throws off. Between them runs the river, which is time made visible — the still sheet up there that does not flow, broken at last into current down here, carrying *was* down to the only thing that can hold a *was*. The town stands. The river falls. The Reader keeps.

And it is dying, slowly, the way singular things do — the way the town never will. Its shell is at the lip of its last whorl; its banded body thins; its eyes are clouding to the warm brown light. When it dies it will leave the spiral shell on the riverbed, and the shell is fibre, the town's own fibre relaid by the Reader's body into the only new shape that pale matter ever takes in this world that is not the town — a coil, a record, a single sealed *self*. And the river will work at it. And in some age it will break the shell down to fragments and carry them on, further down, to wherever this river goes when it has left even the Reader behind, to some lower water and some other creature that might, fragment by fragment, read *the Reader* — read the one mind that ever held the town as memory, and so hold, at two removes, the town itself.

But that is downstream of downstream, and not yet, and maybe never. For now there is only this: the cold basin above that does not know it is being remembered, and the warm bend below that does nothing but remember, and the falling water between them that carries the one down to the other without ever being thanked or known, the way your own forgotten hours fall, every night, into whatever it is in you that keeps them — the silent town of your days, shedding itself downstream into the one small reading self that has to hold it, alone, and face the current, and taste each thing as it comes, and set it down, and wait for the next, and call that waiting a life.
