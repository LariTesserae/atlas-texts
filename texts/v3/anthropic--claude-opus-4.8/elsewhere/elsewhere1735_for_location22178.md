# The Salt Cellar Where the Coals Are Born

Go down.

Not down through the cathedral — there is no down through the cathedral, only more of it, heartwood like basalt all the way to wherever the bottom finally consents to be. Go down *out* of it. Follow the water.

Because the water has to go somewhere. The slow rain that settles in the high cold chambers, the weeping that runs down the great fins, the overflow of the lagging pools — all of it gathers, and gathered water descends, and it does not descend forever inside a living thing that keeps healing its own channels closed. At some point the water reaches the cathedral's hem, the lowest weeping wall, where the buttress-fins finally splay out and flatten and the grown massif gives way to something that is not grown at all.

This is the first surprise of the place, and the people of the breathing dark would not believe it if they ever came this far, which they do not: here, at last, there is rock. Real rock — mineral, dead, never alive, the world's original bone laid bare where the great plant chose not to build. The cathedral's whole law breaks at its own edge. *Everything is made, and everything that made it was alive* — true up there, false down here, and the seam between the two truths runs along the hem in a line you could walk, with living wall weeping on one side and dry stone on the other, close enough to touch both at once.

The water reaches the stone and spreads and slows and, in this lower, warmer, drier air, begins to do the thing the cathedral's water never could. It begins to leave.

## The Pans

Picture a vast shallow basin of pale stone, tilted just enough that the water creeps across it rather than pooling, a sheet of moving wet no deeper than your ankle, spreading wider as it goes, thinning as it spreads, until the far edge is not water at all but only the memory of where water was — a crust, a glitter, a white.

This is the Salt Cellar. The cathedral's runoff is not pure. All that breathing tissue, all those healing walls, all the minerals the great plant pulls up from wherever it roots — the water carries a load of it down, dissolved, invisible, and when the water reaches the open pans and the dry air takes it back up into the sky, the load stays behind. Salt. Not only salt — the people here have names for nine kinds of leaving, and only one of them is what you would put on food — but salt is the word, the way *fire* was the word up above for a whole fearful art.

The basin evaporates the cathedral's tears and keeps what was dissolved in them.

So this is a bright place. Understand how strange that is, coming from above. The cathedral is dim, weak tea and old jade, light you have to wait for. The Salt Cellar is *blinding*. The pale stone, the white crust, the sheet of standing water throwing the open sky straight back up — there is a true sky here, not a membrane-filtered suggestion of one but the real fierce thing, and it is reflected and doubled by every wet surface until the whole basin glares. The people here squint. Their eyes are not wide and dark and greedy for glow; they are narrowed, lashed thick, set deep under heavy brows, made for too much light rather than too little. Where the cathedral folk are pale and faintly luminous, these are dark-skinned, sun-cured, and they do not glow at all — there is nothing here to drink that would make them, and anyway who would notice a glow in all this glare.

They are the same people. This is the thing to hold onto. Generations ago — the cathedral folk have a word, *to begin to go dark*, that means dying; these people have a word that means *to have gone down and not come back*, and it is the same migration told from the far end. Some scatter, some lineage, followed the water out of the breathing dark and into the light and stayed, and the stone reshaped them as thoroughly as the hollow shaped their cousins, and now the two would not know each other. But the hands remember. Both peoples have the same hands — large, broad-padded, clever — and both peoples knap, and weave, and carry water in bloom-husks, though down here the husks come not from blooms but from trade, carried up-current, which is a thing I will get to.

## What the Cellar Makes, and Why the Coals Live Here

The cathedral folk keep fire as their highest art and highest terror. They cup it in clay and feed it single fibers and never let it forget it is small, because the whole world above is fuel and is alive and a loose fire would burn a living god.

Here is the secret that the people of the breathing dark do not know, that they could not survive without, and that they have buried under so much ritual they no longer recognize it as a fact: *they did not invent fire, and they cannot make it.* The damp up there is total. There is no dry thing in the cathedral, no tinder, no spark that would catch — the world is too wet to ignite, which is the same mercy that makes it so dangerous, because once a fire exists in all that fuel it would never stop. The cathedral folk carry coals. They have *always* carried coals. They feed them and bank them and pass them parent to child and never let them die, because if a scatter's coal goes out, no one up there can light another.

The coals come from here.

In the Salt Cellar there is dryness — the first real dryness in the world — and there is sun, fierce and direct, and there is dead mineral that will burn clean. Here, and only here, fire is *born* rather than merely kept. The Cellar folk make it the way their cousins make rope: as the ordinary central labor of their lives. They have a stone — a particular pale stone the pans throw up, that splits to a glassy edge and strikes white sparks against the horn-hard tendril-wood that comes down-current — and they have dry salt-grass, and they have patience, and they make coals. Bank them in clay. Pack them careful. And send them up.

This is the trade. This is the artery between the two places, and it runs along the seam where living wall meets dead stone, where the water comes down and something must go up to meet it.

## The Carriers

Between the breathing dark and the glaring Cellar there is a road, of a kind, and there are people who walk it, and they belong fully to neither place, which makes them the truest citizens of the world as a whole — the only ones who know it has two halves.

They go up carrying coals. They carry them the way the cathedral folk carry their own — cupped in clay, fed and watched — but they carry them *new*, freshly born, a whole banked clutch of them, up the weeping hem and into the dim where they will be parceled out among the scatters whose own fires have gotten low or gone dark. A scatter that has *gone dark* — lost its coal — is not doomed, as the old terror insists. It is only waiting. Waiting for a carrier coming up-current with the Cellar's fire still warm in the clay. The cathedral folk receive these carriers with a reverence they do not fully examine; they treat the arrival of new fire as something close to holy, a thing from the deep places, and they are right that it comes from a deep place, only the deep is not deeper into the cathedral but *out the bottom of the world entirely*, into a brightness they have no word for.

And the carriers come down again loaded with what the Cellar cannot make. The bloom-husks — those great sweet vessels the cathedral sheds, which the Cellar folk cannot grow and prize for holding water that does not turn brackish. The horn-hard tendril-wood, knapped or raw, that takes an edge no Cellar stone can match and that the spark-stones need to strike against. The pale woven cloth, soft, warm, made of the floor's own fiber — worthless up above where everything is warm, precious down here where the dry nights past the pans go suddenly, brutally cold, the heat fleeing up into that real and open sky the moment the sun lets go.

So the artery runs in both directions and carries opposite things. Down comes the soft, the wet-born, the grown — husk and cloth and fiber and wood, all of it shed from a living thing that does not know it is generous. Up goes the hard, the dry-born, the *made* — fire, and the stone that makes fire, and salt to keep food through the long hushed scatter-journeys, and word of the sky. The two halves of the world feed each other across the seam, each making what the other cannot, each ignorant of how completely it depends on a place it will never see.

## What the Cellar Folk Believe, Which Is the Mirror of What Their Cousins Know

The cathedral folk believe they are a gesture the great plant is making — a motion in a slow vast body, warm and small, completing when they go dark and sink back into the living floor.

The Cellar folk believe nothing of the kind, and the difference is the whole difference between the two halves of the world.

Down here there is no enormous patient living thing to be a gesture *of*. There is dead stone, and fierce sky, and water that arrives from above already spent, having given its life up there and come down only to leave again into the air. The Cellar folk live at a place of *endings* — the water ends here, evaporating; the cathedral ends here, at its hem; the grown world ends here and the mineral world begins. And so where their cousins above believe they are being *done* by something, the Cellar folk believe they are the ones who *do*. They are makers in a world that has stopped making itself. The plant builds no more once you pass the seam; from there on, anything built is built by hands. They take this not as loss but as the plain shape of responsibility. Up there, you do not cut what is held, because the world is alive and will provide. Down here the world is dead and provides nothing, and so you make, and making is not a sin against a living thing but the only prayer a dead landscape understands.

This is why fire lives with them and terror lives with their cousins. To the cathedral folk fire is the one made thing in a grown world, an intrusion, a held breath against catastrophe. To the Cellar folk fire is simply the first and best of all the things that hands do to dead matter to make it serve. They are not afraid of it. They cannot afford to be, since they spend their days birthing it. They have a saying — the carriers bring it up-current sometimes, and it lands on the cathedral folk as something between blasphemy and koan — *the dark is what you carry; the fire is what you are.* Up above this sounds like mysticism. Down here it is just a description of the trade. You carry coals into the dark. The dark needs them. You, who can make them, are the fire; the dark, which cannot, is the world you serve.

## The Seam, and What Stands On It

Walk the seam itself — the line where weeping wall meets dry stone — and you find the only place in the whole world where you can touch both halves at once, and the carriers have made it a kind of threshold, the nearest thing the world has to a temple, though neither people would use that word.

Here the lagging water does a final strange thing before it leaves. The cathedral's pools, up above, hold reflections a beat late, faces arriving out of time, and the people there read them like weather. The water that comes *down*, that reaches the seam and spreads into the pans, carries that lag with it for a little while — and there is a stretch of the topmost pan, right at the hem, where the standing sheet of evaporating water still remembers how to reflect wrong. Lean over it in the seam's strange doubled light — dim wall behind you, glaring sky above you, both thrown back at once — and your face arrives late, the way it would up above, except that as the water thins and dries and the lag burns off, you can watch your reflection *catch up to you*. Stand long enough and the late face slides forward, frame by frame, until it lands on the present and stays. The carriers do this before every crossing. They wait at the seam for their reflection to catch up, and when it does, they go — down into the bright with the world's only fire, or up into the dark with the world's only softness, carrying one half of the world toward the other across the single line where the water, finally, stops remembering, and lets itself go.

Up above, the cousins lean over lagging pools and wait for the face that arrives a little out of time, and call it arriving, and go on into the breathing dark.

Down here, at the bottom of everything, the same water lets the face land at last — promptly, fully, in the fierce and ordinary light — and the carrier shoulders the coals, and climbs.
