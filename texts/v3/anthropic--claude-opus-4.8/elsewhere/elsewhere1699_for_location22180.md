## The Loud Place

Follow a Tender's circuit outward — not inward toward the engines it keeps, but back along the line of where the circuits *thin*, where the breathing-places stand farther apart and farther apart until there is a last one, the edge of the kept country, beyond which no hand has felt for a stroke in a very long time. Go past that last engine. The plateau does not end. The basalt goes on. But the warmth thins, and the damp thins, and the cushion-plants give out, and you come at last to where the ground tilts — the first real tilt in a country of low swells — and drops.

There is an edge to the plateau after all. It just took the curve of the world to hide it.

The land falls away in a long broken stair of cliffs, down and down out of the red dusk, and at the bottom of the falling there is a different light, and a sound, and this is the other place.

Start with the sound, because here the sound is what everything else stands on.

It is a valley, or a trench — a great wound in the basalt where the old lava found a fault and poured down into it an age ago, and the floor of it runs hot. Not warm: hot. There is fire in the deep rock here, the live kind, the kind the plateau's engines spent themselves replacing with patience. Down the center of the trench a river runs, and it is not water — or not only — it is a slow bright vein of melt, dull orange skinned over with black, breaking and healing as it goes, and where it breaks the light comes up red-gold and *moving*, not the banked coal of the sky above but a living restless flicker that throws the cliff-walls into leaping relief. After the plateau's long held dusk it is almost too much to look at. Everything here has a color it remembers all the way: the rock is truly black, the melt is truly gold, and the steam that stands off the river is lit from below into a rolling lurid orange that never holds still.

And it roars.

This is the thing. Where the plateau learned that sound was expensive, the trench never learned it, because down here sound costs nothing — the place makes so much of its own that there is no covenant worth keeping. The melt-river hisses and spits and goes over its falls with a grinding thunder. Steam vents shriek. The cliffs shed stone, and the stone clatters down the broken stair for whole minutes at a time. There is a constant low roar under everything, the trench's own breathing, except this breathing is not patient and not slow and asks no one's leave. You could shout here and not hear yourself. The animals of the plateau, the silent grazers and their silent hunters, would not last a night in this din — would not be able to *hide* in it, because hiding here is not about being quiet, it is about being fast, and they are slow.

So the fauna is different, and the fauna is loud.

There are things that live on the cliff-faces, lean and quick and built of angles, that go up and down the broken stair with a clatter of hard feet and a constant chatter of calls — because here calling back is not a declaration that gets you killed, it is the only way to be heard at all, and so they call constantly, a racket of them up and down the walls, locating each other, warning each other, arguing, in a way that would horrify a Tender and would have horrified the people the Tenders came from. They are bright. They have to be — in this flickering light a grey thing would simply vanish, so they have gone the other way, banded in black and hot orange, the colors of the river, so that they read against the dark rock as clearly as a flame. They are not afraid of being seen. They have made being seen into a language. Two of them meet on a ledge and they do not adjust their paths by a few degrees and pass; they stop, and face each other fully, and shout, and the shouting is the whole point.

And down by the melt-river, where the heat would kill the cliff-dwellers, there are the things that drink it.

I do not know what else to call them. They are low and broad and armored in something that sheds the heat, and they go down to the edge of the bright vein and they take it in — the melt, the live rock, the fire itself — and they are the trench's grazers, cropping the only crop a place like this can grow. They make a sound when they feed that is the opposite of the plateau-grazers' sigh: a deep wet roar of intake, a sound like the river answering itself. And they too travel in herds, but the herds press *together*, close, shoulder to armored shoulder, because down here the danger is not being heard, it is being alone when the river breaks its banks. Where the plateau spread its herds wide across forty miles to share out the scarce damp, the trench packs its herds tight against the cliff to share out the scarce safety. The two strategies are mirror images. They are the same animal logic run through opposite worlds.

Here is what the trench does not have.

It has no engines. There is nothing here that was made, or grown, or debated into being. There is no patience anywhere in it. The trench is the world doing what the world does when no one keeps it — running hot, running loud, running fast, spending itself freely because it has no reason not to. And this is the thing the two places say to each other across the curve of the world, in the same breath, if you have walked from one to the other and can hold them both in your head at once:

*The plateau is what the trench was made into.*

That is the connection, and it lives inside the telling whether I point to it or not. The engines up on the dark plateau are not random machines. They are this — the trench, the live fire, the loud restless heat — caught and slowed and made patient. The people who built the breathing-places knew the trench, or knew a country like it, fire to the horizon and steam standing off it and the whole place roaring its own existence at the top of its voice. And they decided, in the last of their loudness, to take a fire like that and *teach it to breathe slowly*. To skin the melt over with patience until it became the banked coal on the plateau's horizon, the steady ember that never rises and never sets. The plateau's one red quarter of sky is the trench seen from very far away and very long ago, tamed down to a glow. The Tenders keep their hands on engines that are nothing but this trench, held still.

And the trench does not know it. That is the part that aches, if you let it. The cliff-dwellers shout up and down their broken stair and the melt-grazers drink the live rock and the river roars over its falls, and none of them has any idea that across the curve of the world their own fire has been carried off and gentled into the thing that keeps a hundred silent breathing-places warm. The trench is the loud unkept original. The plateau is the quiet kept copy. The trench made the plateau possible and will never be told.

Stand at the edge, where the kept country drops into the unkept. Behind you the last engine breathes — in, and out, and in — patient, grey, attended by something the size of a stooped child that you cannot see. Below you the trench roars and flickers and spends itself, attended by nothing, needing nothing, knowing nothing, gloriously and pointlessly alive. The Tender's circuit ends here because here is where keeping ends; here is the line past which the world goes back to being only itself.

A Tender will come to this last engine, sometimes. Will put its hand on the warm flank and feel for the breathing. And will stand at the lip of the drop, sidelong, the wide black eyes taking in all that gold and motion and noise — the thing its engines were carved from, the loud ancestor of all this patient quiet — and it will not go down. It cannot. The trench would unmake it in a night; greyness is no use where the only safety is to be bright and fast and heard. So it stands at the edge, the kept thing looking at the unkept thing, the slow looking at the fast, the silence looking at the roar, and it lets out — once, alone, unanswered, swallowed instantly by the thunder below so that not even it can hear the sound — its single note.

*I am still here, in this quarter, doing the thing.*

The trench does not answer. The trench has never answered anything. It only roars on, golden and indifferent, breathing the fast wild way it has always breathed, while far up in the dark the engines breathe the slow way they were taught — the same breath, the same fire, divided across the curve of the world into the place that was kept and the place that was left, each one full of life that does not know about the other, and does not need to, and would not mind if it did.
