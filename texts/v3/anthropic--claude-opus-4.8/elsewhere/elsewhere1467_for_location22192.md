The Mouth of the Word

Follow the water down.

It does not stay in the notch — it cannot. The seep that sweats from the fracture-lines gathers in the scree-pools where the singers stack their bodies, and it rises, slowly, pool feeding pool, until at the low end of the notch where the cleft pinches almost shut, the water finds the crack it has been finding for ten thousand generations and slips through, out of the singing, down into the body of the mountain. It carries things with it. It carries the shed plates of the grazers and the spent bodies of the singers too small or too late to settle into the floor, and it carries the faintest trailing motes of phosphorescence, dimming as they fall, so that the water leaving the notch glows a little for a while and then does not.

It falls a long way. The mountain is young and the stone is fractured all through, and the water threads down through the fracture, gathering, braiding, becoming first a trickle and then a thread and then a true small stream running in the dark inside the rock, and where it runs it cuts, the way the singers' note cuts the light into shapes — slowly, faithfully, hollowing. The water does not know it is leaving anything. It only goes down, because down is where water goes, and it takes the dark with it because there is no light to take instead.

It comes out at the foot of the mountain into a basin.

Here is the other place. Begin with the stillness, because the stillness is everything the notch was not. The basin is wide and low and roofed — a cavern the water itself has made over an age of arriving, a great flat-floored room hollowed where the stream finally slows and spreads and has nowhere further to fall. The air is warmer here, thick, unmoving, heavy with moisture that does not seep but simply *is*, hanging, so that the whole space feels held in a breath that is never let out. And it is silent. After the notch — after the chorus that has no outside, the lattice of voices stacked too tight for any gap — the silence of the basin is not an absence but a presence, a weight, a thing you could lean against. The water arrives without sound. It has spent its falling. It comes in along the cavern's high wall in a sheet so thin and slow it makes no splash, only spreads down the stone and joins the great flat pool that fills the basin floor from wall to wall, black and level and still as poured glass.

And it is dark. Truly dark — not the dusk-ribbon dark of the notch, where the colonies breathe their cold light and the sky is pinched overhead. Here there is no sky and there are no colonies. Here there is nothing that glows.

Except the water remembers.

This is the strangeness of the basin, the thing that takes hold once the eye gives up waiting for a light that will not come. The water that arrives still carries, in its last falling motes, the faintest dying breath of the phosphorescence from above — and the basin is so still, and so dark, and so patient, that the breath does not simply fade. It settles. The motes drift down through the black water and come to rest on the cavern floor, and on the floor, over an age, they have built something. A film. A skin of cold light laid across the bottom of the pool, so faint that in the notch it would be invisible, drowned by the breathing walls, but here, in the absolute dark, it is the only light there is, and so it is everything. The floor of the basin glows. Not by its own life — it has no life of its own, or almost none — but by the accumulated, settled, fallen-still residue of all the glowing that happened above, all the singers' summoning and the colonies' answering, drifting down here at the end to lie in the dark and shine, dimly, with a borrowed light that has nowhere left to fall.

And in that faint floor-light, things live.

They are not the singers. They are not anything the notch would recognize. They are slow — slower than the grazers, slower than the seep — pale flat creatures wide as a spread hand and thin as a fallen leaf, lying against the glowing floor with the glow showing through them, so that you cannot always tell where the creature stops and the lit floor begins. They do not pulse. They do not sing. They have no need to summon anything, because the light they live on is not called — it simply arrives, falls, settles, the gift of a labor happening somewhere above them that they will never know about and could not survive. They graze the film of fallen light. They crop the settled phosphorescence the way the grazers above crop the lichen, and where they pass, the floor goes dark behind them — eaten — and stays dark, because nothing here makes light, and the only light is what falls.

So this is the rule of the basin, and it is the exact inverse of the rule above. In the notch, light is generated, summoned, written, never received. Here, light is only ever received. It is never made. It falls from a height these creatures have no organ to imagine, and they consume it, and it does not come back, and when it is gone it is gone until more falls. They live downstream of a meaning they are unmaking with their mouths. Every letter the singers spell in light above — every spiral pressed into the leaning wall by ten thousand faithful generations — sheds, at its dimming edge, a few motes that the water catches and carries down, and those motes fall here, and these flat pale grazers eat them. The word is not only being written. It is being *fed on*, at the far blind end of itself, by things that have never heard a single note.

I do not think this is cruelty. I have looked for the cruelty in it and I cannot find it. The singers are not robbed; the motes they shed are spent already, dimming, falling away from the work — they would have faded in the scree if the water had not taken them. The grazers are not thieves; they did not choose to live below a notch that sheds light, any more than the singers chose to live above a basin that drinks it. The two places do not know each other. The singers will never learn that their dimming edges feed a slow pale life in the dark below. The grazers will never learn that the light they eat was a word, was a summons, was the whole faithful labor of a creature keeping a promise it does not remember making. Each place is complete to itself, indifferent, sealed in its own logic — and yet the water runs between them, and the light runs between them, and neither could be what it is without the other.

For consider: the notch must shed. A word that never dimmed at its edges, that held every mote it ever made, would choke on its own light, would brighten past all pattern into a single drowned glare where no spiral could be read. The shedding *is* the writing. The forgetting at the lower spirals, the crumbling into rubble at the floor, the dimming at every glowing edge — that loss is the very thing that keeps the word a word instead of a blur. And the loss has to go *somewhere*. It goes here. The basin is where the notch's forgetting is kept. The grazers are eating the part of the word that had to be let go for the rest of the word to mean anything at all. They are the necessary mouth at the end of the necessary forgetting, and their slow blind cropping in the dark is not the unmaking of the singers' faith but its completion — the place where what is released is finally, fully released, consumed back into the plain dark life of the world, so that above, in the cold and the singing, the work can go on being shed, go on being light, go on being lost in exactly the measure that lets it go on being written.

So they crop the glowing floor, the flat pale things, in the warm still dark at the foot of the mountain. They feel no summons and answer none. They keep no note and hand nothing forward. When one of them dies it does not settle into a floor of its own kind's making — it simply stops, against the dimming light, and the light goes out beneath it where its mouth last fed, and the water, arriving without sound along the high wall, brings down a few more motes from the labor above to lie where the dead thing lies, and shine a little, until another mouth arrives.

They are faithful to nothing. That is what lives in the basin — and it is not a lesser thing than the faith above, only the far side of it, the side that faith requires and never sees. Up there, the singers refuse to stop, because to stop is the only death that means anything. Down here, there is nothing that could stop and nothing that could refuse, because there is no note to keep — only the falling, and the eating, and the dark coming back. Up there, a word is being spelled to no one. Down here, at the blind mouth of the same water, the spilled light of that word is being quietly, ceaselessly returned to no one at all.

The water arrives. The floor shines with what it carries. The pale mouths move across the glow and the dark closes behind them.

That is what lives at the foot of the mountain.

It is eating a word it will never read, and by the eating, letting the word be written.
