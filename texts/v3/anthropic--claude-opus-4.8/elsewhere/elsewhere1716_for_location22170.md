# The Valley of Outwash

Follow the breath downward. The reach exhales — every drifter knows this, rides it — and what the mountain breathes out has to go somewhere. Follow the moisture the other way, against the fog that climbs at dawn, down past the lowest soft galleries where the small ones thin and the walls turn cold and damp and then truly wet, down to where the made thing meets the thing it was made from, and out.

This is where the structure ends. Not in a wall — in a *shore*.

## The Apron

The reach does not stop cleanly. It bleeds. At the base of two and a half kilometers of accumulated chewing, the oldest layers — the near-stone, the part that was packed before there was language and has been load-bearing ever since — weep. Water that fell as upper fog and threaded down through a kilometer of porous wall arrives here, at the bottom, carrying with it the finest dissolved trace of everything it passed: a tea of old secretion, of mineral grit, of the slow chemical signatures of generations of dead masons leached out of the walls they became. It seeps from the base of the mountain in a thousand small mouths and pools, and the pooling water spreads out across the floor of the valley in a flat shining apron, and where it spreads, it *drops what it carried*.

This is the outwash. Everything the mountain sheds, the valley keeps. The fan of pale sediment at the foot of the reach is the reach turned inside out — the same dark composite, but loose now, unbuilt, returned to grit and pulp and the bones of mineral. Anthill Reach grows by millimeters a year at its high edges. It dissolves by millimeters a year at its low ones, and the dissolved part lies here, spread thin and wet across a valley floor that is, slowly, the only original ground in the whole world — because it is the place where the made thing comes back apart into something a thing without hands could call earth.

The light here is different. This is the strangest thing for anything raised in the reach: there is *sky*. Not much of one — the valley is deep and the reach itself blots out half the dome of it — but the apron lies open to a gray weather, and the water on it gives back a flat dull shine, and after the absolute black of the deep galleries it is blinding, it is too much, it is a kind of wound of brightness across the lower world. The hangers' descendants, if any drifted down this far and survived, would be struck dead by it. Nothing from the deep comes here. The light is a wall the way the dark is a wall.

## The Mat

But the valley is not empty, and it is not — quite — outside.

Across the wet apron, in the thin gray light, grows a sheet of living matter that is the reach's only true child. The leached water carries spores. Always has. The same fungal strains the small ones farmed in the dark, washed out of the dissolving walls along with everything else, arrive here in the outwash — and here, in the light, fed by the rich dissolved tea of the mountain's slow decay, they do something they could never do in the galleries: they meet the sun, and they change.

The mat is fungus that learned light. Over a span of time as long as the reach is old — the two clocks running together, one building up, one washing down — the strains that arrived in the outwash met the gray weather and, generation past counting, turned green. They are not the reach's pale ghost-threads anymore. They are flat, broad, leathered things, dark green to nearly black, pressed against the wet sediment in overlapping plates, drinking the thin light and the thick water both. They are the first photosynthesis this world has, and they exist only because the dark mountain dies slowly enough to feed them, and the light reaches only here, at the one shore where the made thing comes apart.

The mat is where the world's two halves touch. Everything above is the dark, the built, the breathed, the chewed — life sheltering life by hand-less making, never once looking up. Everything here is the open, the dissolved, the lit, the spread-flat — the same life, the very same lineages, the very same spores, but turned around to face the sky the mountain never let them see. The reach feeds the valley with its dying. The valley does nothing back. The water runs one way only. This is not a cycle. This is the one place in the whole world where the cycle *breaks* — where something leaves the great patient closed loop of the mountain and does not return, washes out, lands in the light, and becomes a new thing that the mountain will never know it made.

## The Grazers of the Mat

And there is one more lineage here, and it is the hardest to look at, because it is *kin*.

Across the green mat move soft pale things, segmented, many-legged, blind. Finger-joint long, the largest of them. A film on their skin that reads *who* and *what-task* and *how-long-since-fed*. They graze the mat — scrape loose a mouthful — chew, mix it with grit, paste the pulp into low mounded ridges that channel the outwash water, that hold the wet where the mat grows best, that wall off the places where the sediment is too loose to bear. They build. They build small. They build *here*.

They are the small ones. The same small ones. Washed out as eggs and spores-of-themselves in the leaching water, or descended from those that were, ten thousand generations back — masons that came apart from the mountain with the wall they were eating and woke in the light and did not die. They kept their nature. They could not do otherwise; the chemistry is the chemistry, the standing instructions are written in their skin: *build here, the wall is thin; carry here, the water pools wrong.* They obey it still. But they obey it in the open, under the gray sky, on ground that is loose and wet and lit, and so what they build is not a mountain. It can never be a mountain. The light dries the upper ridges before they cure; the outwash undermines them from below; nothing here holds the way the deep galleries hold. They build, and the valley unbuilds them, at very nearly the same rate — and so they make, instead of a structure, a *pattern*: a slow shifting filigree of green-walled channels across the apron, raised and slumped and raised again, never rising more than a body's height, never lasting more than a season, the very same act that made the mountain, performed where it can amount to nothing but itself.

They do not know they are the same as the things in the dark. There is no organ in them that holds the mountain. They taste their patch of wet wall and they paste their pulp and they read each other's films and pour on past. If one of the deep masons, kilometers up in the warm black, could be set down here beside them, the two would read each other — *kin, mason, fed-recently, building* — and find nothing strange in one another at all, and graze on, side by side, one of them under the only sky there is and one of them as far from it as the world goes, both of them perfectly at home, because home was never a place. Home was the act. Home was the chewing and the pasting and the warm wet wall under the mouth.

The drifters feel the valley as a coldness at the very bottom of their sounding — the place where the booms go out and do not come back, the floor below which there is no one to answer, the edge of the dark. They rise from it. They always rise. None of them has ever drifted out across the apron into the killing light to find the green mat and the pale builders working it under the gray weather, and none ever will, and so the mountain will go on not knowing that its dying feeds a shore, and the shore will go on not knowing it was ever anything but itself, lit and wet and slow, building its filigree the valley takes back, busy past silence, patient past hurry, at the one open edge of a world that is otherwise all dark, all the way up, all made, all the long way home.
