# The Spillway

Follow the water down. It is the one thing here that does not stay.

The plateau holds its loop, drinks its own breath, pools and rises and beads again — but no loop is perfect, and at the plateau's southern reach the land finally does what the Condensary-dwellers cannot imagine: it ends. The pale weeping stone breaks off in a scarp so tall its base is weather of a different day, and all the water the rock cannot hold, the runoff the cisterns never catch, the overspill of a million coils and the slow seep of the grooves, gathers at that edge and goes over. Not as a waterfall — nothing here is so sudden. It goes over as a sheet, a permanent silver skin of water sliding down a rockface miles wide, slow as everything is slow up there, taking its time even in the falling.

And at the bottom, where the sheet-water lands and gathers and at last admits to being a river, there is the Spillway. The place the loop leaks into. The place that lives on what the plateau lets go.

## The catch

Begin with the catch-pools, because everything here begins with catching.

The land at the scarp's foot is the inverse of the plateau in every way that matters. It is broken, vertical, full of edges. The falling sheet has carved the soft lower stone into terraces, a vast descending staircase of basins, each one brimming and spilling into the next, so that the whole slope is a thousand-stepped cascade going down and down toward a lowland too far to see. And on the lip of every basin, clinging to every terrace, built into every crack where a wall can be wedged — the Spillway.

This is a city too, but understand what kind. The Condensary grew like a calcium deposit, layered and patient and level. The Spillway grew like lichen on a cliff, clutching, asymmetric, every structure cantilevered out over the next drop, braced and guyed and counterweighted, leaning into the spray. Where the plateau-city was lifted *above* its element, the Spillway is built *into* the fall of its element — houses straddling the runnels, mills with their wheels in the cascade, whole tenements bridging from one terrace-wall to another across a forty-foot plunge of white water, so that to walk across your own kitchen is to walk over a roaring gap on a plank you trust because your grandfather trusted it.

It is loud here too. But it is the opposite loud. Not the deep sustained chest-rumble of the herd — the Spillway's noise is bright and high and broken, the crash and hiss and chatter of falling water hitting stone ten thousand times a second, a white noise with edges in it, a roar you have to shout through. Where the plateau was a held breath, the Spillway is a continuous exhalation, a place perpetually letting go of something at the top of its lungs.

## What they make of it

They catch, and they sort, and they send on.

Because everything that comes over the scarp comes from *up there* — and up there, things go into the water and are lost, spilled, wasted, fallen outside the loop. But here the wasted thing arrives. The Spillway lives on what the Condensary mourns.

The catch-pools strain the falling water through screens and weirs and fine-meshed traps, and what they pull out is the plateau's overspill made solid: bone-meal washed loose from the rendering-vents, tallow-scum that congeals on the cold lower stone, hide-scraps, lost tools, the green flakes of corroded copper coil, the fine grey silt of the slow-green fungus, and — this is the trade that matters — *the fallen.* The animals that slipped the herd's edge. The beasts that wandered too near the scarp and went over, tumbling down the silver sheet, and the rare, the unspeakable, the never-discussed: the people who fell.

Up on the plateau, the fall into the herd is the great private horror, disappearance, being pressed down and absorbed. But there is a worse fall they almost never let themselves think of — the fall off the *edge*, off the southern catwalks, over the scarp, out of the world entirely. And the ones who go over that edge do not vanish. They arrive. Here.

## The Reclaimers

The Spillway people are tall.

This is the first thing, and it tells you the rest. Where the Condensary-dwellers are broad and low and built against falling, the Spillway-folk are long and narrow and built for reaching, for clinging, for working at the lip of a drop — ropy, light-boned, with the hands and feet of climbers and a way of standing braced and leaning that looks, to a plateau eye, perpetually about to fall. They are paler in the bone but darker in the skin, weathered, because the air down here is thinner and the spray throws rainbows and there is *light* — broken, glittering, edge-of-the-world light coming up off the cascade and off the lowland haze, enough to make you squint, and they do squint, their eyes gone narrow and creased where the plateau eyes go wide.

They are fast. To a Condensary-dweller they would read as panicked, untrustworthy, foreign — and they are foreign, are exactly the foreign that the plateau cannot picture, the people from the place where things move. They have to be fast. The catch will not wait; the water carries its gifts past in seconds and a slow hand catches nothing. A Spillway child learns to read the cascade the way the plateau child learns the smooth roll of the walk — learns where the current will throw a fallen thing, which weir takes the heavy catch and which the light, how to dart out on a wet plank and hook a tumbling shape from the white water and dart back before the next surge. *Late* is a concept they live inside. They have a hundred words for *quick* and the highest compliment among them is something like *hands that beat the water* — said of a Reclaimer so fast they pull the gift out before the river knows it's lost it.

## What they do with the fallen

Here is the thing that lives between the two places, and it is terrible and it is tender both at once.

When a beast comes over — and they do, slow tumbling grey shapes in the silver sheet, dead before they land or dying in the fall — the Reclaimers take it, and they render it, exactly as the plateau would. Tallow, bone-meal, hide. But they do it *fast*, and they do it without the taboo, because down here the rule that governs everything up there is simply inverted. Up on the plateau: *take only the dead, never the living, never break the slow.* Down here, where the only thing that arrives is what was already lost: *waste nothing the water gives, and give it back.*

Because the Spillway sends things on. The river that gathers below the catch-pools runs out across the lowland to places the Reclaimers trade with, places with no word for *plateau*, and down that river goes the rendered overspill — the bone-meal, the tallow, the hide, the salvaged copper — the plateau's loss made into the lowland's goods. The Spillway is a junction. It catches what one world spills and feeds it to another. It lives in the leak.

And the fallen people — this is the part the two places will never, ever speak across the distance between them. When a body comes over the scarp, the Reclaimers do not render it. They have a different practice, older than the trade, and it is the one thing they do slowly. They lay the body out on the highest dry terrace they have, the one that catches the most light, and they let it dry. They — who are wet to the bone, who live in the spray — give their fallen the one thing the plateau has no word for that isn't also a word for death. They let them go *dry*. Fully, finally dry, in the sun and the rising glitter, until the wet of the plateau has entirely left them, until they are light and pale and papery as the translucent hands of the very old up above — and then they are wrapped and sent down the river with the trade-goods, out of the loop entirely, out to the lowland, to be *somewhere dry*, which on the plateau means having been nowhere, having been wasted, spilled, lost.

The Reclaimers do not know what they mean by this. It is just what is done. But what they mean — what the practice means, sitting at the bottom of the fall where the loop breaks open — is *this one will not go back. This one fell out of the held breath and into the exhalation, and we will not press them back into the tide. We will finish what the falling started. We will let them be the thing the plateau most fears, all the way, on purpose, with our hands, gently.* They lay them in the light the dead never saw alive. They give them the squint. They send them on.

## What they believe

That nothing is wasted, but everything is *lost* — and that these are not the same, and that the whole art of living is knowing which is which.

The Condensary believes in the loop: in pooling and rising and beading again, in the warm flesh condensed on the weeping stone and running gently down. The Spillway believes in *the gift* — that things come from above, unearned, lost by someone, and that to receive them well is the only grace there is. They are a people of the catch, the snatch, the lucky hand. They do not believe they are condensation. They believe they are *downstream.* That everything they have arrived from somewhere higher, fell, and was caught — the water, the bone-meal, the copper, themselves. Their oldest story is that the first Reclaimers came over the scarp too, fell out of some held breath above, and instead of going back, learned to catch. To live in the falling. To make a home in the place where the world spills.

And so they look up, the way the plateau-folk look down. The Condensary-dwellers stand on the high catwalks and look into the herd with settled, thoughtless love, never once toward the southern edge where their world ends. The Reclaimers stand on the catch-terraces and look *up* the silver sheet, into the spray, toward the lip miles overhead where the water comes from and the gifts come from and the fallen come from — a place they have never been, will never go, cannot picture except as the source, the high country, the breath that exhales onto them everything they own.

Neither knows the other as a people. The plateau knows only that water leaves and does not come back, and grieves it as loss. The Spillway knows only that water arrives and must be caught, and blesses it as gift. The same water. The same fall. One world, breathing in at the top and out at the bottom, the held breath and the long exhalation, joined at the scarp by a sheet of falling silver that one place weeps and the other place drinks.

## Stand among them, then

at evening — though evening means something else here, means the light going long and gold across the lowland haze and the spray catching fire with it, a thing no plateau eye has ever seen. They are out on the terraces, tall narrow shapes braced over the drops, quick hands working the weirs in the last good light, squinting up the great silver sheet for whatever the day will send them down. They are loud and they shout and they laugh, because you have to be loud here, because the water is louder. They are drying their fallen on the high warm stone. They are loading the river-barges with the rendered gifts of a place they will never know exists above them.

They are warm too, but dry-warm, sun-warm, edge-of-the-world warm. The spray is on their faces and they wipe it off, because down here you *can* be dry, dry is the gift and the grace and the gentle finish, and they reach for it with hands that beat the water:

a people downstream of everything, catching the spilled breath of a slower world as it falls, keeping what they can use and blessing what they can't, and sending the lost ones on — out of the loop, into the light, gently, on purpose — down the long bright river to wherever dry things go.
