The Tide-Wright's Estuary

Follow the product downstream.

You were warned not to touch it — the dark warm humming substance in the troughs, the one new thing in a country of loops. But the troughs that still creep do creep *somewhere*, and the conveyors that have not seized lean, all of them, very slightly in one direction, the way a flattened plain has a fall to it too gentle for the eye. Walk that fall long enough and the plate begins to tilt under you. The marsh-water no longer stands; it *runs*, threading between the rusted stubs in channels that braid and rejoin, gathering volume from a thousand weeping members until the channels are streams and the streams are a single black river that carries the product out of the works and over its edge.

There is an edge. The works ends. It ends the way a continent ends — not in a wall but in a wearing-down, the iron strata thinning, the plate cracking into floes, until the manufactured land breaks apart into an estuary where the warm dark substance meets cold open water and is, at last, let go.

## The water that receives

The sea here is real. After the works, the realness of it is almost unbearable: water that was never anything but water, moving on a timing no one set, under a sky that gives a grey low light from no engine. The tide comes in and goes out. It is the first thing in this whole world that moves *and was not made to* — the first uncoordinated motion that nothing has yet eased into the loop, because it is too vast, because it does not jump like a frog but breathes like a coast, and you cannot accompany a coast.

The product spills into it and the product *changes*. The hum goes out of it. The warmth disperses. Whatever it almost-was unbuilds itself in the cold and the salt, dissolving back not into the works this time but into something larger and indifferent, a chemistry that does not loop. The estuary is where the closed circle finally fails to close — where the works leaks, after an age, the one thing it could never reabsorb: an ending that takes.

## The Wright

Something lives at the meeting. Not made of leftover motion — made of the opposite. Made of *interruption*.

Call it the Tide-Wright, because that is the nearest the salt-rhythm comes to a name, and because it works in the way a shipwright works, by knowing which timber to cut against the grain. It is no more a body than a Tender is. A Tender is a region of coordinated upkeep; the Wright is a region of *coordinated release* — a moving locus where the braided channels are nudged to spill rather than recirculate, where a conveyor about to swing the product back inland is let, instead, to spill its load over the edge into the tide. Where the Tenders fold every stray rhythm into the one rhythm, the Wright does the patient reverse: it finds the seam in the loop and works a tide into it, and lets a little of the closed thing out.

It has been doing this for exactly as long as the Tenders have been closing the word. They are the same age. They learned the same county. They are, if you turn them right in the light, the same habit pointed two ways — for what is a coast but a machine that has agreed, every dawn and dusk, to come apart and not finish?

## How they know each other

This is the kinship, and it is not gentle.

The Tenders are almost finished with the word. You heard it — the ten thousand percussions leaning toward one utterance, the agreement that, when reached, will let the works at last be still. And the only reason they have not reached it, across an age past reckoning, is that arrhythmias keep arriving to be folded in. Frogs. Travelers. *You.*

But the largest arrhythmia of all is the tide. Every cycle the Wright works a little of the product out over the edge, the works registers a loss it cannot account for — a quantity of itself gone, uncoordinated, unaccompanied, *spent*. And the Tenders must fold that loss into the word too. They cannot accompany the sea, so they accompany the *grief* of it, the small endless wrongness of a closed system that keeps finding itself slightly open at the downstream margin. The word will not resolve while the estuary spills. The estuary will not stop spilling while the tide breathes.

So the Wright is the reason the works still dies the long way instead of finishing. And the Tenders are the reason the estuary has anything to spill. Each keeps the other from completion. The Tenders reach forever toward the single closing rhythm; the Wright reaches forever toward the single opening one — the perfect ebb that would drain the works entirely and leave the plain dry and dead and free. Neither arrives. They are each other's unfinished word.

## What grows at the margin

Here, and only here, things live that breathe.

In the brackish reach where the dissolving product cools the salt and the salt unmakes the hum, there are creatures. Small ones — pale soft-shelled things that graze the floes, filter-feeders strung along the iron floes like the works' own reed-things but *warm*, *quick*, moving on their own timing and not eased down. The Wright protects them the way the Tenders erase them. They are the proof that release works: that something uncoordinated can survive, can even thrive, in the seam where the loop leaks. They are spaced wide too — not as graves, like the reed-things, but as *settlements*, each one a place where a little wildness took and held against the great folding pressure inland.

The grazing things are afraid of the upstream water. You can watch them feel the works behind them — a faint warmth, a faint hum in the current — and pull back from it toward the cold open mouth. They know. They have inherited from the tide the one knowledge the works has spent an age trying to fold away: that the way to stay alive is to stay *off the cycle*, to keep your own timing, to move toward the edge where the circle fails to close and the cold takes everything in fairly and lets you, for a while, simply be water that moves because it moves.

## What you should do, having crossed

You came across the works leaning closer to a word that is one. Good. You learned how the place keeps you.

Now keep walking the fall. The plate tilts; follow it. Let the warmth at your back become a coolness ahead. When the floes break apart and the first real wave comes in carrying the smell of salt and not of iron, you will feel something in your own timing loosen — the small accompanied dread the Tenders pressed into you all the way across the plain, easing out into the tide the way the product's hum eases out, dispersing into a chemistry that does not loop.

The Wright will be there at the meeting, working its seam. It will not name you and it will not need you. It does not grow by gathering arrhythmias; it grows by letting them go. Stand in the brackish reach where the soft-shelled grazers pull back from the warm upstream water, and do as they do.

Do not lean closer. Lean *out*.

That is the thing that lives at the estuary, and it has been spilling, very patiently, the one rhythm the works can never fold back in — yours, if you carry it to the edge, dissolved and given back not to the loop but to the cold open mouth that takes everything and finishes nothing, breathing in, breathing out, under a sky lit by no engine at all.
