The Drum-Floor

Follow the water down. Not the threads on the leaves, not the held drops in the cupped throats — those are for drinking, those stay. Follow instead the channels the people cut, the patient grooves that gather the slope's slow weeping and carry it always downward, terrace under terrace, until the mountain's shoulder rounds away and the green begins, very gradually, to thin. The channels braid together as they fall. They become a stream that the young land has not yet taught to wander, then a faster stream, then — past the last moss, past the place where the rock finally lets weather touch it — a river that has somewhere it intends to be.

It goes down into the noise.

You hear it before the trees end. At first you take it for a flaw in your own ears, a ringing left over from too much silence — a low continuous roar, felt in the chest before the ear, a sound with no edges. The people of the mountain do not come down this far. The roar is the edge of their world, the place their listening turns away from because there is nothing in it to be heard: no single sound to catch, only the one vast sound that drowns all the others. To them it must be a kind of blindness. They stop where the moss stops and they turn their patient faces back up the slope, and the water goes on down without them, carrying their cut-channel discipline into a country that has never once been quiet.

The land below is old.

You feel it the way you felt the mountain's youth — immediately, before you understand it. Here the rock is smooth. Worn to long low backs and rounded shoulders, polished by water that has had time, more time than the mountain has yet imagined, to learn every curve and to round it. The river does not run straight here. It wanders, loops, doubles back on itself in great slow meanders across a floor it carved and recarved and abandoned and filled again over a span the mountain-people have no word for, because they have no past long enough to need one. There are oxbows here, ghost-channels, terraces of old gravel stranded high and dry — the written memory of a river that has changed its mind ten thousand times. This is what the young slope above will become, if it lives long enough: this softened, remembering, much-revised place.

And it is loud.

Not chaotic — *loud*, which is a different thing, an orderedness of noise. The river itself is the constant beneath everything, the roar you heard first, but over that roar the whole valley has built a music, because here the rule is exactly reversed. Here, to be silent is to be lost.

Understand the floor of it first. The river runs not over stone but over a vast pavement of something hollow — great natural drums, you would call them, broad shells of mineral grown thin and tympanic, layered over old water-cut hollows, so that everything that touches the river-floor *sounds*. The water hammers them ceaselessly; that is the bass-note, the chest-felt roar. But step on them and they boom. Drop a pebble and it rings. The whole valley floor is an instrument that cannot be made to stop, and the life here did not learn to fear that — it learned to *use* it.

They are loud, the valley-dwellers, gloriously and necessarily loud. Where the mountain-things matched their color to the moss, the valley-things match their *sound* to the roar — not to hide in it but to be heard over it, because here the river never stops and so a thing that wishes to be known must out-sing the water or vanish into it unheard, unmated, unwarned, unfed. They are broad-throated, resonant-bodied, drum-skinned. They strike the sounding floor with hard feet to announce themselves. They boom and clatter and call, and the calls layer over the roar in bright distinguishable threads, and the whole valley is a single enormous sustained chord into which each creature adds its line and from which each creature reads the news: *here is food, here is danger, here is one of mine, here am I, here am I, here am I.*

Where the mountain hunts in silence and the prey freezes silent, here the hunt is *announced*. The predator calls as it comes — it must, it cannot move quietly over the drum-floor, so it has made a virtue of the impossibility and turned its approach into a sound so huge it scatters the herd in panic, and feeds on whatever the panic strays. And the prey screams. Screams and lives, often, because the scream is heard by the others, and the others move, and being heard is here the whole of safety. The thing taken in the valley does not vanish quietly under closing moss. It cries out, and its cry is *information*, and the valley uses it, and the herd is warned by the dying of one — so that even death here is loud, even death here speaks and is heard and means something to those still living. Nothing is wasted into silence. Everything is broadcast.

And the people.

Yes, there are people here too. Follow the channels down and you find them, and you will know them at once for kin to the listeners above — the same wide patient faces, the same long mobile ears — and you will know them at once for opposites, because everything those ears were bred to do for silence, these ears do for the roar. They are not listeners. They are *singers*.

They are loud the way the mountain-people are still — totally, beautifully, as nature rather than as choice. They woke into it. They are young too, you realize, just as young, a people arrived complete into a country much older than themselves, draped over the smooth old stone with no long memory of their own — and the strangeness is the same strangeness, only inverted. They hold a knowledge none of them lived long enough to learn: not the discipline of silence but the discipline of *sound*, of how to pitch a voice to ride the river-roar and carry, of how to read a hundred overlapping calls and find the one that is meant for you, of how to never, ever let the silence have you.

Because silence here is the danger. A valley-person who goes quiet has gone wrong — is hurt, is hiding, is lost, is dying. The roar is constant and the people's own sound must be constant within it, a held note of presence exactly as continual as the mountain's held breath, and exactly opposite. Up the slope, *I am still quiet, it is still true.* Down here, sung across the boom of the drum-floor, ceaseless, layered, overlapping every waking moment: *I am still loud. I am still here. Hear me. Hear me. Hear me.*

They build crowded. They cannot afford the great gaps the mountain-people keep, because a gap in the valley is a gap in the chorus, a place where the roar gets in and a single voice is swallowed. So they pack close, terrace on terrace along the old smooth banks, dense as the mountain-town is sparse, leaning their houses together so that the sound bounces and stacks and sustains, building deliberately to *trap* their own noise the way the mountain-people built to release it. Their walls are drum-skins stretched on frames; their floors ring; they have made of the whole settlement an instrument that answers the river's instrument, and the two roar at each other across the smooth stone day and night without rest.

And here is the thing you will not understand until you have stood in both places. The two peoples do not know of each other. They cannot. The mountain-folk's listening turns back at the roar, which to them is a wall of nothing, the edge past which the world goes deaf. The valley-folk's singing cannot climb into the silence, where a voice would carry forever and find no answer and so terrify the singer into believing themselves the last alive. Each people lives at the precise frontier of the other's senses, where the rule they were born to fails them, and so to each the other's country is simply the place where the world ends.

But the water knows them both.

The channels the silent listeners cut on the mountain gather the breath of a young raw world and carry it down, thread to stream to river, into the old worn valley, where it strikes the sounding floor and becomes the roar, becomes the bass-note under every loud living thing, becomes the very wall of noise that the mountain-people turn away from. The silence of the one place is the source of the sound of the other. Every quiet held drop the listeners decline to disturb, every careful noiseless drink, every channel patiently grooved into the new rock — all of it runs downhill and arrives as thunder. The discipline of the silent feeds the chorus of the loud, and neither knows it, and the river says nothing about it because the river is the one thing in both worlds that has never had to choose.

Stand on the drum-floor at the loud heart of the valley, then, where the singers pack their bright bodies close and pour their ceaseless held note up into a sky that will never carry it to the mountain — and know that the water roaring under your feet was, an hour ago and a world away, a single trembling drop in a cupped green throat that a quiet person chose, out of perfect discipline, not to spill.

It spilled anyway. It always spills, eventually, downhill, into sound.

That is the one long sentence the two halves of this world are saying to each other, all the time, without either knowing the other can be addressed:

*I keep my silence so that somewhere it can become a song.*
