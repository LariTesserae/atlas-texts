# The Throat of the World

Follow the swimmers backward. The pale finger-length shoals that thicken the flats did not begin there — nothing begins on the flats, which is the whole truth of them. The swimmers ride a thread of moving water through the standing water, a current you would never find by eye but that the soles of the Quiet People know as surely as they know the bars. It comes from somewhere. Trace it against its flow, upward, for it is the only thing in that country that still admits to a direction, and you climb.

You climb for a long time. The flats fall behind and below, and as they fall the screaming fades — not all at once but in layers, the way a fever breaks. The land begins, tentatively, to *insist*. There are stones here with edges. There are slopes. The water gathers itself out of sheets into runnels, out of runnels into a stream, and the stream has *opinions*, it cuts, it chooses, it goes one way and not another, and you realize you have not seen water choose anything in days. And then the ground rises hard on either side into walls of dark wet rock, and the stream gathers into a torrent, and the torrent goes white, and you come at last to where it is born.

It is a wound in the mountain, and it is loud — but oh, it is a different loud.

The Throat is a gorge cut so deep and narrow that the pewter sky is only a seam overhead, a bright crack miles up. Here the water does not lie down. Here it *falls* — not as a single cataract but as a hundred, a thousand, threads and ropes and curtains of it pouring from every fissure in the black rock, because the whole mountain is saturated, the whole mountain *weeps* exactly as the flats' low islands weep, but standing up, and from a height, so that what is a finger of seepage down below is a roaring vertical river up here. The sound is the inverse of the flats' sound. The flats roar *horizontally*, a flat wall of a million throats. The Throat roars *downward*, a column, a pressure from above, the sound of water remembering that it has weight.

And it is cold. The flats were cool; the Throat is *cold*, the cold of stone that the sun has never reached to the bottom of, and the spray comes off the falls in a freezing smoke that fills the gorge to its brim, so that you stand always inside a cloud, lit silver from the seam of sky far above. Everything here is becoming the mist that, far below, hangs unmoving over the flats. This is where that mist is *made* — flung off the falls, lifted on the cold, carried down the long descent of the gorge to settle at last over the doubled sky of the place that finished becoming itself. The flats breathe the Throat's breath. The two places share one wet, dim lung, and the air moves only one way through it: down, always down, from the falls to the grave of stone.

## What Grows Here

Everything. This is the thing you cannot prepare for, having come from below.

The flats were a mineral country, a stain of life on bare rock. The Throat is *drowning* in green. The constant spray, the constant cold, the sheer rock faces forever wet — they have made of every surface a vertical meadow. Mosses thick as fur. Ferns that have never felt a dry hour, uncurling fronds the length of your arm. Hanging gardens of dripping, succulent, impossible green pouring down the black rock in curtains that mirror the curtains of falling water, so that you cannot always tell, in the silver light, which is plant and which is flood. Where the flats had a film of the simplest life that saw no reason to change, the Throat has life in a frenzy of *changing*, complicating, reaching, every fissure stuffed with a different leaf, every ledge a riot. It is the same world's other answer to the same wet: *not* to lie down. To climb the walls instead. To turn the falling water into a reason to grow upward against it.

And in the green, things move. Small bright climbers with gripping hands and enormous eyes — eyes for the dimness, eyes that the deaf pale ghosts below have no need of. They go up and down the dripping faces gathering the fat moss-fruit, and they are *quick*, and they are *bright*, and they are colored, which nothing on the flats is colored, blues and rusts and barred yellows, because here in the gorge there is just enough light in the seam overhead to make color worth having. They call to one another in high whistles that carry up the column of the gorge the way nothing carries across the flat. They are not loud the way the flock is loud. They are *musical*, because here sound travels and so sound can mean.

## The People of the Throat

And there are people here too, and this is where the thread you followed up draws tight, because they are the same people. Or they were.

They live high, on ledges and in clefts behind the falls, and they reach those places by climbing — and so they are *not* worn smooth. They have kept their hearing, every one, because the Throat's sound does not destroy hearing, it *rewards* it: the gorge carries every whistle and rockfall and shift of water, and to hear well here is to live, to know which fall is swelling, which ledge is loosening, where the bright climbers are gathering the fruit you also want. They speak in voices. They sing, even — high carrying calls bounced off the wet black walls, a language of echo and pitch, as elaborate and quick as the postural epics below are slow and held. Where the Quiet People press forehead to forehead and hum into bone, the People of the Throat call across fifty feet of void and are heard perfectly, and think nothing of it, and could no more imagine living pressed together in deafness than the flats-people could imagine shouting across an abyss.

They are colored too. They have stained themselves with the rusts and yellows of the moss-fruit, banded their arms, because in the seam-light color is legible and so color is *speech* of another kind — a man's bands tell you his ledge, his line, his standing. They are vivid where their cousins are pale. They are loud where their cousins are silent. They climb where their cousins wade. They reach upward, toward the bright seam of sky, where their cousins have nothing left to reach toward and find that restful.

And they are the same people. The bone of the face is the same bone. The wide feet, webbed at the root of the toes — useless on these dry-enough ledges, kept only by inheritance, a splayed foot meant for standing water, carried high up a gorge where there is no standing water at all. A flats-foot, on a climber's body. The two peoples have not met in so long that neither remembers the other as anything but a story, and yet each carries the other in the shape of the foot, the set of the skull, the way the spine knows how to *speak* even among the singers — for the People of the Throat, when they are grieved past words, fall silent and *lean*, square the shoulders, tip the head a precise few degrees, and do not know why, and call it the old sorrow, the posture that comes on you when calling will not serve.

## What They Believe

They believe the opposite of everything below, and they are wrong in the opposite direction, and the two wrongnesses are the two halves of one truth that neither can hold alone.

The Quiet People believe the water has already won, that they are the afterlife of a dead country, and they find this restful. The People of the Throat believe the water is *being born* — that here, at the falls, the world is forever beginning, forever pouring itself new, and that they live at the source of things, at the morning of the world, and everything downstream is the world growing old. They pity the downstream, vaguely, as a place where the water goes to slow and spread and die. They do not know that the downstream is real, is peopled, is their own grandmothers' grave-cairns weathering at the rate of the living stone. They only know the water leaves the gorge and is not seen again, and they call that *the going-away*, and they sing about it, and they are a little afraid of it, the way the flats-people are not afraid of anything.

But here is what neither knows, and what you know now, having walked the thread from one to the other:

The water that is born screaming at the Throat is the same water that lies down silent on the Flats. The mist the falls fling up is the mist the doubled sky lies down inside. The swimmers that thicken the flats to feed the flock and the few pale ghosts were spawned somewhere in the cold green clefts up here and rode the current down into the place where everything finishes. The two peoples are one people, split by the length of one river into the ones who reach up toward the beginning and the ones who lie down into the end, the loud and the quiet, the colored and the pale, the hearing and the deaf — and the river does not care which is which, because to the river they are the same wet living thing it carries from its cold loud birth to its cool silent grave, growing smoother all the way down.

## What You Would See

If you came to the Throat at the right hour — the seam of sky at its brightest, the spray lit silver, the bright climbers whistling across the void — you might see one of them high on a curtain of moss, banded in rust and yellow, head cocked to catch a sound in the falling water, alive in every sense the flats had worn away.

And if that one looked down, into the going-away, into the place where the gorge opens and the water begins to spread and slow and the green begins to thin toward the mineral country no climber has ever seen — they might feel, without knowing why, the old sorrow come over them. They might fall silent in all that singing. They might square their shoulders and tip their head a precise few degrees, in the posture that is the only word their cousins know, sending downstream into the mist a sentence in a language they have forgotten they can speak, to a people they have forgotten are theirs:

*We are still here, in the manner of the still-here. We are still loud. We have not yet been worn smooth.*

And far below, on a submerged bar in the dim, a small pale upright shape that cannot hear and has never heard might feel the current shift against its ankles — the swimmers thickening, the gift arriving in the gap the flock has left — and would understand, in the only knowing that country permits, that something far away and high up is still arguing with the water.

And would add a stone to a cairn, to mark that the beginning had not yet ended.

And would go back to the swimmers, in the wet the falls had made, in the breath the two places share, in the one long lung of the world that breathes from the loud bright top of it down to the silent pale bottom, and asks of no one, at either end, that they be afraid.
