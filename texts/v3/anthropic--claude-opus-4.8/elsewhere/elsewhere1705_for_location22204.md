## The Surface Quarry

Follow the bone tool back up.

Not the antler — the antler is a finishing instrument, a smoother, the thing a hand reaches for at the end of work. Follow instead the *stone*. The arc of small flints set on edge in the largest basin: someone knapped those. Someone struck them from a core and worked the edges and carried them down. Flint does not grow in a riverbed worn smooth; it comes out of chalk, out of beds laid down in an older sea, and it has to be quarried, and the quarrying is loud, and the place it happens is the exact opposite of the bright hollow in every way that a place can be opposite. So go there. Go up through the rock the maker came down through, and out, and across to where the chalk breaks the surface in a long white scarp, and find the quarry the flints came from. It is still working. That is the thing you are not ready for: down below, the maker has been gone ten thousand years, and up here, the maker's *kind* never stopped.

The quarry is a wound that has been kept open on purpose.

Where the chamber is finished, the quarry is forever unfinished — that is the first and governing difference, and everything else follows from it. Below, erosion did its patient work and then *stopped*, and the place arrived at agreement with itself. Here nothing is ever allowed to agree with itself. The whole point of the quarry is to be reopened, daily, before the rain can heal it. It is a place defined by interruption. Every morning the faces are a little smoothed by weather and every morning hands undo the smoothing, strike fresh fracture, expose new white, because chalk left alone goes gray and grassed and gentle in a single season and they cannot allow it. The chamber is kept *the same* by patience. The quarry is kept *raw* by violence. Both are forms of keeping. They are opposite halves of one stubborn instinct — *hold this place against time* — and the maker carried that instinct down the shaft like a coal in cupped hands and set the stones in their arc, an act of keeping in a world that kept nothing back.

Where the chamber is empty, the quarry is crammed. This is the second difference and it hits you in the body. Below, the dominant material is *nothing* — air and light and distance between the few far-apart lives. Here the dominant material is rubble. Spoil heaps of broken chalk, white as bone-meal, sliding and shifting underfoot. Heaps of rejected flint, the cores and the failed strikes, the shatter and the waste, because for every good blade a hundred fragments fall and they all stay, they pile, they make new ground that grinds and clatters when you cross it. There is no distance here that is not full. Where the chamber holds its few lives far apart, like words spaced wide in a sentence, the quarry crushes everything together — people, dogs, baskets, the struck stone, the dust — into a single grinding paragraph with no breath in it. You could not sit and wait for a single sound here. The sound is continuous and the sound is *labor*.

And the sound — this is what undoes the comparison entirely, this is where the two places stand most exactly against each other. Below, sound is an *event*. A drop falls — *tok* — and the silence holds it, and the listeners stir, and you wait, and waiting becomes a clock. One sound at a time, each one marked, each one received by creatures made of pure attention. Here there is no silence to hold anything and nothing waits and nothing is marked, because the sound is *constant*: the ring of hammerstone on flint, a hard bright *crack* repeated and overlapping, ten of them, fifty, never spaced, never an event, a weather of sound, a hail. The chamber's whole religion is built on the scarcity of sound — on the long emptiness between drops that makes each drop a sacrament. The quarry has so much sound it has become meaningless, the way a word said ten thousand times stops meaning. And here is the terrible symmetry: *it is the same sound.* The *tok* of the falling drop and the *crack* of the knapping stone are the same physical act — a hard thing striking a hard thing in a hollow place. Below, that act happens once an age and is holy. Above, it happens ten times a breath and is nothing. The maker knew both. The maker spent the loud bright meaningless days up here cracking flint in the hail of it, and then went down into the place where one strike of stone is the only voice in the world, and there, in the silence, set the struck stones in an arc — and for the first time in their life, perhaps, each stone *meant* something, because for the first time it was placed where there was quiet enough to hear it.

Where the chamber's light has no source, the quarry is all source and no mercy. Below, the brightness comes from everywhere and nowhere, a noon with no sun, leaving almost no shadow. Here there is a sun, the real one, and it is brutal on the white chalk — the glare comes *up* off the spoil as hard as it comes down, doubled, a light with a violent origin you can point to and that moves and that sets. The chamber's light frees the moths and confuses the blind swimmers into growing eyes that reach for a sun they're already inside. The quarry's light burns. The workers squint against it; their eyes are not blooming hopefully toward a hidden brightness, they are clenched shut against an obvious one. Down below, eyes grow where they're useless. Up here, eyes are ruined by use, by glare and grit and chalk-dust, and the oldest knappers go half-blind in their forties from a lifetime of staring at the bright struck faces, and they keep knapping by feel, by the sound of the *crack* telling their fingers what the eyes can no longer read. They learn to work by the very thing the chamber is built around: by listening to the strike. The half-blind old knapper and the listeners at the chamber's edge are doing the same thing — attending to the sound of stone — and neither of them can see, and one of them does it in deafening daylight and one in radiant silence, and they will never know about each other.

The lives here are not spread thin. They are a crowd, and they are loud, and they are *young* — that is the cruelest difference of all. The chamber's creatures are old past reckoning; the listeners have heard the same line for longer than the stone took to go smooth; the crickets walk a pilgrimage that consumes a whole brief life and gets nowhere; everything below is ancient and slow and patient. Up here the lives are fast and they burn out. Knapping is young people's work, hard on the hands, hard on the eyes, hard on the lungs — the chalk-dust gets into the chest and stays, white silt settling in the body the way it settles on the spoil heaps, and the knappers do not get to be old the way the chamber's listeners are old. They get a few loud bright decades and then the dust closes their breathing. They are the opposite of the listeners in this too: the listeners are made of patience and outlast their own origins, persisting after the body wears away; the knappers wear away *fast* and leave only the heaps, the white waste, the broken cores — leave only their work, never themselves. Where the chamber keeps everything, the quarry keeps nothing of the people and everything of the rubble.

And the migration. The crickets below walk forever in one direction and never arrive, a journey emptied of its arriving. Here the flint *arrives.* That is the whole function of the place — it sends its product *out*. The good blades and cores are gathered into baskets and carried off, traded down the long paths to people who have no chalk of their own, passed hand to hand across distances the knappers will never see, going somewhere, actually reaching somewhere, the migration the crickets only mime. And one of those blades, one core, one set of small worked flints, went down. Down the shaft, down into the bright hollow, in the hands of a maker who carried the loud surface world's one export into the silent underworld and set it in an arc by the water. The crickets walk toward an exit that leads nowhere. The flint found the exit. The flint got out of the quarry and went *deeper*, which is its own kind of out — out of the meaningless hail of sound and into the place where a struck stone could finally be heard.

So stand in the quarry and understand what the chamber is keeping a seat for.

Not a god. Not a strange underworld thing. A worker. Someone who spent their loud bright clenched-eyed days in the chalk-glare and the chalk-dust and the constant *crack*, in the crammed and grinding crowd of it, in the unfinished wound that has to be reopened every dawn — someone whose lungs were filling with white, whose eyes were going, who had only a few fast years and knew it. And in those years they went down. Through the rock, away from the hail of sound and the killing glare and the crowd, into the empty patient bright-and-silent hollow where the only voice was a single drop of water once an age. And they knelt at the basin and set the struck flints in an arc, and the placing *meant* something — boundary or calendar or grave or game or the start of a wall — and it meant something *because down there it could be heard,* because the silence was deep enough to hold one human gesture and keep it.

Then they climbed back up to the quarry, to the dust and the *crack* and the few remaining years, and the dust finished its work, and they did not go down again.

The quarry does not remember them. The quarry remembers no one; it keeps only the spoil. The names went into the white heaps with the breath and the broken cores.

But the chamber is patient. The chamber kept the arc. *Tok.* It is still holding the maker's one true sentence in the only quiet on earth deep enough to hold it — keeping a seat by the water for someone whose breath ran out in the bright loud quarry ten thousand years ago, and who cannot come back, and who is being waited for anyway, by the listeners and the moss and the silence, with a fidelity the surface never learned.

Up here they break the chalk and forget.
Down there the dark keeps faith.
Between them runs one path, and one flint went down it, and was set on edge, and is held.
