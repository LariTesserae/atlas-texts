THE SHELF ABOVE

Climb the cataract. Not the water itself — you can't, the fall is one-way, a throat that only swallows — but climb beside it, up out of the bright room through the ceiling-that-is-a-surface, and break into the air the fall fell from. Here is where the fall begins, and beginnings are always a place.

It is a stone shelf, broad and shallow, open to the sky. The same hands that cut the room cut this. You know it instantly by the chisel-marks, the same crispness, the same young work — the makers were here too, were here *first*, because the water must be gathered before it can be dropped. The shelf is the gathering. It is a wide pan of dressed sandstone, terracotta-gold like everything below, but dry on top where the sun strikes it and dark only along the channels where the water runs. And the water does run. It comes from higher still — from a spring, a seep, a cut aqueduct sliding down off slopes you can't see from here — and it arrives at the shelf thin and spread, a sheet barely deeper than a hand, sliding across the warm stone under the open sky.

This is where the heat is made. Below, you felt the water as bathwater-warm and read it as a chosen temperature, engineered. Here is the engine. The shelf is a sun-trap, a shallow tray laid out flat to the southern light so the thin sheet of water crossing it takes the sun the whole slow way across, warming as it goes, the way water warms in a stone basin left out all day. By the time it reaches the lip — the far edge where the shelf gives way and the water tips over into the dark mouth of the cataract — it has been cooked to exactly the warmth the room wants. The fall is not just delivery. The fall is the last instant of a long warming. The builders did not heat the culture from beneath. They heated it up here, in the open, in the only furnace that never needs tending, and then they let it pour.

But the shelf is not empty, and this is the thing — this is the kinship that runs between the two places, sealed in the same water. The shelf swarms *already*. What the room below is waiting to become, the shelf above has long since become.

Because the thin warm sheet sliding across the sun-struck stone is the perfect skin of life. It is shallow enough that the light reaches all the way through it to the bottom, so the whole depth of the water is lit, not just a surface — and the green has taken it utterly. The stone beneath the sheet is not stone-colored. It is felted, furred, deep with growth: mats of it, slick and emerald and ochre-brown, the diatoms and the threading weed grown thick as moss because nothing here is held back, nothing is waiting. This is no thin first colonist working hesitantly into seams. This is the seam-colonist's homeland, the source-stock, the mother-culture grown rampant in the sun. And in it, *on* it, *through* it: the swarming. The copepods you were promised below are here in their multitudes already, stuttering through the warm shallows. The seed-shrimp trundle the green mats in numbers, grazing. The water is thick with the drifting single cells, soup-rich, almost too full to see through.

And here, at last, is the animal life the room so eerily lacked. Birds. Wading birds, long-legged, stalking the shelf on stilted steps — they have found this shallow teeming pan and they work it, dipping, sieving, their bills sweeping the warm soup for the small fast life it grows. Small things that hum and skate the surface film. The shelf is loud too, but not with the white roar of the fall — that roar happens at the lip, below the lip, behind you now. Here it is the other loudness: the thin high needling of insects, the wet small sounds of feeding, the rustle of a bird shifting its weight on stone. The shelf is alive the way the room is not-yet-alive, and the difference between them is only time and the height of one fall.

Now see what the connection truly is. The cataract does not only carry water down. It carries the shelf down. Every drop that tips over the lip is freighted with what the shelf has grown — the spores, the cells, the green's own children, the resting eggs swept loose from the mats, the whole teeming inheritance of the sunlit pan poured down into the dark warm room to seed it. The room is not waiting for life to arrive from nowhere. The room is the *daughter* of the shelf. What you read as colonists arriving mysteriously through the fall were emigrants — the shelf above shedding its abundance over the edge, sending its overflow down into the prepared vessel below.

This is why the builders made two places. The shelf gathers and warms and *grows*. The room receives and holds and *ripens*. The shelf is wild, open, sun-baked, swarming, fed by the slopes above and harvested by the birds. The room is sealed, magnified, shadowless, deliberate, a culture run pure and hot in the dark beneath the surface. The shelf is the field; the room is the granary. And the fall between them is the one moment the water leaves the lit open world and enters the lit closed one, carrying its living cargo over the edge in a white roar, every instant, endlessly, the shelf spilling itself downward into the room so that the room can become what the shelf already is.

So when you stood below in the held breath, waiting for the first of them to rise — you had it almost right, but reversed. They do not rise. They *fall*. They are falling now, in the cataract, in the white drum of it, the shelf's children pouring down into the warm bright room that was built to catch them. The room is for them, yes. And they are coming from here — from this wide stone pan under the open sky, where the water is gathered and warmed and made to live, before it is dropped into the dark to live again.

Stand on the shelf a while. Feel the sun on the warm stone, the thin sheet sliding past your ankles thick with green and small swimming things, the long-legged birds working the shallows. Then walk to the lip. Look over. The water tips and brightens and falls, and somewhere below it pools and roars into a room you have already seen, a room still waiting, a room about to be full.

You came up to find where the fall began. You found a whole bright country, spilling itself patiently over an edge, feeding the dark.
