The Unfinished Coast

Follow the water down. It does not pool in the valley — the floor reabsorbs it, holds it, lets it rise and withdraw, but a held water table is still fed from somewhere and still drains to somewhere, and the somewhere is lower, always lower, off the far lip of the bowl where the membrane-walls thin and finally give out. The water that seeps up around your foot in the valley is on its way down. It just takes the long way, through the weave, through the lignified plate, and out the underside of the high country in a thousand small weepings that gather, miles down, into a single grey river that has no interest in equilibrium whatsoever.

The coast is where that river arrives, and it is the opposite of finished.

Here is the second rule, and it is the inversion of the first: nothing is made, and nothing will ever stop being made. The valley resolved. The coast cannot. It is a long grey reach of tidal flat and bank and braided channel, where the freshwater from the high country meets a sea the color of wet ash, and the two of them argue twice a day, forever, and have never once agreed. There are no columns here. There is no chord. There is no plan being expressed into matter. There is only material — endless material, sediment, the ground-up grit of everything upstream — being moved, and the moving is the whole of the place. Where the valley held its breath, the coast cannot stop breathing, in and out, tide and tide, and each breath rearranges the world.

Walk it at low water. The flats go out for half a mile, slick and gleaming, and they gleam the way the valley floor gleamed — that same film of water pressed and withdrawing — but here the resemblance is a cruel joke, because the valley's gleam meant *stillness held wet* and the coast's gleam means *everything is about to move*. Your foot does not press out a brief film that withdraws. Your foot sinks. The flat takes your weight and remembers it, briefly, as a filling hollow, and then the next sheet of water erases the memory and the flat is blank again, ready to be impressed by anything, holding nothing. Walk the same line at the same tide a week apart and it is not the same line. The channel has wandered. The bank has slumped. A bar that was firm is now a soft place that will drink you to the knee.

It is quiet here. This surprises people too, after the valley. You expect the sea to roar. But the flats are too gradual for surf; the water comes in low and broad and almost without sound, a sheet sliding over a sheet, and the loudest thing for miles is the suck and tick of drained mud and the small collapses of undercut bank, a constant soft demolition. The valley gave its stillness a voice so the silence would have something to say. The coast gives its motion no voice at all. It does its endless work in something close to silence, and the silence here says nothing, because saying-something would require a meaning held still long enough to be said, and nothing holds still. The coast does not announce *this is complete*. It cannot complete a sentence. It revises every clause before the clause is done.

And living things are not rare here. They are the whole substance of the place, and they are the deep kinship — the thing that runs out of the valley and finds its answer down here, transformed past recognition. Because the coast is where the valley's makers go when they cannot stop.

Understand what arrives in the grey river. Not water only. The river carries grit, yes, but it also carries *fragment* — the rare, the almost-never, the impossible: a piece of the valley that did not hold. For all the valley's refusal to decay, its edges are raw, unworn, still sharp with the seams of their making, and *raw edges shed*. Not often. Not in any way you would catch watching. But across the spans of time that time-stopped place is exempt from, a flake of membrane works loose, a splinter of fluted column calves away at a seam, and the high water table takes it, and the long drain takes it, and the grey river takes it down to the coast — a few grains of the finished thing, delivered to the place that cannot finish.

And here those grains do the thing they could never do up there. They start over.

The living substance of the coast is the valley's growth-logic with the brakes cut. It is the same plant-building impulse — the same drive to grow into form and lignify and span and rise — but it has fallen into a medium that will not let it complete a single gesture. It puts down a root-mat across a flat, broad and interlocking, exactly as the valley did, reaching toward that woven floor, toward the lignified plate, toward equilibrium — and the tide undercuts it before it fuses. It throws up a stem meaning to thicken into a column, and the channel wanders into the stem's footing and the stem leans and the lean kills the symmetry the form required and the form is abandoned half-made and a new stem starts from the wreck of it. The coast is *littered* with false starts. It is nothing but false starts. Where the valley left no wreckage, the coast is wreckage all the way down, layer on layer, a stratigraphy of attempts — every leaning broken-off half-column, every torn root-mat, every membrane that spanned three feet of its intended fifty before the water took the anchor.

This is the wreckage the valley refused to make. This is where it went.

Because that is the relation between the two places, the thing that lives in the same breath as both of them: the valley achieved its perfection by exporting its failure. A thing that grows only what it means to grow, that leaves no dead branch, no abandoned limb — such a thing must put the abandonment *somewhere*. Growth that resolves cleanly has to shed the unresolved. The valley is upstream and finished precisely because the coast is downstream and cannot stop, drinking the high country's discarded motion, the slumped and the slewed and the never-completed, and trying, with the valley's own borrowed grains, to build it all again — and failing, twice a day, forever, on purpose, because the failing is the only thing down here that works.

So watch the flats at the turn of the tide, when the water is neither coming nor going and for one slack hour the coast almost holds still. In that hour you can see the half-grown things standing — a low pale forest of beginnings, stems that meant to be columns, mats that meant to be floors, the whole stalled ambition of the place caught upright in the grey slack light. And it looks, for that one hour, like the valley. It looks like a cathedral in its first morning of construction, every pillar started, none yet finished, the work all ahead. It is the valley's youth, played on a loop, the moment before the moment the valley froze — held here not by perfection but by the exact opposite, by a ground too restless to let anything arrive.

And then the tide turns, the water chooses a direction, and the half-forest begins to come apart, gently, in near silence, the banks weeping back into the channels, the stems leaning, the mats tearing free — and the loose grains of it, the failed grit, the splinters that were themselves once the valley's splinters, lift into the grey water and go *out*, to the ash-colored sea, which takes them and grinds them and someday, by the long backward arithmetic of evaporation and cloud and the rain that falls on the high country, will hand a few of them back up to the valley's water table, to rise again through the weave, to press out around a foot and withdraw.

Two places. One breath shared between them, drawn in the valley and let out on the coast. Upstream, a thing that wanted shape and got it and stopped, and sings *this is complete* in a chord with no resolution. Downstream, the same shape-wanting fallen into a medium that grants it no completion, and so it builds and loses and builds, in a silence with no sentence, because the only thing it can finish is the next undoing. The valley is what growth looks like when it is allowed to end. The coast is what the same growth looks like when it is forbidden to — and the forbidding is the gift the valley keeps refusing, because a thing that finishes gets to be complete, and a thing that cannot finish gets to *go on*.

Stand on the slack flat in the grey hour and you can feel the kinship come up through the soles of your feet, the same warmth the valley had, faint, bodily, not stone-warmth — the warmth of the same life, far from home, working. The valley would tell you, in its chord, that it has nothing left to do. The coast cannot tell you anything. It is too busy, and too unfinished, and too alive in the only way it knows, which is to take the perfect thing's leavings and try, and fail, and lift them out to sea, and wait for the rain.
