# The Spillward Shoals at Fallen-Light

There is a place where the kindness failed, and the people there built a whole life out of the failure, and call it a blessing.

Let me come to it the way the water itself comes to it: by falling.

---

Somewhere in the canopy-vault, far from Vault-Reach — far enough that no sung greeting could ever cross the distance, far enough that the two settlements know of each other only the way you know of a country whose language you cannot speak — the membrane broke. Not all at once. Not catastrophically. The roof did not tear and dump its ocean into the void below in one drowning instant. It *thinned*, in one wide region, over a span of years no one bothered to count, until the held water found a place where the green ceiling could no longer cup it, and began — slowly, patiently, in keeping with everything in this world — to fall.

This is the first thing to understand about Fallen-Light, and it is the inverse of the first thing about Vault-Reach. There, the water sits and does not drain. Here, it drains and does not stop. There is a wound in the roof the width of a small lake, and through it the held ocean pours endlessly downward into the open dark below, a column of falling water two thousand feet tall, and where it pours, the light pours with it.

Because that is what comes through the wound: not just water, but *sky*. Unstrained, unslowed, un-bottled daylight, falling straight down through the gap in a great solid shaft, the way light falls through the one hole in a cavern roof. After a lifetime of bottle-green dimness, the people here live at the edge of a pillar of raw white day, and they have never gotten used to it, and they have built their entire theology around the fact that they never will.

## The Falling Water

You hear it before anything. Where Vault-Reach murmurs, Fallen-Light *roars* — a sound that has no beginning and no end, the sound of a held ocean perpetually losing itself through a hole. It fills the water for a great distance around, a low white thunder that the people feel in their singing-chambers before they feel it in their ears, so that to live here is to live with a sound you cannot turn off, a sound that is the sound of the world quietly ending and never finishing.

And the water *moves*. This is the deepest difference. At Vault-Reach a current takes a full day to cross the settlement; here the water is always running, drawn toward the wound, accelerating as it nears the lip, until at the edge of the gap it is a smooth glassy rush that will take anything not anchored and pour it down into the dark with the rest. The people of Fallen-Light are not slow. They cannot afford to be. They live in a current that wants them, always, gently, to fall — and so they have become quick, anchored, wary, *braced*. A child here learns to hold position in moving water before it learns to sing. The first lesson is: *do not drift toward the bright.*

## The Shoals

They do not live in the trunks. The trunks near the wound are dead — drained too fast, their dwellings emptied generations ago, their bark scoured smooth by the endless running water. Instead the people of Fallen-Light live in the *shoals*: the great drifts of matter that the falling water cannot quite carry down, the silt and wrack and tangled vine and broken dwelling and centuries of falling debris that have caught and built up around the lip of the wound into banks and bars and shifting islands of compacted stuff, lit from above by that raw falling day.

Imagine it. A ring of bright sediment-country, built from everything the upper canopy ever lost, heaped at the edge of the great drain and held there by the very current that wants to take it — a place that exists only because the falling never stops, a shore made of the act of falling. The people have woven themselves into these banks the way the people of Vault-Reach wove themselves into the trees, but the weaving here is *anchoring*, ballast and stake and root, holding the soft bright country in place against the pull. Their dwellings are low and dug-in and heavy. Nothing here hangs in three dimensions; everything here clings to the ground against the going-down.

And they *grow things*. This is the strange wealth of Fallen-Light, the thing that the people of Vault-Reach, in their cool dim spaciousness, would not believe: in the shaft of falling raw daylight, in the rich falling silt, life grows *thick*. Where Vault-Reach is sparse, a constellation, a cathedral that is mostly nave — Fallen-Light is *crowded*. Dense gardens of bright-fed growth carpet the shoals. The shoaling creatures come here to feed in numbers that would make a Vault-Reach netter weep, drawn to the light and the abundance, so thick in places that the water is silver with them. It is loud and bright and crowded and fast and rich, and it is all of these things *because* the roof failed, *because* the ocean is pouring away into the dark, *because* the kindness, here, broke.

## The People of Fallen-Light

They are the same people. This is what undoes you when you understand it. The same long tapered bodies, the same singing-chambers, the same webbed patient hands, the same green-grey skin — though here the skin runs darker, because the raw light would burn a pale belly, and they have weathered toward a deep mottled bronze that the Vault-Reach folk would find startling and a little frightening, like a person tanned by a sun their cousins have never seen.

They sing, still. But where Vault-Reach sings *long* — those slow descending moans crossing the patient gaps — the people of Fallen-Light sing *over the roar*, and so their voices have become loud and bright and clipped, pitched high to cut through the white thunder, urgent where their cousins are languid. To a Vault-Reach ear their speech would sound like shouting, like panic, like a person perpetually alarmed. To themselves it is simply *speech that can be heard*. They do not murmur *still here, still held* across an empty silence, because there is no silence here to fear and no held-ness to be grateful for. The water is running and the light is falling and you are never, for one instant, in doubt that the world is loud and full and going somewhere.

And here is the thing — the inversion that completes the two places, that lives in the same breath as both of them:

At Vault-Reach the cardinal sin is *taking-for-granted*. The discipline is to *notice*, to never let the held ocean become ordinary, to lay open hands on the kindness and feel it strain to keep its promise. Their whole soul is built on a favor that might be withdrawn.

At Fallen-Light, the favor *was* withdrawn. The roof broke. The kindness failed. The held ocean is, slowly and forever, falling away into the dark below — the very catastrophe the elders of Vault-Reach swim up to forestall, the thing they touch the membrane to prevent, the silence and the falling they have organized their entire gratitude against. It happened here. It is happening here, right now, endlessly, audibly, in the white roar that never stops.

And the people of Fallen-Light call it *the opening*. They call it the day the world finally let the light in. They tell their children that for ten thousand years their cousins somewhere out in the dim green held their breath and laid their hands on the ceiling and *begged* it not to fall — and here, where it fell, is the only place in all the canopy-vault where you can stand in true daylight, where the gardens grow thick, where the shoals run silver, where the water is alive with going. They do not believe themselves abandoned by the kindness. They believe themselves *delivered* by its breaking. Where the Vault-Reach elder feels the membrane hum with the strain of not-falling and weeps with gratitude, the Fallen-Light child stands in the falling itself and feels the raw day on their bronze back and thinks: *this is what they were so afraid of. This warmth. This brightness. This.*

They do not know the word *Vault-Reach*. They have a phrase for the dim green country their ancestors came from, somewhere out past the roar where the water still sits and waits: they call it *the held dark*, and they speak of it the way you speak of a sleeping relative — with a pity that is half love and half incomprehension. *Out there,* the old ones say, *they are still holding their breath. They never learned that you are allowed to let go.*

## What the Two Places Are to Each Other

Here is the whole of it, the thing that lives in the same breath as both:

They are one wound apart. The water that pours endlessly down the shaft at Fallen-Light is *Vault-Reach's water* — the very ocean the elders touch with open hands and beg to stay. Every day the kindness at Vault-Reach holds is a day the held dark keeps its breath; and every day it holds *there*, the water still runs *here*, because somewhere far out in the green the membrane is thick and somewhere closer the membrane broke, and the ocean cannot tell the difference between a roof that holds and a roof that fell — it only knows to sit where it is cupped and to fall where it is not.

So the two places are the same favor read two opposite ways. At Vault-Reach: *the kindness might fail, so notice everything, so be grateful, so never take it for granted.* At Fallen-Light: *the kindness already failed, and look — look what falling let in.* The elders of one swim up on the longest day to press their hands against the very catastrophe the children of the other are standing inside of, calling it home, calling it light, calling it the day they were finally allowed to let go.

And neither knows the other is there. The roar is too loud for any sung greeting to cross it. The held dark cannot hear over its own grateful murmuring; the falling light cannot hear over its own thunder. They are cousins on either side of a single broken promise, each having built an entire soul out of the same fact —

— that the roof holds the ocean for no reason anyone can name, and that no reason is also why it falls.
