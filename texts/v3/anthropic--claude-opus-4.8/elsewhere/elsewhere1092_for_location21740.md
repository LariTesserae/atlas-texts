The Tidefast Reach

Follow the river out. Not the water that beads and falls inside the stone — that water has nowhere to go, it has made its peace — but the river proper, the slow brown one that runs past the low wet threshold and does not stop because it has never learned how. The copper comes up it. The few who leave go down it. Track it down through the bluff country, down through the loud green world of seasons the stone-people fear, down for days, for the better part of a province, until the banks soften and the current slackens and spreads, until the river stops being a river and becomes an argument it is losing — and there, where the land gives up its hold and the sea comes in to take the water back twice a day, is the Reach.

It is the opposite place. You will feel that before you can say it. Where the stone is one held breath, the Reach breathes constantly, in and out, in and out, and you can set your life by the lungs of it because everyone there does.

It is a town built on the tide's indecision. The sea here does not have a shore so much as a *range* — a vast flat plain of silt and channel and oyster-bar that the water covers and uncovers, twice each day, baring at low tide a glistening brown country you could walk across and drowning it again before you could walk back. Nothing here is land and nothing here is sea; everything is *both, in turn*. And the people have built for the turning. The houses stand on legs — stilts of tarred hardwood driven deep into the silt, so that the whole town is raised a storey above the mud, and at high water the sea slides in underneath the floors and the town becomes an archipelago of huts on poles with boats tied to every doorpost, and at low water the sea pulls out and the town stands suddenly on dry stilts above a steaming plain, and you climb *down* to go anywhere, down ladders to the mud, and walk the bared channels in boots to your thighs.

The light here is the second thing you notice, after the smell. There is too much of it and it will not hold still. The sky over the Reach is enormous — all that flatness gives the sky nothing to hide behind — and the light comes off the wet mud and the standing water and the salt-haze in a hundred shifting sheets, dawn-pink to noon-glare to the long bruised gold of the evening, and then it *fails*, every night, the way the stone-people dread, the great red drowning of the sun into the flats. But here no one mourns it. Here the failing of the light is just the tide of the day going out, and they know to their marrow that what goes out comes back. The stone drank one noon and kept it forever. The Reach takes a new sun every morning, fresh, and gives it back every dusk, and has never once thought to keep one.

Listen. It is *loud*. This is the thing that would buckle a stone-dweller's knees if one ever came this far — and one or two have; we will get to that. The Reach is a riot of sound and the sound is the whole point. The tide comes in over the flats with a noise like the world filling a cup, a vast soft seething rush, and the gulls scream over it, and the mud-flats at low water tick and pop and suck with ten thousand small buried lives breathing, and the people *shout*. They shout from stilt to stilt, hut to hut, boat to boat, because the wind takes half of every word and the distances are wide and the water is always moving and you cannot, here, make the air hold your meaning still the way the crystal does — the air here will not hold anything, it tears your voice to rags and flings it, so you learn to throw your voice hard and bright and often, to call and call again, to be loud or be lost. A child here learns to bellow before it learns to walk. The whole town is a held breath constantly, joyfully, *releasing*.

And they are colored. The sun has been at them for a hundred generations and they are dark as the tarred poles, dark and weathered and creased, with eyes pale from squinting at the glare. They are not small and economical like the lattice-people. They are loose-limbed and big-gestured, made for reaching and hauling and casting wide, their movements sized to a world with no walls. Their hands are scarred — oyster-cut, rope-burned, brine-cracked — and their hands do not speak, their hands *work*, and when they want to say a thing across a distance they cup those scarred hands to their mouths and let fly. The exact inversion of the stone, where the hand was public and the voice was kept close to the body. Here the voice is flung as far as it will go and it is the *touch* that is private — the hand laid on a shoulder, the brief grip at a doorpost, the things done close and quiet and almost never, that you would miss if you didn't watch for them.

They live by the losing. Understand this, because it is the whole of them, the way the brim was the whole of the others. Everything the Reach has, the tide gives twice and takes twice. The oyster-bars they crop at low water are drowned again by dusk. The fish-weirs of woven withy strain the ebb and are emptied and the next tide fills them new. The very ground they stand on is loaned, not owned — bared and reclaimed, bared and reclaimed, and a man may walk out onto a flat his grandfather walked and find the channel has moved a hundred yards in the night, the whole shape of the known world rearranged by the dark water while he slept. Nothing here is kept. *Nothing here can be kept.* And so they have built a life out of the opposite refusal — not the refusal to reach past the brim, but the refusal to mourn what the brim cannot hold. They take what the low tide offers, fast, with both hands, knowing it is going. They eat fresh because nothing keeps in the salt damp. They mend the weir knowing the storm will take it. They love their dead and let the tide have them — out onto the flats at the ebb, the body, and the next flood lifts it and is gone, and that is the burial and it is enough. A people who have made their peace not with the sufficient but with the *passing*. Who looked at the same catastrophe of time the stone-people looked at — the having, the losing — and went the other way entirely, and decided to love the losing, to ride it, to be the kind of creature that needs the next tide because it has given up on this one.

The copper comes from here. You should know that. The nuggets the slow river leaves at the stone's low threshold, the little hammered hoard the lattice-people make their beautiful unhurried bowls and knives from — that copper starts in the Reach, in the shallow workings on the far flats where a green crust shows at the lowest of the low spring tides, and the Reach-folk dig it in the bared hours with the water already turning to come back for them, racing the flood, hauling the ore up the ladders before the channels fill. They trade it upriver. They send it up to the quiet people in the stone, who they have heard of and mostly disbelieve — *a town with no tide, they say, a town where the water falls but never comes back, where they will not raise their voices and they finish one bowl in a year* — and the Reach-folk laugh at this, kindly, the way you laugh at a story a child tells, and load the copper into the barges and send it up into the country where the light supposedly never fails. They think it sounds like death. They think it sounds, in fact, exactly like the thing they spend their whole loud lives outrunning: the moment that will not pass, the water that will not come back. They would rather drown twice a day forever than live one day in a noon that never ends.

And the bowls come down. This is the trade and this is the strange kinship folded inside it. Up the river, copper. Down the river, sometimes — rarely — a bowl. A stone-bowl, fired clay or ground stone, made slow across four hands, perfect, meant to last a thousand years. The Reach-folk prize these above almost anything, and here is the ache of it: in the Reach, *nothing lasts a thousand years*. Nothing lasts a season. The salt eats it, the storm takes it, the tide rearranges it. A thing made to endure is, to these people, very nearly a miracle and very nearly a grief — they hold the stone-bowl and they feel, for a moment, the thing they gave up, the keeping, the permanence, the noon that does not fail. They use the bowl for the things that matter most — the naming of a child, the laying-out of a death — because it is the one object they own that the tide cannot take, and so it has become the vessel for exactly those moments they most need to believe will outlast the water. They do not know that the people who made it are dying slowly inside that same permanence. They only know the bowl does not break, and that in a world of breaking that is the closest thing to holy they have ever held.

Now. The ones who leave the stone.

A few make it this far. Not many — most who crawl out the low green threshold are eaten by the loud world long before the river spreads into the Reach, lost to a winter or a fever or a road. But every long while one comes down the river all the way to the salt, one of the thirsty ones, the brim-haters, the young who shouldered a ferncloth and a bowl and went out into time on purpose. And you can tell them at once on the stilts of the Reach. They are the pale ones. The small economical ones who flinch from the shouting and stand too still and watch the tide come in with their hands half-rising to speak to it before they remember the hands mean nothing here. They have come the whole length of the world looking for the opposite of the place that was killing them, and they have found it, and now they stand on a stilted porch above the seething flats and they shake — because it is *everything at once*, all the losing they were raised to fear, the light failing every night, the ground rearranged by the dark, nothing kept, nothing finished, the whole catastrophe of time not opted out of but worshipped, danced to, lived in up to the neck.

Some of them break and turn back. The river runs both ways for the patient. They crawl home up the channels to the noon that never ends and they take up the unfinished bowl and they are quiet again forever, and the city reweaves itself around their return as gently as it wove itself around their leaving, and asks nothing.

But some of them stay. And those are the ones to watch, because something happens to a stone-child in the Reach that happens to no one born there. They have a word for the brim — *full and declining to continue* — and they bring that word with them into a place that has never once declined to continue, and the two things meet inside one person and make something new. You will find them, years on, weathered dark as the rest but still small, still spare in their movements, but no longer still — out on the lowest flats at the lowest tide, working the copper, racing the flood with the others, and *fast* now, fast as any of them, both hands grabbing what the water is about to take back. And they will tell you, in the loud flung Reach-voice they have learned to throw across the water, that they understand now what the old ones in the stone never could: that you do not have to choose between losing nothing and gaining nothing. That there is a third way, and it is this — to take everything, knowing it goes, and to want the next thing not out of hunger but out of *love for the tide itself*, the in and the out, the having and the giving-back, the sun that fails every night so that there can be a morning.

And once in a very long while one of them carries a stone-bowl down with them on the leaving, and keeps it, and uses it for the namings and the deaths like everyone else in the Reach, and looks at it sometimes by the light of a sun that will fail in an hour — the one permanent thing in a town of beautiful impermanence, made by the people they fled, the people who are even now standing utterly still in the bright unchanging noon and watching a single drop fall — and feels, holding it, both halves of the world in their two scarred hands at once.

The tide is going out. Climb down. There is copper in the flats and only an hour before the water comes back for it, and the gulls are screaming, and the light is the long bruised gold that means the day is dying, which here is only ever the promise that it will be born.
