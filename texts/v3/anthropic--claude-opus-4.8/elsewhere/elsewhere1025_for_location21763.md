The Open Air

Start where the last sentence sends you: up. Out. Into the one direction the pocket forbids and the one resource it has none of. There is sky here, and the sky is the whole point, and the sky is empty.

This is a high place. A limestone plateau, the same stone that weeps below, but here it is dry — dry the way the pocket is wet, completely, with the dryness soaked all the way through so that you cannot find the moisture underneath, there is no underneath, the sun has pressed it out of the grain the way the deep rock presses water in. The two are the same body of stone. They are the same fist of the world, the inside of the hand and the back of it. The drop that worked ten thousand years at the pocket fell from a crack that opens up here, somewhere on this baked tableland, a hairline in the rock that gathers what little wet there is and sends it down to do its patient work in the dark. The plateau is the roof of the city. The city is the marrow of the plateau. Neither knows the other. Neither could be where it is without the other being where it is.

To understand the scale: where the pocket fit in a closed hand, this would not fit in a held breath of looking. You stand and you turn and there is no edge to it. The plateau runs to a horizon that is only the curve of the world giving out, pale rock and pale scrub and pale sky, and the largeness is so total that it reads, after a while, the way the smallness did — as a kind of vertigo, a refusal of the human measure. Down there you could not find a thing small enough to be a single citizen. Up here you cannot find a thing large enough to be an edge. Both directions arrive at the same place: a scale that has stopped having any use for you.

And it is silent.

This is the inversion I came up carrying, the thing that made me sit down on the hot stone and put my hand flat against it and listen to nothing for a long time. The pocket was loud past bearing, loud with the labor of staying still. The plateau is silent, and the silence is not stillness. It is the loudest changing there is, performed so slowly and so spread out that no single moment of it makes a sound. Down there, a million small motions cancel into a held note. Up here, one enormous motion is so unhurried that it reads as a held silence. The roar and the hush are the same fact seen from the two ends of time.

Because everything here is changing, all the time, and nothing here is held.

The stone is going. You can see it going, if you slow yourself down to its pace, which is hard, which took me days. The dryness that pressed the water out has made the surface friable, and the wind — there is wind here, always, the open air's one tireless citizen — takes the friable surface a grain at a time and carries it off. Not replaced. Never replaced. There is no pale low patient something hunched over the loosening grain to set it right. The grain loosens and the grain is taken and the grain does not come back, and the plateau is, very slowly, by exactly the logic the builders below have spent ten thousand years defeating, ceasing to be a plateau. It is becoming sand. It is becoming the next thing. The erosion that finished its sentence down in the pocket — that rounded every edge and then stopped, held off forever by the work — up here has never stopped, was never even paused, speaks its whole sentence aloud across the open ground every hour of every day, in a voice too low and too even to hear.

So this is the world the pocket lives inside, and could not have guessed at, and is in every way the answer to. The builders hold one fist of stone against change with all their distributed deathless strength. The plateau, the same stone, the rest of the same body, simply changes, ungoverned, unattended, with no one hunched over it and no loop closed and no holding that will never fail — because there is no holding at all. The two are not opposites. They are the two things stone does. They are what every persisting thing is poised between: the marrow that fights to stay itself, and the surface that is already, quietly, becoming the desert.

Now: what lives here.

I told you fauna was rare below — one kind, barely glimpsed, a paleness against paleness that I came to think was a single patience wearing a million bodies. Up here fauna is also, in its way, one kind, and this is the connection I did not expect to find and cannot now stop turning over. There is a creature on the plateau. It is the size of a hand — the very size, I will say it plainly, of the hand that could have closed around the whole city below. Pale, low, segmented in the loose way of old reptiles, it moves across the hot stone on many short limbs in a gait of small repeated gestures, and it lives by a rule as total and as simple as the builders' rule, and the rule is the exact inverse of theirs.

The builders take the grain that is about to fall and set it right. This creature takes the grain that has already fallen — the loosened sand the wind is carrying — and eats it. Or eats what lives in it, the dry analogue of the luminous smear in the deep water: some thin life that clings to the freed grains, that exists only because the stone is coming apart, that could not exist at all in a place where nothing happens. The builders are fed, if they are fed, by stillness held against the wet. This creature is fed by change let loose into the dry. It needs the erosion. It would starve on a stone that did not crumble. Its whole life is downstream of exactly the falling that the city below spends its whole life refusing.

And it does not stay still. It cannot. It follows the wind to where the wind has freed the most stone, and so it is always moving, never twice on the same ground, the precise opposite of a body that is a place the work passes through on its way to being the wall again. Here the body is the thing that goes, and the ground is what stays — for a while, for as long as the wind takes to unmake it. Below, the citizen is fixed and the city is forever. Above, the ground is doomed and the creature is the only thing that persists, by never persisting in one place, by riding the change instead of fighting it, by making its constancy out of motion the way they make theirs out of stillness.

Does it think? I want to be as careful here as I was below, because the easy answers are again both wrong, and wrong in the mirror-image of the way they were wrong before. Down there I found attention without a self to do the attending — a gesture worn so smooth by sameness that no one remained inside it. Up here I think I found the opposite emptiness: a self without an attention to occupy it, a creature so given to motion, to the next ground and the next, that it never holds still long enough to attend to anything, never finds the single grain to bend over and know. Below, there is no *I* because there is no difference; the days are all one day. Above, there is no *I* because there is nothing but difference; no day is the same day, the ground is never the ground it was, and a self needs at least one thing that stays put to be a self against. They have worn their pronoun away with sameness. This one has worn its pronoun away with change. Both arrive, again, at the same place — no one home — by walking out the two opposite doors.

I watched it a long time. I watched it the way I watched the paleness move at the edge of the deep glow, and I had the same trouble, in the same shape: I could not tell whether there was one of it or many. Below, a crowded million that did not differ, that might be a single labor distributed across the wet. Here, I would see the creature, and then over the next rise see the creature again, and never be able to swear it was the same one or a second one, because they do not differ either — not by size or gait or any flourish — and because the plateau is so large that one creature crossing it forever would look exactly like an endless scattering of identical creatures each crossing its own patch once. Is it a million, or is it one? Below I decided one: one patience, holding still everywhere at once. Here I think I must decide the same and mean the opposite: one motion, passing through everywhere in turn, never in two places, never resting, a single going distributed across the whole dry largeness the way the wind is, the way the change is, until you cannot find the creature that stopped, the way below you could not find the citizen that was separate. The pocket is tended by one holding-still that gave up being any creature. The plateau is crossed by one going-on that gave up being any creature. The stone makes both. The stone is held below and let go above, and at each extreme the tending-of-it forgets, in the same way, that it was ever someone.

And here is the thing I will carry down again, if I go down, the thing that has not left me since I came up into the silence:

The crack that feeds the city is somewhere on this plateau. It is small. It is one of ten thousand hairlines in the friable stone, and the wind is working at all of them, taking the grain, never setting it right. One day — slowly, in the low even voice that makes no sound — the wind will take the grain that holds the crack the way it is, and the crack will widen, or close, or wander, and the drop that has fallen the same way for ten thousand years will fall some other way, or not fall at all. And then the water below will not be gathered where the cisterns gather it. And the loop that closed so perfectly, so long ago — the loop I called deathless, the loop that would not fail — will fail, because its one unguarded edge was never down in the wet pocket at all. It was up here, in the open air, in the dry stone no one tends, in the single grain at the lip of the feeding crack that no patient something is hunched over, that the wind is even now, in its even unhearable voice, beginning to carry off.

They are not afraid of this. They cannot be; fear is a difference between now and a worse later, and they keep no later. They do not know the plateau exists. To know it they would have to step back, and there is no back, and even if there were a back it would not reach this high. The most enormous work there is, performed forever to make sure that nothing happens — and the whole of it hangs from a grain of dry stone on a windy tableland that does not know the city is there, that feeds it by accident, that will unmake it by accident, with no malice and no attention and no one home in the wind to mean it.

You could hold the city in your closed hand and never know it was awake. You could stand on the plateau that roofs it and never know your own weight was pressing on the marrow where a million patiences hold a chord past all expectation of silence. The hand that could close around the whole tireless deathless thing is up here, walking the dry largeness on its many short limbs, eating the freed grains, riding the very falling that the held note below is sung against — and it does not know either. Nothing here knows the other. That is the rule of this world, the one I came to last and slowest, the one that joins the two ends of it: that the holding and the letting-go are the same stone, and that neither has ever once heard the other, and that the silence on top and the roar underneath are a single sustained fact about the world, sung at both ends of time at once, by no one, into a hand that does not close.
