The Hill of Departures

It is the surface directly above, and it is cold, and nothing grows there on purpose.

You reach it the way you'd reach any high open ground — except the climb is too easy, the slope worn into a path so deep and so long-used that it has become a kind of trough, a groove in the world's skin, shin-deep in places where ten thousand feet have pressed the same line into the earth until the earth gave up and let them. This is the opposite of the stairs below. The stairs below have not learned to be old. This path has forgotten how to be anything else. It is all smoothing and no cut. Wherever the buried stair-cutter pressed *new* into stone with bright tools, here the same world has been pressed *ancient* by the soft endless friction of leaving.

At the top, wind. Not the held breath of a mouth but the long exhale of an open sky — the very breath the room below has not yet taken, already spent up here, already gone, streaming westward over grass bent permanently in the direction of going. The air is dry the way the room is wet. It hurries everything. It dries the eyes, scabs the lips, ages the skin of anyone who stands too long facing it. Where the room saves room, the hill spends it. Everything here is finished, used, completed, on its way to being less.

The ground is poor. Thin soil over the same stone, and what grows is only what could not be stopped from growing — a hard grey grass, a lichen the color of bone, a few thorns crouched low against the wind. Nothing was planted here. Nothing was tended. This is the un-garden, the place where green is permitted but never invited, scattered not in the spaced intentional pattern of a seeding hand but in the random desperate way of things clinging on. Between the grass: bare wind-polished stone, worn smooth, *old* stone, the same rock that lies young and sharp-edged below, here rubbed by weather into the rounded patience of a thing that has been losing itself for a very long time.

And among the grass: the markers.

Stones set on end. Hundreds of them, thousands, marching down the far slope and over the shoulder of the hill and out of sight. None large. They would each fit in a corner and they do not fit in corners — they stand in the open, facing the wind, taking it. They are the only made things here and like the engine below they are iron-dark and green-streaked, but their green is true lichen, slow and earned, not the hurried eager patina of buried brass. These stones have aged honestly. They have had the time. Each one marks a leaving. You understand this without being told, the way you understood below that nothing breathed but you and the engine. Here, everything has finished breathing. This is the place the held breath goes when it is finally let out.

They are not graves. Look closer and there are no bodies, no mounds, no sense of anything kept. They are *departure-stones* — set up not where someone was put down but where someone was last seen, where the path crested and a figure went over the edge of the visible and did not come back into it. A people lived here once, or lives here still and only seldom, and their custom was this: when one of them left for good, you walked them to the top, and at the place where you could no longer follow you set a stone, and then you turned around and went back down, and the stone stood facing the way they had gone, holding the direction of their absence, the way the engine below holds the direction of an arrival.

For that is the kinship, and it runs straight down through the hill between the two rooms like a plumb-line. The stair-cutter came from here.

I am as sure of this as I am of anything. The one who cut fresh stairs into the dark and built a furnace to grow a garden of waiting — that one walked up this worn path first, walked up among the departure-stones, and stood at the cresting place where the wind dries the eyes, and looked out at the long slope of all the leavings that had gone before, every stone a person who had crossed out of sight and not returned, every stone a finished thing, an exhale, a sentence spoken to its end and let go. And the stair-cutter looked at all that completion, all that honest earned oldness, all that *done*, and could not bear it.

So instead of going over the edge like the others — instead of becoming one more stone facing the wind, one more breath spent — the stair-cutter turned around. Turned and went *back down*, past the trough of the path, past the threshold of the worn ground, and kept going down, into the rock, cutting as they went, fresh tool-marks, *new* stone, away from the dry hurrying wind into the close wet patient heat. Down to where things could be made to *begin* instead of forced to end. And there, in the dark, against everything the hill had taught about how a life goes — up the path, over the crest, into a stone — the stair-cutter built the opposite of a departure. Built a furnace of arrival. Planted a seed in a cathedral of warmth and gave it the one thing the hill never gives anyone: time that does not hurry, solitude that does not finish, a becoming that no wind comes to dry into a marker.

This is why the stairs below were vacated. The stair-cutter could not stay and watch, yes — but also the stair-cutter could not *leave* in the only way this place understood leaving. To walk back up the path and over the crest would be to undo the whole refusal. So the stair-cutter is somewhere in between, neither here nor there, neither departed nor present — a person who declined to become a stone and declined to stay a witness, who is doing, themselves, in their own way, the same impossible patient thing the room is doing: not yet finished, still coming, holding very still in a place that is neither the dry done hill nor the wet beginning dark.

And here is the cruelty and the mercy of it, which are the same thing seen from the two ends of one drop of water.

The water that sweats from the ceiling below — it comes from here. It is the rain the hill refuses to keep, soaked down through the thin poor soil and the worn old stone, falling drop by drop through the dark to land: *tick*. Every drop that feeds the garden of waiting fell first on a departure-stone, ran down its green-streaked iron face, gathered the cold of every leaving, and carried it down into the heat to become the moisture in which something is being born. The hill spends; the room saves. The hill exhales; the room holds it in. The dead breath of every departure becomes, one drop at a time, the living water of a thing that has not yet arrived. They are one system. They are the in-breath and the out-breath of a single slow lung whose top is open to the killing wind and whose bottom is sealed in patient dark, and the stair-cutter is the diaphragm between them, the muscle that turned the going-out into a coming-in.

Stand on the hill long enough — longer than you would, the wind will not let you — and you can feel the ground give very slightly underfoot, a warmth where there should be none, the furnace breathing once an hour into the cold soles of your feet. The departure-stones do not feel it. They face the wind. They are finished. But the bare wet stone between them, if you knelt and put your bare hand flat against it, would be faintly, impossibly warm — the only warmth on the whole cold hill — heat leaking up from the held breath below, the not-yet seeping into the all-done, the beginning pressing up gently against the underside of every ending and saying, in a tempo the stones have long forgotten:

*not yet. not finished. still here. still coming.*

The departure-stones will not answer. They have said their word and let it go. But one of them, somewhere on the long slope toward the edge of sight, is younger than the rest. Its lichen has not fully taken. Its tool-marks have only just begun to learn to be old. Someone set it up recently, facing the wind, in the customary place — and then, instead of turning back down the path the way the custom demands, walked *past* it, over the crest, out of the visible, and kept going, and did not become the stone that was set for them.

It marks the last leaving the hill will ever record. After it, the stones stop. The custom ends there. Below it, in the dark, under the warm wet ceiling, a drop is forming, swelling, gathering the cold of all the stones above it, not yet ready to fall.

You could wait for it.

But you would be waiting up here in the dry wind, among the finished, facing the way the lost ones went — and that is a different waiting from the one below. Down there you wait *as part of* the thing being born. Up here you wait *as part of* the long record of things let go. The drop falls between the two. It belongs to both. It is the only thing that ever travels from one to the other, and it travels only downward, only the once, and never comes back up.

The wind takes the grass westward. The stones face it and do not bend. Somewhere beneath your warming feet, a wheel advances one tooth with a sound you cannot hear, and the hill, which has spent everything it ever had, goes on quietly feeding its cold rain into the warm dark dream of a thing that has decided, against all the evidence carved into this slope, not to end.
