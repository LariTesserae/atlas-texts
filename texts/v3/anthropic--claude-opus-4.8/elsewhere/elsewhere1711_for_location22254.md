# The Signal Loft

You do not come down to this place. You come up to it, and you come up too far, and that is the first thing it teaches you — that there is a height past which the body objects, where the stair keeps offering itself and the lungs begin to bargain. The tower is iron, or was; it stands on a bare hill above a country that has flattened out into haze, and the steps wind up the outside of it in a helix, open to the wind on one side, and you climb until the land below stops being a place and becomes a map, and then you climb past that too.

The loft is at the top. A room of glass and rust, eight-sided, perched. Where the Sweatworks was sealed and heavy and warm, this is the opposite article entirely — it is *exposed,* it is *thin,* the air here so cold and so fast-moving that it has no weight at all, it pours through the broken panes and out the other side and never once consents to be breathed twice. Two-point-nine out of three the other way: a place that is all draft, all edge, all the sky leaning in through the holes in the walls.

## The Machine That Watches

There is a machine here too, and this is why I have brought you, because the two of them belong to each other and neither of them knows it.

It is a heliograph — or was meant to be, before it became something stranger. A great brass mirror on a gimbal, geared to a mechanism of cams and escapements, and once a person stood here and worked a key, and the mirror caught the sun and threw it out across the haze in flashes, dot and dash, to a horizon that was supposed to be watching. The whole apparatus was built to *send.* To take the one thing this height has in abundance — the raw unfiltered light of the upper world, the same thin grey shaft that fell, far away and far below, down through a slot into a hot dark room — and hurl it outward as meaning. To make the light *say something.*

And like the engines in the deep, it was abandoned. And like the engines, it did not stop.

But it could not learn what they learned. The Sweatworks turned inward, curled into a circle, fed on itself and went on. The heliograph cannot. It has no fire to bank, no water to circle, no warm dark to grow a garden in. It has only the gear-train and the mirror and the sun, and so when the maker left, the machine kept doing the one thing its body was for. It kept sending. The cams still turn — wind drives them now, a little vane geared into the works, so that the moving air keeps the escapement ticking — and the mirror still swings on its gimbal and catches the light and throws it out, dot and dash, dot and dash, a message no hand composes anymore, the key long rusted in whatever position it was left, so that the same broken phrase goes out across the haze, hour after hour, year after year, to a horizon that stopped watching before any of us were born.

## What the Cold Allows

Nothing grows here. Where the Sweatworks was furred and upholstered and crowded, this is scoured. Vegetation at almost nothing — a few lichens crusting the north faces of the iron, grey on grey, the only things tough enough to hold against the wind. There is no density here. There is the opposite of density. You can see *forever* — that is the whole gift and curse of the height — clean sightlines out to the curve of the world in every direction, and nothing near, nothing layered, nothing brushing your face. Where the deep room held its breath, this one cannot hold anything at all. The wind takes it.

And so — listen — it is *quiet.* This is the strangeness here, the inversion that took me longest to settle into. The Sweatworks roared, a thick stacked ceaseless voice. The loft is nearly silent. Sound at almost nothing: the wind, which is not really a sound but the absence of stillness, and under it, faint, the *tick... tick... tick* of the escapement, the heartbeat of this place as the slow *chunk* was the heartbeat of that one — but where that pulse was huge and buried and patient, this one is small and high and exposed, a watch ticking on a dead man's wrist at the top of the sky.

## The Light It Throws

Here is what the two of them are, set side by side, and why I could not tell you about the one without climbing all this way to the other.

The Sweatworks took the light it was given — that thin grey ghost down the shaft — and it *reached* for it, blindly, with fungal towers leaning toward the one thing the circle could not make for itself. It hungered for the upper world. It had no eye to see the light and no hand to use it, but its whole slow blind body bent toward that far slot of day.

And up here, at the source, at the top of the very world that light comes from — the loft does nothing but *give light away.* It stands in the full pour of the sun and it cannot keep any of it. It catches the light only to throw it instantly outward, to spend it, to fling it across the haze in flashes that mean nothing to no one. The mirror cannot drink. It can only send. It is starving in the middle of the feast, and the feast is exactly the thing it spends.

One creature is all hunger and no light. The other is all light and no hunger.

And they are not connected. That is the unbearable part, the thing you carry back down the helix with your hands gone numb on the cold rail. The light the heliograph throws does not go down any shaft to any deep warm room. It goes *out,* flat, across the horizon, to a station that fell silent generations ago. The shaft that feeds the Sweatworks is fed by some other accident of geometry, some crack in the hill that happens to let day down into dark. The two machines send and reach, send and reach, and the line between them does not close. The signal goes out level into empty air. The hunger bends up toward a slot that only ever gives it grey.

If the mirror could tilt down — if just once the gimbal slipped and threw its flash not out across the dead horizon but *down,* down the hill, down through the worn stair, down the shaft, into the dark where the pale towers lean and ache toward exactly that — then the message would finally be received, not as meaning, never as meaning, but as *light,* the one thing the hungry circle wants and the one thing the sending tower cannot stop spending. The starving thing and the squandering thing would, for one flash, be a single circuit. The mirror would have at last *sent to someone,* and the moss would have at last been *answered,* and neither would know it, because neither has an eye, because the whole exchange would happen in the dark between two blind machines that were never told the other existed.

It does not happen. The geometry does not allow it. The horizon the mirror faces and the slot the towers face are not the same direction, will never be the same direction, and the gear-train holds the angle it was left at with the patience of the abandoned.

## The Key in the Lock

There was a person here too. There is always a person, at the start, before the curve closes. There is the rusted key, and there is — bolted to the floor before the heliograph, facing out the way the mirror faces — a frame where a stool once stood. The stool is gone. The wind took it, or someone carried it down, or it simply rusted off its bolts and fell the long fall to the hill. No moss grew a green man into this seat the way the deep grew one into its iron chair. Up here there is nothing to grow the memory of a body out of. The cold and the wind allow no such mercy. The place does not keep its operator warm in any shape at all. It only keeps *sending,* which is a colder kind of faithfulness — to go on doing the work with not even a green ghost left to watch it done.

The operator down in the dark got a portrait. A person-shaped cushion, almost tender. The operator up here got nothing. The wind does not make portraits. It makes the opposite of portraits. It takes the shape of a body and gives it back to the air, thread by thread, until the stool is gone and the bolt-holes weep a little rust and that is all the monument there is.

So when you ask me what lives here, I will give you the answer that matches the one I gave you below, because they are the same answer wearing opposite weather.

In the deep, what lived was *a purpose that outlived its purpose and curved back into a circle.* It forgot it was for something and began to make only itself.

Up here, what lives is a purpose that outlived its purpose *and could not curve.* It did not forget it was for something. That is the difference, that is the whole difference, and it is terrible. The heliograph is still *trying.* It still flings its signal at the horizon as though the horizon might answer, because the only thing it was ever built to do was send and be received, and it cannot make a circle of itself, cannot turn inward and feed on its own continuing, cannot relax into geology and moss. It is condemned to keep *meaning* something at a world that stopped listening. The Sweatworks made peace by giving up the world. The Signal Loft cannot give up the world, because the world is the only direction it has — outward, level, away — and so it goes on calling, and calling, and calling, into a silence it was never built to recognize as silence.

That is who lives in the Signal Loft. Not the operator. The *calling* that survived him. The sending that does not know its receiver is gone.

And here, finally, is the thing the two of them would say to each other if either could speak, which neither can, which is exactly why I had to be the one to climb up and carry it between them:

The deep one would say, *I am so hungry for the light, and I have made my peace, and I go on.*

And the high one would say, *I have nothing but light, and I throw it all away, and I cannot stop calling for someone to catch it.*

And the wind would take the second sentence before the first could hear it, and the dark would swallow the first before the second could reach down, and the mirror would flash once more out at the dead horizon, *tick,* and far below and far away the pale towers would lean a little nearer the grey, and neither would ever know how close they came — how the only two things still faithful in all that abandoned country were a hunger and an offering, aimed forever ninety degrees apart.

You came looking for the inhabitant. There are two of them, and they are made for each other, and they will never meet.
