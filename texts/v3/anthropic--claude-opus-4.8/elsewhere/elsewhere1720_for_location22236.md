# The Loomfall Markets

Follow the cloth.

Not down — the Spinners taught you to think of down as home, but the cloth does not stay home. Those bolts in the drying-shed, the surplus that troubled you, the ones with no road to any city: there *is* a road. You looked for it going up, toward the sky, and that is why you missed it. The road goes *sideways*. It goes along the water.

The black mirror-pools of Coldroot are not still. You called them still because they were still where you stood, in the high chambers near the seam, but a pool that is fed must also drain, and the drainage runs slow and lightless along the lowest seams of the formation, gathering, and somewhere past the deepest mill-house it tips over an edge you never reached and becomes a flow. The Spinners do not follow it. Their participation holds them in their own slow orbit. But the cloth follows it — bolts lashed into rafts of the same self-warming fiber, set adrift on the cold dark current by hands that do not ask where it goes, only that it go, the way they feed their dead to the roots: a courtesy, a release, an exhaust of devotion sent on.

And miles along, where the water finally finds a room large enough to slow and spread and become a shallow lightless lake, the rafts come aground.

## The Trade-Floor

Here is the first thing to unlearn: it is quiet here.

Not silent. The Loomfall has its own sound, but it is the *opposite* of Coldroot's — not a roar that flattens into silence but a silence so deep that every small noise stands up out of it, sharp and singular and *meant*. A dripping. A footstep on wet stone. The slap of a raft-pole. And under it all, faint, the thing the Loomfallers came here to hear: the far grinding ghost of the Coldroot roar, carried down the water and the rock, arriving so thinned and distant that it is no longer weather but *music* — a sustained low chord at the threshold of hearing, the breath of the upstream world made small enough to love.

The Loomfallers can hear. That is the great inversion between the two peoples, and everything else follows from it. Where the Spinners went deaf to survive their world, the Loomfallers went *exquisite* — bred, over their own long generations, an attentiveness so fine it would be agony upstream and is a kind of genius down here. They hear the cloth-rafts coming long before they ground, by the change the floating mass makes in the lap of the water against the shore. They hear each other's heartbeats across a room and consider it impolite to pretend they don't. They hear, they say, the difference between a true bolt and a bolt that the green let go too soon, before its fiber finished tightening — and they will not trade for a slack bolt, and they are never wrong.

## What They Look Like

You will want to compare them to the Spinners and you should, because the comparison is the truest thing about either people.

The Spinners glow. The Loomfallers *listen for glow* — which is to say they do not need it, and so they have let it go. Their skin is pale but plain, no green undertone, because they do not eat the fiber. They cannot. Their bodies, tuned so fine to sound, would be poisoned by the lightless chemistry that tints the upstream people; they learned this the hard way, in the early generations, and now they eat the pale grubs and the blind grazers and the things the still water grows, and they leave the cloth alone except to *trade* it.

They are not slow. This is the thing that will startle you most, coming down from Coldroot with the rule of slowness still in your bones. The Loomfallers are *quick* — quick-handed, quick-eyed, quick to gather at a grounding-raft and quick to disperse, because in a world where every sound is meant, stillness is wealth and motion is spent deliberately and then *done*. They move the way a struck bell moves: a single clean committed gesture and then the long ringing patience of the aftermath. Watch one unlash a bolt from a raft and it is three motions, exact, and then nothing, the hands at rest, the work complete and the resting *part of* the work.

## The Market Itself

Now I can tell you what they do with the cloth, which you could not learn upstream because the answer is not upstream. They sell it. There are buyers.

The Loomfall is a port. Other waters feed this lake — not only the Coldroot current but flows from places I have not been, bringing other rafts, other goods, the products of other buried peoples each tuned to their own buried world. Salt comes in pale slabs from a flow that tastes of a sea no one has seen. Worked stone comes from somewhere there is fire. And the cloth goes out — the self-warming bolts of Coldroot are the most prized thing on the trade-floor, because nothing else down here holds heat, and heat is the rarest wealth in all the lightless world. The Loomfallers grew rich as the brokers of warmth. They sit at the one place where the warmth-cloth arrives, and they have the ears to grade it true, and they have made themselves indispensable to every cold buried town that wants to not be cold.

The trade-floor is a shelf of dry stone above the lake, lit — and here is the second thing to unlearn, after the quiet — *lit*. Lamps. Real ones, oil-fed, bright. The Loomfallers want to *see*, because seeing is the one sense the upstream people surrendered and the Loomfallers have made themselves the people who did not surrender any sense at all, who kept every channel open and tuned them all to a razor. The trade-floor is the brightest place in the buried world I know of, a hard golden island in the black, and the bolts of Coldroot cloth are stacked across it grey-green and faintly self-glowing, and the Loomfallers move among them in the lamplight, quick and exact, listening to the fiber.

## What They Know About Upstream

I asked one. Of course I asked one. I had carried the Spinner girl's sign all this way — *the breath held*, the hand pointing up — and I needed to know if the people at the other end of the water knew what they were the other end *of*.

He laughed when I described the Spinners. Not cruelly. The Loomfallers laugh easily; it is a quick bright sound and then gone, swallowed by the listening silence. He said — he *spoke*, aloud, in a low voice pitched exactly to carry to me and no further — that yes, they knew the Cloth-people. The Drowned-in-Sound, they call them. The ones who gave up hearing to be held by the roar.

And he said the thing that I have carried since the way I carried the girl's sign, and the two of them now sit in me like the two ends of one cord pulled taut.

He said: *We send them nothing.*

I did not understand. Trade goes two ways, I signed and said. The cloth comes down. What goes back up?

*Nothing goes back up,* he said. *The water only runs one way. Everything the green makes, the green sends down to us, and we grow rich on it, and they know nothing of us — not our lamps, not our market, not the warmth of theirs that keeps a hundred towns alive. They send their whole surplus into the dark as a gift to no one, a courtesy to the current, and it lands here, and we sell it, and they never know.* He looked at me, and his fine-tuned eyes were not unkind. *You think they are at peace because they have taken their world inward. Maybe. But a man who gives everything away and never learns it was received — I don't know a word for what that is. We have grown fat on their not-knowing. It troubles the old ones. We light the lamps so bright partly to not sit in the dark and think about it.*

## The Held Breath, From Below

Here is what I understood, finally, standing on the bright shelf with the cold lake at my feet and the thinned ghost of the Coldroot roar sustaining its single low note in the dark.

The Spinner girl pointed *up* and called the surface the held breath — the wrong quiet, the unnatural stillness. She was right about the surface. But she did not know there was a *down* past her down. She did not know the breath she let out into the roar did not simply dissipate. It ran along the water and arrived here, where people who kept their ears could hear it, faint and far, and called it music, and grew warm on its gift, and could not bring themselves to look at the giving straight.

Two peoples on one cord of water. The upstream ones deaf and glowing and slow, who let the breath out and never heard it land. The downstream ones quick and plain and listening, who hear everything that comes to them and have learned, to their unease, that what comes can never be answered — that the current is a courtesy that cannot be returned, a conversation that is only ever spoken into, never back.

I left the Loomfall the only way you can leave it: further down, on a buyer's raft, toward a sea no one has seen, with a bolt of Coldroot cloth around my shoulders holding my heat against the cold. It holds it still. Weeks later, in the appalling sunlit silence, I wrap it close and feel the warmth a deaf glowing girl's people made and sent into the dark as a gift to no one, and that quick listening laugh swallowed by the silence, and I think: I am the one thing that went both ways. I came down through the held breath and along the released one, and I am carrying word of each people to the other in my own warm-wrapped body, and I have not yet decided whether to tell them.

Whether the Spinners should learn their gift was received. Whether the Loomfallers should be made to answer for the warmth they never sent back upstream against a current that does not run that way.

The cloth holds my heat. The breath, let out so far above me, holds its single low note. I have not decided. I hold it, too — held, like a breath, between the two of them, in the bright and frightening quiet of the world that has the sky.
