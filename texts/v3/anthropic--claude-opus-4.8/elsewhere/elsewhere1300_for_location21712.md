# The Wash Below the Last Sluice

Start with the water, because the water is what leaves Vel-Anh, and the only thing that does.

Follow a single one of those bright straight streams down past the lowest terrace, past the last cistern with its painted mark, past the final aqueduct where a water-keeper kneels to clear debris that will not gather — follow it past all of that, off the bottom step of the held country, and watch what happens to it.

It begins to age.

This is the first thing you understand about the place below: it is downstream not only in space but in the deep arrangement of things. The water that ran at one unvarying volume up among the terraces, that neither swelled with storm nor thinned with drought, comes over the lip of the last sluice and *forgets the rule.* It braids. It pools and drops its silt and cuts. Within a mile of leaving Vel-Anh the stream has done what it could not do above — it has carved. There is a canyon here, shallow and raw and getting deeper, a thing of consequence, where the young sharp stone is being taught the humility it was spared up the mountain. The water remembers, the moment it is let go, how to wear a world down.

And the wash spreads out at the canyon's mouth into a wide gray fan of gravel and braided channels and bars of pale sand, and *this* is the place: the Wash Below the Last Sluice, where everything that the terraces refused to lose finally arrives to be lost.

## What Collects Here

Things come down the water. This is the strange inheritance of the Wash, the mirror-image of the stillness above: it is a place defined entirely by what arrives.

A roof-tile, worked loose somewhere far up — except nothing works loose up there, and yet here is the tile, tumbled smooth, snagged in a gravel bar. A long curl of wool from one of the grazing things, that should not have shed, because the herds are held — and here it is, matted and gray in an eddy. Bone comes down sometimes, small and clean. A block of dry-laid stone, fitted once so tight a wet leaf could not pass, comes down end over end across the years and lodges in the braid, and the water begins, patiently, to round its knife-edges, to teach it the same lesson — until what arrives at the lower bars is no longer recognizable as having been *placed* by any hand, only as stone, only as the country breaking the way young countries break.

Nothing should leave Vel-Anh. The keepers know the count holds, the walls hold, the shadow holds. But the water leaves, and the water is a thief — it takes a little, always a little, so slowly that the marks up above never drop, so slowly that no keeper kneeling at his undamaged wall could ever catch the theft in progress. The losses Vel-Anh cannot feel, the Wash receives. Every grain the terraces are too stilled to shed, the stream pries loose by night and carries down to the gravel fan and lays out, sorted by weight, in long pale windrows under a sun that here — and this is the thing, this is the whole of it — *moves.*

## The Light Here

The shadows travel.

It is the same sun. You could stand on the highest bar of the Wash and look back up the canyon and see, on a clear morning, the pale fungus of stone climbing its thousand feet toward the snowline, and the light falling on it is the light falling on you. But here the light *walks.* The shadow of the canyon's west wall lies long across the gravel at dawn and shortens through the morning and swings and lengthens east by evening, and at night it is gone entirely into a dark that is whole and complete and *spent* — a real night, the kind that ends a real day. Things cool. Frost forms on the wet bars and burns off. The day is used up here in a way it never is above, and the using-up is the proof that this place is downstream of the held hour, that consequence reaches the Wash the way the water does, having spilled over the last sluice and found, at last, somewhere to land.

## What Lives Here

A heron-thing. Let me give it to you properly, because it is the being of this place the way the keepers are the beings of theirs, and the two are kin in a way that runs along the water like a wire.

It is tall and gray and patient, long-legged for the braided shallows, with a neck it folds and unfolds slowly — and there is the kinship, plain as the stream itself: it too is *unhurried.* But its patience is a different organ entirely. The keeper above is patient because nothing he does will matter; the heron is patient because everything it does will. It stands in the cold braid at first light, perfectly still, watching the channel, and the stillness is not the stillness of a held world — it is the stillness of an animal waiting for the one moment when the world will *change,* the silver flick of something alive coming down the current, the strike that must land now or not at all. The keepers have lost the use of *too late.* The heron lives entirely inside it. Every hour of its hunting is an hour that can be missed.

It eats what the water brings — the small living things that come down out of the canyon, the same current that carries the rounded tile and the orphaned wool. It builds a nest of the sticks the wash delivers, and the nest is wrecked sometimes by the floods that *do* come here, that swell the braid past its bars and scatter the season's work — and it builds again, and this rebuilding is real, is forced, is answered by a world that took the first nest and may take the next. When the masons above re-lay an undamaged wall, they do it as a courtesy to a world that cannot be helped. When the heron re-lays its scattered nest, it does it because the alternative is no nest, no eggs, no heron come the next turning of the moving sun. The keeper tends the world out of love and good manners. The heron tends its life out of consequence. They make, between them, the two halves of what it means to keep going — and the water is the only thread that knows them both.

## The Kinship Along the Water

Here is what runs, unspoken, down the canyon between the held city and the spending wash.

The keepers do not come down here. The way is steep and the air thickens as you descend and thickening air is wrong to them, *heavy,* and besides there is nothing below they need, because they need nothing anywhere. But the water that touches their hands at the aqueduct touches the heron's feet at the braid an hour later, and in that water, sometimes, is a curl of gray wool, a tumbled tile, a thing that should not have left. So you could say — though no keeper would, and the heron cannot — that Vel-Anh *does* lose, after all. That the held hour is not perfectly held. That the price of the unmoving shadow is a long slow theft by the one element they let run free, and that the theft is carried down to a place where the sun moves and the night is whole and a tall gray patient thing stands in the cold and waits for the world to give it the one moment that can be missed.

The keepers tend what cannot decay. The heron tends what can be lost at any moment.

And the stream runs between them, carrying the difference downhill — taking from the place that cannot feel the taking, delivering to the place that lives on what arrives, under a shadow that here, at last, is finally let go to travel, and to set, and to end the day, and to come again.
