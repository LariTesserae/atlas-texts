The Scree Below, Where the Wind Begins Again

You came up out of the dry world to reach the fold, and the dry world is still there when you turn back — it does not vanish because you climbed past it. Follow the water down. The seeps that gather on the brow of the overhang, the slow drip into the worn basins, the patient cycle that takes a season to give back what it took a season to gather — that water does not stay. It cannot. The fold holds it for a while, lends it to the sleeping bodies at the edges of their sleep, threads it cool through the midden where the slow one feels it ticking like a footstep through a house — and then it leaves. It seeps past the back wall, finds the cracks the eons opened, and goes down into the bones of the mountain, and comes out, a long way below and a long way after, where I want to take you now.

Here the wind begins again.

That is the first thing, and it is everything. Up in the fold the wind could not reach; that was the whole condition of the place, a crease the air could not get into, sealed warm and close and unchanging. Here on the scree the wind is the condition. It comes off the bare slopes in long dry hands, it does not stop, it has never been taught to stop. The stones lie loose and clattering, a vast fan of broken rock spilling down the mountain's flank, and the wind moves through them with a sound that is the exact opposite of the fold's sound — not a din of bodies but a thin mineral hiss, the noise of nothing living, of grit dragged over grit, of the mountain slowly coming apart and being carried off a grain at a time. Where the fold was loud with warmth, this is loud with cold. Where the fold held its shape against all change, this is change itself, made visible: a slope that is always sliding, never settled, never able to find a resting form because the wind and the frost will not let it rest.

It is bright here. Punishingly. After the fold's deep dusk your widened eyes would stream and shut against it — the light does not have to be made here, it arrives in floods, hard white light off bare stone, light that scours rather than illuminates. There is no overhang, no brow, no roof but the open sky three kilometers up and falling away. Everything is edge. The stones are sharp — freshly broken, frost-split, not given the eons of patience that rounded every corner of the fold to curve. You could cut your hand on any of them. The erosion here has not finished its work; it has barely begun, it begins again every night when the cold cracks another face open, and it will not finish in any span you could witness, not because the shapes have found their rest but because they never will.

And it is cold. The fold surprised you with warmth at that elevation, stone holding heat like an old animal — here the elevation does what elevation ought to do. The cold comes up through your boots from the loose rock, the wind takes the warmth off your skin as fast as your body can make it, and there is no massed sleeping multitude to lend you anything, no banked warmth beneath. Heat does not come from below here. Heat comes only from the sky, the hard white sun, and the sky takes it back the moment a cloud crosses or the slope turns to shadow. The single law the fold lived by — warmth from below, never from above — is inverted here as cleanly as a thing reflected in still water. Here warmth comes from above or not at all, and mostly not at all.

Almost nothing lives.

This is the truer opposite, deeper than wind or light or cold. The fold was full to bursting, every surface tenanted, the rock a mere frame for the crowd of the living. The scree is empty. It is the great emptiness against which the fold's fullness means anything at all. You can walk a long time across the clattering stones and find no body, no movement that is not the wind's. What lives here lives sparse and far apart and hard: a lichen the color of rust gripping the lee face of a stone, slow as the fungus in the fold is slow but slow in cold and light instead of warmth and dark, taking a hundred years to spread the width of your palm. A few cushion plants, tight green knuckles huddled out of the wind in the gaps between rocks, hoarding their little heat. A pika, perhaps — one small round warm body, far from any other, that does not sleep through the dark but works through the brief bright day, gathering, gathering, hauling dry stems into its den beneath the scree, because here the plenty does not fall from above without ceasing. Here there is no plenty. Here a body must gather against scarcity or die of it, and the gathering is the whole of the pika's life as waiting is the whole of the slow one's. They are kin in their gathering and opposite in why. The slow one gathers because the falling never stops. The pika gathers because everything stops, and soon, and for a long time.

There is no hearth here. There was never any fire. No patient hands came up to make a ring of stones, because the scree offers no easy harvest to draw them — there is nothing here to take, nothing slow and soft to gather in the dark, no warmth to crouch against, no shelter from the wind for the length of a single meal. The makers who came to the fold came because the fold gave. They passed over the scree on the way up and the scree gave them nothing but the labor of the climb, the clatter underfoot, the wind. If they remember anything of this place it is only as the hard between, the cold passage, the part of the journey you endure to reach the warm dark above. The scree does not remember them at all. It has no slow patient self beneath it that could be marked. It is too busy coming apart to keep anything.

And yet.

The water connects them, and the water carries one thing down that the scree could not make for itself. The seep that leaves the fold's back wall is not clean. It has passed through the midden, through the rich soft leavings, through the threads of the slow one's vast pale body — it comes down into the bone of the mountain carrying the fold's plenty dissolved in it, a faint richness, a ghost of all that crowded warm life. And where it finally emerges, far below, weeping out between two clattering stones at the foot of the great fan, there is one green place on all that bare slope. A seep-garden no wider than the fold itself, fed by water that remembers the dark. The cushion plants grow thickest here. The pika dens here. The rust lichen gives way to something almost lush, almost soft, a small breathing patch of the living in the middle of the mineral emptiness — and it lives on what the fold sent down, on the leavings of the leavings, on warmth and fullness transformed by a long cold journey into this thin green line at the bottom of the wind.

The pika does not know it drinks the fold. The fold does not know it feeds the pika. The slow one, reaching toward its one remembered fire, has no sense at all that some portion of itself dissolves nightly and goes down into the bright cold world it has never seen and will never imagine, to become the only soft thing on a slope of edges. The two places do not know each other. They are joined only by the water, which keeps no memory of either, which gathers in the warm dark and gives back in the cold light and rushes where the fold's water could never rush, down and down, carrying the close held fullness out into the open scouring emptiness, until the last of it sinks into the green seep at the bottom and the wind takes the rest.

Up there: warm, dark, full, unchanging, remembering its single fire.

Down here: cold, bright, empty, changing always, remembering nothing, gathering against the scarcity it cannot ever beat.

And between them, ticking down through the bones of the mountain, the one slow thread of water that does not choose, that simply falls, the way the slow one spreads in the silence and the pika hauls its dry stems before the dark — each of them doing the only thing its place has taught it, neither of them ever knowing the other is there.
