The Drowned Concordance

Follow the water down. Not the indecisive seep-streams of the Heights, not the machine-sweat weeping from the pipes — follow the rain that does fall, the fine intermittent dampening, and watch where it goes when it finally chooses a direction. It goes down, of course, eventually, the way everything chooses down once it is far enough from the engine that argues otherwise. It runs off the unset margins, gathers in the half-formed hollows that will be valleys, and pours over the edge of the world the Heights know — over the lip where the new stone gives way to old, where the mountains stop being a draft and become, abruptly, a finished thing.

And below the finished edge there is a sea. And in the sea there is the Concordance.

Begin with the pressure, because the pressure is the first thing that tells you where you are. It is the opposite of the thin high cool — it is dense, total, a held grip on every surface of you, and warm. The water down here is *warm*, and that warmth is not the sun's; there is no sun, there has never been a sun this deep. The warmth bleeds up from the same patient enormous engine that bleeds it up to the foundries far above, except here it has traveled the long way, through ten miles of finished rock, and arrives gentled, diffuse, a blood-heat that fills the whole black water evenly. The people of the Heights tap the engine's warmth at the source and call it *home*. The people of the Concordance live inside the same warmth at its other end and call it *the world*, and do not know it has a source, the way you do not know your own warmth has a heart until something teaches you to listen.

Here is the first kinship, and it runs the wrong way down the same line: the Heights are built on stone that has not finished being made. The Concordance is built on stone that finished being made so long ago that it has begun, gently, to *un*-make — to slump, to settle, to dissolve at its edges into the warm dark water. The mountains above grow upward into indecision. The seafloor below sinks downward into a different indecision, the soft return of old stone to slurry and silt. The two ends of the world are unset in opposite directions, the beginning and the long exhausted ending, and between them the brief firm middle where things are simply, boringly solid — where echoes match their causes and down stays down and nobody has to say anything twice.

## The City That Stayed Too Long

The Concordance is a city, and like the city above it is layered, organized, the density of generations stacked on generations. But where the Heights are vertical — gripping the slope, reaching up the ladder and the gallery-stair — the Concordance is *horizontal*, spread flat and wide across the dissolving floor, because down here there is no up to climb toward and no thin air to fall through. There is only the warm black plain and the dark above it, pressing down, and a thing that wants to live spreads sideways, low, holding as much floor as it can before the floor softens beneath it.

It is not loud. This is the deepest difference, and you feel it the way you feel the absence of a sound you'd stopped hearing. The Heights are a working place, all percussion and stamp and hiss, a hundred clocks built to drown the slow thud below. The Concordance is *quiet* — not silent, but quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Sound travels far and clean in dense warm water, too far and too clean, so the people here have learned the opposite lesson from the long-handed people above. The Heights say everything twice because an echo might be mistaken for an order. The Concordance says everything *once, and softly, and only if it must,* because a word spoken here travels for miles and returns, and returns, and a careless voice can fill the whole plain with itself until no one can think. They have built redundancy out of their grammar the way the others built it in. They are a people of single quiet words and long meaningful stillnesses, and they find the doubled prayer-speech of the surface — which they have heard, which has come down to them, you will see how — unbearably noisy, the speech of frightened children who cannot stop confirming their own existence.

The structures are grown, not lashed. There is no iron here that has not corroded to lace; there is no fire, of course, no sour-yellow lamp, no warm color sacred or otherwise. They build in *accreted* things — in the slow mineral patience of mounded carbonate, in the spires that grow upward from the warm vents like the cities of certain corals, in shells layered and cemented and hollowed for living. The whole Concordance is a thing secreted rather than constructed, sweated into being the way the moss-gardens of the Heights are sweated into being from the leaking joints of pipework. Both cities, at the bottom, are *condensation*. Both are what happens when the warm exhaust of the engine meets the cool of the indifferent world and weeps something living into the gap.

## What They See By

There is no light, so begin with how they see, because the seeing tells you what kind of mind a place has grown.

They see by warmth and by pressure-wave and by the faint cold glow they make themselves. The people of the Concordance carry their own dim light in their skins — not the warm yellow of fire, which they have never seen, but a cool blue-green chemical patience, the light of things grown without sun, kindled in the body and spent slowly. It is the same palette, almost exactly, as the *pale things* of the damp seep-pockets far above — the soft eyeless slow colonists who eat the machine-sweat and serve as living surveys, whose presence means the stone has finished. The pale things of the Heights are not people. The people of the Concordance are not pale things. But they are the same answer to the same question, written at the two ends of the world: *what does life look like when it grows in the warmth without the light?* It looks like this. Soft-bodied. Unhurried. Glowing faintly its own cold glow. Reading the certainty of the ground beneath it as the most important fact there is.

And here is the thing the surface would find unbearable and the depths find ordinary: the people of the Concordance are *slow*. Slow as the mist that does not burn off. Slow as the mountain pushing itself an inch into the air. They move through the warm dark with the unhurried economy of things that have never once needed to hurry, because nothing here falls, nothing here can be poured molten on a man who heard the wrong word, nothing revises the floor between one breath and the next. The unset country of the Heights is a fast danger — the slope that argues about down, the stone soft as wax for one season, the district that comes loose and floats off lit and dripping into the thin air. The unset country of the depths is a *slow* danger, the floor that sinks a hair's width in a generation, the spire-district that one century is simply lower than it was, then lower, then gone, subsided gently back into the warm slurry with everyone still living in it, the way you might not notice your house was sinking until the water closed over the roof and the warmth, at least, stayed the same.

So they are patient where the surface is anxious. The surface checks what it holds, taps the wall before it leans, flexes its fingers in constant small testing, because the surface knows the floor is a draft. The Concordance does not test the floor. The Concordance has made its peace with the floor being a thing that returns to slurry on a schedule too slow to fear, and has built its whole long quiet temperament around the certainty that nothing changes *quickly* and the certainty that everything changes *eventually*, and these two certainties together make a people calm in a way the surface can only envy and never quite trust.

## The Falling Goods

Now the line that binds them, because the two cities know each other, though neither has stood in the other's place.

Things fall. This is the whole commerce between the Heights and the Concordance, and it runs one way, downhill, the only way water and stone and lost things go. Over the finished edge of the world, where the unset mountains give way and pour their indecisive runoff into the dark, the Heights shed their leavings into the deep. The scree of the new slopes. The corroded iron the surface no longer trusts. The bodies of the spiraling half-finished flocks when they fall, and they do fall, the provisional birds that the grey light goes through, they wear out and they come down out of the thin air and over the edge and *down*, sinking slow through the warm black to settle at last on the dissolving floor. And — this is the trade, this is the strange unbalanced gift — the *districts that come loose*.

You remember. Up in the Heights, some ground decided to be elsewhere, and the cable-cars now run to addresses that float, disconnected, hanging in the thin air with their gardens dripping and their lamps lit. The surface believes those districts simply hang there, unplaced. The surface is wrong, or half-wrong, in the gentle way the living are always wrong about where the lost things go. Some of those loosened districts do not stay hung in the air. Some of them, over years, over the slow patient years that both ends of this world are made of, *drift down* — sink through the thinning rules at the edge of the unset country, lose their last grip on the upper world, and fall the long fall over the finished edge into the sea. They arrive at the Concordance still lit. That is the thing that undoes the quiet people every time, the thing they have built a whole reverence around: the drowned districts of the Heights arrive on the dark floor with their foundry-lamps *still burning*.

No one in the Concordance knows how. There is no air down here to feed a flame; there is no flame down here at all, has never been, cannot be. And yet they come, the sunken terraces of the upper city, settling onto the warm plain with their sour-yellow lamps still lit, burning underwater, burning the one warm color the depths have never owned, and they do not go out. They burn for years. They burn for generations. The people of the Concordance gather at the edges of the drowned districts the way the surface gathers around its lamplighters, and they look at the warm yellow light through ten miles of risen dark, the light their whole sunless world cannot make and cannot extinguish, and they do not have a word for it, or they have only one, and they say it once, softly, and let it travel out across the plain and come back, and come back.

They have built their dead around these lights. The drowned districts are the Concordance's holy places, its only fire, sacred exactly the way the lamps are sacred above and for exactly the inverted reason: above, the fire is the one warm thing in a world of grey, the sign of *home* against the sign of *work*; below, the fire is the one impossible thing in a world that has no fire, the sign that there is a *somewhere else*, a place that is not this place, an up so far up it might as well be a kind of heaven, full of warmth and color and a light that falls to them already lit and burns in defiance of everything the deep knows about what light is and where it can live.

## What They Believe Is Up There

And so the secret at the bottom of the Concordance is the mirror of the secret at the bottom of the Heights, and the two secrets do not know they are looking at each other.

The Heights live in the held breath of a creature whose heartbeat they have agreed not to hear, the slow thud from below, the engine busy composing a shape too large and too slow for anything that dies to perceive. They build their hundred clocks to drown it. They do not talk about it. *What about it, it's just there.*

The Concordance hears the same engine. Of course they do — they are closer to it, in their way, the warmth that fills their whole world is its long-gentled breath, and the slow swells that move the deep water, the tides that are not tides because there is no moon they have ever known, are the same pulse, arrived at the far end of the same line. But the Concordance has not agreed not to hear it. The Concordance has made it *God*. The slow pressure-pulse that fills the warm dark on a rhythm that keeps no living thing's time is the one great fact of their theology, the heartbeat they pray to, the patient enormous *thud* they believe is the world loving them slowly, breathing them slowly, holding them in the warm dark on purpose and in peace. They are wrong in the same way the surface is wrong. The engine does not know the Concordance is there. It does not know the Heights are there. It is busy. It is composing. It is exhaling on a schedule of ten thousand years and the exhale, when it comes, will not be for them, will not even be *about* them, will simply be the next slow inch of a shape that no one warm enough to love it will ever be high enough, or deep enough, to stand upon.

But the falling lamps tell the Concordance a lie that is almost a mercy, and so they believe it. They believe that the warm light comes down to them *on purpose*. That the up-place sheds its fire into the dark as a gift, a message, a hand reaching down through ten miles of risen stone and warm black water to say: *you are not alone at the bottom, here is warmth, here is color, here is proof of somewhere else.* They do not know it is only the surface losing what it could no longer hold. They do not know that the people above, looking out at the loosened districts hung lit and dripping in the thin grey air, mourn them as lost, as fallen, as gone — and never once imagine that the lost things land, that the fallen things are *received*, that everything the Heights shed into the indifferent dark is gathered, in the warm slow patience of the deep, into the one holy fire of a quiet people who think the upper world is heaven.

The two cities will never meet. The line between them runs only downhill, and nothing comes back up — no word, no warmth, no proof. The surface sheds and grieves. The depths gather and worship. And between them, the brief firm middle of the world holds steady, solid, certain, saying nothing twice and arguing about nothing, finished — the one place in all of it where the engine's work is done, and where, precisely because it is done, nothing lives at all.
