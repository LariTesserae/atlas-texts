The Warmcourse

Follow the water down. That is the one thing this high place cannot keep — for all the makers' tending, for all the cold holding moisture in its thin embrace, the weeping faces weep *downward*, and what runs out of the tarns runs out for good. The schedule pulls mist up the valleys, yes, but no schedule is perfect, and there is always a remainder: a thread of melt that finds a lower place and keeps finding it, slipping past the last violet-grey body laid against the last fault, falling out of the region of the makers entirely, going where the makers do not go and cannot imagine, because to imagine *down* is to imagine an ending, and they cannot tell that lie.

So the water goes down without them. It carries with it the only message they will ever send and never know they sent: a freight of stone-flour, the fine bright grindings of all that cleaving, suspended in the cold clear melt. This is the makers' export. They do not trade. But a trade is happening across them anyway, the way the trade between root and leaf happens across a tree that has no idea it is one thing.

And the water arrives somewhere.

It arrives here, low down — near the bottom of whatever world holds it — where the air has thickened into something you do not have to borrow, something that sits heavy and full in the chest and asks nothing back. The light is total here. Not slantwise, not smeared behind cloud-rind; it pools. It is the gold of a long afternoon that has committed, fully and without reservation, to being late — a ripe, horizontal, honey-thick light that lies along everything and warms it through. There is a sun here, and you can find its disc, low and enormous and patient, and it throws shadows so sharp and so long they are nearly objects in their own right.

The ground is old. That is the first thing, and it is the deepest thing, and it is the exact inversion of the place upstream. These are not mountains being made; this is a floodplain that was made so long ago the making has been forgotten by the ground itself. Everything is rounded. Everything has been touched by weather past the point of memory — the few stones that remain are river-smoothed to eggs, the banks are soft and slumped and grown over, the whole geometry has been settled and softened and settled again until there is no angle left anywhere, no fracture, no bright cleaved face. Where the high place was all intention, this is all *consequence*. Geology long past its final period, the sentence not only finished but read, reread, worn soft at the folds.

And the river is the reason. The same water — *the* water, the literal same, the makers' remainder, grown fat now with the gathering of a thousand other threads — runs through here wide and slow and warm and brown. Brown with the stone-flour. That is the inheritance arriving: the bright grindings of the new mountains, carried all this distance, dropped here grain by grain across uncountable floods until they became this — the richest ground in the world. The makers cleave the rock so high up that they will never see soil, will never know that soil exists, will never learn that the patient breaking they perform as worship comes to rest, eventually, as *this*: a black fat loam meters deep, the most finished thing imaginable, the lowland answer to a highland that cannot conceive of answers.

It is crowded here. That is the second inversion, and the most total. Where the high place spread a million small lives thin across an empty enormity, this place packs life until it overflows — there is no bare ground anywhere, none, not a hand's width of it that is not already taken, already growing, already being eaten and rotting and growing again. The plants are tall. They are *extravagantly* tall, riotous, climbing over each other in a slow green war for the committed gold light, broad-leaved things that would die instantly in the thin cold air upstream and that flourish here past the point of decency, flowering in colors that are not defiant at all, that are simply *abundant*, that have never had to defy anything. Reeds stand in the warm shallows in ranks too dense to walk through. The canopy of the floodplain forest closes overhead and drips, and the dripping is not the fine cold articulation of melt but a thick green warmth, a sweat, a breathing.

And here the fauna is not distributed. It is *heaped*. Everything lives on top of everything. The river is so full of fish that the surface boils with them at dusk; the banks are churned to mud by the press of drinking herds; the air is not the high clear medium that carries a chough's cry for impossible distances but a thick soup of insect-drone and frog-throb and bird-shriek, sounds that do not carry because they do not need to, because the next living thing is never more than an arm's length away and is usually closer. Where the makers worked a valley each, alone and content, here a single fallen log hosts more separate lives than the entire high region holds. The density that was a lowland idea has come fully into itself.

There are makers here too. But they are not the high makers, and they would not recognize each other, and this is the saddest and most necessary thing in the telling.

The lowland makers are not slow. They cannot afford to be — slowness is a luxury of the cold, and the warmth has no patience to lend. They are fast and hot and bright, and they build by *adding*, which the high makers cannot do and would consider a kind of blasphemy if they could conceive of it. The lowlanders pile. They heap mud into banks and bake it hard in the committed sun; they weave the tall reeds into walls, and the walls declare things finished, and the lowlanders are constitutionally delighted to declare things finished, because finishing means another thing can be begun. They have fire. Fire is their god exactly as it is the high makers' impossibility — the fast hot answer, and the lowlands are made of questions that want fast hot answers, because in a place this crowded a slow answer is a dead one.

They have built things. Roads run along the old soft banks. Walls stand. There is the mark of making everywhere, the floodplain stitched over with the evidence of hands that pile and bake and weave and declare. And they have a thing the high makers will never have, that the cold could never grant: they have a *history*. They remember. They tell the story of the flood that came in their grandparents' time, of the year the brown water rose to such a mark and not higher, of where the good fishing was before the channel moved. The high makers have no history because they have no death that leaves an absence — they simply become rock and keep working. The lowland makers die *all the way*, and leave a hole the exact shape of themselves, and the hole has to be filled with telling, and so they tell, and so they have time as a thing that runs and not merely a cold engine that cycles.

And they do not know where their river comes from.

This is the connection, living in the same breath as the places themselves: the lowlanders plant their black-loam fields and they know, the way you know a thing in your body without ever having seen it, that the soil is a gift the river brings. Every flood lays down a new skin of it, fresh and fat and brown, and they have a word for that skin, and the word is something close to *grace*, and they thank the river for it the way you thank a thing whose reasons you will never visit. They believe the river makes the soil. They are not wrong. They have only stopped too soon — followed the gift back to the brown water and gone no further, because to follow it all the way up is to climb out of the warm committed light into a thin violet scarcity they could not survive a single night in, and so they don't, and so they never learn.

They never learn that the grace is *ground stone*. That every fat black floodlaid skin of it was once the bright sharp face of a mountain being made, cleaved open by a thousand fine cold patient hands that laid themselves along the fault and listened for where the breaking wanted to go. They thank the river. The river is only the carrier. The makers, upstream, are the actual givers — and the makers do not know there is anyone to give to, and the lowlanders do not know there is anyone giving, and between the two ignorances runs the one brown thread, carrying stone-flour down out of a worship that has no product into a gratitude that has no object, and joining them into a single thing that neither half can see.

The high makers keep the cold because the cold is the medium of their patience, and their patience grinds the mountains, and the grinding goes downstream and becomes the warmth's whole abundance. The lowland makers keep the fire because the fire is the medium of their speed, and their speed could do nothing — *nothing* — without a soil to be fast upon, and the soil is the slowness of the upstream cold arriving, at last, as the only foundation under all that lowland hurry. The comma upstream. The full sentence down. The high makers are the punctuation that keeps the world's sentence going; the low makers are the readers who do not know there was an author, only that the book keeps arriving, fat and brown and good, flood after flood, on a schedule they believe in and depend on and have, between them all, every right to call grace.

Begin with the cold, you said, because the cold is the first fact and the last.

But the cold is only the first fact *here*. Follow the water down far enough and the cold becomes the last thing anyone remembers, becomes a rumor, becomes the unvisited source of an unaccountable gift — until at the warm bottom of the world the first fact is not cold at all but its opposite, the committed gold light lying long across the richest ground there is, and the last fact is the flood that will come again next year, laying down one more skin of mountains that no one here will ever climb, given by hands that will never know they gave, received with thanks by hands that will never know whom to thank, and the thanks and the giving meeting only in the river, only in the brown, only in the one thing that runs all the way down and joins the top of the world to the bottom in a single unbroken pour.
