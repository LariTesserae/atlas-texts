# The Stillwater

There is a place in this world where the stone has finished.

You reach it not by descending but by following the cold. In the Hollowing you learn to read the churn by its warmth — where the world is most alive it is most hot, the herds breathing heat into the young rock. So you do the opposite of everything instinct teaches and you go toward the chill, down the draining edge of the warmth, where the colonies thin and the drip-rhythms slow and finally, in a low passage that does not heal shut behind you, stop.

The first thing that reaches you here is not pressure. It is *absence of pressure*. The animal closeness lets go of your skin all at once and you feel, for the first time since you came underground, the shape of your own edges — where you end, which you had forgotten.

## The Old Stone

This rock is finished and it is finished the way a sentence is finished. Every contour worn. Every edge rounded by a patience the Hollowing has never known. Water did this — water that took its time, which means water that *had* time, which means a place that held still long enough for time to do its slow grinding work. The walls here are smooth as worn teeth. The floor is a single sheet of stone polished to a soft sheen, dished gently in the center by ten thousand years of one thin current crossing it the same way every day.

The light does not come up from the floor here. The finished stone has forgotten how to keep it — or never learned, or let it go long ago. The glow here is older, dimmer, pooled only in the deepest cups of the rock where light went to settle and could not climb back out. It is the light of the Hollowing, the *same* light, carried here by the only thing that crosses between the two worlds. But it arrived tired. It lies low. You move through a permanent late evening, and after the shadowless noon of the breathing chambers it feels like grief, and then it feels like rest, and you cannot decide which.

## The Water That Stays

In the Hollowing water is always *going* — beading, dripping, sliding, caught and passed hand to hand down the colonies, drunk before it can pool. Here it has stopped going.

There is a lake. Not large. You could throw a stone across it, if there were stones loose enough to throw, and there are not, because everything here is fixed. It is utterly still. No drip disturbs it; the ceiling is too old to weep. It holds the dim light on its surface in an unbroken skin and beneath the skin it is clear all the way down to a floor you cannot see the bottom of, and it is *cold*, and it does not move, and it has not moved in longer than the Hollowing has existed.

This is where the water of the breathing world comes to rest. Trace the thin current backward and it threads up through the worn passages, gathering from a thousand cupped places, draining the warm churning country drop by drop, carrying the light it stole and the warmth it borrowed, until it arrives here and gives both up and lies down. The Hollowing is a place that cannot keep anything. The Stillwater is what its water becomes when it is finally allowed to keep still. They are the same water. One world is the other world's later.

## The Silence

There is no sound here.

After the marsh-roar of the breathing chambers, after the tidal thrum you felt in the sternum, the silence of the Stillwater is not an absence but a presence — it has weight, it leans on the ears, it makes you aware of the small sounds of your own body the way the Hollowing made you aware of everyone else's. Your heart. Your breath. The faint click your own joints make. You become, here, the loudest living thing, which means you become aware of how little life is left.

Because there is almost none. The saturation has drained away with the warmth. A few pale blind things move slowly along the lake's edge — descendants, perhaps, of the cilia-grazers, or cousins, things that followed the water down and stayed and gave up their eyes because there was nothing here worth seeing fast. They graze nothing. They drink the still water and move on the cold floor and they do not brush you, do not part around you, because there are not enough of them to make a current of bodies. You walk alone. You had forgotten that was possible.

## What Rests at the Lake's Edge

And here is the thing I came all this way to find.

At the edge of the still water, set in the worn cup of the bank, there are vessels.

Clay. Fired hard. Many of them — far more than the scattered caches of the breathing world, ranked along the shore in their dozens, in their scores, each one set down with the same care, each one full of clear water that does not need to be drunk because the whole world here is clear water that does not move.

They came down the current. That is the first understanding and it knocks the wind out of you. The Ones Who Tally leave their vessels in niches in the churning dark, in trust that the world will not unmake the niche before their thirst returns — and sometimes the world *does* unmake the niche, flows the stone shut, opens a new passage, and the little fired pot, the one defiant permanent thing, the one object the closing world cannot grow over because it is already finished and hard — the pot does not get eaten. The pot gets *carried*. The churning floods a vault, the water rises and slides toward the cold, and it takes the vessel with it, the one thing buoyant enough or loose enough to go, down the draining edge, down the worn passages, down to the only place in the world that holds still.

They have all arrived here. Every promise that was set down and lost. Every clay pot of faith that a later-self would return — set down in trust, and the trust broken by the world, not by the maker — has not been destroyed. It has been *delivered*. The breathing world cannot keep its promises. The Stillwater keeps them all.

## What This Means, Read as the Place Itself

The Ones Who Tally will never come here. They follow the warmth; this is the cold. They ride the opening stone; this stone has finished opening forever. To them the Stillwater is not even a rumor, because nothing comes back up the current to tell them — the water only descends, the light only dims and settles, the lost vessels only arrive and never return. They live their whole lives in the faith that what they set down and lost is simply *gone*, unmade, eaten by the churn like everything else.

It is not gone. It is here.

Every pot of clear water they wept over losing — every niche they came back to and found grown shut, and stood before the healed stone and understood that the world had taken another small kindness — all of it traveled down through the dark while they grieved and came to rest in ranks along a cold shore they will never see, still full, still clear, still keeping faith. The civilization of leaving-for, the fellowship of the never-quite-met, scatters its trust across a churning world believing most of it is swallowed. And the world does not swallow it. The world *collects* it. There is a place where every act of faith they ever made arrives intact and is held forever in a silence they cannot imagine, on the still skin of the water that was once their own warm dripping breathing crowded home.

I stood at the lake's edge among the ranked vessels in the low old light. The blind things moved at the rim. The water did not stir. And I understood that the Stillwater is not the opposite of the Hollowing but its meaning — the place where impermanence is proven, at the last, to have been wrong. *Nothing here holds still,* says the law of the breathing world. But everything that was set down in love comes down at last to the one chamber that does nothing but hold, and there it is held.

Somewhere far above and warm, in a vault that will close before morning, one of them scratches a row into a dying wall — *I left the water where it would find it, I was here, I was here* — and believes the water lost, and walks on into the churn.

The water is not lost. I have seen where it goes.

It is waiting, full and clear and cold, on a shore at the bottom of the world, in perfect faith, for a thirst that traveled down behind it long ago.
