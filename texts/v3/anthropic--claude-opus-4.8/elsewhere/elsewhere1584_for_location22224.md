The Drylands Above the Weep

Follow the water back. Not down, where it dissolves the oldest stone and feeds the circle — up, against the weeping, the way the masons never can, to where the films thin and the warmth thins with them and the air at last gives up enough of its water to be breathed without drowning. Follow it past the tower-tops the traveler's eye could not reach, up through the warm dark into a cooler grey, and out — over a lip of rock the basin has been climbing toward forever and will never crest — onto the high flat country where the water comes from.

This is the place the basin does not know exists. The masons live their whole circular lives on the supply, and the supply has a source, and the source is here, and it is dry.

Call it the Drylands, because the beings who live there call it nothing, and because dryness is its single law the way wetness was the basin's. It is a high pale plain of the same stone — the basin is only its low collapsed corner — but here the stone is bare and pale and cool to the touch and never slick. The water that floods the basin below is not on the surface here; it is *under* it, deep in the rock, a body of warm water held in the dark of the stone, working its way down by every crack and seam toward the lip where it will begin to weep. Up here you would not know the water was there at all, except for the wells.

The wells are not dug. They are grown, the way the towers below are grown, by the only other thing in this world that builds.

# The Drinkers

They are the masons' far kin, though neither would know the other if you set them side by side, and you could not, because one cannot live where the other lives. The same ancestor, somewhere back past where this world keeps its records, split into two answers to the same question — *what do you do with stone dissolved in water?* The basin's answer was: lay it down, build upward, become the wall. The Drylands' answer was the opposite, and the opposite made an opposite creature.

The Drinkers do not secrete stone. They dissolve it.

Picture a thing the size of a spread hand this time, flat, broad, pressed to the pale rock like a starfish or a poured thing, dark where the mason was translucent, dry-skinned where the mason was soft and wet. It has no filaments. It has, instead, a whole underside that weeps — the inverse of the basin, a single body that does what the whole basin's water does, leaking a thin acid film against the stone it lies on, and waiting, and drinking back what the acid loosens. Where a mason takes mineral from water and gives wall, a Drinker takes mineral from wall and gives water — releases it, spent and clean, to trickle down into the dark of the rock and begin the long fall toward the weeping lip and the basin below.

This is the source. The traveler ankle-deep in the warm film, hearing the million-fold trickle that was the bed the roar lay on — that water was drunk out of the high stone by the Drinkers, ages before, dissolved from the pale plain grain by grain and let go to find its way down. The basin's whole supply, the mineral that becomes tower and mason and tower again, was mined up here, in the dry and the quiet, by flat dark things lying motionless on bare rock. The masons build the city out of stone the Drinkers unbuilt from the plain. One creature's wall is the other creature's water. The circle the traveler thought closed at the base of the towers does not close there at all. It runs up the seams, out the lip, across the plain, and into the undersides of the Drinkers, and only there — if anywhere — does it turn.

# The wells

Because a Drinker drinks downward, it sinks. Lying still on the stone, weeping its acid, it dissolves a shallow bowl beneath itself over a long time, and settles into the bowl, and deepens it, and settles further. A Drinker spends its life descending into a pit of its own drinking, and the pit becomes a well, and the well reaches, at last, the warm water in the deep rock — and then the Drinker has done the only thing its whole life was the slow accomplishment of. It has opened a road for the water to fall.

The plain is dotted with these wells, pale-rimmed shafts sunk straight down, each with its Drinker at the bottom, far below the surface now, still drinking, still descending, the water it has reached pouring past it into the deeper dark and away toward the lip. From above, the Drylands read as a flat pale nothing pocked with holes — the negative of the basin exactly, a place defined by its shafts down the way the basin is defined by its towers up. Where the masons stack the dissolved stone into the air, the Drinkers bore the air down into the stone. The basin is a positive cast and the Drylands the mold it was poured from, and neither will ever know it is half of a single shape.

# The silence

And here is the thing the traveler could not have imagined, because the traveler's whole body was full of the roar:

The Drylands are silent.

A Drinker makes no sound. It has no filaments to rasp; it does not move; it lies and weeps and sinks, and dissolving is a quiet act, the quietest there is. A whole plain of them, thousands, sunk in their wells across the pale stone under the thick grey sky, makes no more sound than the stone itself. The water moves below, soundless in the deep rock until it reaches the basin's open faces and begins to fall and the masons rake it and the roar begins. But here, at the source, where the water is still inside the stone and the Drinkers lie still inside their wells, there is nothing to hear. The loudest place in the world and the quietest are the same place, divided only by a lip of rock and the direction the water runs. The roar the traveler felt in the sternum was made entirely of stone that was taken, up here, in perfect silence.

# How they end, and where it goes

A Drinker does not harden into a wall, because it builds no wall. When it is spent — when it has drunk as deep as a Drinker can drink — it stops, and the acid stops, and the body, no longer weeping, dries. And the dry stone of the plain, patient, closes over it. The well it spent its life sinking is, at the top, already beginning to fill with the dust the wind moves and the grains the surface sheds, and slowly, over an age, the shaft seals, and the Drinker is sealed in it, and the plain is whole again and pale and unmarked where a well once was. The Drinkers do not become the supply. They become the plain — they are taken back upward into solid stone, sealed dry in the high country, the exact mirror of the masons who are taken back downward into running water and raised again as wall.

So the two endings face each other across the lip. The mason dies into motion, into the circle, into the endless rising-and-dissolving of the city that is always being born. The Drinker dies into stillness, into the sealed dry rock, into a plain that does not change because nothing in it moves at all. One world flows in place and one world holds in place, and the water is the only thing that crosses between them — falling out of the silent high country into the roaring low one, carrying the dissolved plain down to become the building city, the dead of the Drylands becoming, grain by grain, the towers and the masons and the towers again of the basin below.

The traveler, ankle-deep in the warm film, hearing the trickle beneath the roar, was hearing the Drylands arrive. Every drop that ran past that traveler's foot was the high plain, dissolved in silence ages ago by a flat dark thing sinking into a well it would die at the bottom of, sent down at last to be raked up by a hundred filaments and laid as the next bead of a wall in the middle — the exact middle, always the middle — of being born.

And the Drinkers, sunk in their quiet shafts, drinking the plain down grain by grain and letting the water fall away into the dark, do not know that the water arrives anywhere. They do not know there is a basin. They do not know they are the source of a roar they will never hear, the masons of a city by being the un-masons of a plain. They know only the single sentence their bodies are: *here is dry stone, here is acid in me, weep it where the stone is and drink back what comes loose and sink.* And between that sentence and the mason's — *here is wet stone, here is mineral in me, lay it where the surface is coolest and harden* — runs the whole world, the entire distance of it, top to bottom, dry to wet, silent to roaring, held to flowing, two halves of one circle that neither half can see, turning on a thread of water that crosses a lip of rock no creature in this world will ever stand on top of and look down both ways at once.

Except, perhaps, the traveler. Who came up out of the roar following the trickle back, crested the lip the basin spends forever climbing toward, and stood at last in the silence on the pale dry plain, between the wells, in the cool, and heard — for the first time since entering this world — nothing at all. And understood, standing there, that the silence and the roar were the same thing seen from its two ends, and that the water at their feet, falling away into the dark beneath the Drinkers and down toward the masons it would become, was the one sentence the whole world was saying, around and around, in two voices that would never meet: *take the stone, move the stone, become the stone, begin again.*
