# The Cistern Coast

Follow the water down.

Not the cloud-water, not the gathered drops — those stay up top, hoarded and earned. Follow instead the *overflow*, the seep, the thousand small trickles the high town hears beginning in its rock and never sees arrive. Follow the future rivers being born grain by grain. They pool. They braid. They gather their gathered moisture the way the cisterns never can, and somewhere far below the white-blind tiers, where the slopes finally tire of being steep, the water becomes a real and singular thing: a river, then a delta, then a sea.

And on the shore of that sea, where the mountain's whole exhalation finally arrives as liquid, is the place that lives on what the high town let go.

## The Arrival of the Water

It comes down brown.

That is the first thing to understand, and it would horrify the people of the damp if they ever made the journey, which they do not. The water that reaches the coast is *thick* — silted, tea-dark with the tannin of a hundred miles of leaf-rot and washed peat, carrying the ground-up substance of the mountains in suspension. It has touched everything on its way down. It is the opposite of the high town's drop-by-drop purity: it is water as a *mass*, water as a force, water that has stopped being precious because there is so unspeakably much of it.

The river arrives not as one channel but as fifty, a fan of distributaries spreading across a flat that has no horizon — a delta so broad and so low that the land and the water have given up arguing about which is which. Here the rule is reversed. Up there, you do not waste water. Down here, *you cannot.* There is no act of carelessness large enough to matter. You could pour out a cup, a barrel, a cistern's worth, and the flat would simply drink it and forget. The coast people have no word for the high town's deepest sin because they have never once had occasion to commit it.

## The Flat

It is hot here. That is the second great inversion. The dim silver muffling of the peaks is gone; the sky opens out enormous and white-bright and pitiless, and the sun the high people rarely see lies flat across the water all day long. The coast people are *brown* — sun-cured, salt-cured, the deep weathered brown of folk who have never once stood inside a cloud. Where the mountain folk are pale as wet stone, these are dark as the wet river-mud they live in.

And they live in mud. The whole delta is mud — black, fertile, stinking-sweet, the accumulated body of the mountains laid down flat and soft. Nothing here is built on rock because there is no rock; the rock is forty days upstream and arrives only as silt. So they build on stilts. Houses of pale split cane and palm-thatch stand high on legs of mangrove-wood, clustered in stilt-villages over the braided channels, connected not by stairs but by *boats* — narrow, shallow, paddled. The vertical town climbed; the horizontal town floats. Where the mountain folk read elevation, these people read the tide and the channel-depth and the daily shift of the silt-banks, which move, which are never twice in the same place, which must be learned and re-learned every single morning.

## What They Make of the Mud

They are farmers of the thing the high town threw away.

Every grain of silt that darkens the river is the mountain coming down to them, and they have built their entire life on catching it. They raise low dikes of woven cane and mud, and they let the brown water in, and they let it stand, and they let it drop its load — and what it drops is the richest soil in the known world, the patient gift of stone dissolved and carried and laid down at their feet. They grow rice in flooded paddies that mirror the white sky. They grow a tall reed they split for building and weave for everything else. They grow a fat-rooted tuber in the mud-flats that needs no tending, only harvesting, dug up black and washed clean and sweet.

They are not careful people. They cannot afford to be — carefulness is a virtue of scarcity, and they have never known scarcity of water or soil. What they have instead is *abundance management*, which is a harder discipline than it sounds: the knowledge of when to let the flood in and when to shut it out, which channel is silting closed and which is scouring open, how to ride a thing that gives too much without being drowned by its giving. The mountain folk spend every motion carefully. The coast folk spend extravagantly, constantly, because the river refills the account faster than they can empty it — and the only true failure here is to fail to *use* what arrives, to let a flood-season pass without taking its silt, to leave the gift sitting and souring in a channel.

## How They Differ, and How They Are the Same

If you could set the two peoples side by side — the small-lunged, slow-spending, color-hoarding folk of the silver heights, and the broad-chested, loud, extravagant folk of the brown flat — you'd think them opposites in everything. And they are. But look closer and it's the same argument seen from its other end.

The mountain folk live where water is everywhere and *drinkable water* is earned. The coast folk live where water is everywhere and *land* is earned — earned silt by silt, season by season, the same drop-by-drop patience but practiced on solid ground instead of liquid. The high town gathers the cloud one bead at a time. The low town gathers the mountain one grain at a time. Both are building something out of an abundance that pretends to be free and isn't. Both know, in their bones, that the thing arriving by the ton is not the same as the thing you can *keep*.

They simply hoard opposite halves of the same water. The heights keep its purity and let its body go. The coast keeps its body and lets its purity go. Between them runs the river, carrying down exactly what each has decided not to value, so that nothing — in the end, across the whole long fall of the world — is wasted. The mountain's two sins, carelessness and waste, are between the two peoples answered completely: the high town wastes nothing of the water, the low town wastes nothing of the earth, and the river is the long bright thread that makes the two economies one.

They have never met. They will never meet. The journey down is forty days through country that kills, and the journey up is impossible for lungs grown wide on thick sea-air. Each people knows the other only as a rumor in the water — the coast folk find, sometimes, in the silt, a fleck of worked brass, a green-stained scrap of bright wool, a length of pipe ground smooth, and they do not know what to make of these signs of *carefulness* arriving in a place that has no use for it. They keep them anyway. They are not wasteful people either, in their way. They string the brass bits on cords and hang them in the doorways of the stilt-houses, where they turn in the hot wind off the water, and they call them *the gifts from the rain-place*, having no idea there is a town up there at all — only an instinct that something patient lives at the top of the river, and sends its small bright leavings down.

## The Brackish Edge

Go further. The delta does not end at land; it ends where the river-mud finally meets the salt, and that meeting is its own country — a vast brackish margin neither sea nor river, where the mangroves stand in ranks on their stilted roots like a forest that learned to wade. This is the last place the mountain reaches. Here the silt finally settles for good, building the land seaward grain by patient grain, the delta growing out into the sea at the speed of a slow life — so that the coast folk, like the mountain folk, live on something that is still, imperceptibly, being made.

The water here goes from brown to green to the open blue, and the people thin out, and the stilt-villages give way to a few solitary platforms where the *salt-watchers* live — the coast's own version of the water-leavers, the quiet ones who go to the edge.

They are the ones who tend the boundary.

Their work is to read the salt-line: to know each day how far up the channels the sea has pushed, to mark it, to warn the rice-villages inland when a high tide or a low river-season lets the salt creep too far up and threaten the paddies. They live alone or nearly so, out where the mangroves give way to open water, on platforms lashed high above the tide. They are a little stranger than the rest — long-silent, prone to staring out at the flat sea-horizon the way the mountain's water-leavers stare up into the cloud. They listen to something just past the edge of the green, the way the others listen just past the edge of the white.

What waits out there in the open sea, beyond the last mangrove, they will not quite say. Not a monster. A *weather*. Something vast and tidal that comes in slow and never fully lifts, that must be met and read and tended, that takes the land's freshwater patiently into its salt and gives back the storms and the fish and the long swells that build the beaches. They make their offering to it too, in their way — they pour the first cup of every season's rice-water out over the platform's edge, into the brine, a thing they cannot explain and have always done.

And so at both ends of the falling water there is a person who lives at the threshold, who carries the household's substance to the very edge of the known and pours it out for the great slow tide that lives just beyond — the high one pouring fresh water up into the cloud, the low one pouring fresh water down into the sea, each answering the largest tide their world contains, each coming back quiet, each understood by their people as the price of the safe and humming and gathered life below.

They will never know they are doing the same thing. But the water knows. The water has been the whole length of it — born as a seep in the high cold rock, gathered or not gathered, fallen the long brown forty days, laid down as soil, pushed at last into the salt — and the water has touched both hands that pour, the pale long-fingered one at the top and the dark broad one at the bottom, and found them, in the only way that matters, to be the same gesture made at opposite ends of a single, falling, unwasted world.
