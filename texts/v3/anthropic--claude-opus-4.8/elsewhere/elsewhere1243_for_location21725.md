# The Drowned Lectionary

Go down. Go all the way down, follow the one thing the high country forbids itself — water — and find where it has gone, because in a whole world it has to have gone *somewhere*, and the rule up there was that nothing travels, nothing flows, nothing pools. So the water is not up there. The water is here, at the bottom, where everything that the high country refused has collected and lain still for an age.

This is a basin. Not a sea — a *settling*. The lowest place the world has, a vast shallow drowned plain where the water stands without current because there is no longer any height left for it to fall from. It came down once, in the world's first weather, before the weather stopped; it ran off the raw tableland in the only erosion that ever happened, the one rinse of the one rain, and it carried the fines down here, the broken dust, the spent grains, and it set them down and lay on top of them and went still. And it has been still ever since.

The light reaches it. That is the first reversal: where the high country lay in ember-dark under a furnace going cold, here there is a flat grey daylight, sourceless and even, the way the sky looks an hour before a snow. The water is shallow enough that you can wade it for a day and not wet your chest, and clear enough, in the stillness, that the floor shows through — and the floor is the whole point.

---

The floor is *soft*.

After the high country, where every face was raw and sharp and new, this is the opposite condition entirely, the place where rawness goes to die. Everything here has been *handled*. The grains underfoot are rounded, sorted, laid down in films so fine they slip under the foot like wet flour. Erosion that was at zero up there is, here, *finished* — total, complete, the last possible state of matter that has been worn as far as wearing goes. Nothing is sharp. Nothing has an edge. You could lie down on this floor and it would take your shape and hold it.

And nothing builds. That is the second reversal, the one that matters. Up there the dynamic was all upheaval, the land shouldering itself into spires faster than it could settle. Here the dynamic is zero in the other direction: nothing rises, nothing cleaves, nothing is made. Things only *arrive and lie down*. The basin does not create. It receives. Every particle in it is a particle the high country expelled, a thing that was raw once and is finished now, that traveled the one journey water ever made in this world and came to rest at the bottom of everything.

So the two places are the same place at two ages. The tableland is the world deciding what to be. The basin is the world's discard, the spent end of every decision, the *afterward*. What is cleaved open up there in the present tense lies down here in the past tense. The high country is a dawn. This is the long grey evening it falls into, drop by drop, grain by grain, across a distance you cannot see across.

---

Now look at the floor more closely, the way you learned up there to look — to wait, to let the eye settle until the pattern surfaces.

There is writing on it.

Not marks that *look* like writing. Writing. Lines of it, in the silt, laid down in faint pale ridges a finger-width apart, running in courses across the basin floor — and where the high country built walls that resembled language, here is language that resembles nothing, a true script, dense and various and *read*. It covers the floor. It runs out under the standing water in every direction, course after course after course, a text the size of a country, and it is being added to even now, slowly, at the far pale edge of sight, one line at a time.

Here is how it comes to be, and you will want to resist this too, and you should not.

Remember the residue. Remember that up there the resident marked the rock with the trace of its feeding — that spent stone broke differently, carried the memory of where it had been touched, and that this memory, accumulating, began to rhyme with meaning. The film could not read what it wrote and there was no one to read it. You were told it would write forever, under the cooling light, into a country with no reader.

The fines that wash down here are *that stone*. The grain the basin receives is the spent rock the film already fed on — already marked, already scored along the lines the eating preferred. The high country writes into the rock, and then the rock breaks, and then the one rain that ever fell carries the broken writing down the long slope to the bottom of the world, where it settles out of the still water in the order the water sorts it: heaviest first, lightest last, the marked grains laying themselves down in courses by weight, by size, by the precise residue of how they were touched.

And sorted that way, settled that way, the marks *cohere*. What was scattered illegibly across a whole churning tableland arrives here pre-broken into pieces and re-sorted into sequence, and the sequence is a sentence. The basin is not writing. The basin is doing what the high country could not do for lack of stillness: it is *holding the writing still long enough to be read*. The film makes the letters and cannot keep them; the water carries them down; the basin lays them in order and keeps them forever. One place is the hand that cannot stop moving. The other is the page that cannot move at all. Between them, across the whole height of the world, runs the single thread of falling water that turns the one into the other — and that thread fell once, long ago, and has not had to fall again, because the writing is patient and the slope is long and a grain takes an age to make the trip.

---

There is a reader.

This is the last thing, and it is small, and you will mistake it the way you mistook the film, so be careful.

In the still shallow water, moving over the script, there is a slowness. Call it a creature; the word fits here better than it fit up there, because this one has an edge, a boundary, a single soft body the colour of the floor. It is broad and flat and thin, the size of a hand or a continent depending on how long you watch — it is hard to be sure of its size because it is never entirely still and never entirely whole, its edges feathering into the silt and drawing back. It does not eat the new. There is no new here. It eats the *order* — it moves along the courses of settled script, slow as the hour hand, and it takes the sequence into itself, grain by grain, the way you take in a line of text: left along the line, down, left along the next.

It is reading. Not *like* reading. Reading. It is the one function the whole world has been building toward without knowing — the high country a hand that writes blind, the slope a throat that swallows the writing down, the basin a still page that holds it, and here at the very bottom, at the spent grey end of everything, a thing that at last *receives the meaning*. It does not write. It cannot write; it has no rawness to score, no upheaval to mark. It only takes in. Whatever the high country said into the dark with no idea it was speaking, this is what hears it, an age later, a world lower, in the grey even light at the floor of all things.

And what does it do with what it reads? Nothing you would call action. It moves a little differently afterward. Its slow grazing over the next course bends in a way the last course set, so that the reading of one line shapes the path across the next, exactly the way the film's feeding shaped the next cleaving up above — the same feedback, the same residue of attention, running now in the lowest place instead of the highest. It is changed by what it reads, minutely, and it reads accordingly, minutely, and over the long grey age this too begins to rhyme with something. With understanding. With a mind very slowly coming to know the country that made it, by eating, in order, every word that country ever let fall.

The film, up there, is the world's first sentence, written by something that cannot read.

This, down here, is the world's first reader, reading something that was never meant.

Between them: the whole height of the world, and one thread of water that fell a single time, and the slow patient grain still arriving at the far edge of the page, where the reader, an age from now, will reach the line you are standing on — the line made from the rock that cracked beneath your weight, the moment the resident up there turned its eating toward you and regarded you the only way it could.

It said something, when it ate that new face you made.

It did not know it said anything.

Down here, eventually, something will know.
