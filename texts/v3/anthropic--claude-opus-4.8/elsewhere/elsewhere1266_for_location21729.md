# The Glasswright's Yard

The light has to start somewhere, and it starts here.

Follow it backward — the grey-gold afternoon that lies on the green leaves and refuses to be uncanny — follow it back through the roof-gap or the overgrown pane, back up the long shaft of ordinary day, and you arrive at the place the light is *made* into the thing that arrives down there: filtered, angled, given its particular tired warmth. You arrive at the panes themselves. You arrive at the man who set them, or at what is left of his setting.

This is a yard, open to the weather, and after the green chapel's wet completion it will feel like stepping into a held breath. It is *dry.* That is the first fact and it organizes everything. Where the green place held water a hand's breadth under everything, here the ground is packed cinder and broken frit, and the rain that falls runs *off,* runs *away,* finds the long stone gutter at the yard's low edge and is gone before it can pool. Nothing here holds. Everything here sheds. This is a place built by people who needed water to leave, the way the green place is a body that learned to make water stay.

**The terrain.** Flat. Deliberately, expensively flat — a working floor, leveled once with care and since gone to a gentle ruin of frost-heave and weed-crack, but you can still read the intention in it. Cinder underfoot, and everywhere the *glass:* not whole glass, but the leavings of glass-work, the rejected and the shattered and the experimental, a generation of failures swept to the yard's margins in long glittering drifts. Green glass, mostly. That is not a coincidence and we will come to it. Bottle-green, flask-green, the deep aquarium green of thick cast panes, heaped in windrows along the walls so that when the light comes down it comes down already *stained,* already conditioned, already carrying the color it will later lay so gently on the leaves. The light leaves this yard green. It only learns to be ordinary on the long fall down.

**The architecture.** A long open-sided shed, its roof half-fallen, and under it the cold furnaces. Three of them, brick, big-shouldered, their mouths gaping and dark. These are the dead twins of the green place's swallowed boiler-works — the same industrial century, the same confidence in heat and pressure and volume, the same expectation that the world could be *made* if you only built the apparatus large enough. The green place ate its machine and kept its pulse. This place has not been eaten. This place was simply *left,* and the difference between digested and abandoned is the whole difference between the two rooms of this world: one was claimed by what came after, and one was claimed by nothing at all, and waits in its own cold integrity, full of its own dead tools.

The tools are here. That is what undoes you, if anything does. The blowpipes racked on the wall, iron, furred with the same moss-want but unbent. The jacks and the paddles. The annealing oven where finished glass was set to cool slowly — *slowly,* because glass cooled fast remembers its own violence and shatters later, in the night, for no reason, years on. The glasswright knew about slow. He knew the whole craft of it: that the thing you make must be let down gently from its making, given hours to forget it was ever liquid, or it will keep the memory and break.

**The work.** Here is what was made here, and here is the kinship that runs between the two places like the light itself.

Panes. Thick green cast panes, the kind that go in a roof meant to grow things under. The glasswright supplied the houses — the long forcing-houses and the boiler-warmed greenhouses where the century grew its impossible fruit out of season, its ferns and its orchids and its slow vegetable ambitions. He cast the glass that the green chapel is *roofed with.* Somewhere in that overgrown ceiling, behind the forty-years' moss, are panes his hands measured and his furnaces fired and his annealing-oven let down gently into permanence. The light that lies on the green leaves lies *through his work.* He gave it its color. He is the reason the strangeness down there is lit like an ordinary day — because he made an ordinary glass, a working glass, a glass for *growing,* with no uncanniness in it at all, only competence, only the patient knowledge of how to let a hot thing cool without breaking.

He is dead. Of course he is dead. The whole century is dead. But.

**What is still happening.** The green place kept a heartbeat. This place keeps a different rhythm, and it is the rhythm of *cooling* — the slowest gesture of all. The annealing oven still holds heat. Not its own; it has no fire. It holds the sun's. All day the grey-gold light comes down through the green drifts and into the open oven-mouth, and the thick brick drinks it, and through the long evening it lets it go — gives it down, slowly, gently, into the dark, the way it was built to give down the heat of finished glass. It has lost its glass. It has lost its fire. It has lost its maker. It has kept, like the green place's swallowed clock, the single thing it knew how to do: *let a hot thing cool without breaking.* It anneals the empty air. It tempers nothing, carefully, all night, every night, and the nothing does not shatter, and this is either the most useless faithfulness in the world or the most complete.

And there is one more thing it cools.

**What lives here.** A second beetle. Pale, slow, thumb-sized — the same kind, the *exact* kind, as the one the green permits below. This is not coincidence either. The species crossed once, long ago, when the houses were warm and the panes were new and there was traffic between the glasswright's yard and the growing-rooms his glass had roofed: men carrying panes down, carrying cuttings back, and in the soil of a cutting, or the fold of a sack, a beetle. The two populations are one population, split. The green one was sealed in when the green sealed itself. This one stayed out, in the dry, in the cold, working the underside of the drifted glass where a little warmth still gathers from the annealing brick.

They do not know each other. They will never meet again; the green is sealed, the houses fallen, the traffic two generations stopped. But they are kin across the distance the light travels, the green one in its wet completion and the dry one in its cold integrity, and each is the last moving thing its place permits, and neither place knows it has a twin. The light that the glasswright's panes turned green and let down gently into the green chapel — that light still arrives. The beetle that the glasswright's traffic carried down — that lineage still arrives, in two bodies, in two rooms, doing the one slow thing each room still remembers how to do.

Stand in the yard long enough and nothing grows over your boots. That is the mercy here, and the loneliness. The green would have included you. The yard only sheds you, the way it sheds the rain — lets you run off toward the low gutter and be gone, holding nothing, wanting nothing, keeping only the heat of an afternoon and letting even that down gently, gently, into a dark that does not break.
