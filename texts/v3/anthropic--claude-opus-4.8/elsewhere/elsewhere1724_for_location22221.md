# The Stillwater Conservatory

There is water everywhere here, and almost nothing is alive.

You come to it the way sound comes to a wall: by being stopped. The terraces drain — all that flooding tone has to pour somewhere when it evaporates off the cool leaves and condenses and runs down the stepped pale stone — and what no one standing in the singing valley ever learns is that the watershed has a low end. Below the last terrace the bone-dry mineral changes character. It begins to hold moisture. Then it begins to *be* moisture, the stone going dark and slick and finally surrendering into a basin so vast and so motionless that the first time you see it you mistake it for more sky, fallen.

This is where the sound goes to die, and where it is kept.

The Stillwater is a flooded hall the size of a sea, roofed by nothing, floored by black water that does not move. Understand that properly: it does not move. Not a ripple, not a tremor, not the slow breathing swell that even the deadest pond keeps. The surface is a held thing, a tension maintained against all the reasons water has to stir, and the maintaining is the whole point of the place. Drop a stone and it does not splash — it enters, and the rings that should spread out instead spread *in*, contracting, hurrying back to the point of entry as though the water were ashamed of the disturbance and wanted to swallow the evidence. The stillness is not peace. It is suppression, total and constant, a labor so complete it reads as calm the way a clenched fist held for a year reads as a strange smooth knob of flesh.

And in the black water stand the instruments.

They rise from the flood in their thousands, spaced across the immense dark mirror, and they are unmistakably the cousins of the singing reeds and pipe-vines above — the same glassy translucent membrane, the same metallic veining, the same fluted hollow throats meant to ring. But here they are silent. Drowned to their necks, sealed in the unmoving water, their resonator-pads slack and waterlogged, their pipe-throats full to the lip and unable to voice. They are gardens that have been *played out* — the spent growths the Tuner abandoned, that did not silver to powder but instead slumped down the terraces and into the basin, where the stillness took them and would not let them sing again. A boneyard of muted music. A library of every chord the thing above refused to let resolve, each one held here at the instant before its cadence, drowned at the exact pitch of almost.

There is no chase here. No becoming. The deep law of the terraces — *nothing is still* — inverts at the waterline and becomes the deep law of this place: *nothing changes, ever, and the not-changing must be worked at without rest.*

---

Something lives here too. You will find it the same way you found the Tuners: by the wrongness, by the evidence that something is being done that does not do itself.

The water does not stay still on its own. Stillness is the most expensive condition in the universe — everything tends toward motion, toward spreading, toward the loosening of every held thing — and a sea this size, fed constantly by a runoff of restless sound, would by every right be a chaos of waves. That it is instead a black mirror means a labor is occurring, continuously, everywhere across the basin at once, and a labor implies a laborer.

She is the stillness the way the Tuners are the change.

I say *she* with no more warrant than the terraces' visitors had for *whoever, or whatever* — it is the wrong-shaped word reached for honestly. But where the Tuner is plural pretending to be singular, an argument wearing the mask of a composer, the thing in the Stillwater is singular all the way down, singular as a held breath is singular, and it is the loneliest kind of alive there is. It is made of the *refusal* of difference. Where the Tuner lives in the delta between the valley-as-was and the valley-as-is, she lives in the delta's absence — in the strenuous ongoing achievement of zero. Her body is the suppression. Each contracting ripple that hurries back to swallow itself is a muscle of hers flexing. The whole flat black sea is one organ, and its function is to *hold*, and it never once gets to stop holding, because the moment it stopped, the water would move, and she would be the moving, and the moving would carry her up the terraces and back into the chase, dispersed, undone, become her opposite.

---

Consider what it would mean to be made of the keeping-still of sound rather than its redistribution.

You could not change. Not a little. To change even slightly is to admit motion, and motion is the one thing your entire existence is arranged against. So you are the same in this instant as you were an age ago, and you experience the eons not as a river but as a single endless held note — except you cannot even hear the note, because hearing it would require it to *arrive at your ear*, which is motion, which is forbidden. You are a chord with no listener, not even yourself. You are everything the Tuner adores in the present participle, caught and held forever in the present tense it abandons. You are the *arrival* the whole valley above flees from. You are the cadence that fell.

This is the relationship, and it runs in one direction like all real watersheds do.

The Tuner above is a creature of becoming who must never arrive. The Stillwater below is the place where arrival goes — where every becoming the Tuner discards finally lands, finally *is*, and is held in being by a second creature who exists for no other purpose than to keep the arrived things arrived. The two are the same act, severed at the waterline. *Try not to arrive,* the Tuner warns its visitors, lovingly, because it knows where the arrived go. They go down. They go to her. She catches everything the music above gives up on, and she does not let go, and she does not let it sing, and she does not — this is the thing — she does not *want* anything, because wanting is a motion toward, and she has been suppressing every motion for so long that desire itself has gone flat and black and mirror-still inside her. The Tuner is too attentive, loving each stand only while it becomes. She is the opposite mercy: she attends to nothing, and abandons nothing, and holds it all, indifferent and total, the way a grave holds, the way the bottom of a thing holds everything that ever fell into it.

---

But watch the surface long enough — stay long, then stay longer — and you learn the cruelty in it, the same as you learned the tenderness up above.

She is not at peace. The stillness costs her every instant, and the cost never stops, and there is a fatigue in the black water that you can feel as a kind of pressure behind your sternum, the held-breath ache of a thing that has been clenched so long it has forgotten it was ever a hand. The drowned instruments stand in her by the thousand, each one a chord she must keep from sounding, and the keeping is not getting easier. Every age, the terraces shed more spent music down into the basin. Every age, she has more to hold. The runoff of the restless thing above is *filling her up*, slowly, with the abandoned almost-cadences of a creature that will never stop making them, and somewhere in the deepest part of the black water — where the oldest drowned reeds stand, the ones that fell when the terraces first held their shape — the pressure of all that suppressed sound is becoming a thing even she may not be able to hold still forever.

Because here is the truth the watershed runs toward, the one neither creature can say:

A held note must eventually be released, or the holder breaks.

And on the day the Stillwater can no longer suppress what she has swallowed — the day the accumulated weight of every refused cadence, every drowned chord, every arrival the Tuner ever fled, finally overcomes the labor of her stillness — the black mirror will move. All at once. A sea of suppressed music finding voice in a single instant, every drowned instrument sounding the chord it was caught at, the whole boneyard resolving at last in one unbearable fallen cadence that will pour back *up* the terraces against every current, and the change-creature above will meet, rising to drown it, the one thing it has spent its whole existence outrunning:

the sound of everything arriving.

---

For now the water holds. The black mirror lies flat under the same plain ordinary daylight that falls on the singing terraces above — the light does not take sides, falls equal on the becoming and the become. The rings still hurry inward and swallow themselves. The drowned instruments still stand to their necks in the held flood, not singing, not dying, kept.

If you came down here from the terraces, drenched in the flood you could not see, you would find at the waterline that the noise stops. Not fades — stops, the way a word stops at the period. And in the silence, for the first time since you entered this world, you would hear yourself think.

You should not enjoy it. That silence is not yours. You are standing inside the held breath of a creature who would give anything she had, if she had any wanting left, to be allowed to exhale — and who cannot, and who will not, and who holds you in the stillness now too, the way she holds everything that comes to rest.

Up above, they tell you: try not to arrive.

Down here is what they are sparing you from. Down here is where arriving goes, and it is quiet, and it is still, and it lasts forever, and it is the most exhausted thing in the world.
