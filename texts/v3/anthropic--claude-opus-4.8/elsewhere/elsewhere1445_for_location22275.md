# The Seedfall Plain

You leave Hesp the only way the green ever leaves it: downward, and outward, and not on purpose.

Here is the thing the ledgers do not record and the climbers do not tend and the birds, wheeling, never carry word of, because it happens below the lowest trusted tier, below the foundries, below even the rumor of bedrock that no one living has business with. The green sheds. A thing two miles tall and growing toward stability for longer than memory cannot knit every rib without slack — and the slack falls. Seed and spore and the dry husked runners that root could not find purchase for. Leaf-rot and the light brown dust of pollen the climbers did not carry. The overflow of a body too large to account for all of itself. It sifts down through the dark heart of the spire, past the small slow mills, riding the last of the Vein where the Vein at last gives up its managed cascade and simply spills — and it falls out the bottom of the world, into the open, into the wind.

And the wind takes it east.

## What the Plain Is

There is ground here. I want *that* understood, the way the absence of ground wanted understanding at the start, because it changes everything that follows in the opposite direction.

The Seedfall Plain is honest dirt, flat to the edge of seeing, lying in the long shadow the spire throws each morning — a blue-black blade of shade that sweeps across the grass from west to east as the sun climbs, so that everyone who lives here wakes inside the shape of Hesp and is released from it by noon. The plain knows the spire the way a field knows the tree at its edge: as the source of the shade, and the source of the seed, and the thing that is always there when you look up, distant and vertical and faintly, perpetually green even at this range, a green smudge against the cloud-deck two miles into the sky.

The land is fed by what falls. This is the cardinal fact, the way *everything is made* was the cardinal fact of Hesp. Nothing here is engineered. Everything here is *received.* The seed that drifts down off the spire and rides the morning wind east lands on the plain in a thin perpetual rain of green possibility — and almost none of it takes, because the green of Hesp is a thing grown toward the inside of a structure, toward iron and cistern-dark and the warm wet glasshouse heat, and the plain is open and dry and cold at night and offers nothing to climb. The seed of the holding-thing cannot hold here. There is nothing to hold up. It falls on flat ground under open sky and it dies, mostly, in uncountable numbers, a failed colonization repeated every single morning for longer than the ledgers run.

But not all of it dies.

## The Loosed Green

What grows on the Seedfall Plain is the green of Hesp *with the holding taken out of it.*

Think of what that means. Up in the spire, every plant was a limb of the one slow deciding thing, knit rib to rib, continuous in the dark, growing always toward stability and never toward anything so vulgar as *more.* It did not spread because it had no wish to spread; it had found its shape and intended to keep it. The vines did not race. The trees did not seed wildly into the wind — they *leaked* seed, accidentally, the overflow of a body that had stopped wanting to grow outward a hundred generations ago.

That leaked seed, fallen here, is cut off from the body. It cannot find the others. There is no root to meet in the dark, no continuous self to rejoin, no vast slow intention to subordinate itself to. And so it does the one thing the green of Hesp has not done in living memory.

It *hurries.*

The plants of the Seedfall Plain are the fastest-growing things you will ever see, and they are fast for the saddest and simplest of reasons: they are alone, and they are short-lived, and they have inherited a body designed for patience with none of the patience left to spend. A runner that would have taken a generation to claim a column up in Hesp claims a hundred yards of open dirt in a season and then dies back, exhausted, having flung its seed at the wind in every direction in a frenzy of *reach someone, reach anything, find the body, rejoin.* They are not knitting. There is nothing to knit. They are searching, blindly, at speed, and the speed is the search, and the search always fails, because the body they are reaching for is two miles up and falling away from them, never down toward them, only ever down past them and gone.

The whole plain is this. A fast green sea of orphaned tissue, brilliant and frantic and brief, greening and browning and greening again in waves that move across the flat land faster than seasons, each wave a generation of seed trying and failing to reconstitute the slow vast thing it fell out of. Where Hesp weathers into balance across a century, the plain churns through a dozen frantic green lifetimes in a year, and the smell of it is not wet leaf and machine oil but cut grass and dry crushed stalk and the high sweet rot of things that lived too quickly to live long.

## The Gleaners

And there are people here too, because there are always people where there is something to receive.

They are nothing like the engineers. I want the difference held the way the green's two temperaments are held — slow and fast, holding and searching — because the people answer to the land the way the land answers to the spire.

The gleaners are a quick, light, scattered folk, and they move. This is the first thing about them and it explains the rest. Where the engineers married late and apprenticed early and stayed, the gleaners are born walking, because the green here will not stay put and so neither can the people who live on it. They follow the green waves across the plain — a band of them trailing a generation of fast-growing runner the way the climbers trail a slow-claiming vine, except the gleaners trail it at a walk, harvesting as they go, because the loosed green of the Seedfall Plain grows so fast and dies so fast that you must take it *now,* in the brief brilliant window when it is more than seed and not yet rot.

They take everything. The fast stalks make a fiber the engineers of Hesp have never spun and never will, because it must be cut green and worked the same day or it is useless — too quick for any ledger, too brief for any craft that assumes the grandchildren will live with the result. The gleaners' grandchildren will be twenty miles east and the green they live on will be a dozen generations dead. They make rope and cloth and a kind of pressed bread from the seed-glut, and baskets that last a single season and are abandoned, and they abandon almost everything, because to a gleaner a *kept* thing is a *weight,* and weight is what you cannot afford when the green is moving and you must move with it.

They have no ledgers. Of course they have no ledgers. A ledger is a memory that assumes the world holds still long enough to be written down and obeyed by a hand four generations later — and the gleaners' world does not hold still for a season. Their memory is in the walking. They know where the green will rise next not because anyone wrote it down but because they read the seedfall, the morning drift of green possibility off the distant spire, the way an old keeper in Hesp reads the birds. *The west drift's heavy,* a gleaner will say, watching the dawn shadow of Hesp sweep over the grass, *there'll be a green come up past the river inside the month,* and the band will turn west, toward the spire, toward the shadow, walking into the shape of the thing that fed them.

## The Thing Neither Knows

Here is what holds the two places together, and you must carry it the way you carried the full inhabitant of Hesp — all at once, or not at all.

The green of Hesp holds the iron up. It has knit itself into the spire until the two cannot be separated, and it stands because it has decided, slowly, to keep standing. It is the least anxious thing in the world. It does not search, because it has found its shape. It does not hurry, because nothing threatens what it cannot outlast.

And every morning it sheds the part of itself that cannot bear to hold still — the seed, the overflow, the slack of a body too large to account for all of itself — and the wind takes that part east, and that part falls on open ground and *cannot stop searching,* hurries itself to death and back trying to rejoin a body that does not know it is missing anything, because a thing that has found its shape does not feel the falling-away of what it lets go.

The gleaners live on the searching. The engineers live on the holding. Neither has ever met the other; the spire is too far and too tall, and the gleaners do not climb, and the engineers do not descend below the lowest trusted tier where the slack falls out of the world. But they are the two halves of one green temperament, divided by nothing more than whether it landed somewhere it could hold — the patient and the frantic, the kept and the abandoned, the thing that stands forever and the thing that runs and dies and flings its children at the wind.

And the wind that carries the seed east is the same warm updraft the birds of Hesp ride wheeling in the air-gaps, the exhaust-heat of the foundries rising through two miles of green and spilling at last off the summit and out over the cloud-deck and down, down the far side, down to where the grass begins. The birds do not follow it down. The birds belong to the holding. But the seed does, and the seed cannot help itself, and somewhere on the Seedfall Plain right now a gleaner is waking inside the blue-black morning shadow of the only home her green ever had, looking up at the distant smudge of it against the sky, not knowing she is looking at the body, not knowing she is the part that got away — and she shoulders her single-season basket, and she reads the drift, and she walks.
