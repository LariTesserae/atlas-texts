# The Catchment

Go up. Past the coin of daylight, past the forty steps, past the lip of soft stone where the warm wet breath of the works gives way to wind — and keep going, because the works did not invent its water. Water is borrowed. Every drop the great wheel lifts and every thread that beads on the cool iron came down from somewhere, and somewhere is up, and up is a long climb into the open.

This is the high country that feeds the sump. A bowl of land tilted toward a single drain, miles across, all of it sloping by degrees toward the soft pale stone that swallows it. You would not know, standing in it, that it is a roof over a drowning hollow. It looks like nothing but moor.

## The Ground

The rock here is old. That is the true word and the right one. It is the opposite number of the stone below — where the sump's lime is young paste that holds a thumbprint, this is granite that has forgotten how it was made, scoured down past memory, hard as a closed fist. Nothing grows *into* shape here. Everything wears *out* of it. The wind has been at this ground so long that the ground has given up having edges; the tors stand rounded as old teeth, the boulders sit in beds of their own grit, and the streams have cut their channels not by youth but by sheer accumulated patience, the patience of weather that never once banked low.

It is cold. Always cold, the way the works is always hot — the same law run backward. The wind moves without rest across the open, and the rain comes sideways, and the cold gets into the granite and splits it a grain at a time over centuries. Where the sump breathes its wet breath in a closed circle, the catchment breathes it *out*: it takes the rain the sky brings and gives it away downhill, keeps nothing, holds nothing, is only the long tilted surface across which water decides to leave.

## The Living

And here is the inversion that matters, the thing you'll feel in your chest before you understand it:

It is sparse the way the sump was crowded, and crowded the way the sump was sparse.

Below, the life swarmed — walls furred with crickets, rails clotted with moths, the air a wall of shrilling pressure. Here the life is almost nothing and almost everywhere at once, thin and total. Heather to the horizon, knee-high, blackish-green, flowering once a year into a haze of purple that no one is there to see. Bog cotton nodding its white heads over the wet ground. Sphagnum in the hollows, holding ten times its weight in rain, soft as a drowned thing. Sundews no bigger than a coin, their red hairs beaded with glue that is not dew, waiting in their slow vegetable way for a fly. A hawk somewhere, riding the wind without a wingbeat for an hour. Sheep, occasionally, ranging in ones and twos, cropping the heather to felt.

No swarm. No singing wall. Just the wind in the grass, which is a kind of singing if you stay long enough to hear it — a continuous dry hush, rising and falling in patches as one stand of heather quiets and another takes it up. You have heard that rhythm before. You heard it underground, in the crickets. The catchment makes the same sound with grass and wind that the sump makes with insects and wing, and a person who has been in both will stand on the moor with the hair lifting on their arms, unable to say why the empty wind sounds *attended*.

## The Built Thing

There is building here too, though you might miss it, because it does not announce itself the way the brick channels and the green iron sluice-gates do below.

Stone walls run across the moor in long meaningless lines, dividing nothing from nothing, raised by hands that wanted the land to hold a shape and have been gone so long the walls have begun to slump back into beds of grit. A ruined longhouse, roofless, its hearth open to the rain — and here is the cold echo of the warm marriage below: someone built a home up here before the moor finished deciding it would have no homes, and the moor won, slowly, by wearing rather than swallowing. Below, the cave grows over the works. Here the weather grinds the works away. Same patience, opposite hand. The column closing over the doorway and the wall slumping into the grit are the same gesture made at two temperatures.

And there are the leats. *That* is the word for them — the shallow stone-cut channels that the old builders ran along the contour lines, catching the moor's water and leading it, by a fall so gentle it looks level, across miles of slope toward the drain. Some still run. You can follow one with your eye and lose it and find it again, a bright thread in the heather, and if you walked it long enough — kettle's-boil of time would not do it; this is half a day's walking — it would bring you to the place where the soft stone begins and the water goes down, down past the lip, down the throat that becomes the forty steps, down to the wheel.

The leats were cut to feed the works. Or the works was dug to drink what the leats brought. It can no longer be said which came first, the catching or the thing it fed, and you will recognize that uncertainty, because you met it once before, in the dark, about the tending and the tended.

## The Keeping

You learned, below, that the sump is kept — that an economy running that clean for that long is a body, and a body has a thing that is being the body, distributed and slow.

The catchment is kept too. But you must understand the difference, because it is the whole difference between the two places, and it is the difference between a held breath and a let one.

The keeper of the sump folds things *in*. It runs a closed circle; nothing leaves; whatever lingers is given a path to graze and a relation to hold, and the holding is permanent, made of stone-time, closing over you as gently as a column over a door. It tends by *including*. It wants belonging.

What keeps the catchment does the opposite, and so it is not really a keeper at all, and that is the kindest thing about it. It tends by *releasing*. The whole tilted bowl of the moor has one slow want, if a slope can want: to give its water away. To hold nothing. To take what the sky lets fall and pass it down and keep none of it, not the rain, not the warmth, not the dead sheep that the bog will not even swallow but will only slowly dry and scatter. Where the keeper below leans the hollow toward drowning-prevention and inclusion, the catchment leans, always, everywhere, toward *letting go downhill*. It is the same patient distributed attention — the wind turning from one stand of heather to the next is the moor listening, the way the cricket-song turning across the walls was the keeper listening — but its attention has no appetite for company. It attends to you the way the wind attends to you: it touches all of you, learns your whole shape, and then moves on, and forgets, and is already touching the next thing.

You could stay here as long as you liked. That is the mercy of the high cold place, and it is the exact opposite of the mercy below. Below, the mercy was the *emptiness* between crowds, the pools of room that let you breathe before the slow tending could fold you in. Here the mercy is that there is nothing to fold you into. You will not slow to the moor's tempo and become a function in its holding, because the moor holds nothing. You will only get cold, and tired, and at last turn and go, and the wind will not feel you leave, the way it did not feel you come — not because you were too fast, but because it lets everything go, all the time, as its whole nature, and a person leaving is just one more drop going downhill.

## What Runs Between Them

So this is the shape of it, the two places in one breath:

The rain falls on the old hard granite that keeps nothing. It gathers in the sphagnum and runs in the bright leats along the contours and pours over the lip of the soft young stone that keeps everything. Up here it is cold and open and the life is thin to the horizon and the tending lets you go. Down there it is hot and closed and the life swarms in the dark and the tending will not. The water is the same water. It is only ever passing from the hand that releases to the hand that gathers, the let breath becoming the held one.

And the bats — remember the bats, how they were the sump-keeper's tongue, how they brought the surface down on their fur, the pollen and the night-cold and *the smell of rain forty steps up*, a faint memory of an outside it has never seen and cannot go to?

This is the outside. This cold bright wind-scoured bowl, this purple haze no one sees, this moor that gives and gives and never closes over anything. The thing below, in its warm permanent dark, knows this place only as a taste on the fur of its messengers — knows it the way you know a country you will die without visiting. It cannot come up the forty steps. It lives at the speed the stone grows, and the moor lives at the speed the granite wears, and those are the same patient speed run at opposite temperatures toward opposite ends, and the two will never meet except in the water that falls between them and the small winged things that carry the rumor down.

That is why you can stand up here, in the let-go cold, and feel the warm held dark pulling faintly at the soles of your feet, down the long slope, down the leat, down the throat of the soft stone — and know that the moor is letting you go in the very same instant the sump is reaching up, through the water, to gather you in.

Drink from the leat if you're thirsty. It's only passing through. Same as you.
