The Throat of the Hill

Follow the seep backward. Not down, the way the water wants to take you, terrace to terrace to the still pools — but up, against the trickle, toward the place the warmth is coming from. The hollow is where the water lies down to rest. But water that lies down was, before that, water that ran, and before it ran it was driven, and the driving has a source, and the source is not at rest at all.

The ground tells you on the way. The soft pale lime thins as you climb the few hand-spans of mounded earth and keep climbing past where the mounding should have stopped. The stone hardens, darkens, takes an edge. The seep narrows from a weeping of every seam into a few definite threads, and the threads run hotter against your hand, hot enough now to flinch from, and you understand that the blood-warmth of the pools below was already a cooling, already a thing half-given-up by the time it reached the basin. Here it has not been given up. Here it is still arriving.

The hill is not large either. Like the hollow it is a single feature, a thing you could walk around before you grew restless. But where the hollow is a basin the hill is a vent — a blunt cone of dark rock cracked open at the crown, and from the crack comes the breath that the whole low country has been borrowing. You hear it before you reach it: not the round spaced note of a drop into a pool, but a low continuous working, a churn, a deep wet labor that never pauses to take stock of itself. Steam stands over this too, but it does not stand in still pearl ropes — it tears off the crack in gouts, fast, and shreds, and is gone, replaced, gone, replaced. The air does not hold its breath here. It cannot. The breath is moving through too hard.

Almost nothing grows, and for the opposite reason. In the hollow nothing grows because nothing is asked of anything; here nothing grows because too much is asked, too fast, too hot. The rock near the crack is bare and glazed, scaled with bright raw mineral that has had no time to soften — fresh sulphur in lemon crusts, iron bled out red and not yet gone purple, salts thrown up still sharp-edged and stinging. These are the same colors that stain the hollow's walls. You know them now for what they are: not the hollow's own, but cast up here and carried down, the deep earth's pigment flung out at the vent and only later laid to rest, drop by drop, on the cool patient terraces below. The hollow's quiet stains are this hill's loud effluent, gone calm with distance the way a shout goes calm by the time it is an echo.

It is hot here, and unevenly. This is the great difference, the one you feel in your body before you reason it. The hollow had no hot pockets and no cold; it had arrived at one even warmth and held it. The hill has nothing settled. A step to the left and the ground scorches; a step back and it merely bakes; lean toward the crack and the heat slaps you. The warmth here has not yet been spread out and made fair. It is raw and local and arrives in surges, pulsing up from below in a rhythm you can almost count and never quite — a working throat, swallowing and giving, swallowing and giving, with the deep machinery of the planet behind it.

The light is not low. Down in the basin you could not find the hour; up here the hour finds you. The hill stands clear of the cloud-lid, in open sky, and the sun comes down hard and direct and pins sharp black shadows behind every scaled ridge. There is glare. There is the white dazzle of steam catching full light and the hard dark of the lee side. You always know where you stand. You always know it is day, and which way the day is going, because the shadows lean and the heat changes face as the sun moves. Nothing here has stopped being subject to the hour. Everything is still inside time, still being turned by it.

And it is loud — not with noise from outside, for no wind crosses this bare crown either, but with itself. The churn. The hiss of steam through narrow rock. The occasional deeper cough from far down the throat, a shifting, a release, a wet detonation muffled by depth that you feel in the soles of your feet a moment after you hear it. Where the hollow had the deep quiet of a thing that has finished happening, the hill has the unceasing din of a thing that is happening now and now and now and shows no sign of meaning to stop.

Life passes through here too, but differently. No blind soft drifters — nothing soft could last a tide of this water. No eyeless thing resting on a ledge — there is no rest to be had, and eyes are sharply useful where the ground itself shifts and stings and the next surge must be read and dodged. What lives at the throat lives fast and lives armored. There are hard-shelled things in the runoff channels where the water has cooled just enough to be survived, scuttling, snatching, fighting the current and each other, eating the bright films before the films can even properly establish. They have eyes. They have hurry. They want, visibly, constantly, and they move toward what they want with the whole urgent insistence of creatures who know the place will not feed them if they merely wait. They are everything the hollow's residents have surrendered: appetite, motion, the answering of consequence. They are the same broad kind of life, run the other way — toward reaching instead of away from it.

There are signs of people here as well, and this is where the kinship turns sharp and clear. Not flat stones laid for kneeling. Not a niche to hold one quiet thing. Here there are channels — cut, deliberate, the soft outer rock chiseled into runnels that take the scalding water and lead it away, downslope, in the very direction the hollow lies. There are the burnt rings of fires that were used, not contemplated. There are more potsherds, and these are different: thicker, scorched, some half-vitrified where the clay sat too near the heat — vessels made to be filled at the source and carried, working vessels, not offerings. And there is a worn path, the most telling mark of all, beaten into the dark rock by feet going down and coming back up, down and back, down and back. The same hands that knelt in the niche below cut these channels above. The same people. They did not only come to the hollow to be quieted. They came first to the hill to be *given* — to take the warm water at its source, to carry it, to use it, to fill their thick scorched pots at the throat of the world and bear it down the beaten path to wherever they lived between the two places.

This is the connection, and it runs in the water, and it runs in their feet, and it runs only one way. The hill gives; the hollow receives. The hill throws up heat and mineral and motion in raw surges, under open sky, in noise, in time; and the long descent through the soft low ground takes all of that and spends it, cools it, slows it, spreads the heat fair and lays the bright pigment down to age into quiet stain, until what arrives in the basin is the same gift gone gentle, the same breath let out. The people knew both ends of it. They climbed to the loud bright giving place and filled their vessels at the source, and they descended to the dim warm finished place and set the vessels down and knelt. They needed the throat for what it gave and the hollow for what it had stopped needing. Between the wanting and the rest they made their lives, on the beaten path, carrying warm water downhill — which is only what the water does anyway, through every seam, without them, and will keep doing when the path has healed over and the channels have filled with their own slow stone.

For the hill heals nothing and keeps nothing. Where the hollow absorbed the foot-shape and quieted it over, the throat simply throws up new crust across the old, buries the burnt rings under fresh raw mineral, chokes its own cut channels with the very salts it spits, and labors on. It is not at rest and it is not waiting and it is not, either, becoming anything — it is only the source, doing the one loud thing it does, the swallowing and the giving, the surge and the surge, day after counted day under the moving sun, sending its heat down into the soft country to be made calm by everything that lies between, so that far below, in a small full basin where nothing changes, a single drop can fall from a high overhang into a warm pool with a clean round note, spaced so far apart that you wait for the next one without knowing you are waiting — and never once suspect that the patience of that pool was, a long way uphill and not so long ago, a thing that screamed out of the earth.
