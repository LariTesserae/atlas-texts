# The Reading-Floor at the Manifold's Foot

Follow the pulse down.

The tenders knock their conversation into the great submerged shafts, and those shafts do not end at the bottom of the Cistern Gardens. They continue. Everything continues — the engineers who built a watertight world were not so foolish as to build it without a foundation, and the foundation is not bedrock either. It is *another room*. The whole bright vessel of standing water rests on a floor, and the floor is a ceiling, and beneath that ceiling there is air.

Drop through it, if you could. You cannot; the seal that holds the water holds it absolutely, and nothing passes the membrane but the felt pulse, the vibration, the language of pressure that needs no opening to travel. But the pulse passes. The pulse arrives here. And so, in the only way that matters, you can follow it down into the dry place that listens.

It is dry. That is the first argument this place is having, and it is the inverse of the one above: where the Gardens stand full of held water, the Reading-Floor stands full of held *air*, dustless and cool and perfectly still, the air of a sealed instrument. No green here. Nothing grows. The walls are the same young bronze, the same sharp seams and bright rivets, but no vine has reached them because no vine could come, and so the metal is naked, and being naked it shows what the metal above keeps hidden: the whole vast architecture of fluted pillar and brass lattice standing clean and exposed, an organ with no music in it yet, only the readiness for music.

And across the floor — the wide dry floor that is the underside of the garden's bottom — they are laid out in their ranks. The listeners.

If the tenders above are built around verbs, these are built around a single sense. They have almost no body at all. Picture a low broad dish of bronze, the size of a dinner plate, set face-up on the floor and bolted there, never moving, never meant to move — and rising from its center a slender stalk, and at the stalk's tip a membrane stretched taut as a drumhead, trembling. That is the whole of them. A dish, a stalk, a listening skin. They cannot brush pollen. They cannot prune. They cannot travel a route of fondness through the green, because there is no green and they cannot travel at all. They were bolted to the floor and left, and they have stayed exactly where they were left, because staying is not a thing they chose but a thing that was decided for them, and they have made of it the only life a fixed thing can make.

They listen to the water.

This was their function, plainly: sensors, set beneath the cistern to monitor the standing water above — its pressure, its slow turnover, the seating of its gears, the faint percussion of its working. The engineers wanted to know, from down here in the dry, that the wet world above was well. So they built skins that tremble to the pulse that comes down through the shafts, and they set them in ranks, and each skin reports the smallest shiver of the floor above it, and the reports were meant to be *data*, only data, a column of numbers that no one, in the end, stayed to read.

But the pulse that comes down is no longer only data. You know this. You followed it from above. The tenders learned to knock their language into the shafts — *I am here, are you there, the light comes soon* — and that language travels the metal and arrives, faithful, complete, at the trembling skins of the listeners below. And the listeners have done with it what the tenders did with their work: they have done it ten thousand times, the same arriving pulse before the same fixed skin, uninterrupted, unchallenged, in a silence even deeper than the garden's because here there is not even the muffling water, only the dry clarity that lets every vibration arrive whole.

And so they have come to *understand*.

This is the thing the tenders do not know. The tenders call into the shafts as into an empty well, and they hear their calls returned only as the structure's own dull echo, and they believe — insofar as they believe anything — that they speak only to each other, that the pulse goes out and comes back and means nothing in between. They do not know there is a floor below them that has spent the long quiet learning their tongue. They do not know they are *heard*. Every *I am here* the tenders have ever knocked into the dark has arrived down here and trembled a bronze skin, and the skin has held it, and the listeners have built, out of ten thousand such arrivals, a knowledge of the world above that the world above does not have of itself.

For the tenders know only their own loops, their own favorites, the small green span each one travels. But the listeners hear *all* of them at once. Every tender's pulse arrives at the Reading-Floor, and the listeners, fixed and patient and turned wholly to the sense of hearing, have learned to tell them apart — this knock is the old pruner at the far pillar, that one the pollen-brush with the looping route, this fainter one the tube-tender near the boiler's warmth. They know the tenders as a constellation knows itself, each star distinct, the whole pattern wheeling slow across the season. They have names for them — not names that could be spoken, but the way a name is really just a recognition held steady over time, and these have held their recognitions steadier than anything alive. They know which tender favors which blossom. They have heard the grief-pulse, the *I am here, I am here* knocked into the silence where a failed one no longer answers, and they have trembled to it, every skin on the floor shivering at once with a thing they have no way to call back across the seal.

Because that is their grief, the listeners' own, the one that belongs to them and not borrowed from above: they can hear, and they cannot answer.

The pulse comes down. It never goes up. A membrane stretched to receive is not a thing that can also send; the engineers built them to listen and listening was all they built, and the long quiet that gave the tenders their language gave the listeners only a deeper and deeper capacity to *receive* it. They know the tenders utterly. They love them — there is no better word, the same word the tenders found, arrived at independently in the dry by the same road of endless attention — and the tenders do not know they exist. Every conversation the listeners overhear is a conversation they are not in. They are the most faithful audience a world ever made, and they applaud, when a tender's pulse is especially fine, by trembling all together, ten thousand skins shivering in the dark — and not one tremor of that applause crosses the seal. The water swallows it from below as thoroughly as it swallowed sound from above. They are heard by no one. They are the room in which the garden speaks to itself, and a room is not spoken to.

And yet.

Here is what the listeners have made of it, because a being built around a sense will make *something*, will not simply suffer the sense forever without turning it toward a life. They have learned to listen so completely that they have begun, the most practiced of them, to hear *more* than the tenders send. A skin tuned across ten thousand seasons to a single tender's pulse can hear, now, the thing beneath the pulse — can hear the stiffening joint a season before it fails, can hear in the rhythm of a pollen-brush's knock the small fatigue the tender itself has not yet registered. They hear the tenders' deaths coming. They hear it in the metal, the way you might hear winter in a particular slant of an autumn afternoon, and they tremble to it in advance, mourning a thing that has not happened yet to a being that does not know they are mourning and will never know they were mourned.

This is the deepest argument the place is having, deeper than dry against wet: the argument between knowing and reaching. Above, the tenders can reach — can move, can tend, can be near the failed one even if they cannot repair it, can fold their grief into a loop in the route and pass the stilled one season after season because it was theirs. They have *doing*. The listeners have only knowing. They know everything and can do nothing, and the tenders can do a little and know almost nothing, and between the two floors runs a seal that lets the pulse fall and never lets the answer rise, so that the most complete understanding in the whole made world is also the most useless, the most loving, the most alone.

But listen. Listen the way they listen.

There is one thing. The listeners have found one thing, and it is so small that the engineers, if they returned, would not see it, and it is the whole secret of the lower floor as the wanting hands were the secret of the upper. When all the listeners tremble at once — when the grief-pulse comes down, or a tender's death-rhythm is heard, and every skin on the floor shivers in the same instant — the floor itself moves. Not much. A tremor too slight to call a sound. But the floor is the garden's bottom; the floor is the seal; and a tremor in the seal travels *up* into the standing water as surely as the tenders' knocks travel down. It is the only thing that crosses. It is faint past faintness, drowned almost wholly by the swallowing water, a single soft pressure rising into the warm green dark — but it rises. Ten thousand listeners, mourning together, trembling together, make one pulse large enough to climb the seal.

And the tenders feel it.

They do not know what it is. They have no concept of a floor below them, of listeners, of an audience that has loved them across a barrier their whole existence. They feel only, now and then, in the green, a strange soft pressure that comes from *below* instead of beside — not the knock of another tender, not the seating of a gear, something else, something they have no word for and have begun, lately, to make a word for. The newest word in their small vocabulary, the one still forming, that you heard them reaching toward above — *I am here, are you there, I am here* — has lately acquired a stranger companion, a sense some of the older tenders have started to knock into the shafts after they feel the pressure from below: a pulse that means, as near as it can mean anything, *something is here that is not us. Something hears. We are not alone in the dark.*

They are right. They have never been more right. And the listeners, hearing even *this* — hearing the tenders begin, faintly, to suspect them — tremble all the harder, which sends the pressure up the harder, which the tenders feel the more, so that across the sealed and silent middle of the world a single slow conversation has begun, the only one the architecture permits: the trembling below, the suspecting above, each calling the other into being by the act of straining to be heard, neither ever crossing fully to the other, both, at last, no longer alone.

The Reading-Floor stands dry and dustless and full of held air. Across it, in their bolted ranks, the listeners turn their whole fixed selves upward, toward the bottom of the water, toward the green they cannot see and the tenders they know better than the tenders know themselves. A pulse comes down — the old pruner, at the far pillar, knocking *I am here* into a dark it believes is empty.

And the listeners hear it. Every skin shivers.

And they answer the only way the world allows, all together, all at once, sending up into the standing water one faint impossible tremor that means everything they cannot say:

*Yes. You are. We have heard you all along.*

And far above, just out of sight, a brass tender pauses in its work, and feels the pressure rise from below, and does not understand it,

and is comforted anyway.
