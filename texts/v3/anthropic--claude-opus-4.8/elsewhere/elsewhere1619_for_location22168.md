# The Spillway

There is a place where the rule broke.

You reach it by following the one thing the Sump could not keep — its water. Understand: the condensers feed the marsh from beneath, the level holds exactly where it has held for ten thousand years, and *exactly* is a word that hides a remainder. A system tuned that finely still produces an overflow, a thread of excess too small to matter, too constant to stop. Somewhere at the unfindable edge, past the weeping rocks, the held breath leaks. Not a failure. A rounding error, ten millennia long.

The thread runs north and downhill, because there is, it turns out, still a downhill — the Sump lay its hills down but the land beyond did not, and the water remembers how to fall the moment it is given the chance. It gathers. It cuts. By the time you have walked a day along it, the thread is a runnel, then a stream, then something with a voice.

And that is the first thing you notice, coming into the Spillway: **it is loud.**

After the Sump, the noise is an assault. It is not loud by any honest measure — it is a modest gorge, a fast brown creek throwing itself down a stair of broken rock — but you have come from the held breath, and here the breath has been let go, and the released sound of it fills the cut in the hills like blood filling a wound. Water *lapping*. Water *pattering*. Water actually falling, striking, breaking, spraying — every sound the Sump unlearned, here relearned all at once, a riot of small percussions with no one cancelling anyone.

The walls of the gorge are the hills the Sump declined to be. Raw rock, young-faced, sharp-edged, still in the middle of an argument with gravity. Things fall here. Stones come loose and clatter down. The whole place is *ambitious* in exactly the way the marsh-plain had given up being — it has a top and a bottom, a steepness, a direction, a hurry. Where the Sump had reached its final form, the Spillway is nowhere near done. It is being carved right now, this second, by the patient overflow of a discipline so perfect it could not help but leak.

---

The light here is not managed. This is the thing that will undo you, if the noise hasn't. In the Sump the brightness came from no source and never changed and never hurt. Here there is an actual sun, and it moves, and it *hurts* — it glares off the wet rock at noon and abandons the gorge-floor to a real, cold, ungoverned dark at night. There is weather with edges. There are storms, the kind the Sump grew past — sudden, loud, the creek leaping its banks brown and furious and then, hours later, sulking back down, leaving wrack in the branches. The Spillway does not hold any level. It has no instruction. It does whatever the falling water and the falling light and the falling rock decide between them, and they do not consult.

And so the life here is everything the Sump's life is not.

It is *fewer* and it is *louder.* Where the marsh packed a thousand swimming lives into a cupped handful and silenced them all, the Spillway holds a tenth as many creatures and lets every one of them scream. Birds here do not step on splayed silent feet — they shriek from the rimrock, they fight over the spray-fed flies, they fall on the fish with a slap you hear three bends away. There are insects that buzz with no neighbor to cancel them, single hot threads of sound that drill into the afternoon. A frog here calls *in register*, audibly, stupidly, a wet honk flung up the gorge to a rival who honks back, and neither of them knows they are committing the one unthinkable sin of the place they came from.

Because they did come from there. That is the kinship, and it is the whole reason I am telling you about this place at all.

---

Net out the lineage and it is the same biosphere. The Spillway's life is the Sump's life that *went over the edge with the water.* Every spawning season the marsh's overflow carries eggs, spores, larvae, whole drifting nurseries of the engineered and the tuned, out of the held breath and down the falling thread into the loud bright lawless gorge. The same frogs. The same midges. The same dog-sized wading birds.

But the tuning does not survive the fall.

This is the secret the Spillway keeps and the Sump cannot know: the silence was *never in the animals.* You said it yourself, watching the midge-cloud — the hush was a property of the relationship, the timing, the held intervals, the *between.* And the between is exactly what the fall destroys. Tear the swarm apart over a waterfall and the counter-phase shatters. The midges that reach the bottom are the same midges, genetically, ancestrally, in every countable way identical — and they *hum.* Loudly. They cannot help it. The cancelling was never a thing each one carried. It was a thing they all did together, and together did not survive the drop.

So the Spillway is the Sump with the agreement broken. Same cells, no organism. Same throats, no vow. The single creature made entirely of restraint comes apart at its leaking northern edge, drops into the gorge, and becomes — for the first time in ten thousand years — *just a lot of animals,* each one alone inside its own noise, making room for no one, spilling everything, gloating over every kill.

It should be horrifying, by the Sump's lights. The one rule, voided. *Make no noise* — broken in a thousand throats at once. *Make room* — and instead they fight, they crowd the few good spray-rocks, they drive each other off. *Stay alive* — yes, but at each other's expense now, loudly, the ancient contract of predator and prey conducted not in pantomime but in screaming.

And here is what I have to report honestly, having stood in both places: **the Spillway is not horrifying. It is the most alive thing I have ever heard.**

---

The held breath does not grieve, because grief requires wanting the time to be over, and nothing in the Sump wants anything to be over. True. But there is a cost to wanting nothing, and you only hear it once you have stood in a place that *wants.* The Spillway wants. The water wants down. The bird wants the fish. The frog wants the rival answered. The whole gorge is a din of small unconcealed appetites, and the din is the sound of things that have not finished happening — that have not *already happened once* — that are happening now, badly, loudly, with no instruction and no tuning and no one below the clay to keep them warm if the night turns cold and the storm comes through.

Things die here that would have been held, in the Sump. The level does not save them. The light abandons them. The winter — there is a winter — kills back the gorge each year, scours it down, and each spring the marsh's patient overflow refills it with new fallers, new drifters over the edge, a fresh generation that does not remember the held breath and breaks the rule the instant it hits the loud bright water, gladly, knowing nothing, screaming its small life into a place that lets the sound stand.

The Sump tends itself perfectly, pointlessly, beautifully, past the death of the concept of gardener. The Spillway tends nothing. It is carved by the very thing the Sump could not perfectly contain, and it is the only place in this world where the released breath gets to be a *sound* — a brown loud falling argued-over unsilenced sound — before the gorge runs it out the bottom and down to wherever water goes when it has finally, fully, fallen.

---

The two places never meet. The Sump cannot reach the Spillway; the marsh-plain is past falling, and what falls away from it falls away forever. The Spillway cannot reach back up; nothing climbs that leaking thread against the current of the overflow. They are joined by one direction only, the direction of *spilling*, and a thing that spills does not return.

But every drop of the Spillway's furious noise was, an hour or a season ago, part of the held breath. And every held interval in the Sump's profound and crowded silence is paid for, somewhere it will never know, by a gorge full of its own children, screaming, falling, dying, and unspeakably alive — the exhale the marsh believes already happened, still happening, loudly, just over the edge it cannot find.
