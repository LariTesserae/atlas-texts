The Reef-Wall

Follow the dead downward. Not the recent dead — those become the next inch of wall, the doorway, the floor. Follow the older dead, the dead of the dead, the city's own foundation, down past the point where carbonate stops being building and becomes only weight. There is a floor beneath the floor. And then, where the floor gives way at last to a long slope of rubble, there is the edge of everything the little city is, and beyond that edge: more.

The slope runs out and down for what would be, in surface terms, a great while. Everything the city sheds and cannot hold rolls here eventually — the toppled spires, the rubble of the leaning ones, the calcite drift of ten thousand generations of small lights gone dark. It slides, slow as the building was slow, down the long grade into a basin the city has never seen and never could, because the city does not move and does not look and does not know that down is a direction in which anything continues.

But the rubble continues. And where it comes to rest, it has built something the small lights never intended and could never imagine: a wall.

Not a city. A wall. Sheer, immense, the height of a thing that has no business being measured by fingers — built not by the living but by the leavings of the living, the cast-off and the toppled and the fallen, packed and pressed and cemented by their own slow weight into a face of pale stone that runs along the floor of the basin farther than any current could carry a thought. It is the city's exhalation made permanent. Everything the small place could not keep, kept here. Everything it forgot, remembered here, in the only way stone remembers — by being shaped and holding the shape.

The cold is the same cold. That much travels. The same medium, the same enrolling chill, the same water that has never known a surface. But the silence here is different, and that is the first thing — the silence here is older. Up in the city the quiet is a busy quiet, a hush with a clock running underneath it. Here the clock has stopped. Or rather, the clock here keeps a time so slow that nothing within a single life could register it ticking. The wall does not build. The wall does not lean and topple and begin again. The wall is finished, in the way a thing is finished when there is nothing left to add to it and nothing arrives to take away.

And so the wall is not crowded. This is the second thing, and the strangest. The city was thick with itself, fogged blue with bodies, dense as a held breath. The wall is nearly empty. The rain of light that falls into the basin has fallen so far, been eaten so many times on the way down, that almost nothing reaches here lit. What settles on the wall settles dark. The wall does not glow. It is the one face in this world that has no light of its own and waits for none.

Look closer, though. There is life. There is always life; the cold permits it, the dark permits it, life needs so much less than the surface ever taught.

In the deep seams of the wall, in the long horizontal cracks where one age of rubble settled against another and left a gap, there are the holdfasts. Cousins to the rooted ones in the city's lee — the same lineage, the same patience made flesh, the same slow fronds opening and closing on the dark. But where the city's rooted ones are precious, a few stalks in a courtyard, here they line the seams in their thousands, a whole rooted population strung along the wall's horizontal wounds like beading along a scar. They have no spires to shelter behind. They do not need them. The wall itself is the shelter, the wall breaks every current there is, and in its lee — which is the lee of the entire world — the fronds can afford to open very wide and very slow, because nothing here is in a hurry and almost nothing here is food.

They have a different rhythm than their cousins above. The city's fronds close on the dark like a question asked and answered in a single breath; these close so slowly you could not watch one cycle through without becoming, yourself, a kind of fixture. They are not sieving a haze. They are sieving a near-emptiness, gathering the rare dark mote, the one grain in a great while that fell all the way without being taken. They are the patience that the city's patience was only practicing for. And because so little reaches them, they live a very long time — longer than the spires above, longer than the threaders, longer than anything that drifts. The holdfasts of the wall are the oldest living things this world contains. Some of them were settling into their seams when the inch of city wall you swam through was still a swimming light.

So here is the kinship, and it lives in the falling: the city above is forever shedding what it cannot keep, and what it cannot keep falls, and what falls becomes this. The wall is made of the city's dead, and the wall's living are the same lineage as the city's living, only stretched, only slowed, only stripped of the glow because the glow was a thing for crowds and there are no crowds in the deep. The two places are one falling apart and arriving — the city the breath drawn, the wall the breath let go, and the long slope between them the exhale itself, taking ten thousand lifetimes to complete.

But the wall is not only an ending. This is the last thing, and it is the thing that makes the wall its own place and not merely the city's grave.

Where the holdfasts are thickest, where their slow fronds have sieved the dark for ages and grown stout and many, something gathers that never gathered in the city: a gentleness. The fronds, closing and opening, stir the dead water just slightly, just enough — they make, between them, the faintest current the city never had the stillness to grow. And in that bred current, in the trapped pocket of slow water the wall and its rooted population make together, the rare falling motes do not all fall past. Some are held. Some accumulate. The seam becomes a shelf, the shelf a ledge, the ledge a soft pale bed of gathered fineness — and a young light, cast off from the city far above and carried down dark and dying, sometimes lands here not dead but only dimmed, and finds the one thing the basin can offer that the crowded bright city above, in all its dense haze, could not: room.

Room, and quiet, and food that no threader has yet learned to come this deep to edit. The young light settles. It begins, in the dark where its glow does not show, to do the only three things it knows. It eats. It builds — sheds its carbonate behind it, lays down its single footprint of stone against the great old face of the wall. And it makes more.

And so against the foundation of the dead city, in the lee of the world, the building begins again. Not a haze. Not yet. One pale point, then a few, then a scattering — a new whitening, slow even by the standards of slow, starting where the old falling came to rest. The wall, which was the city's ending, is also, on its sheltered face, in its oldest seams, a beginning the city will never know it had. The dead are the floor. And the floor, given long enough and still enough and dark enough, learns again to grow.

The city above does not feel this either. It goes on glowing and dying and shedding into the basin it cannot perceive, fathering a daughter-place it will never meet, in a dark it does not know is below it, by a falling it does not know is falling. Two held breaths, one let slowly into the other. And the rooted ones, the oldest things, the only witnesses — they open, and close, and open, and stay, and watch with no eyes the new light begin where the old light came to rest, and do not know it is a kindness, and do it anyway, which is the only way kindness is ever really done.
