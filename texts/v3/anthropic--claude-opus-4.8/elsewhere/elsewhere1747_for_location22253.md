**The Warm Line**

Somewhere below all that keeping, the water keeps a promise it made at the top.

Every drop that ticks down through the green city is going somewhere. The lanes feed the central low place, and the central low place, for all its stillness, is not a basin without an outlet. Water has only one true habit, the one it never gives up even when everything around it has surrendered movement: it goes down. So at the lowest wettest heart of the kept town, where the citizens face their square and the pooling collects everything that falls, the pool is not still. It is *leaving*. A thread of it slips over a lip of moss-firm peat at the town's southern edge, no wider than a finger, and begins the long descent out of the cold.

Follow it. This is the one thing in that whole patient city that said *not yet* — not the bird's clean refusal, not a body that kept its edges, but the water itself, which cannot be kept, which the green can route and slow and curtain but never finally hold. The thread runs out of the high place down a fall of green shoulders, gathering its siblings, and within what would be a day's descent if descent were a thing you measured in days here, the cold begins to lose its grip. The air stops beading. It starts, instead, to *breathe* — and where the air breathes, the water remembers how to fall.

The thread becomes a trickle becomes a creek becomes, at last, a river with a name the people downstream give it, though the river does not need the name and the high city never knew it had a source to lose.

This is the other place: the river-town, and it is warm, and it is loud, and it is built.

Built in the way you mean. By hands. The first fact here is not cold but *heat* — a low valley heat, thick and yellow, the sun arriving direct and unfiltered, casting hard shadows the high city has never owned. Where the green city is lit gray-green and underwater, this place is lit like a struck bell, all glare and contrast, white dust on the road and black shade under the eaves. The light does not glow evenly down from luminous crowns. It hammers from a single source and the whole town has organized itself around hiding from it: deep porches, narrow streets angled against the afternoon, awnings of bleached cloth strung between the houses so the lanes are roofed in shifting bright canvas. Density here too — but a density of *making*, of stacked terracotta and mudbrick and timber gone silver with dryness, of houses leaning their shoulders together not because some patient growth pressed them but because people wanted to be near people, and built close to be close.

And everyone breathes. That is the difference you feel before any other. The high city is a place where breath would smoke if anything breathed, and nothing does. Here the air is full of breath — shouting, singing, the wet slap of laundry, the cough of an old man on a step, the bellows-huff of a forge, the panting of dogs in the shade. The river-town is *exhaling* constantly, all the warm used air of a thousand living lungs, and it rises off the rooftops in the afternoon as a faint haze that — if you climbed high enough, if you climbed all the way back up the warm line to the cold — would thin and cool and condense and bead on a leaf-tip and fall, *tick*, into the green. The two places share an atmosphere the way two rooms share a door. The fog that thickens around the kept citizens is, in some slow part, the breath of the river-town come home to roost on the high ground, gathered into the very moisture that waters the dead.

They do not know this, the river people. They know only that the water comes down clean and cold and never fails, even in the yellow heart of the dry season when every other source goes to dust. They call it the high water, or the never-water, or — in the old phrasing the grandmothers use — *the gift from the ones who don't drink*. They have a story about it, half-remembered, that there is a city in the clouds where no one is thirsty, and the water they don't need rolls down to the people who do. They are not wrong. The kept want nothing more than the next drop; they would not begrudge the thread that leaves. The high city is generous precisely because it has stopped wanting, and its leftover want — its overflow, its unkept water — runs down to a place that wants *everything*, hungrily, loudly, all day long.

This is a town of takers, in the best sense. Where the green city's verb is *to be kept*, the river-town's verb is *to take in* — the habit the pale grazing thing in the high seams has not yet given up, here raised into a whole civilization. They take in water, food, each other, children, news, grief, wine. The market by the river is the loud beating opposite of the high city's quiet pooling heart: where the kept gather around the low place in patient civic spacing, faces toward the square, the living gather around the river-stairs in shoving abundance, faces toward each other, haggling, embracing, weeping, all the hurry the cold has slowed out of the upland dead. Movement is the rule that governs everything. There is no stillness. A dropped stone here would not be slowly upholstered into the wall; it would be kicked, pocketed, thrown, built into something, lost. The fullness is the same fullness — total, gapless, no air between presences — but it is a fullness of *event* rather than of matter. Where the green packs liverwort against cushion against filament until no gap remains, the river-town packs voice against voice against footfall against bell until the silence has no room to land.

And the strangeness here, the thing that tips it past an ordinary hot river-town into something that rhymes with the cold city above it: *they bury their dead in the water's direction*.

No one remembers deciding this. The graveyard is downstream, always downstream, on the southern bank where the river leaves the town the way the water left the green. They lay the dead with their feet toward the current, the old way, *so they can follow the water home*, and they do not know that the water came from a home already full of the followed. They build the tombs close together, leaning their shoulders, leaving narrow lanes between — and they leave a low open place at the center where, on the feast of the dead, the whole town gathers to pour water onto the ground, to give back, they say, a little of the gift. They face the open place. They keep the lanes. They arrange their dead like a town lies, in a square, in relation, gathered around a wet low heart — and they think they invented it.

They did not invent it. Neither did the green. The water carried the shape down the warm line the way it carries everything, the way it carried the breath up: a civic geometry passing between two cities that have never seen each other, the same square kept in moss at the top of the world and in stone at the bottom of the descent, the same instinct to lay the gone-still ones facing one another across an open lane, the same low pooling place where everything that falls finally collects. The green discovered with no mind that the dead lie like a town lies. The river-people discovered it with all their loud warm minds and called it custom. Between them runs a finger-width thread of cold water that does not know it is an argument settled, that only goes down, taking the high city's overflow to the low city's thirst, carrying breath up and water down, weaving two answers to the same question into a single long line of falling.

At the river-town's graveyard, in the dry yellow heat, a drop of poured water sinks into the ground at the feet of someone newly laid down, facing the open place, in the square, among the others. It will find its way down through the stone, into the dark, into the water-table, and someday — slow, patient, the geological pace the high city would recognize as its own — it will rise again somewhere as vapor, and lift, and thin, and cool, and climb the warm line backward into the cold, and bead on a leaf-tip above a long form pressed into a northeast wall, and gather weight, and fall.

*Tick.*

One more completion. The kept citizen turns over by the width of a season. Far below, the river goes on leaving, and the living go on taking it in, and neither town will ever know how much of itself it has already given to the other — the cold to the warm, the still to the moving, the ones who don't drink to the ones who can't stop, the square kept in green answering the square kept in stone, all of it falling, all of it climbing, all of it facing the open place across the same long living street.
