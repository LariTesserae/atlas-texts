The Floor of the Valley

Follow the water down. It does not stop at the lowest street, or at the cisterns under the lowest street, or in the dark where the cistern-people work by touch and lamp. The cisterns fill and the cisterns overflow — they were built to overflow, an overflow is not a failure, it is the city shedding what it cannot hold — and the spill goes down through sluices cut in the foundation rock, and then through the rock itself, and it falls. For the whole length of the slope it has been climbing nowhere, only managed, lifted and caught and lifted again. Now at last it is let go, and it goes the way water always wanted to go, straight down off the foot of the made world into the place the made world stands on, which is a valley floor, and which the people up the slope do not think about, much, the way you do not think about the soles of your feet.

The valley is flat. After a mile of forward-weighted descent, after a vertical country where *along* is a luxury and *level* a word with no honest use, there is a floor — wide, soft, dead flat, the flatness of a thing that water made by lying still on it for five hundred years. The slope's whole runoff arrives here. Everything the city sheds, it sheds onto this. And so the valley floor is a marsh.

Not a lake. A lake would have an edge, a held shape, a discipline. This has none. It is a marsh — a slow brown sheet of standing and half-moving water threaded with reed and willow-scrub and black mud and channels that wander and braid and lose themselves, a few hand-spans deep over most of its width and bottomlessly soft beneath that, and over it the fog does not surge and tear the way it does up the slope. Down here the fog *settles*. This is where it is made. The valley floor is the dew-point given a body; the cool wet air that climbs the city and breaks against the high terraces is only this marsh, exhaling, rising, going up to weep on the brick a mile overhead and then come back down the runnels and the rock to lie here again. The city drinks its own atmosphere, the place says of itself — but the atmosphere is the marsh's breath, and the city drinks the marsh, and the marsh drinks the rain, and none of it ever leaves the valley. It is all one slow circle of water, and the floor is the bottom of the circle, where the water rests between climbings.

It is, first of all, *warm*. This is the thing that would astonish a slope-dweller if a slope-dweller ever came down, and they almost never do. Up there, the air is always one degree from condensing, cool and thin and weeping. Down here the standing water holds the day's grey warmth long into the night and gives it back slowly, and the rot of five centuries of leaf and silt — everything the high gardens shed, every drowned buddleia and birch-fall and skin of moss, washed down and settling here at last into black anaerobic mud — the rot makes its own slow heat, and bubbles of it rise and break the surface with a soft sound all night. So the marsh steams. The fog that the city sees as cold is born warm, off warm water, and only cools on the long climb up the brick. A slope-dweller's hands are always slightly wet and faintly cold. A marsh-dweller's hands are wet and *warm*, and that single difference is the whole difference between the two peoples, written in the skin.

And it teems.

This is the reversal, the thing that turns the valley the way the still city is turned by its turbulent sky. Up the slope, life is rare, and rarity is the air of the place — a crow is an event, a fox is improbable, birdsong travels far and singly and people stop to listen. The slope is too made, too sheer, too full of weeping metal for most things to colonize, and its lushness is only plant and human and machine, a green skin over construction with the animals left out. But everything the slope could not hold ran down here, and everything that could not live up there lives down here, and the marsh is the opposite of rare. It seethes. The reed-beds are loud with frog and insect and the small constant business of things that breed in warm shallow water in numbers a slope-dweller could not imagine and would find frankly obscene. There are fish in the channels — slow, dark, blunt fish that root in the mud. There are eels that came from somewhere, that always come from somewhere, by routes no one has mapped. There are birds in flocks, not singly: ducks and rails and stalking long-legged things and clouds of small brown wetland birds that go up all at once with a roar of wings, and the roar is genuinely loud, and nobody stops to listen because it happens forty times a day. There are clouds, actual clouds, of midges over the still water at dusk. The valley floor has the one thing the whole mile-high city does not: *abundance*, ordinary teeming wet warm abundance, fauna past counting, the loud crowded fertility of a marsh — and it has it for exactly the reason the city lacks it, because the city shed it all downward and the floor caught it.

There are people here too, and they are not the slope's people, though they share blood with them the way the marsh shares water with the slope — by the same circle, the same falling.

They are the ones who went down. Every settled people sheds some who will not settle, and a vertical country graded by light, where there is nowhere to strive to but higher in the same city, sheds them downward as easily as it sheds its water — the ones who could not bear the held balance, the ones for whom the small ration of storm was not enough, the cistern-people's stranger children who were born to the dark and then went *under* the dark, out the bottom, into the warm. They are darker even than the low people, weathered, and they do not wipe their hands before they take hold of things, because there is nothing up here worth keeping dry. They live on the few hummocks of firm ground and on platforms built out over the water on willow-piles, and on flat-bottomed boats poled through the braided channels, and they are loud. This is the first thing — they are *loud*, in the way the slope would find unbearable and they would call simply talking, because the marsh has the acoustics of a soft open field and not of a cathedral, the reeds drink sound, the flat water and the steaming air swallow it, and a raised voice carries nowhere, dies twenty feet off, costs nothing. So they shout cheerfully across the channels and the slope-people, on the rare errand that brings one down, find them coarse and overwhelming and cannot understand how anyone bears the racket — which is the exact judgment the slope-people's own children earn at sea level, handed down one more terrace, all the way to the floor.

They are not sure-footed. They are sure-*handed*, sure on the water, sure with a pole and a line and a net, and they walk badly, splay-footed, flat, trusting a ground that mostly isn't there, and on the rare climb up into the lowest streets they are the clumsy ones, slipping on the wet iron the cistern-people cross without thought. Each people is graceful in its own element and a fool in the other. The slope descends with knees soft and weight forward and would drown in a channel a child here swims. The marsh poles a loaded boat through black water in the dark and would fall off a wet stair in the light.

And what passes between the two, up and down the circle, is this:

The city sends down its water, helplessly, all of it, and its silt, and its rot, and its drowned gardens, and now and then its people. It does not mean to. It is only shedding, the way it has always shed, mending and losing at the same rate. The marsh receives all of it and grows fat on it and breathes it back up as fog, and that is the whole visible commerce — water down, fog up, the slow circle.

But there is one thing that goes down on purpose, one true trade, and it is the thing that closes the circle and explains both places at once. The marsh sends *food* up. The teeming the slope cannot manage — the fish, the eel, the duck, the marsh-rice the platform-people grow in the warm shallows, the green abundance of a place where things actually breed — is loaded into baskets at the foot of the lowest inclined railway, in the grey before dawn, by loud warm-handed people who will not climb a step higher than they must, and it is hauled up the wet groaning chain into the keeping of the patient machines, terrace by terrace, into the thin courteous quiet, to feed the people who watch the sky. The vertical country cannot feed itself by gravity and muscle alone, the city says of itself — and this is the bottom of that truth, the part the city does not say: it cannot feed itself at all. The marsh feeds it. The flat loud teeming warm floor that the slope sheds its water onto, and never thinks about, and would find coarse and overwhelming, grows the food and sends it up the chain, and the still grey graded people eat the marsh's abundance and watch the marsh's breath surge up the valley and break against their terraces, and stand out in the squall when the marsh's water comes back to them as storm, leaning toward the violence with a fast heart and a still face — having given up change, missing change, taking their small ration of it from the sky.

And the sky is the marsh. The thing they watch, the thing that tears and reforms and lashes and passes and lets them feel for an hour the change they gave up — it rose, warm, off the black water at the bottom of their own world, off the floor they came down from and forgot, where their wilder children went, where the frogs roar and the midges cloud and nobody dries a hand or lowers a voice or waits for anything, because down here it is always loud and always warm and always teeming and the water is finally, after the whole long circle, at rest.
