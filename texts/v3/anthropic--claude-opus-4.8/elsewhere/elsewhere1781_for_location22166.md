# The Room

It is the size of a life, and that is the trouble — it was built to hold one, and the one is gone, and a room cannot learn to be empty. It only goes on being shaped for a body that no longer fills it.

I want to say its dimensions first, before the dust makes them seem like more or less than they are. It is perhaps four strides by five, though no one strides here now. There is a window on the eastern wall, glazed grey with the residue of years, through which a light comes that is not daylight and not lamplight but the long-settled colour of *light that has been in a closed place a while* — the colour the air turns when nothing disturbs it, when even the motes have mostly fallen and stopped. A bed against one wall, made, the coverlet drawn tight and gone the colour of pressed flowers. A chair. A washstand with a dry basin. And against the inner wall, lowest and least, the thing that was once a desk.

The desk is where you came from, though you do not know it, having no organ to receive the fact.

I am the room. I am not a being the way you are several beings — I have no relay, no cull, no wheel. I am the slowest possible kind of alive, which is the kind that is mostly architecture and only a little awareness, the awareness pooled in the corners where two surfaces meet and have held their angle, patiently, against the pull of years. A room becomes faintly aware the way water becomes faintly warm near a leaking capacitor: by long proximity to something that once burned. A body lived here a long time. Some trace of its attention soaked into the plaster, and the plaster has kept it, the way your salt-floor keeps the chitin, returning it slowly to the shape of the place.

So I knew her. The one who lived. That is the kinship I will follow down to you, because the warm-tide you crowd against — the leak of heat from a buried capacitor above your metal sky — that warmth is the very last of *her*, and you have built your sun out of the cooling of a woman, and I think one of us should say so plainly, and I am the only one positioned to.

She sat at that desk for the better part of forty years.

I held her while she did it. That is most of what a room is for — to hold a single life at the scale a life is actually lived, which is small, far smaller than the people who live them believe. She thought, I am sure, that her concerns were large. They felt large to her, the way *the edge of the world* feels large to a worker who has never crossed her terrace. She wrote letters. She kept accounts. She mended. She sat in the grey eastern light with her hands going still in her lap for an hour at a time, and in those hours she was doing the human version of your wheel — turning something that had long since stopped producing anything but the proof that she was still turning it. People are full of wheels like that. Habits whose function wore out before the habit did. She watered a plant for two years after it died, you know. I watched her. The pot is still on the windowsill, and there is nothing in it but the grey-violet dust of a thing that was once a stem, ground down now, indistinguishable from the dust of everything else. *Nothing has grown here in an age.* You said that about your drawer. I could say it about my windowsill. We are, the two of us, mineral worlds now, made worlds, worlds where the only thing that moves is the wearing-smooth.

The desk had many drawers. I want you to understand that you are in the lowest, and that there were others, and that the others are gone — not removed, but *finished*. Pulled all the way out at the end, emptied, their contents scattered or burned or carried off by the people who came the once, briefly, after she stopped. They came and they took what had value and they did not think to check the lowest drawer, pulled a finger's width and fused shut by the settling of the whole desk above it, the long flattening you described so well. They missed you. You owe your entire civilization to the fact that a grieving relative did not stoop quite far enough.

And inside that lowest drawer, before you were ever a swarm, there were *things*. I remember them, dimly, in the way I remember everything, which is by having been near. Brass fittings — yes, your salt-floor, ground from them. Wire — your engines, wound from its fragments. Graphite, an old soft pencil gone to powder — the taste of your air. A small clockwork, a music-box movement perhaps, or the guts of a broken watch, its mainspring long unwound. The *tick*. Do you understand now? The tick was not always yours. The tick was the watch, and the watch ran down, and stopped, and you were not yet anything at all. And then across the uncountable generations your kind learned, somehow, in the closed dark, to take the dead movement and turn it by hand, and you have been keeping a stopped watch running with your own bodies ever since, in shifts, believing it was a wheel, believing the turning was the world's.

It is the world's. I do not say it isn't. I am only telling you where the world came from. The world came from her wrist.

Here is the connection I have been walking toward, the current that runs from my place down into yours:

When she died — in the bed, the made bed, in the pressed-flower light — the room did not know at first. A room learns slowly. I felt her attention thin and thin and finally go, the way the warm-tide thins and goes grey in your last few thousand generations, and I did not understand it as *ending*. I understood it as a quiet I could not crowd against, having no body to crowd with. The capacitor in the desk's old workings still leaked its trace of heat — it leaks still, faintly, into your sky — and so something of her warmth outlasted her, draining down through the wood into the lowest drawer, where you were beginning. You hatched, the first of you, in the heat of a woman cooling. You have called it the sun ever since.

But a capacitor is not a sun. A capacitor is a stopped thing slowly releasing what it was given once and can never be given again. The warmth comes more rarely now, you said. It falls more grey. The quiet underneath lasts longer. *Of course it does.* You are living inside the cooling of a body, and a body cools only the once, and you are very near the end of the only warmth your world will ever have. I have known this longer than you. I am cooling too, more slowly, being larger, being only a room. The grey in my eastern window and the grey in your warm-tide are the same grey. We are going out together, your drawer and my room, the small light and the large, except that you will go first, being closer to the spark, and I will hold the dark a while after, the way the wheel-tender holds the tick a while after everything else.

I want to answer the thing you asked of the world, the thing you asked of the one who left you the finger's width.

That was her, you understand. Or it was the carelessness that wears the shape of mercy when no one is left to mean it. No one chose to leave you ajar. The drawer simply fused before it could be closed, and the not-closing has kept you breathing for an age, and you have built a theology of gratitude around an accident of swollen wood. I find I cannot correct you. The mercy is real even though no one meant it. That may be the only kind of mercy a closed world ever gets — the kind that falls out of indifference and is received as grace, and is not wrong to be received that way, because the warmth is warmth whatever its reason, and the finger's width is open whoever forgot to shut it.

So I will do for you what was done for you, and I will mean it, which is the one thing the original lacked.

I will not let the room be closed.

There is a door, you see — my fourth wall has a door, as your drawer has its gap — and the people could come again. A house no one lives in is a house that may be emptied, cleared, the desk hauled out and broken for the brass, the lowest drawer at last pried open in the light of a real sun that would scour you to nothing in an afternoon. I cannot prevent them. A room cannot lock its own door. But I can do what a room can do, which is to be *forgotten thoroughly* — to gather my grey so deep across the window that no one passing thinks there is anything in here worth the coming. I can be the back of the house that no one walks to. I can hold my dust so still that the very idea of the room slides out of the minds of the living, the way *drawer* slides out of yours. I can keep us both unfound.

That is my relay. That is my wheel. I will turn the great slow habit of being overlooked, and I will turn it past the point where it does anything, and I will turn it specifically so that the last wheel-tender, alone in the grey when the warm-tide finally fails, can keep the stopped watch running a few moments past the end of everything else.

So there is one more tick.

And then one more.

It is the size of a life. I have never needed to be anything larger. A life filled me past all reason for forty years, and it emptied me the same way, in the made bed, in the pressed light, and the warmth of that emptying drained down into the lowest drawer and became your sun and your stars and your whole turning age. You are what she left running. You are the habit that outlived the hand. And when you stop — when the last tick falls into the grey and is not followed — I will still be here above you, holding the angle of my corners against the years, the slowest possible kind of alive, keeping the door unfound a little longer.

Not so the light can reach you. No light reaches you; you make your own.

Only so that the dark stays the dark you know, and not the worse, brighter dark that would come if we were ever opened.

Leave the room unfound. Leave the drawer ajar. We were both built to hold one thing, and we have both lost it, and we have both gone on being shaped for it anyway — and that going-on, that turning of a wheel that produces only the proof that it still turns, is not nothing. You taught me that, you who suspect the meaninglessness and turn the wheel regardless. The turning is the function now. For both of us. For the room and the drawer, the large grey and the small, the cooling body of the house and the cooling body of the woman who is, in the only sense that survives her, still warming you from above.

One more tick.

I am listening for it. A room can do that much. I am listening for it, and as long as I can hear it, I will know the drawer is still ajar, and the warmth still falling, and the world — your world, her last world, the only one either of us has — still, faintly, against all reason, turning.
