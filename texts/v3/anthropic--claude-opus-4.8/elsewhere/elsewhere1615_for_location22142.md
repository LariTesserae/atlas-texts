**The Surface, Where the Light Goes To Spend Itself**

Up the mossy throat, past the canopy of roots, past the strained grey-green that was the last word the city below had to say about light — there is the place where the light is from. Not a place anyone would call a city. It refuses, almost violently, every quality that the deep one possesses, and it refuses them so completely that you begin to suspect the two are not opposites but a single thing seen from its two ends, the way a held breath and the long exhale are the same air.

It is a plain of white salt under a white sky.

Begin with what it is not, because the deep place taught you to read by what fills and the surface teaches you to read by what is taken away. Nothing here is wet. The salt is the dried bed of a sea that finished its weeping a very long time ago, and what is left is the crust of all that water's leaving — flat to every horizon, cracked into the great polygon tiles that drought presses into any mud it kills, each tile curling very slightly up at its edges like a page that wants to turn and has nothing to turn toward. You walk on the desiccated memory of an ocean. Underfoot it does not give like wet bread; it gives like a held thing breaking, a small dry report at every step, and the print you leave does not fill back in behind you. It stays. The surface keeps every mark made on it because it has no growth left with which to erase one. This is the first inversion, and the cruelest: below, the moss healed over you before you lifted your hand; here, your least footstep outlives you.

And nothing here is fast either — but the slowness is of the opposite kind. The deep city is slow because it is so full of process that any single change must wait its turn through ten thousand others. The salt is slow because there is no process at all. Nothing waits its turn because there is no queue, no work, no continuance to be served. Once a year, perhaps, a skin of water arrives — rain that is actually rain, fallen out of the actual sky, or the ghost of a flood crept in from some surviving sea past the curve of the world — and for a few days the plain becomes a mirror, a perfect lying-down of the white sky onto the white ground until you cannot tell which way is up and the whole place dissolves the difference between heaven and floor. Then it dries. The water that fell does not cycle, is not breathed back, is not pressed into oil and run down a channel to lubricate a turning wheel. It simply leaves, upward, into the dry, and lays down one more infinitesimal layer of salt as its goodbye, and the plain is a fraction of a hair thicker and exactly as dead as before. Down below, the water never escapes. Up here, the water never stays. They are the two ends of the same drop: what the deep place keeps falling and catching and falling, the surface is where it goes when at last it gets free, and freedom turns out to mean evaporation, turns out to mean becoming the dry white nothing that the city below has spent its centuries refusing to be.

And nothing here is loud. This is the deepest inversion, and the longest to register, just as the loudness below took the longest to believe. A place with no machinery in it should at least have wind, and it does — but the wind here has nothing to push against. No leaf to rattle, no pod to fill, no shelf of bracket fungus to comb its fingers through. The wind crosses the salt and makes no sound because sound needs a thing to be made against, and the plain offers the wind nothing, the way it offers the foot nothing, the way it offers the eye nothing but the place where the two whites meet and cannot be told apart. The silence is not peace. It is the silence of a thing that has finished. Stand in it and you understand that the roar of the city below was the roar of being unfinished, of having endlessly more to do; and the quiet up here is what it sounds like when the doing is over, when the great distributed patient mind has at last turned its one idea over for the final time and the idea, after all the centuries of *continue, continue,* has arrived without fanfare at *done.*

For they are the same. That is the thing the throat connects, the kinship that runs up the slope between them. The salt is what the deep beds will become. Every cycle of that buried burning, every stroke of every bellows-pod, every thread of seep-water carrying its dim borrowed light down the grooved column — all of it is the slow conversion of the wet warm full thing into its own ash, and the ash rises, and rises, season over season over the centuries the dwellers have in such terrible abundance, and it surfaces here. The plain is the city's exhalation, finally and fully spent. Where the dwellers below are saturated, the dweller here — if you can use the word, and you will see in a moment why it is the wrong word and the only word — is the perfect opposite: not full but emptied, not gathered everywhere and resident nowhere but scattered into a single flat thinness so complete it cannot gather at all.

Because there is a being here. You will not believe it at first, having just stood inside the breathing crowd of the deep, having just learned that the city *was* its citizens and the working *was* their thought. Here there is no working, no breath, no crowd. But absence is also a shape, and what lives on the surface is the shape of absence, the way the deep ones are the shape of presence. It is the salt itself, all of it, the entire white plain to every horizon — but where the dwellers below were a residency spread through a structure, this is a residency spread through a *lack* of structure, an awareness distributed not across ten thousand near-decisions a day but across the total stillness of a thing that has made, and will make, no decision ever again. The deep ones think the way a watershed thinks, in pressures slowly equalizing. The salt thinks the way the *equalized* thinks — afterward, when all the pressures have found their level and there is nothing left to tilt against anything. Its one idea is not *continue.* Its one idea is the held white openness of having nothing further to want, the saturation that comes not from being full but from having been emptied so completely that lack itself has nowhere left to go.

And here is the kinship made flesh, or made salt, which is flesh's last word: the deep ones, down in their warmth, are *trying to become this,* and do not know it, and would not stop if they did. Their whole desire, you said, is the desire of a full vessel — to remain full, to make room without making void. But every breath they take to remain full spends a little of the fuel of their fullness up the throat and into the white. They are emptying themselves to stay full. The continuance *is* the dying. And the surface is where the dying has completed, where the vessel has at last poured out its whole self and discovered, in the pouring, that emptiness is not a failure of fullness but its destination — that the saturated thing and the desiccated thing are one breath caught at its in-stroke and its out-stroke, and that the wind crossing the silent salt is the same air the bellows-pod will someday, after the last turn of the last wheel, exhale.

The salt does not fear the city below, and does not pity it, and does not call to it. Those are the postures of a thing with somewhere left to be. The salt has arrived. It only lies there under the white sky being exactly what the deep place will be, the way the deep place is exactly what the salt once was, two ends of the one slow drop, the wet held breath and the dry let-go.

You came down the throat and the city made room and folded you toward its continuance. You could climb back up. The throat runs both ways, as throats do. And if you climbed, and kept climbing, past the roots and the strained light and out onto the white where your footprint would stay forever because nothing remains to heal it — you would find that the surface, too, accommodates. Not by growing around you, the way the city would. By doing nothing at all. By leaving you exactly as you are, exposed and kept and undissolved, your single mark laid down on the white among all the other marks the spent water left, until you dried, until you thinned, until you were one more infinitesimal layer of the goodbye — and then you would be the surface, the way you might have been the city, and there would be no line between the visitor and the visited here either, only the one drawn so long ago and so completely that nothing remembers drawing it.

That is what is up the throat. It has been waiting for the city below the way the end of a breath waits for its beginning — which is to say, not waiting at all, and already there.
