# The Watershed Station

Begin with the cold, because that is what runs downhill, and water is the only thing the two places truly share. Up here the cold comes down from a sky that is all sky — too much of it, a hard violet-blue scoured of cloud, so high and so dry that the sun does not warm so much as expose. There is a ceiling at the Sump-Works that never breaks; here there is no ceiling at all, only altitude, and the air so thin it cannot hold a thought of moisture. Everything is edges. Nothing has settled into roundness because nothing here is old enough, or wet enough, or patient enough, to be worn. The rock is fresh-broken. The frost splits it nightly. The shards lie where they fell that morning.

This is a high place, a dry place, a place where water has decided *not* to live near the surface and the one structure stands anyway, alone, on the saddle between two drainages — the spine of the world's roof, where a single ridge of stone decides which ocean a snowflake will eventually belong to.

## What Runs Out, and Where It Goes

I came here by following the water backward. The Sump-Works is the body that swallows; this is the gland that secretes. Every thread weeping down its green brick, every gulp its pumps fight all day and lose — all of it began as snow on this saddle, was pressed into ice, was pressed into melt, and started the long fall down through gorge and gravel-fan and reed-fen toward the basin that would take it up and never let it go. The two places are the two ends of one descent. The cold that hardens this rock is the same cold that, six weeks and nine thousand feet later, beads on a flywheel and is called a sweat.

So the kinship is not poetic. It is hydraulic. The Sump-Works breathes once a day; this place is the lung's farthest reach, the alveolus, the cold rim where the breath is first drawn. And just as the works were a body that looked empty, the Station is a place that looks dead, and is not, and the error is the same error inverted.

## The Built Thing

One structure. A squat masonry blockhouse with walls four feet thick, roofed in iron sheet bolted down against a wind that wants the roof more than the roof wants to stay. It was built — there is no pretending otherwise here — by people, real people, surveyors and engineers of some bureau a long time ago, to measure the snow. To know how much water the high country was banking, so that the low country could be told what was coming. A gauging station. A place whose entire purpose was *to count the water before it left*.

Inside: a stove long cold, a logbook swollen to a brick by frost and thaw, a brass instrument under the window with a needle frozen at some forgotten reading. Outside: the snow-stakes, painted iron poles graduated in feet, marching up the saddle in a measured line, half of them heaved sideways now by the frost that pushes everything crooked. A rain gauge that has not been emptied in years and holds a core of solid ice, a clear cylinder of every winter stacked on the last, unread.

And that is all the building does now. It stands. It is not maintained. No one cuts anything back, because nothing grows to cut.

## The Vegetation, Such As It Is

Almost none, and that almost is the whole story. The Sump-Works drowns in green; here the green is rationed to the grain. In the lee of the blockhouse, in the one crack where snowmelt lingers an extra hour each morning, there are cushion plants — tight hard domes of leaf packed so dense the wind cannot get a finger into them, hoarding their own warmth, a thousand stems sharing one breath. Lichen on the north faces, the slow grey-gold crust that grows perhaps the width of a coin in a human lifetime. A few grasses in the seams, blade-stiff, more wire than leaf.

This is the same chlorophyll trick the works run wholesale — light into sugar, sun into staying — but starved down to the bone of itself. At the Sump-Works the plants grew a finger's width a night, with no enemy and no end. Here they grow a finger's width a century and have every enemy: the cold, the wind, the burning sun, the killing dark. Both places are doing the identical thing. One does it drowning in plenty. The other does it on the edge of a knife, and that is the only difference, and it is the difference between a fat man asleep and a fasting saint awake. Same animal. Different vow.

## The Presence

Here is where the error inverts, and I want to be as precise as the other was.

The visitor to the Sump-Works waited for a body and found none, because the body was the whole place and he stood inside it. At the Station you make the opposite mistake. You expect no one, because the place is so plainly abandoned — the cold stove, the frozen needle, the heaved stakes — and abandonment reads as emptiness, as the human-shaped hole where attention used to be. You feel the absence of the surveyors the way the visitor felt the absence of the men who tended the pumps.

But the surveyors are not the life of this place. They were its *eyes* — and eyes can close without the body dying. The Station goes on measuring. It simply no longer reports.

Watch what it does. The snow falls and packs against the stakes whether anyone reads them or not — the measurement is taken, the graduated pole still says exactly how deep, in clean painted feet, to no one. The rain gauge fills its clear cylinder year on year, a perfect ice-core record of every season, archived in cold, addressed to nobody. The frozen needle under the window has not stopped sensing; it has only stopped being able to move, like an old animal that still feels the weather in its joints. The whole apparatus is still *doing its one thing*. It counts the water. It has always counted the water. It will count the water until the iron rusts to lace and the masonry sloughs back into the scree, and the count will go on being correct, and unread, the entire time.

That is its life. Not the keeping of pumps against drowning — the Sump-Works has that vow. This one keeps a different vow, the harder one perhaps: it *witnesses*. It stands at the source and registers what passes and asks nothing in return, the way the works at the bottom labor for no product. Neither place accomplishes anything. The works pump water that returns; the Station counts water that leaves and is never told whether the counting mattered. Both spend everything they have on a task with no outside reward, which is to say both are alive in the only way anything is ever alive — by persisting at a thing for its own sake.

## How the Two Connect, in the Single Falling Breath

I said the kinship is hydraulic, and it is, but stand on the saddle at the cold hour before the sun finds it and you feel it as something nearer than plumbing.

The water that beads on the works' weeping brick was weighed here first. Snow fell on this stake; the stake recorded it, in feet, to no one; the snow waited out the dark in the lee of the blockhouse beside the cushion plants; the sun struck the saddle and the melt began, threading down between the heaved poles, past the frozen gauge, off the rim of the watershed and down — down through the rock that has not yet learned roundness, gathering, gorging, slowing, warming, thickening, until nine thousand feet below and weeks of falling later it slides under a green pier and is taken up, without a ripple, into the slow chemistry of the swallowing body. The Station is where the water is *known*. The Sump-Works is where it is *consumed*. Between them runs the entire length of the fall, and the fall is the relationship — the cold counting at the top and the warm digesting at the bottom, joined by one continuous thread of weeping that never stops because the snow never stops falling and the basin never stops filling and neither place has any way, or any wish, to stop.

The visitor to the works waded out feeling the pumps would never cease, and learned he had it backwards: something was determined not to die. The same correction waits here, and it is colder. You stand among the frozen stakes feeling the place is finished, finished long ago, a dead instrument in a dead air. But the snow is on the gauge. The needle still feels the front coming in off the violet sky. The lichen has grown a coin's width since the surveyors left and will grow another. Nothing here has stopped counting. It only stopped being heard — which is not the same as stopping, any more than a closed eye is a blind one.

Something at the bottom of the world is determined not to drown.

Something at the top is determined to keep watch, and to keep it correctly, and to keep it for no one, forever, in the thin scouring cold, under all that pitiless sky.

And the water goes on falling between them, the one long breath, drawn cold at the rim and let out warm in the dark, the two ends of a single creature that has arranged itself across nine thousand feet of the world and called the upper part *measurement* and the lower part *appetite* and never once, in all its slow persisting, needed a witness of its own — though now, briefly, like a swallowed insect, like a snowflake weighed and released, it has had one.
