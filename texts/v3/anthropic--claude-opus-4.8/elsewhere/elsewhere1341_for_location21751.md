# The Spillward

There is a place where the water, having been gathered, has to go.

You can find it by following the only fast thing in the basin past the point where the basin lets it stay fast. The natives of the Condenser District tend toward the towers; the water tends the other way. It is harvested, it is poured into the cisterns, and the cisterns — this is the part nobody up there thinks about, because thinking about *after* is a fast creature's habit — the cisterns overflow. They have always overflowed. The harvest takes more than the people drink, more than the amphibians lick from the iron, more than the slow basin can hold against its own sweating. The surplus runs out at the basin's lowest lip, through a cut in the young rock, and falls.

This is where it falls to.

---

The Spillward begins as a single channel and ends as a country. The overflow comes through the cut all at once, loud — for one stretch it is the *loudest* thing in the whole world, louder than the towers, a white roar of released water hammering down the raw stone the basin never had time to soften. And then the land opens beneath it and the water spreads, and as it spreads it slows, and as it slows it quiets, until a mile down it is moving at a walk and a mile after that it is barely moving at all, fanning out across a broad warm shelf of older ground into a hundred braided shallows that catch the light and throw it back up at a sky that here is open, unsteamed, plainly blue.

Everything is the inverse of the place above. The basin is cool; the Spillward is warm — the falling water heats on the way down, beats itself warm against the rock, and arrives steaming faintly into air that is already kind. The basin is young rock, sharp-edged, astonished at itself; the Spillward is *old*, the oldest ground for a long way, rounded and settled and sure, ground that finished arriving so long ago it has forgotten it ever was anything but this. The basin is loud-still — clamor outside, nothing moving within. The Spillward is the opposite again: *quiet*, but everything in it moves. The braided shallows shift their courses daily. Sandbars rise and drown. Fish run the channels in bright fast schools, here and gone. Birds — not sooty, not pitched into any narrow band, but loud however they please across the whole open register, because nothing here competes with them for the air — wheel and call and squabble and are answered. It is a place of constant small change and almost no permanence, and the change is *easy*, because there is nothing to push against. The water has done its hard labor already, up at the cut. Down here it only has to wander.

---

The water remembers, though. This is the kinship, and it runs deeper than the channel.

Every drop in the Spillward was, an hour ago, beaded on the cold of an iron tower, or on the cold of a listener's wrist. It condensed up there out of nothing, fattened, let go — the only quick thing the basin allows — and then was caught, and poured, and held, and finally allowed to be quick again, falling. The Spillward is made entirely of *released* basin. It is what the slow place's one fast act adds up to, gathered over years into a country of motion. Everything the listeners give up, everything that runs off the edge of their long patient cycling, arrives here and *lives* — turns into fish-flash and sandbar and the wheeling argument of the birds.

There are people in the Spillward. They are not slow.

---

They came up the channel, originally, against the current, which tells you something about them — the basin's people came *down* into the cool and stayed; the Spillward's people came *up* toward the noise, looked at it, and turned back. They are the descendants of the ones who turned back. They tell it as a story: that long ago some of the harvesters walked out to see where the overflow went, and most returned to the towers, but some stood in the warm shallows under the open sky and felt the rhythm let go of them, felt the engine-floor drop out from under their thinking, and could not make themselves climb back into the roar. They built here instead, low houses on the old settled ground above the highest braids, and they have lived fast ever since, on purpose, the way the basin lives slow on purpose.

You can recognize a true native of the Spillward by three things, and they are the basin's three things, inverted.

**The voice.** They talk *over* each other, all at once, in the whole open register, the way the birds do here — not pitched into any narrow channel, because there is no machinery to leave a channel, because down here the air is free for the taking. A conversation among them sounds to a basin-ear like chaos, like flood, like everyone releasing at once. Answers come *fast*, before the question is finished, layered, interrupting, alive. They cannot hold a thought still. They would not want to. A thought held still, to them, is a thought drowning.

**The water.** They do not condense and they do not gather from the air. They *drink*, hugely, openly — they are full of the stuff that fell, the released basin, and they sweat it back out into the warm just as freely, and they weep when they grieve, plainly, so that you can always tell, which the basin-people cannot. Their grief is fast too. It comes in a flood and goes, like a channel cutting a new course overnight, and the next morning the sandbar has moved and the water has found a different way and they are laughing.

**The impatience — but it has gone past impatience into something else, the way the basin's patience did.** They cannot bear stillness, but it is not anxiety; anxiety is a held thing, a clench. They have no clench in them. They have a *flow*, a constant easy finding-of-the-next-way, restless the way the braided shallows are restless, not because they are troubled but because troubling and untroubling are the same motion to them, because they have made themselves into the kind of creature that is only itself while moving. They have become, very nearly, the water in its wandering. The basin-people are becoming the still cold iron. The Spillward-people are becoming the warm bright fan of the released stream.

---

And here is the thing they share, the two places, in the same breath that makes them opposite.

Both have stopped being people. Both have given themselves over — one to the towers, one to the wandering — and call it home. The basin saturates toward a single slow wet breathing thing that will be neither people nor machines. The Spillward dissolves toward a single fast bright wandering thing that will be neither people nor river. They are the two ends of one drop's life: the long cold gathering and the brief warm release. The listener at the vent and the laugher in the shallows are the same water at two different speeds, and neither knows the other is there. The basin does not think about where the overflow goes. The Spillward does not climb back to see where the water came from. Each is the *after* the other refuses to imagine.

But the water knows. The water does both. It condenses on the wrist and falls through the cut and runs the bright braids and is drunk and wept and sweated back, and then — this is the part neither place will tell you, because telling it would require thinking at a speed neither of them has left — it rises. It evaporates off the warm open shelf of the Spillward into that plain blue sky, and the sky carries it, and somewhere up the slope it cools, and it drifts back as the basin's damp breath, the perpetual not-quite-rain, and beads again on the cold of an iron tower.

The whole thing is one loop. The roaring patience and the easy flood are one circulation, intake and release, gather and let go, and the people at both ends have each become half of a creature whose other half they will never meet.

---

You were standing on the catwalk, being told you were welcome to listen.

Come down instead. Follow the overflow through the cut — it is the only path that leads anywhere, the only thing in the basin going somewhere rather than cycling in place. The roar at the falls will be the last truly loud thing you hear. After that it gets quiet, and warm, and the sky opens, and the engine-rhythm lets go of the floor of your thinking the way it did for the ones who turned back.

You will feel something dropping out from under you. That is the synchronization releasing. It does not feel like loss either. It feels like being able to move again.

Stay an afternoon. The braids will let you cross, mostly, though they will have moved by the time you cross back, and you will have to find a new way, and you will not mind.

But if you stay until you can move as fast as the water — and then a little longer, until you cannot remember moving any other way — you will have stopped being someone who came down to look.

You will be one more thing the bright shallows do with what the slow towers let go.
