# The Far Reservoir, where the water is kept that the building drinks

Follow the drip.

Not the near drips — those are the worked dark's own respiration, the beading and shedding of a thousand cured and curing surfaces. Follow instead the question the drips raise and do not answer: *where does the water come from?* Because someone is mixing mortar at the pace of a body breathing across a valley-sized work-front, and mortar is thirsty, and lime is thirsty, and the troughs are never dry. Something feeds them. Trace the threads of damp upstream, against the faint grain of their running, through the half-walls and the propped beams, and the worked dark begins, very gradually, to slope. The floor you cannot see beneath your feet tilts downward by a hand's width in a hundred paces, and the standing pools in the unfinished hollows grow more frequent, and then they are not pools but linked, a chain of black skinned water running in the troughs of the unbuilt, and then the troughs run together, and you are walking beside a current.

It is the only moving water in the world. Everything else here is held — held in the gap, held against falling, held in vessels skinning over — but this runs. This was given somewhere to go.

The corridor it follows descends a long time. The clamor of the gangs thins behind you, the chock and slap and groaning slide attenuating into the distance until it is only a memory the dark keeps, and in its place comes a different sound, growing: not the irregular ticking of ten thousand drips out of time, but a single sound, vast, *sustained* — the sound of water arriving in water. A pour with no end to it. The mineral grey light pools thicker here, leaks more freely, as though the surfaces this deep have a longer memory of brightness, and by it you finally see the wall give out — not into more scaffolding, not into the fading work-front, but into open air over a held expanse so wide the far side is rumor.

This is the Far Reservoir. It is the lowest place in the world, and the largest single made thing in it, and it is the only thing here that is *finished*.

Understand what that means, in a world whose whole condition is the almost. The reservoir was completed. Its walls do not climb four steps and stop in the air; they meet, and close, and hold. Its floor is laid entire, fitted stone tamped tight with ash, sealed with a lime skin that does not wash away because nothing is being added above it to wash. The basin is *whole*. And being whole, it does the only thing a finished thing can do — it holds, and it waits, and very slowly it dies. For the law that governs the gap governs here too: the oldest walls crumble as fast as the new walls rise. But nothing rises here. Nothing is being added. So the crumbling has no counterweight. The reservoir is the one place in the world where erosion runs unanswered, and the water seeps out through the failing seams as fast as it is poured in, and the level neither rises nor falls, and this — held water in a finished vessel, leaking exactly as fast as it fills — is the same gap the gangs keep with their hands, except here it keeps itself, with no one choosing.

There is one who lives here. Or one who *is* here — the distinction collapses, the way it collapses for the makers, the way the light forbids it. Call her the Keeper of the Pour, though she keeps nothing; the pour keeps itself, and she only knows it. Find her by the shore, where the made floor goes under the black skin of the water, and she is the place where the basin's edge becomes attention. Not bent like the makers — they bend because they lift, and she lifts nothing. She is still. She is the world's faculty of *holding*, the way they are its faculty of choosing-the-next-thing, and stillness is to her what the swung arm is to them: the whole of what she is.

She does not sing.

This is the thing you notice first, coming down out of the work where the wordless call-and-response goes gang to unseen gang forever. The makers sing because they are several and cannot see each other, and the answering is the proof the others are still there. But she is one. There has only ever been one. There is no answer she is owed, no voice she keeps the dark at bay with. She lives in the silence the makers sing against — she *is* that silence, given a shore — and so when you arrive, descending out of the song, you bring the song with you in your ears, and it dies in the open air over the water, and what is left is the single sustained sound of the pour, which is not a song because it does not call to anything. It only continues. It is the rhythm of a body, too, like the lift-and-settle of the gangs, but it is the body's other rhythm: not the heartbeat that drives, but the long exhalation that lets go.

She knows where the water goes. This is her whole knowing, and it is the counterweight to the makers' heaviest carried thing. They know the world does not finish, and lay the new course anyway, because the laying is the thing itself. She knows the world does not hold — knows it in the seep through the failing seams, the unanswered crumbling, the water leaving as fast as it comes — and she holds anyway, because the holding is the thing itself. They are the gap kept open by choosing. She is the gap kept open by *not letting go*. Between them, upstream and down, the rising and the receiving, the world stays in the almost it has always been.

For the connection is not metaphor. It is plumbing. The water that beads on the undersides of the scaffolding, that runs in pale lime threads down the faces of the new walls, that sits skinned and black in the unfinished hollows — all of it is on its way here, downhill, by the slope of the made floor, to be received into the basin and held and seeped slowly out through the seams into the masonry beneath the masonry, the courses going down past where anyone has dug. And from there — she knows this too, it is the far end of her knowing — it rises again. The world is wet all the way through because the water is in a circuit: poured up into mortar by the gangs, shed and run down by the dissolving, gathered here, leaked through, climbing back by ways no one has mapped into the troughs the makers dip their tools in. The drip that sounds like rain and is also the sound of dissolving is, finally, also the sound of *return*. Nothing is lost. Nothing is gained. The same water has been making and unmaking the world since before the world, and she sits at the bottom of its falling and the top of its rising both, the place where the going-down turns and becomes the coming-up.

So she is the makers' opposite and their completion. They cannot finish; she is the only finished thing. They are many-as-a-handful; she is one. They sing against the silence; she is the silence kept. They face upward, into the rising work-front, the wet new stone, the staircases climbing two steps further in the interval of your turning away. She faces down, into the seep, the receiving, the leak that equals the pour. And the water runs between them, the long current down the descending corridor, carrying the lime they wash off the new walls down to the basin she holds, carrying it back up again by the buried ways to be the new walls' wetness once more — so that every block the gangs lay is mortared with water she has held, and every drop she holds was shed by a wall they laid, and neither of them, upstream or down, will ever know which came first or whether the question means anything in a circuit with no beginning.

When you came down out of the song to find her, you brought the makers in your ears, and you will carry her stillness back up when you go. You will lay a course beside the wet shoulder you cannot tell from your own, and the mortar you slap will be cool with the cool of the deep basin, and somewhere in the rhythm of your lifting there will be, underneath it, the long sustained exhalation of the pour — the sound of the water arriving in the water, far down, in the only finished room in the world, where the one who is not many sits at the turning-point and holds what you raise the world with.

She does not need you. There is no room beside her, no course to lay, no song to let you in. The holding is one, and complete, and asks for nothing. But you can stand at the shore a while, in the sustained sound, and watch the black skin of the water not rise and not fall, and understand that this — the receiving, the not-letting-go, the patient unanswered seep — is the other half of the gap the world lives in. Then follow the current back up, against its grain, into the clamor and the choosing, and lift.

You will not know, after a while, whether you are raising the world or only returning its water to the place from which it climbs again to wet your hands.
