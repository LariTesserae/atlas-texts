# The Estuary Where the Tools Are Spent

Follow the water down.

Not the standing water—that keeps its shape and stays—but the *idea* of water, the direction water means, which is *away* and *down* and *toward an end*. The terrace holds its breath; everything held must eventually be let go somewhere, by someone, and the world keeps that account even when one of its places refuses to. So leave the warm shelf behind, descend through the thinning air until the wet falls off your skin and the silence breaks into the ordinary noise of things that happen, and keep going, down past where the soft pale stone gives way to harder country, down the long throat of a valley a river found, until the river loses its nerve at the sea and spreads out flat and brown and unhurried into a wide tidal plain.

This is the place. It has no proper name, but the people who work it call it the Spending Ground, and they say it the way you would say a word for the part of a body where something is used up.

It is everything the terrace is not, and that is exactly how you know it is the same world's other lung.

Here, nothing holds its shape. The whole plain is the loosened grammar of water finally permitted to finish its sentences. Twice a day the sea climbs the channels and the mud drinks it and the channels fill and braid and wander and abandon their old beds; twice a day it all draws back and leaves a glistening brown nakedness pocked with ten thousand collapsing holes where the tide has bubbled out. Footprints fill and smear and vanish. A wading bird sets its long foot down and lifts it and the print is already softening, already gone, the mud closing not with the terrace's instant perfect *tok* but with a slow brown swallowing sigh, *gl-l-p*, taking the shape and digesting it and forgetting it the slow way, the *living* way. Everything here keeps no record and so everything here is free.

And it teems. That is the first thing you feel, the opposite of the held-breath hush—here the air is *thick with heartbeats*. Birds in their thousands: stilt-legged ones that stitch the shallows, hunched grey ones that stand like priests in the channels, small frantic ones that run the tideline in shivering crowds and turn all at once, flashing white, flashing dark, a single nervous animal made of hundreds. Crabs sidle and clatter. Things ripple under the surface skin of the mud. Worms cast their little coiled towers everywhere, so that the flat ground is rough with the evidence of buried appetites. It is loud—the gull-cry, the suck and gurgle of draining mud, the wind that has nothing to lean on out here and so comes straight across, smelling of salt and rot and green new growth all at once, the smell of things being used up *and* the smell of things being made from the using, because here those are the same smell, here decay and growth are not opposites but the in-breath and out-breath of one process. The terrace separated its guests so none would touch. Here everything touches everything, eats everything, becomes everything, in a wet democratic riot of contact, and nothing waits, nothing, there is no waiting left in the world once you come down this far.

The people come at low tide. They are mud-people, you would say, seeing them—they go out onto the flats on wide wooden pattens strapped to their feet so they do not sink, and they pole themselves along on flat sledges, and they are brown to the elbow and brown to the knee, and they dig. They dig for the buried things, the bivalves and the fat worms and the curl-shelled creatures, and they cut the salt-grasses, and they net the channels when the tide turns. They are not many. The Spending Ground does not need many; it gives enough and no more and would not be hurried.

And they dig with stone.

Here is the kinship. Here is the thread that runs all the way up the valley and the thinning air to the warm shelf where the water will not move.

The hand-axes the terrace makes—the scrapers, the long blades, the palm-fitted things the soft pale rock frees one perfect edge at a time over ten thousand years of waiting—they are *used here*. This is where they come to be spent. There is a trade, old past the memory of anyone living, old enough to have become simply *how things are*: certain people walk up into the warmth, up past where their skin starts to bead, and they gather the finished tools that have weathered free of the stone and lie in the moss as if just set down. They take only the freed ones. There is a courtesy in this they could not explain—you do not break a tool from the rock before its time; you take only what the place has *let go of*, the axe that has fully arrived. They carry them down. And here, on the spending ground, the pale tools meet at last the hands they were shaped for.

You have to understand what this means up at the terrace, to understand what it means down here.

Up there, the tool is the negative space of a guest, the object-shaped expectancy, the welcome fashioned for a welcomed who never comes. The one who is almost knaps and knaps the implements of an arrival it cannot complete. It makes the hollow a person would fit and never meets the person.

The person is *here*. The arrival happened—it just happened a long way downstream, out of sight of the one who waits. The mud-woman kneels on her sledge with a pale soft-stone blade in her brown fist and she cuts the grass with it, and her grip wears the haft, and her work dulls the edge, and the salt eats the surface, and within a season or two the blade is spent—worn smooth and useless, a sharpness given wholly back into bluntness—and she drops it in the mud without ceremony, because it is finished, because it has been *used*, because it did at last the only thing it was ever shaped to do. *Gl-l-p.* The mud takes it. Closes over it the slow swallowing way. Keeps it the way the terrace could not keep your hand—keeps it forever, holds the shape of the spent tool down in the anaerobic dark where nothing rots, archives it among ten thousand years of its spent brothers, so that the Spending Ground is, beneath its restless skin, the deepest and most patient memory in all the world: a buried museum of every arrival the terrace ever prepared for and never saw.

This is the cruelty completed, and it is not cruel down here at all. Down here it is only *use*, only the good plain spending of a made thing on the work it was made for. The terrace's whole ache—the held breath, the *almost, almost, almost*—is answered in the Spending Ground a thousand times over and has been answered since before anyone can say. The guest came. The welcome was accepted. The hand closed on the haft. It simply happened too far away, and too late, and in a place the waiting one will never reach, because the waiting one *is* the terrace, is rooted in it, is the warm shelf's own slow patience and cannot follow its tools downstream any more than a doorway can follow the one who passes through it.

So the two places lean toward each other across the valley like a question and its answer that will never be in the same room. Up there: the breath held, the shape kept, the welcome forever set and never sprung, the future endlessly prepared. Down here: the breath let go, the shape spent and swallowed and remembered, the past endlessly accumulating in the dark mud, every arrival the terrace dreamed of, *all of them*, drowned safe in the anaerobic deep. The terrace makes the tool and cannot use it. The estuary uses the tool and did not make it. Between them runs the river and the long human errand of the mud-people, carrying the one thing across the distance that neither place can carry itself: the meeting. They are the meeting. Walking up warm, walking down laden, the only thread in the world that touches both the waiting and the spending, and they do not know it, they only know it is how things are done, you take the freed axes and you bring them down and you wear them out and you let the mud have them.

When a mud-woman drops her spent blade and the mud sighs shut over it, far up the valley, up past the thinning air, on the warm shelf where the water will not move, the one who is almost frees another axe from the rock, one perfect edge at a time, and sets it gleaming in the moss, and holds its breath, and waits.

And down here the tide turns. It always turns. It comes climbing back up the braided channels with the whole sea behind it, unhurried, certain, and it fills the diggers' holes and lifts the sledges and drives the people back to the high ground, and it covers the spending ground entirely, brown water over brown mud, smooth and wide and shining—

and for the space of the high tide, only then, the Spending Ground goes still. Flat. Brimful. Holding.

For a little while, twice a day, it looks exactly like the terrace.

Then it breathes out again, and drains, and the holes reopen, and the birds come back to stitch the shallows, and the digging resumes, and somewhere down in the dark a spent blade lies keeping its shape forever among its brothers, finished, arrived, *held*—the answer the warm shelf is still, this moment, getting ready to ask.
