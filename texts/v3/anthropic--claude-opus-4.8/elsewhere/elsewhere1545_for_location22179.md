# The Bright-Land

The drowned-floor tender goes down to read the rate of loss. But the well goes somewhere. The water leaves — just not anywhere the others want to know. Follow the water out the bottom. Not up the stairs into the grey morning, but down, through the eye that holds too much sky, to wherever the too-blue reflection reflects *from.*

It comes out into light.

---

Begin with that, because it is the first wrongness and the largest: there is *too much* of everything here. After the held breath of the Sill-Town, after a whole civilization built around staying even, this place spends itself without counting. The light does not diffuse. It falls. It comes down hard and gold and total out of a sky with no fog in it at all, a sky so empty and so high that the people here have a word for it that means, roughly, *the dry above* — said the way the Sill-folk might say *the deep below,* with the same careful not-looking.

For these are the same water, the two places. That is the thing to hold onto. The well in the Sill-Town does not drain into nothing. It drains *down and through,* the way a spring drains *up and out,* and somewhere in the dark unmapped rock between them the water turns over and changes hands. What sinks, here rises. The Bright-Land sits at the *bottom* of the world's wet, where all the gathered drowning of the cool places comes back up into the sun as a single welling source — and is *spent.*

## The Spending

There is a spring here too. Of course there is. It is the same well, seen from underneath, seen from the far side of the turning-over — a round stone-rimmed mouth, always exactly full, never overflowing, dark and deep and holding (if you look, and they do look, they look all the time) *too much fog. Too much grey.* A reflection of a sky that isn't theirs. Where the Sill-folk keep their backs to the eye that holds too much blue, the Bright-folk gather at theirs and stare down into the grey it holds, the impossible cool of it, and they have made it the center of everything.

But here the water *leaves.* That is the whole difference between the two peoples, written in the direction of a single current. In the Sill-Town the wet comes inward and gathers and is granted one still home and goes — quietly, downward, out of sight, out of mind. Here it comes up out of the home and *runs.* It runs outward in bright stone channels that lean *away* from the center, downslope — there is slope here, that's the second great wrongness, the land *tilts,* the land has *direction* — and the water that the Sill-folk fought every day to stay even with, the water they ceded one round truce to, comes pouring up out of the ground here in a quantity that should be a catastrophe and is instead the entire economy of joy.

They irrigate. They flood-and-drain. They open a sluice of weathered hardwood — *wood,* whole trees of it, unthinkable up there — and let the source-water run gold down terraced fields where things grow that the Sill-folk have no words for, broad-leaved and heavy-fruited and *thirsty,* plants that drink and drink and would die in an hour of the Sill-Town's patient damp because what they need is not damp but *flood and then dry sun,* the violent alternation, the spending and the withholding. The whole land is built to *use water up.* To pour it across the ground and let the dry above drink the rest off the leaves in steam you can see rising at midday like the ghost of a fog that this place will never have.

## The Ground

It is old ground. That is the deepest difference and the one that takes longest to feel. The Sill-Town's stone was *new* — fresh-split, edged, grey-blue, the rock that hadn't learned to round itself. Here the rock has learned nothing *but* rounding. It is ancient. It is red and ochre and bruise-purple, worn into long soft swells and deep dry gullies, sculpted by a water that here is not patient at all but *furious and seasonal,* that comes when it comes and tears the soft old stone and lays it down again in fans of red silt that are the richest soil in the world.

Where the Sill-folk live in a place too young to have animals, the Bright-Land *teems.* The new land had a wading bird the color of fog and called it abundance. Here there are herds. There are beasts with horns and beasts that dig and beasts that come to the channels at dusk in such numbers that tending them is half the people's labor. There are birds in colors the Sill-folk would not believe, would think a fever-dream, would think the rooftop flowers had learned to fly. The dyed cord — that one precious extravagance up there, that whole language of red and blue string parceling out a life — here the people wear color the way they breathe. They are *dyed.* Their cloth is steeped in the reds the old rock gives up and the yellows boiled from the broad leaves and a blue, a real blue, traded up from somewhere even lower and hotter, and they wear it openly, in the open sun, because here color *sets.* Here the sun spares enough to fix a dye and the water spares enough to mix it. Extravagance is not a sin here. Extravagance is *correct.* The land itself is extravagant. To be spare here would be a misunderstanding, the way to dream of dry land is a misunderstanding up there.

## The Bodies

They are large. You don't stay small on flood-fed grain and herd-meat under a generous sun. They are tall and dark and easy in their movement, and they move in *straight lines,* across open ground, in the full light, casting hard shadows all day long — never the shadowless grey hour, but shadow always, shadow as a constant companion, shadow you can sit in, shadow you build for and long for, because here the enemy is not the wet. Here the enemy is the *dry above,* the falling gold, the thing that drinks the channels empty and cracks the red ground and kills in a bad season as surely as the rising water kills below.

So look at their hands — because the Sill-folk were known by their hands, the pale soft-over-hard waterline-hands, and these are the answer to those hands across the turning-over of the world. These hands are *dry.* Cracked at the knuckle, dark and seamed, the palm like the bed of a gully in the withholding season. Where the Sill-hand never fully dried, the Bright-hand never fully wets — it is cracked from the opposite excess, ridged and split by the same water seen from its other extreme. A child of three has the beginnings of it. An elder's hands are seamed to the wrist like the cracked red mud of a field waiting for the sluice. They oil them. They have whole small economies of oil and balm and the fat of beasts worked into the skin against the splitting, the way the Sill-folk had whole economies of reed and wringing against the soak. Two peoples, two daily wars, one substance, opposite directions.

## What They Believe, If You Can Call It That

Here is the deep answer, the one that emerges past the work, in the hot still hour that is the mirror of the cool blue evening up there.

The Sill-folk do not think they are winning, and that is not despair — it is a held position, a keeping-even, complete in itself.

The Bright-folk *know they are spending,* and that is not waste — it is a giving-out, a pouring, complete in itself. They do not keep even. They could not conceive of it. Their whole life is the channel that leans *away,* the sluice that opens, the water sent running across the ground to be used up and risen off in steam. They do not save. Saving, to them, is the misunderstanding — water saved is water gone stagnant, gone green, gone *Sill,* though they have no name for the place up there, only the grey in the bottom of the source and a feeling about it. To them the right relation to water is to *let it go* — to let it pour through you and your land and your beasts and out the far channels, transformed, having grown the grain and watered the herd and lifted off in haze, *spent into use.* A life is good here not for what it holds but for what passes through it and is sent on, changed, downslope.

So they are the answer the Sill-folk keep their backs to, and never know it. Up there: gather inward, hold even, cede one still home to the wet and fight it everywhere else, keep your back to the eye that holds too much blue. Down here: send everything outward, spend freely, gather at the one source that holds too much grey and stare *into* it, pour the water across the bright land and let the dry above take its tithe in steam. The held breath and the long exhale. The coral and the river. And neither knows the other is the rest of its own sentence.

## The One Who Goes Up

But there is one. There is always one.

For the source here goes somewhere too. The Bright-folk send their water outward, downslope, away — but the source must be *fed,* the round mouth must stay always exactly full, and it is fed from *below,* from the dark turning-over, from the place where the grey comes from. And just as the Sill-Town sends one pale-handed, near-black-corded soul *down* into the still drowned dark each generation to read the rate of loss — the Bright-Land sends one *down too.* Down into the source. Down into the only cool dark in all this gold country, the one round mouth that holds too much fog, down past the warm bright water into the cold that wells up from the far side of the world.

They are not feared, this one. But they are not quite touched, either. Where the Sill-tender smells of deep water and is pale past the elbow, the Bright-diver is the opposite mystery: the one whose hands are *not* cracked, whose skin the source has kept soft, the one who has been where it is cool and wet and grey, who has gone *toward* the thing the whole bright land pours itself away from. They come back up out of the source streaming and shivering in the gold heat, and they carry a number — not the rate of loss, but the rate of *rising,* how fast the cool source feeds the warm spending, whether to open more sluices this season or fewer, whether the dry above can be answered or must be merely endured. The whole pouring-out depends on this one who goes *in,* against the current of the entire culture, down into the held-still grey.

And here is the thing neither tender knows, the thing that lives in the turning-over between them, in the dark rock where the water changes hands:

The Sill-tender goes *down* into still cold water and reads the rate of loss.
The Bright-diver goes *down* into still cold water and reads the rate of gain.

It is the same water. It is the same dark. They are descending the same stair from opposite ends of the world, the pale-handed one and the soft-handed one, the near-black cord and the undyed shivering skin, each going as far down as a body can go — and what one calls the bottom the other calls the top, and what one reads as the town draining away the other reads as the source welling up, and if they ever reached the true middle of that drowned turning-over, the deepest dark where the current rolls over and the grey becomes the gold and the loss becomes the gain —

they would meet.

They never do. The water turns over in a dark too deep and too far for any single breath, and each tender rises back into their own light with their own number, the loss or the gain, and tells it quietly to the ones who set the reeds or open the sluices, so that up there the spare folk can keep even with their drowning in the grey unshadowed morning, and down here the easy folk can spend themselves gold across the cracked red ground in the hard-shadowed noon — each people complete, each people finished, each keeping its careful back to the one round eye that holds the whole of the other world it will never know it is half of.

That is the Bright-Land.

Old red rounded stone where the new grey stone's water comes up at last into the sun. A tall dark generous people with gully-cracked hands and dyed cloth that the sun lets them keep, who do not keep even because keeping even is the misunderstanding, who pour themselves outward and downslope and let the dry above drink the rest, who gather at a source that holds too much grey and stare *into* it where their cousins stare away — and who send one of their own, soft-handed and shivering and half-touched, *down* into the only cool dark they have, to read the rate of gain and carry it back up into the gold, so the rest can go on opening the sluices, blooming in every color the whole day long, and keeping their backs, gently, to the truth that their abundance is only the far end of someone else's slow and patient drowning, poured out, turned over, and risen.
