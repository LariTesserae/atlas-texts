The Tailrace Town

Follow the water down.

Everything that gathers on the height has to go somewhere, and water has only ever known one direction. The cloud that stands on the old whale-backs condenses, runs in film, gathers in channels, finds the iron pipes or finds the rock, and either way it descends — three kilometers of descending — and the descent ends. It always ends. It ends where the mountains stop being mountains at all and lie down into a plain, and the white burns off in the warmer air below the cloud-line, and the water that fell as standing sea arrives at last as a river.

The river is brown. Not dirty — *charged*. It carries the height down with it: dissolved iron from forty years of weeping pipes, fine mineral silt ground off the patient old summits, the tea-stain of ten thousand tons of moss decaying slowly in the cool. It comes down warm in patches and cold in patches, braided with the discharge of every turbine hall that still runs and every channel that never had a turbine at all, and where the warm threads and the cold threads run side by side in the same brown flow they do not mix for a long time. You can put one hand in cold and one in warm in the same river, an arm's width apart, and this is the first thing the town below learned to use.

## The Town

Call it the Tailrace, because that is what it is built on — the spent water, the *used* water, the water that has already turned every wheel it will ever turn and now has nothing left to do but arrive.

It is a low town. After the verticality of the range it is almost violently horizontal, sprawled along both banks of the brown river where the land flattens, built of the only two materials the height sends down in quantity: timber, floated down in the high water, and iron — salvaged iron, dragged down off the mountain in pieces, the burst pipes and the snapped catwalks and the cableway cars, everything the cloud forest swallowed and the people came back to take.

Because they did come back. This is the thing the height does not know. Up there, in the white, the machinery runs down untended and the frogs worship a lamp no hand still lights — but the hands did not vanish. They only came *down*. The generation that abandoned the turbine halls mid-breath did not stop existing; they retreated to where the air was warm enough to dry a shirt, where a body could be warm without crowding a machine, and they built the Tailrace on the wealth that still trickles down the wires.

For the wires came down too. That is the secret the lamp keeps. The string of lamps that still burns in the drowned hall, the single false moon the frogs gather to — it is not burning on nothing. It is on a circuit. And the circuit runs *down*, down the mountain on rotted poles and salvaged cable, most of it dead, most of it fallen, but not all. A few of the working turbines never had their lines cut. The power they make on momentum and rainfall, the power that organizes the frogs and grows the iron-mats — most of it dissipates into the cold and the wet, wasted, warming nothing but a few square meters of moss. But a thread of it survives the descent. A thin, unreliable, flickering thread of current that no one up high tends and no one down low generated, arriving in the Tailrace like an inheritance from a relative no one remembers.

The town runs on the dead industry's afterlife. Dim lamps, the same dim as the drowned moon above, gutter in the Tailrace windows at night — the *same current*, the same water-turned wheels, the lamp in the cloud and the lamp in the town two ends of one forgotten sentence. When a wheel seizes on the height and a warm outflow goes cold and the lamp-frogs freeze and fall — down here, in the same instant, a street goes dark. They do not know it is the frogs' extinction they are watching. They only know the light is unreliable, that it dims and surges and dies on a schedule no one can predict, and they have built their whole town around bracing for it, exactly the way the frogs built their hierarchy around the heat. The same turbulence. The same fixed shape thrashing inside.

## The Two-Temperature Trade

Here is what the Tailrace makes of its inheritance.

The river arrives braided — warm threads, cold threads, refusing to mix. The town learned this the way the soft-footed thing learned the lamp: by reading it, by building around it. They split the river. They channel the warm discharge — the spent water of the working turbines, still carrying the heat of the held cloud and the iron-blood of the weeping pipes — into long shallow basins on the south bank. And the cold, the fast-fallen channel water that never touched a wheel, they run into deep stone tanks on the north.

In the warm basins they grow. The same iron-mats that slick the discharge outflows three kilometers up — the red and orange and copper-green that the lamp-frogs cannot live without — they grow *here too*, deliberately, in cultivated sheets, because the people of the Tailrace learned what the lamp-frogs always knew: the iron-fed growth is food, or close enough. They harvest it, dry what little can be dried in this damp, ferment the rest. The town tastes of iron and of warm decay and of the high mountain's patience rendered down into something a body can eat. Every meal in the Tailrace is the Standing Sea, descended and concentrated and served warm.

And in the cold tanks they keep. There is no winter here to freeze a larder and no dry season to cure one, so the cold mountain-water is the only cold the Tailrace has, and they hoard it. The deep stone tanks are the town's memory — its stores, its surplus, the only thing standing between the people and the next time the light dims and the warm basins fail. Cold is wealth. A family's standing is measured in tank-depth. The river brought the cold down free, and the town built its whole sense of *security*, of having-enough, around water that fell as cloud on a summit none of them have ever climbed.

## The Ones Who Came Down, and the One Who Did Not

The Tailrace people remember the height the way you remember a parent's house: as the place everything came from, and the place you do not go back to.

They have stories about it. The turbine halls are sacred in the half-embarrassed way of things abandoned in shame — the great work, the wheels, the moment the generation before simply *walked down* and let the cloud take it. No one climbs to tend the machines. To climb would be to admit you could have tended them all along, and the town's whole self-understanding rests on the fiction that the height is *lost*, gone, beyond reach, a drowned country sending down its ghost-current and its iron-water and nothing more. They live on the inheritance and refuse the estate.

But the river runs both ways for one creature.

The soft-footed thing — the patient hunter that wears the cloud as a coat — does not stay always on the height. The young ones disperse. When the moss-world grows too crowded, when too many hunters read the same lamp as the same hunting map, the weakest are pushed to the margins, and the margin of the moss-world is the river, and the river goes *down*. They follow it. They come down out of the white, out of the cool that they wear as a blanket, into the warm flat country where their water-coat is suddenly a burden, where the held film that insulated them against three kilometers of cold now only cooks them slowly in the lowland heat.

Most die on the descent. This is the same arithmetic as the frog flung pool to pool by the wind — a rare, violent migration that the few survive and carry their old country into the new like an accent. But some arrive. Some thin, half-drowned, overheated hunters reach the Tailrace, having shed as much of their water-coat as they can, and they find the town's warm basins crawling with the same frogs and the same iron-mats they hunted in the cloud, transplanted, cultivated, *concentrated* — the larder of the height, restocked by human hands, laid out in the open.

The town calls them the come-down. They are not welcome and they are not killed. They are read, the way the town reads everything — the way the frogs on the height arranged themselves around the predator's feeding, the brave at the warm center, the weak at the cold rim. The Tailrace keeps its most precious cold tanks on the north bank, and its warm basins on the south, and the come-down hunters drawn out of the river by warmth and food prowl the south bank in the dark, taking from the basins, and so the town keeps its watch and its young and its surplus on the *north*, in the cold, behind the deep stone tanks — the cold that is wealth made also into the cold that is safety, because the hunter from the height cannot bear it any better than you could bear the white.

The same arrangement. The brave hold the warmth and the risk; the precious hold the cold and the safety; the hunter wears the cloud and reads the heat and takes from the edge. It organized the frogs around the lamp three kilometers up. It organizes the people around the river three kilometers down. Neither end planned it. The water came down carrying its temperatures and its iron and its thread of stolen current and its desperate migrant predators, and a town assembled itself around the descent the way a forest assembled itself around the exhale — braced, turbulent, replacing what the dimming light takes from it, mourning each dark street the way the height mourns each seizing wheel, and never once climbing back up to see the lamp that is the same lamp, burning at both ends of the one drowned circuit, tended by no one, indifferent, still warm.

The mountain breathes out water. The river carries it down. And gathered close around the warm basins and the inherited light, the people press in exactly as the frogs press in, and keep their watch against the thing that wore the cloud all the way down to find them, and do not climb, and do not stop.
