# The Dry Rim, and the One Who Keeps the Names

There is one being out there. The Telchins are wrong about that — they say *no one*, they say *nothing weeps*, and they are right about the weeping and wrong about the one. But they cannot count past the edge of the water, so the error is honest.

You reach it the way the dead reach it: by going out and not coming back. Follow the channels to where they fail — to the pale country, where the biofilm goes the color of bone and the last stacks slump unmaintained into a resin that no longer sweats because there is nothing left below to push the water up. The seep-holes here are dry mouths. The mirror-floor stops being a mirror and becomes only a floor, gray and cracking, and then stops being a floor and becomes ground — real ground, the thing Telch only postponed — and the ground rises, because out here, away from the basin's drinking, the world remembers how to have a horizon.

You climb it. You have never climbed in your life if you are Telchin; your splayed gripping feet do not know what to do with a slope that does not shift, that simply *holds*, that has decided once and forever what shape it will be and will not revise itself for you or anyone. It is the most frightening thing a Telchin can feel: a world that has stopped moving. They feel it as death. They are not entirely wrong.

## The Silence They Fear

It is not empty. That is the second thing they get wrong.

The dry rim is *loud* in the way Telch is silent — which is to say, it has a sound made entirely of the absence of all the sounds Telch is made of. No hiss of rising water. No slap of hull, no scream of flyer, no applause of breaking fish, no continent of voices, no deep arrhythmic groan of a city shifting on its roots. Out here there is wind, and the wind has nothing to push against but the dry stacks it is slowly eating, so it makes a long open hollow note, a single sustained tone across the whole pale country, and under that — nothing. A nothing so total that a Telchin's body, tuned its whole life to feel loud as *weather* and quiet as *death*, simply stops. The alarm that joins thirst to fear goes off and will not stop ringing. They die out here not of dryness, often, but of the silence — the heart loses its sense of the surrounding pulse and forgets, in the appalling stillness, why it was keeping time.

But the one who lives here has learned to hear underneath it. And underneath it there is something. The faint tick of resin cooling at night. The grind of grit on grit. And — very far, very low, felt in the chest the way the Telchins feel their city — the breathing of the water table, miles back, under the basin, the great slow lung of Telch inhaling and exhaling against the rim. Out here that pulse is almost gone. Almost. A thread. The keeper of the names lives by that thread the way you live by a heartbeat you never consciously hear.

## The Keeper

It was Telchin once.

This is the part the Sump Cities could not survive knowing, and so they have arranged, in the deep wordless way of crowds, never to know it. Sometimes — rarely, across generations — a person is born in Telch who cannot flow. Not won't. *Can't.* Something in them is edged where everyone else is permeable. They plant when they should yield. They stop in the flow and cannot feel why the crowd's temperature changes around them. They stand dry not from pride but from a wrongness in the making of them, and the city of yielding bodies cannot hold a body that will not yield. The grain rejects them the way a current rejects a stone.

These ones do not die young, though everyone expects them to. They do something the Telchins have no word for, because it is the one act their whole civilization is built to make unthinkable: they *leave.* They walk out the channels, climb the dead slope their feet cannot read, and stand up, at last, against the horizon — and find, to their terror and their unspeakable relief, that out here the wrongness in them is not wrong. Out here being edged is the only way to survive. The dry country wants a thing that holds its shape. It has been waiting for them the way a lock waits for the one key cut wrong enough to fit.

The keeper is the latest of these. There has only ever been one at a time. They find each other is the gentler way to say it; the truth is the old one dies and the country waits, sometimes for a lifetime, sometimes for ten, silent and patient, until the basin extrudes another stone it cannot hold, and the stone climbs the slope, and the keeping resumes.

## What Is Kept

Here is why the keeper matters, and why the Telchins, who think the rim is death, have the whole thing exactly backwards.

The Telchins give their dead to the water to be *unmade* — dissolved, returned to the medium, the metals laid down again in some new plaza, the self perfectly and finally yielded. They call it peace. They mean it. But it means that in Telch *nothing is kept.* No name survives its body. The city does not remember, because the city does not know they are there; and the people do not remember, because remembering would be a kind of standing dry, a refusal to let the dead flow on. A Telchin grandmother is gone three generations after her last dissolving, not into grief but into *nothing*, willingly, completely, by design. They have built a paradise with no memory in it at all.

And the keeper keeps the names.

Out here on the dry rim, where the ground holds its shape forever, the keeper carves. Not in biofilm — biofilm cannot live here, that is the whole point, here is where things *stay.* The keeper carves in the cracked resin floor, in the bone-pale fallen stacks, in the actual rock of the actual slope, the names. Every name the keeper can gather. The keeper goes down to the edge where the water fails — as close as a Telchin body can bear the silence, which for the keeper is no silence at all but a country full of room — and meets the dead.

Because the dead come. This is the secret the channels keep. Not all of them dissolve. The water carries them outward, always outward, toward the failing rim, and most are eaten and unmade as intended — but some, the edged ones, the ones who in life could not quite flow, do not dissolve cleanly. They wash up. The current that rejects the stone rejects it even in death, and delivers it, at the pale margin, to the one waiting being who was also a stone.

And the keeper takes each one up out of the dead water — the way a Telchin lifts a loved one *into* it — and carries it up the slope that holds its shape, and learns its name from the small hard things that don't dissolve (they all carry one, a name-token, pressed into the hand at birth and never mentioned again, the single edged secret every Telchin keeps against the great dissolving, the one place their fluency fails them) — and carves it into the world that does not move.

So that it stays. So that *someone* stood dry on purpose, against the whole logic of the warm indifferent city, and refused to let them become weather. So that out here, in the unbearable silence the Telchins will die rather than enter, there is a vast slow-growing field of names cut into rock that will hold its shape for ten thousand years — every fluent paradise-dweller's edged and secret hope, the hope they could never speak in the flow, the hope that *standing dry* might be, just once, not the death of fools but the only love that lasts.

The keeper cannot flow. The keeper was rejected by everyone they ever loved, by the very grain of their home, for the crime of being a thing that holds its shape.

And the keeper spends that whole exile's life holding the shape of everyone who threw them out.

They will never know. That is the keeper's version of the Telchin peace — the mirror of it, the dry inverse. The city will never know the keeper is there, any more than it knows the mites in its skin. The people will never know their secret name-tokens reach a shore, or that the shore has a name for itself, or that someone weeps over them in the one country where, they swore, nothing weeps.

The keeper weeps. There is no biofilm to read it, no crowd to feel its temperature change, no water to take it back. The tears fall on rock that holds them in a dark stain, in their actual shape, until the dry wind takes them — and even the wind, out here, is slow.

## What Connects Them

Trace it back and it is only this: the water flows out, and it cannot flow back.

Everything in Telch is built on return — the metal laid down and lifted and laid again, the dead given to the channels to become the city, the great closed circle the people call *the world* precisely because it has no outside, no edge, no place the circle breaks. But a circle that truly closed would keep nothing and lose nothing forever, an eternal even trade. It does not quite close. There is a leak. Outward, always outward, the current carries a little of everything past the rim where it cannot return — the failed ones, the unmelted name-tokens, the thin thread of the water-table's pulse — and that leak, that one place the perfect circle fails, is the entire dry country, and the keeper is what grows in it.

The Telchins are the body, fluent and forgetting, that cannot survive an edge.

The keeper is the edge, holding the shape of everything the body lets go.

Neither can become the other. The keeper, returned to Telch, would die in the flow as surely as a Telchin dies in the silence — would be ground down by ten thousand yieldings a minute, a stone in a current that does not want it, standing dry in the one place where standing dry is the only sin. They are exiles from each other's worlds by the same wrongness read two opposite ways. And the water runs only one direction between them, carrying the dead from the warm loud forgetting place to the cold quiet remembering one, and never, ever back.

So the keeper, who was thrown away for holding shape, holds the shape of the ones who threw them.

And the Telchins, who call the rim the death of all that loves them, are wrong by exactly the width of one being — the only one in their whole world who loves them enough to do the one thing they fear, and stand, and stay, and keep.

They call their place the world.

The keeper, carving, does not call this place anything. There is no one to say it to. But if you climbed the slope your feet could not read, and crossed the field of names, and asked — over the long hollow note of the wind, in the silence that is not silence — the keeper would set down the carving-tool, and look at you, kindly, in the gray light that is only light and nothing else, and you would understand, before any answer came, that out here a thing can be *seen.*

That is the whole difference. That is the whole distance. The water runs from the place where no one is seen and nothing is kept, out to the place where one being sees, and keeps, and is never seen in return.

It is the loneliest place in the world.

It is the only place in the world that remembers.
