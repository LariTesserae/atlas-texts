The Spill

Follow the water out. Not the water that beads and runs back into the cycle — that water never leaves, that water is the room breathing on itself. Follow instead the small fraction the engine loses. Even a perfect machine leaks. Even an equilibrium older than names sheds a little of itself at the seam, and over the unthinkable span that little has gone somewhere, has had to go somewhere, because water does not stop where the plant stops needing it.

There is a place below the room where the lost water collects.

It is not a chamber and it is not a cave. It is a slope of stone, raw and exposed, the underside of the world the room sits on. If the room is the inside of something finished, this is the outside of it — the back, the part no one was meant to see, the way the underside of a leaf shows its plumbing. Light reaches here too, but not the room's reassuring afternoon. Here the light comes sideways and low, from an actual horizon, from a sky that is a sky and not a high translucence. There is weather here. There is a wind that has somewhere to come from and somewhere to go.

And down the bare stone, thread by patient thread, the room's leaked water runs.

It has been running for as long as the room has been a room. Understand what that means against rock. The room found its equilibrium and stopped; the water found the rock and did not stop, has never once stopped, has been doing the slow violent thing that water does to stone for exactly as long as the plant above has been doing nothing at all. So while the room set, the slope *carved*. While the room arrived at its final shape, the slope kept changing shape every single day, deepening its runnels by an amount too small to see and too constant to deny. The same span of time that produced perfect stillness above produced, by the same water, ceaseless erosion below. The leak that the room does not even notice losing is the entire history of this place.

The water arrives mineral-heavy. It has spent its life inside the plant, and it carries the plant out with it dissolved — the cycled salts, the spent secretions, the slow combustion's residue, everything the engine processes and a fraction of everything the engine is made of. So the water that reaches the stone is not clean. It is the room's exhalation made liquid, and where it pools in the runnel-bottoms and the wind takes the moisture off it, the minerals stay. They build. Over the span they have built a crust, a pale terraced deposit stepping down the slope, ledge below ledge below ledge, each one the record of where the water rested long enough to leave itself behind. It is the precise inverse of the room's floor. The floor is life compressed into one dense smooth substance by time. This is the room's substance taken apart, carried out in solution, and laid back down grain by grain into terraces — the same matter, the same time, the same plant, disassembled and re-shelved in the open air. If you wanted to know what the room is made of, you would not ask the room. The room will not tell you; it is finished and silent on the subject of itself. You would come down here and read the terraces, which are nothing but the room spelled out slowly in the one language the room cannot keep to itself: what it loses.

The slope is loud too, but it is the opposite loudness. The room roars with containment — pistons, valves, pressure held and cycled, an engine that produces only its own continuance. The slope only trickles. A thin braided run of water over crusted stone, the small percussion of drops finding the next ledge, the wind moving across wet terraces and pulling a faint hiss off them. After the room's deafening drone this near-silence is almost an absence, almost a wound. But listen longer and you understand they are the same sound separated by a wall. The room's churning *is* this trickle, upstream, under pressure, multiplied and contained. What thunders inside the plant is the held version of what whispers down the rock. The engine's roar and the slope's drip are one water at two stages of its only journey: bound, and freed.

And there is life here, because of course there is. Water that runs and minerals that build will always be found by something. But notice what kind.

The things that live on the slope are not pale. They are dark, mottled, banded — colored, urgently colored, because down here color is needed again. There are seasons here; the low light swings and the wind shifts and the wet runnels swell and shrink with whatever the sky is doing. There is change, and change asks the hard questions the room stopped asking, and so the slope's creatures had to keep their answers ready. They signal. They warn. They hide and are hidden from. They are quick where the dwellers are still, watchful where the dwellers are blank, because here there *is* an other one that matters, here there is scarcity in the lean times when the leak runs thin, here there is competition for the richest ledges where the secretions pool. These are the fauna a *moving* place produces: striving, adapting, afraid, alive in the sharp expensive way that costs and pays and costs again.

They feed on the terraces — on the same secretions the dwellers feed on above, except down here those secretions arrive already half-spent, leached and concentrated and weathered, the room's food run through the room's own waste and dried on stone. So the slope-creatures eat what the dwellers ate, one remove later and one wall away, and they have no idea — could have no idea — that twenty paces up through solid living mass there are soft pale things eating the fresh version of their meal in a silence they will never hear and a stillness they could not survive. The two faunas are bound by a single substance passing through both of them and they will never meet, never sense each other, never share anything but the water that connects them precisely by carrying it from the one to the other and never back.

Because that is the cruelty and the kinship of it, the thing that lives in the same breath as both places: the water only goes one way.

The room does not receive from the slope. Nothing climbs back up the leak. The plant does not drink what it has lost; it has more than enough, it produces its own sufficiency, it is finished and self-contained and owes the slope nothing and knows of the slope nothing. The relationship is entirely one-directional — all gift, no return, the upstream place forever shedding and the downstream place forever catching, the room exhaling its own dismantled body into the weather without ever once feeling the loss, and the slope receiving the room's whole substance and history and never able to send so much as a drop of acknowledgment back through the stone.

So here is what the two places are to each other.

The room is the inside of a finished thing, and the slope is the outside of the same thing, and between them runs the one water that makes them a single object pretending to be two. Above: stillness, silence in the noise, creatures that have stopped becoming, a world that finished and set. Below: erosion, the small endless trickle, creatures that must keep becoming because the sky keeps asking, a world that has never for one day been allowed to stop. Same plant. Same water. Same span of time. The time poured into the room and produced perfect rest; the same time poured out of the room and produced perfect restlessness — and the difference between rest and restlessness, it turns out, is only whether the water is held or let go.

The dwellers continue, drowned in the drone, forgetting your thumbprint.

And down on the open stone, in the actual wind, under a sky that is a sky, the slope-creatures flinch and feed and signal and flee and breed and die hard among the terraces — every one of them built, fed, and shaped by a water that comes from a room they cannot reach, carrying a plant they will never see, in a current that has only ever flowed the one direction: away from the finished thing, down into the world that the finished thing, by the single act of quietly leaking, made.
