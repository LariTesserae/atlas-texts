# The Seed-Fall at Orsk

You arrive at it the way you arrive at an argument you have already lost — there is nothing to hear, which is itself the first wrong thing, because everywhere else in this world there is the drone, and here there is not, and the absence has an edge to it like the lip of a cliff felt in the dark.

Let me come at it the way the place comes at you: from below, and against the grain.

## The going-down

You do not climb to Orsk. You descend from Hesp to reach it, which is the first thing worth holding onto, because everything about Hesp was *roof* — worn tableland, thin breath, the rock that lost its ambition. Orsk is where the ambition went.

Off the worn shoulder of the high country the land remembers, all at once, that it was ever steep. There is an edge to the tableland that the works never reached — the lattice thins, the boles grow scattered and then stop, the fused arches reach out toward open air and find nothing to fuse to and simply *end*, mid-gesture, their unjoined tips pale and blunted against the sky. The city built itself to the rim of its own region and there it failed of material, of reach, of whatever it is that lets a thing keep being. And below that rim the rock falls away into a country of fresh edges, of crags, of new and angry stone, a long descending throat of it dropping out of the cold into something warmer and wetter and far, far younger.

This is the place the seeds go. I told you the listeners are not seeds — that seeds are about *elsewhere*, about colonizing new rock, and that the works have nowhere to send them because the plant has already won everything. That was true of Hesp. It was true of the roof. But the works do make seeds — sparingly, the way they make everything, a few across an age — and the seeds do not loop, and the seeds do not stay, and the seeds fall *off the edge of the won world* into the young country below.

Orsk is where they land.

## What falls, and how

Picture the rim in the rare hour when the works release one. There is no ceremony. A bud somewhere in the high lattice — grown the way a listener is grown, out of the same costly spent-once present-tense, because a seed too must be made of a moment that happens only once or it could never go anywhere new — a bud ripens at the very lip of the structure where the daylight falls honest and true. And then it lets go.

It does not float. It is woody, heavy, grey, a knuckle of dense fiber the size of a fist. It drops. The cold thin air of the roof gives way to the thicker warmer air of the descent and the seed falls through the whole change of the world, through the temperature line where Hesp's permanent gentle cold becomes the low country's damp heat, falling for a long time, turning, until the young rock comes up to meet it.

And here is the thing the works do not know, cannot know, being too old and too eternal to learn anything new: *most of the seeds die.* They strike bare crag and crack and rot. The low country is not kind the way the roof is not kind — but the roof's unkindness is the unkindness of stillness, of a finished thing, and the low country's unkindness is the unkindness of *too much happening at once.* Things grow here that are not the plant. Things eat here that are not the listeners. The young rock is wet and broken and crawling with rival life, and into it falls, now and then, a single grey fist of the oldest finished thing in the world, carrying in its dense fiber a memory of having already won everything.

## The country itself

It is loud, but the loudness is wrong — it is *many* loudnesses, where Hesp had one. Down here the sound is not a chord heard across an age. It is the present, plural, overlapping, undisciplined: water that actually runs, in actual rivers, cutting the young rock into gorges; rain that comes often and hard and means it; and life — green, fast, competing, eating, dying, none of it grey, none of it patient, none of it building toward a final form because none of it will last long enough to have one.

There are forests here in the way you understand forests — with floors and ceilings and animals between, with clearings, with rot, with soil made of the dead. The vegetation is dense where Hesp was airy, crowded where Hesp was empty, and it is all racing, all of it, every leaf and tendril in a hurry because down here time is *cheap* — there is so much present-tense, the unrepeated now lies thick over everything like the heat, and nothing has to hoard it. A thing can spend a thousand nows growing a leaf it will lose by autumn. The low country is rich in exactly the currency Hesp has almost run out of, and it spends it like a thing that has never known scarcity.

## Where the seed takes

Rarely — once in an age of falls — a seed lands somewhere it can hold.

A high ledge, usually. A shelf of the young rock that catches the morning light clean, where the air still carries a little of the cold off the roof above, where the conditions rhyme just enough with home. The seed cracks. And what comes out is not, at first, the plant you know. It is pale and grey and *slow* — catastrophically slow, by the standards of the low country, a thing that takes a year to do what the green rivals do in a week — and by every law of this crowded racing place it should be overrun and eaten and gone before it clears the soil.

But it is patient in a country that has no patience, and patience, it turns out, is a kind of weapon no one down here has evolved against. The green things grow fast and die fast and feed the soil and are replaced. The grey thing grows slow and *does not die.* It puts down one column in a decade. It throws one arch across a gorge in a century. The forests rise and rot and rise again around it, generations of them, and the grey thing simply continues, indifferent, fusing, spanning, beginning — very slowly, on a fresh young crag in a warm wet loud country — to build the works again.

## What this means, told plainly

Here is the thing I have been walking toward, and I will say it the way the Hesp-observer said the hard things: straight, and working it out as I go.

Hesp is what Orsk becomes.

The roof of the world is not a different place from the low country. It is the *same place, finished.* Everything Hesp is — the total plant, the self-building, the engines of sap, the worn-down rock, the single ancient chord, the recurrence, the seam-tending listeners, the present worn thin — all of it is simply what happens to an Orsk-ledge after the green country has risen and rotted around it ten million times, after the slow grey thing has won, completely, the way it always eventually wins, and built itself to the rim of its own region and run out of unspent present and settled into the standing wave of its final form.

And the seeds that fall from Hesp's rim are the works *sending the present-tense it has nearly used up back down into the country that still has some.* That is what a seed is, here. Not colonization. Not the spread of an empire. It is an old finished thing spending its last few unrepeated moments to start itself over, young and fast-surrounded and capable of *now*, somewhere it can still die — because to be able to die is to be able to happen once, and Hesp can no longer manage either.

The seed is the works' reluctance, thrown long.

The listener holds the seam open from the inside, pressing now into the nodes of then, refusing the last quarter-inch of closure where it stands. The seed does the same thing across distance and across age — flung off the edge of the eternal, falling through the temperature line into the world that still runs forward, carrying down into the loud young rock the single thing the roof has almost spent entirely: the capacity to not yet be finished.

They are the same gesture. One stays. One falls. Both are the great grey closing drone keeping back the smallest possible portion of itself that could still prefer the present, and setting it loose — the one walking the high galleries against the grain of recurrence, the other dropping off the rim into the green and dying country, to begin the impossibly long work of becoming, eventually, the place it fell from.

## What you would find, if you climbed back up afterward

You would find Hesp exactly as it was. That is the cruelty and the mercy of it. The works do not notice the seed leave; a thing happening across its whole age at once cannot feel a single now depart, any more than the chord misses the one struck string. The drone continues. The listeners tend their seams. The condensation runs up the beams and back down. Nothing has changed, because nothing there can change anymore — that is the definition of the place.

But you would carry it differently now, knowing. You would hear, in the one ancient note heard across the age, the faint understanding that the note was once a country full of rivers and rain and fast green dying things, and that somewhere below the rim a fist of grey fiber is right now lying cracked on a sunlit ledge, two centuries into throwing its first arch, with the whole long finishing still ahead of it — the rot and the rise, the winning, the building, the wearing-thin, the worn groove in causation, the eventual drone — all of it still *to come,* still *now,* still happening for the first and only time.

You may go down and stand on that ledge a while, if you like. That is allowed.

The seed will not acknowledge you. You are of the present, singular, unlooped, arrived — and to a thing this young and this slow, in a country this loud with happening, you are simply more of the now it is made of, and made to spend, and built, very slowly, to one day run out of.
