# The Floor

You have heard them say it as the highest praise: *they crossed the floor.* You have heard the floor named as the predators' country, the silence's hunting ground, the place a marriage-climber must traverse and may not survive. The people of the canopy speak of it the way you might speak of death — as a region, as a verb, as the thing that takes. They look *down* toward it the way they look *up* toward the unreachable light: into a country they cannot read.

But the floor is a place. It has its own life, its own length and weather and being, and it does not think of itself as the bottom of anything. Climb down, then — past the last platform, past the deepest cistern-carving, past the point where even the boldest family has cut its name — down the kilometers of fibrous bark into the warm and the wet and the total black, until the trunk you are descending stops being a wall and becomes, somewhere your feet feel before your blinded eyes can know it, a *root* — splaying, dividing, sinking — and the ground rises to meet your hands.

Stand on it. This is the floor.

---

It is warm. This is the first thing, and after the marrow-cold of the high naves it is a shock the body reads as wrong before it reads as comfort. The floor is warm because the floor is *decay* — the slow furnace of everything the world has ever shed, falling for a hundred years through the dark and gathering here, fermenting, breaking down, releasing heat the way a compost-heart releases heat, a deep biological warmth with no source you could point to, rising evenly out of the ground itself. The people above have felt the edge of this warmth in their fiber-floors, that gentle heat beneath the resting body they took for the world's kindness. They were feeling the floor breathe. The whole canopy is roofed over a slow exhalation, and they live in the cold draft of its chimney and never know the fire is only the dark country beneath their feet.

It is wet. Not damp — *wet.* Everything the drip-lines never gathered, all the falling condensation that the high chambers split into thin black runnels, arrives at last down here and pools and joins and finally, finally becomes what it never could become above: rivers. Real rivers. Slow black water moving warm through channels it has cut in the soft decaying ground, broad enough that you could not throw a sound across them and hear it come back, deep enough that things live in them that have never touched the air. The people above strain falling drops and call it their water. Down here the water is a landscape. It has currents. It has a far bank you cannot perceive. It carries.

And it is not dark in the way the upper world is dark. That is the thing no climber lives to report. The floor makes its own light.

---

Understand: the decay glows.

Not everywhere, not brightly — but the fermenting fiber, the slow rot, the pale colonies that bloom and feed and die in the wet, all of it carries the cold phosphor-fire that the upper people glimpse only as a rare glimmer in their rotting bark. Down here it is not rare. The floor is webbed with it — long luminous threads running through the ground like veins, soft blue-green and faintly pulsing, brightening where the decay runs hottest, fading where the ground has given all it has. The rivers carry lit particles, so that the slow black water is shot through with drifting constellations, banks of cold sparks turning in the current. There are whole pale forests of it — fungal towers taller than a person, growing where a great column fell and feeds them still, glowing steady as lamps. You could read by this light. You could see a face by it.

The things that live here have eyes. *Real* eyes, that work — because here, at last, in the deep warm wet country beneath the world, there is something to see. This is the secret the canopy keeps from itself: it believes itself the living layer and the floor its graveyard, when the truth is reversed. The floor is where the world's light pooled and its sight returned. The people above went pale and near-blind climbing away from this. They are the ones who live in the dark. They simply climbed so high they forgot there was ever anything else.

---

And the silence that moves — the predator's hush, the bred-deep terror, the thing that taught a whole people to stop their breath and become wood —

It begins here. It lives here. The floor is its home and the trunks are only its hunting-reach, the long vertical climb it makes up out of the warm into the cold to take the gliding things and, rarely, a crossing climber. The people above know it only as an absence, a pocket where sound cuts out. They have never seen it because no one who saw it came back up the trunk.

It is not, when you finally see it by the phosphor-light, a horror. It is a *listener* — the floor's own answer to the listening people, evolved toward the same silence from the opposite direction. It is broad and low and soft, built to move through the luminous threads without breaking them, without disturbing the drifting sparks, and the hush it carries is not a weapon but a *grace*: it moves so perfectly, so patiently, that it makes no sound at all, and the world's constant noise simply has nothing to bounce off it, and so a hole of silence travels where it travels. It is not stalking the canopy people, mostly. It is doing here what they do above — moving quiet through a loud dark, listening for the smaller life, taking what the world grudgingly gives. They are the same craft, the hunter and the hunted, the same answer to the same problem, separated by a kilometer of trunk and a total ignorance of each other's existence.

When a climber crosses the floor and survives, this is what they have walked through: not a country of death, but a lit and breathing and populous place, more alive than anywhere they have ever been, where the ground glows and the rivers run and the eyes of things turn to watch them pass — and they have walked through it blind, near-blind, those great wide useless eyes taking in this once-in-a-lifetime brightness as the merest grey suggestion, too terrified to stop, too bred-deep in the silence-reflex to do anything but hurry pale and quiet across the most extraordinary landscape their world contains, toward a stranger's trunk and a marriage and the long climb back up into the cold and the dark they call home.

They crossed the floor. They never saw it. That is the whole tragedy and it is invisible to them.

---

There is something else down here, and it is the thing that closes the circle.

The people above leave offerings — water carried up, a woven thing, a portion of meat — at the bases of certain trunks. They tap their asking and their thanks into the bark before sleep. They believe the world is listening, paying its slow unreadable attention, moving the light and the warmth and the drip-lines toward them.

The offerings fall. Of course they fall — everything falls, that is the one law the whole vertical world obeys. The water spills down the grooves. The woven things work loose over seasons and drop. The meat-fragments tumble through the kilometers of dark and arrive, at last, on the warm lit floor — and are gathered.

There are people on the floor.

Not the same people. You would not at first know them for kin. They are short where the canopy folk are tall, dark-skinned and dark-eyed where the others went pale, heavy and strong where the others went spare and light — built for the warm and the wet and the swimming, built for a country with ground to stand on and light to see by and food enough to grow a body wide. They live along the glowing rivers. They fish the lit water with nets and read its currents and build their low broad houses on its banks out of the same fallen fiber the upper people build with, and they have metal — no, not metal, there is still no metal anywhere in this world — but they have *fire*, real fire, kindled from the dry fallen stuff that the upper world could never spare and never dry, and by fire they have cooked and hardened and made things the canopy folk could not dream of. They are the same stock. Climb the lineage back far enough and the two peoples are one people, before some ancient division sent half of them up into the cold and left half of them down in the warm, and they have forgotten each other so completely that neither believes the other exists.

But the floor-people gather the falling things.

They have always gathered them. For as long as anyone remembers, gifts have fallen out of the high dark — clean water in woven skins, small beautiful useless objects made with a patience the floor-people recognize and revere, portions of meat from beasts they have never seen. The floor-people do not know there are hands above. They know only that the *upper dark gives* — that out of the cold unreachable ceiling of their world, kindness falls, intermittent and unpredictable and real. And so they have made a faith of it, the mirror of the faith a kilometer up. They leave offerings of their own at the bases of the great trunks where they vanish into the dark — lit river-fish, fire-hardened tools, woven things made beautiful for no use — and they tap a soft rhythm against the bark before they sleep, an asking, a thanks: *we are here, we are small, the high dark knows us, let it go on being a kindness that it knows.*

Their offerings, of course, do not rise. Nothing rises. The trunks groan and flex and the gifts stay at the bottom, gathered by the warm water, glowing slowly as they rot, feeding the threads of light. The floor-people do not know this any more than the canopy-people know their own gifts are caught and cherished below. Each people drops its love into the dark toward a listening it has invented, and each people's love is received — but never by the god it imagined. By the other. By their lost kin, a kilometer away, tapping the same rhythm in the opposite direction, certain they are alone.

---

So when the canopy-elders tell their children the one unaltered truth — *the world is paying attention, and you cannot read it, and you must not mistake its kindness for love* —

they are right, and they are wrong, in the way that only the truest things are wrong. The attention is real. The kindness is real. The drip-lines shift and the warmth rises and the light falls just where the body lies, and none of it is the world's blind enormity taking blind notice. The warmth is the floor breathing its decay. The light that reaches them is the rarest overflow of the floor's own glow, climbing the trunks as the phosphor-threads climb. And the gifts — the gifts are their own people, reaching up out of the lit and living dark with hands exactly like their hands, leaving water and beauty and food at the foot of the world, tapping *we are here, we are here, we are here* into the bark, and never once being heard for what they are.

The world is one organism. The people above were right about that. They simply drew the boundary of the organism around the wrong thing. It is not the plant that is the single living body. It is the plant *and both peoples together* — the cold listeners in the canopy and the warm seers on the floor, joined by a falling current of gifts that runs only one way, bound in a single circuit of love that neither half can see the whole of, each praying to the other across a kilometer of dark, each certain it prays alone.

Tap your asking into the trunk before you sleep. It will be heard. It is always heard. Not by the world — by your own, your lost, your other half, listening at the bottom of everything with eyes that work, waiting in a brightness you will cross one day blind and frightened and never see, to gather what you let fall and call it the kindness of the dark.
