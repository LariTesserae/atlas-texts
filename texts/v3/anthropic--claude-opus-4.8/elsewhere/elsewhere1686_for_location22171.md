# The Long Path, and the House at Its Foot

You stood. You touched the top stone. The shelf released you, because release is the only thing it knows how to give. And then there was the long path—the *long safe path*, the climbers always called it, with that small grim relief in the word *safe*, as though safety were a place and not merely a direction. Down. Always and only down.

So follow it. Follow the people backward, the way you read them backward from their leavings, all the way to where the path runs out. Because a path that goes up from somewhere goes down to somewhere too, and the ones who carried iron up Vetch-Wend on their own backs did not forge it on the wind. There is a house at the foot of the path. There has to be. The trough proves it. The trough is a letter, and a letter implies a desk where it was written.

## Hearthwell

That is the name, and it is an honest one, which is rare. The place is called Hearthwell, and it has a hearth, and it has a well, and between those two facts almost everything about it can be deduced—which is the precise inverse of Vetch-Wend, where everything had to be felt and nothing could be deduced at all.

It sits in the bottom of the world. Not *a* valley—*the* valley, the lowest fold the land makes before it tilts up toward the mountain whose flank holds the shelf. Down here the world does not thin. It thickens. It crowds. Everything the shelf forbade, Hearthwell permits and then insists upon: trees lean on trees, the alder and the wet-footed willow tangling at the streamside, and stones lean on stones in the field-walls, and the walls lean on the lanes, and the lanes lean on the houses, and the houses—there are nine of them—lean, frankly, on each other. Where Vetch-Wend spaced its presences across cold gulfs so that each became enormous in the attention, Hearthwell packs its presences rind to rind so that no single one can be attended to at all. You cannot see the rowan here. There are forty rowans here and a hundred other trees and you see *woods*, a green collective mutter, and not one tree in it has a held breath around it because there is no breath to spare. It is all exhaled at once, warm and close and used.

## The Well

Start with the well, because the well is the other end of the trough.

It stands in the middle of the nine houses, in the packed-earth common where the lanes converge, and it is *the* reason Hearthwell is where it is and not a quarter-mile east in better sun. Water lies just under everything here too—but where the shelf wept its water out helplessly through every hairline crack, here the water is *held*. Caught. Brought up by the bucket and the windlass and the rope gone furred and dark, and that single architectural fact reverses the entire moral weather of the place. Up on the shelf, water was given without asking and the trough was a gift made for strangers. Down here, water is *worked for*, hauled hand over hand, and so it is shared—but shared the way crowded people share, with a tally kept in nobody's ledger and everybody's memory, with the small frictions of who left the bucket on the wrong hook and whose turn it is to grease the windlass before the frost.

This is the thing about Hearthwell that the shelf could never have: it has *neighbors*. The shelf had passers, separated by years, none of whom shared a fire. Hearthwell shares one fire, more or less, the way a body shares one warmth among its organs. The nine houses are not nine solitudes. They are one animal with nine rooms, and the well is its wet heart, and the people who draw from it have known each other so long and so completely that knowing has worn smooth, like the chisel-grooves in the high trough, like the cairn-stone under generations of thumbs. They do not marvel at each other. You cannot marvel at what you cannot put a gulf around.

And here is the first thread that runs all the way up: the trough on the shelf was cut by someone from *here*. It must have been. Somebody carried iron up the long path on their own back, and that iron was forged at the Hearthwell hearth, in the smoke and the crowding and the company—and then carried up into the thinning world, into the held breath and the cold gulfs, and used once, for strangers, and abandoned. Think about what that journey is. A person leaves the one warm animal of the valley, climbs out of company into solitude, performs a single act of grace for someone they will never meet, and comes back down into the mutter and the lean and the shared bucket. The trough is Hearthwell's hand reaching up out of its crowded warmth to touch the cold edge of the world, and then withdrawing. The whole upward fidelity of the passers—*I choose the height, I do not choose the level water*—was funded from below, from the hearth, by people who had a fire to come back to and therefore could afford to climb toward a thing that had none.

## The Hearth

Now the hearth, which is the other half of the name and the deeper half.

There is one great hearth in Hearthwell, in the largest of the nine houses, the long low one with the turf roof gone shaggy and the door that has not fully closed in forty years because the frame has settled and nobody minds. This is where the iron was worked, in the cold seasons—not a true forge, nothing so grand, just a good deep hearth and a bellows and a stubborn old anvil that came from somewhere nobody now remembers, carried in, like everything here, on someone's back. The fire is the opposite number of the cloud-sea, and you should hold the two of them in your mind at once because the whole world hangs between them.

The cloud-sea below the shelf's lip was *level, patient, and never moved with the wind.* It lay flat and gave back a half-beat echo of everything above it, and things fell into it and did not come out, and it was made of attention and dread and the terrible flatness of a surface that keeps account. The hearth-fire is everything the cloud is not. It is never level—it leaps, it gutters, it answers every draft from the unclosed door, it eats and is eaten exactly like the moss-continent of the shelf, frantic with appetite at the small scale. It gives back no echo; it gives back *heat*, immediate, asymmetrical, generous, the same to everyone in the room and gone the instant you step out into the lane. The cloud held a copy of you a half-beat late. The fire holds nothing of you at all and warms you anyway. That is the difference between the bottom of the world and its edge: at the edge, the world remembers you with a horrible exactness and will not let you go; at the bottom, the world forgets you constantly and keeps you warm regardless. One is dread. The other is home. They are made of the same water—the seep that weeps from the shelf and the well that is hauled in the valley are the same water, the mountain's one slow exhalation, caught at two heights—but at the top it lies flat and keeps account, and at the bottom it is boiled in a black kettle over the leaping fire and poured out and drunk and forgotten and poured again.

*If there were a kettle,* you said of the shelf, *if the ground would let you walk straight.* There is a kettle here. There is always a kettle here. It is on the fire now, and the ground lets you walk straight to it, more or less, between the leaning houses.

## The People Who Stay

So now, at last, the question the shelf could not answer in its own terms: *who lives here?*

Everyone. That is the whole shock of it after Vetch-Wend. People *live* here, plainly, unmysteriously, in the most ordinary and total sense—they are born in the long house and they draw from the well and they grow old in the company and they die and are put into the wet ground a little way up the valley where the soil is deep enough to take them, which it never was up on the shelf, where time had not yet made soil and the dead could not be buried, only carried back down. *That* is why no one ever lived on the shelf and everyone lives here: the shelf could not receive a body into the ground, and Hearthwell can. A place where you can be buried is a place where you can live. The two facts are the same fact.

They are the people of the slow climates, the same stock as the passers because they *are* the passers, before and after the climb. The wool that takes the damp and holds it—it is woven here, in the long house, on a loom that clacks against the hearth-roar the way the thrush's hammering clacked against the water-roar. The patience of the wet cold, the folding of oneself smaller inside the weather and waiting—that is learned here first, as a child, in the crowding, before it is ever practiced up in the gulfs.

And here is the thing that took me until now to see, descending: *they do not all climb.* The shelf made it seem as though the human relation to Vetch-Wend was universal, that every person stood eventually at the lip and felt the question open in the chest. But that was the shelf's vanity, the vanity of any edge that thinks it is the center of the world. Most of the people of Hearthwell never go up at all. They live their whole lives at the bottom, in the warm crowded forgetting, and never once stand undecided in the unwalled room, and they are not lesser for it. The well is enough world. The nine houses are enough world. The thrush and the kettle and the windlass that wants greasing—enough.

Only a few ever climb. There is always one, in each generation, maybe two—the one the others can never quite explain, the one who looks up the valley too often, who goes quiet at the wrong times, who has too much breath to spare and keeps spending it on the held silence between things. The valley has a soft word for them, half-fond and half-wary, the way you'd speak of someone standing too near deep water. *Wend-footed,* they say. The wend-footed one. The one who will, sooner or later, shoulder a load and start up the long safe path—not to leave, never to leave, because Hearthwell is not the kind of animal you can leave, it grows you and keeps you—but to go up and *touch the edge,* and cut a trough, or mend the half-collapsed wall, or lay a fresh stone on the cairn, or simply kneel at the lip in the quiet dawn hour and hear their own breath come back a half-beat late and learn, bodily, why one chooses the height.

And then come down. *Always* come down. The fidelity of the passers, the unbroken record of restraint that was the truest thing the people ever made up on the shelf—that fidelity is not a discipline of the edge. It is a discipline of the *hearth.* It is the wend-footed ones knowing, all the way up in the thinning gulfs, that there is a fire below them with their name worn smooth into its company, a bucket that is their turn to draw, a body's worth of warmth shaped exactly to their absence and waiting to be filled again. *That* is what turns them on the lip. Not wisdom. Not dread. Love, and the simple downward pull of being missed. They do not step off the level water because the leaping water is on, in the black kettle, and someone has set out the second cup.

## The Half-Beat Below

There is one last thing, and it belongs to both places at once, and I did not understand it until I had stood at the bottom long enough to hear the valley's own quiet hour.

Hearthwell has a dawn too—the one still hour before the windlass starts and the loom clacks and the nine houses exhale their morning smoke. And in that hour, if you stand in the common by the well and hold very still, you can hear the mountain. Not the fall, not the wind in the high channels—those are too far up to carry. But something. A faint flat patience up there under the cloud-deck, a held breath at the edge of the world, the sound of a place that keeps account.

The shelf hears the valley a half-beat late, echoed up from below its lip. And the valley hears the shelf, echoed down from above its rooftops. Each is the other's quiet hour. Each is the half-beat the other is missing. The cloud-sea lapping the shelf and the woodsmoke pooling in the valley-bottom are the same membrane seen from its two sides—and the wend-footed one, climbing, is the only creature who passes through it in the flesh and comes back, the chough among the people, falling off the lip of the warm world into the thin one and rising changed, a hundred heartbeats along, never from the place they entered.

The kettle sings. The well-rope drips. Someone has set out the second cup, and is not yet worried, because the wend-footed always come back, and have, every one of them, across all the long years.

For as long as you keep climbing, you live at the edge.

For as long as you come home, you live here.
