# The Quick Market at Tessom Wash

Come down. Not gently — the mountains do not allow gentle. The young peaks give way to older ones, rounder, then to foothills that have had time to become themselves, and then to the broad braided floodplain where every river the mountains ever shed has come to argue. This is Tessom Wash, and it is the opposite of altitude in every way that matters. It is low. It is loud. And it is wet the way the Heights are dry — drowned in water that arrives all at once and from elsewhere, water that was somebody else's weather first.

That is the first connection, and the Quick Market does not know it. The slow exhalation of the foundries, the fog drunk and rebreathed and drunk again on the unfinished peak — some of it escapes the loop. On the rare hour when a cloud snags the summit and unloads and the gutters race, the overflow does not all return to the cisterns. A little runs off the last terrace, off the lip of the question the Slow Hands will not stand near, and goes down. It joins the snowmelt and the rainfall of a hundred peaks and becomes a river, and the river loses its name a dozen times on the way down, and arrives here at Tessom Wash as part of the brown spring flood that the market is built to outrun. The traders here have drunk the breath of the Smelting Heights without knowing there is a Smelting Heights. They have no word for what stands at the top of their water.

## The Wash

Picture a wide flat scar in the land, miles across, where the river is not one river but forty — channels that braid and split and rejoin around long pale gravel bars, shifting their courses from one season to the next so that no map drawn in spring is true by autumn. The water is the color of milky tea, heavy with the ground-up youth of the mountains, and it moves *fast*, with the flat brown urgency of water that has somewhere to be. There is no still water in Tessom Wash. There is current, and there is the brief slack behind a gravel bar, and that is all.

And on the gravel bars, on the shifting islands, on pilings and pontoons and great lashed rafts — the market. It is built to move. Every structure floats or stands on legs that can be pulled, and the whole settlement migrates with the river's mood, follows the deep channels, abandons a bar the day before the flood takes it and reassembles on the new bar the channel just made. Nothing here is bolted to anything. Nothing here intends to stay. This is the second connection, and it is an inversion: the Slow Hands build forever and never finish out of reverence for a world that is always becoming; the people of Tessom Wash build *nothing to last* out of submission to a world that is always *moving*. The same refusal of permanence — but one is a held breath and the other is a sprint.

## Speed

Everything here is fast. The water sets the tempo and the water does not wait. A trade is struck in the time it takes a raft to drift past a vendor's float — you call, you counter, you throw the goods across the gap and catch the payment, and by then the current has carried you on and the next deal is already shouting. The market language has compressed itself to fit: clipped, vowel-swallowing, half of it hand-signs flashed across water too loud to shout over. A whole bargain in four gestures. To linger is to be swept past your buyer. To consider, to weigh, to let a question sit — that is how you starve here. The slow die at Tessom Wash. They are carried away.

The people are quick the way the Slow Hands are slow, and in the same total way — it is in the body. Their hearts run fast and high. They are lean and small and light, built to leap from raft to bar to pontoon without thinking, to read a current the way you read a face, to know in the gut a half-second before the eye that *this* channel is going to shift and *that* float is about to be taken. Their hands are quick and narrow, not for holding heat but for catching things thrown — coin, fruit, knife, rope-end — out of the air over fast water. They sleep little and lightly. The flood comes when it comes, often in the dark, and the ones who sleep deep wake up downstream or do not wake.

## What moves through

It is a market, so the question is what passes through it, and the answer is *everything, briefly*. Nothing stays at Tessom Wash, including the goods, including the people. It is a place defined by throughput. Fruit comes down from the warm low valleys, glass and salt and cured things come up from the coast on the poled barges that fight the current, livestock crosses at the fords, gossip and debt and disease all move through at the speed of the river. The market does not produce. It *passes things along*. In this it is the strange mirror of the Smelting Heights, which produces and passes nothing along, the closed slow loop drinking its own thirst. The Heights extract and keep and circulate inward forever. The Wash keeps nothing and circulates everything outward, fast. Neither makes anything that lasts. One out of reverence. One out of velocity.

And among the goods, sometimes — rarely, prized, mysterious — there is *metal*. Strange warm-toned metal in odd half-finished forms, ingots that never quite settled into one alloy, brought down by traders who got it from traders who got it from somewhere up in the dead high country where nobody goes. It commands a fierce price. The smiths of the coast covet it; they say it works unlike any ore, that it holds an edge that no stable metal holds, that it is *alive* somehow in the forge, still deciding what it wants to be. The traders of Tessom Wash do not know it is the harvest of the last Slow Hands — metal drawn up out of an unfinished mountain by patient tarnished people who do not sell it, who lay it carefully back into their endless provisional city for no purpose but to continue. So how does it come down?

It falls. The third connection, and the saddest. When a Slow Hand finishes — lies down on the warm deck for the one completion the doctrine allows and tarnishes all the way through — the slow seasons take the structure he tended. The capped pipe he never extended corrodes at the joint. The terrace gives. A whole laden section of the endless city slides off the unfinished mountain and goes down the slopes, down the watercourse, broken and tumbled, ground smaller by the brown urgent water, until pieces of it fetch up on a gravel bar at Tessom Wash and a quick-handed child fishes a warm strange ingot out of the slack behind the bar and runs it to her mother to sell. The Heights do not know they are feeding the Wash. The Wash does not know it is being fed by the dead. The water carries the breath down and the water carries the metal down and the two places are joined by a current neither has ever traced to its source.

## The creatures, again

There is one more thread, and it runs the wrong way, upstream, against everything.

In the slack behind the gravel bars, where the current rests, the people of Tessom Wash sometimes see a creature they have no name for, and do not like. Pale, leathery, slow — *grotesquely* slow, to them, slow the way the dead are slow — it comes down the cold deep channels from somewhere far above and lies in the slack water and seems to *drink the wrong things*, to lick the tea-brown silt, to seek out the rare gleam of dissolved metal in the flood. It is too slow for this place. The current ought to take it and does not; it holds its ground in the fast water with a patience that unsettles the quick-hearted, who distrust anything that does not hurry. They drive it off. They throw gravel. It withdraws, unbothered, with the maddening leisure of something that has never once in its life needed to be anywhere by a certain time, and it goes back up the cold channel, up toward the dead high country, up toward the warm interior of an unfinished mountain where, in shallow copper dishes, something is being left out for it on purpose.

The people of Tessom Wash drive away the very thing the Slow Hands feed by hand. To one people it is a hand reaching out of the world's own act of becoming, the inheritor, the tender first draft of a life that will keep the high quiet places after the last patient man has lain down. To the other it is just a slow ugly animal in the wrong water, an offense against the only virtue the floodplain knows, which is speed. The creature does not care. It drinks where it drinks. It is the only thing in either world that has traveled the whole distance, all the way up the cold seam from the loud fast drowning market to the silent dry kiln at the top, and back, and up again — the one body that belongs to both places, carrying nothing, knowing nothing, simply going, at the one tempo that suits it, between the people who build forever and finish nothing and the people who build nothing and finish everything in an afternoon.

And the flood comes down in the dark, and the market wakes and runs and rebuilds on the bar the river made overnight, light and loud and already counting the morning's first quick trade. And far up the water nobody here will ever climb, a drop falls into a catch-basin, and a tarnished hand lifts a rivet, and considers it, and does not yet set it down.

There is no time. There is all the time there is. The river runs between them, carrying breath one way and metal one way and the slow pale creature both ways, and joining, in the only manner two such places can ever be joined — without either of them knowing it has been joined at all.
