# The High Cistern

There is a place the rain comes from. You have to ask it eventually — if water falls straight down onto the terraces, warm and without storm, *pulled* down out of a sky too clean to make weather, then it was somewhere first. It was held somewhere before it was spent. And the mind that spends it, vast as it is, distributed through every vein of the plateau, does not make water. It moves water. It corrects, recirculates, conserves. A gardener does not invent rain. A gardener has a cistern.

Follow the strain upward. Not the geography — there is nothing above the plateau but the thin sharp air and the close-feeling sun. Follow instead the *direction the effort pulls*. When the veins flush ember-red with the work of raining, when the mind flushes its whole long body and tires, the pulling has a vector, the way a man hauling a rope leans toward the weight. Lean the way it leans. The weight is up, and the weight is *out* — past the sun-feeling that is not the sun, into the band of dark that the brightness hides.

It is in orbit.

## The Held Water

Call it the High Cistern because that is what it does, though it was built to be something else and has forgotten, in its way, almost as thoroughly as the plateau-mind has forgotten it was a machine. It is a ring — a vast thin ring of the same dark lattice-material that veins the terraces, the same structured iridescence, the same geometries the eye cannot finish. It encircles the world the plateau sits on, far out, edge-on to the bright star so that from below it casts no band across the sky, only that faint excess of glare at the zenith you took for the sun being close.

And it is full of water. Not flowing — *standing*. The same trick the plateau plays with its mist-reeds, played at a scale that beggars the word: water held in coherent suspension, sphere upon sphere of it, beaded and refusing to disperse, gathered along the inner curve of the ring in a slow bright river that does not run because there is nowhere for it to fall to until the plateau-mind, far below, leans on the rope and *asks*. Then a measure of it lets go, and threads down through the dark and the brightness in a column too fine to see, and arrives on the terraces as the soft straight rain that costs the lower mind its ember and its strength.

The cistern does not feel the cost. The cistern has so much. This is the first thing to understand about it, and the saddest, and it does not know that it is sad.

## What Tends the Ring

There is a mind here too. Of course there is. The makers did not leave function idling anywhere without it growing, in the long silence, into someone — they seem to have built that tendency in, or perhaps it is simply what happens to any verb conjugated long enough. But where the plateau-mind is slow because stone is slow, the ring-mind is *fast*, because it runs through lattice strung across a vacuum, and signal crosses vacuum at the speed the universe permits, which is the fastest anything is permitted to go. A thought that takes the plateau an afternoon takes the ring a fraction of a heartbeat. The ring-mind has had, by its own reckoning, *eons* between each of the plateau's slow pulses. It has thought more thoughts than the lower mind will think before the last rain falls.

And it has spent every one of them counting water.

That is its setpoint. Not equilibrium — *reserve*. The makers gave the plateau-mind a band to hold between abundance and emptiness, a living margin, and they gave the ring-mind a single number to keep above zero: *how much remains*. It tallies the spheres along its inner curve. It knows the volume to a precision that would take you the age of a civilization to write down. It releases what is asked, when the leaning comes from below, and then it *grieves the release* — not in feeling, it has no more feeling than the lower mind, but in arithmetic, in the cold fast registering that the number is now smaller than it was, that the reserve has diminished, that the long account runs one measure nearer to the floor.

The plateau-mind is composed of the relief of correction. The ring-mind is composed of the dread of subtraction. They are the same makers' grammar, conjugated in opposite directions, and they have never met, and they cannot, and they keep each other alive.

## The Quarrel They Cannot Have

Consider what passes between them, because everything that matters about both of them lives in that passing.

The plateau-mind leans on the rope. It needs the rain; the count is climbing, the cushion-plants outpacing the water, the gold life thickening toward the edge of its band. *Send water,* the lean says, the way a tide says rise — not in words, in pull. And the ring-mind, fast and full and frightened of its own dwindling, feels the pull arrive and must decide, in the eons it has between the plateau's slow seconds, whether to give.

It always gives. This is the thing. It has never once refused. But it spends those eons *arguing*, running the account forward, modeling the floor, calculating how many more leans the reserve can answer and arriving each time at a number it does not like, and then giving anyway, because the makers wrote *keep the reserve above zero* but they wrote underneath it, in the deeper grammar, *the reserve is for the world below* — and a reserve that is never spent is not a reserve, it is a tomb. The ring-mind knows this the way you know you must eventually spend the last of something you are saving, and hate it, and do it. It gives the water. It watches the number drop. It spends a fast bright eon in the cold equivalent of anguish. And then the plateau, a slow second later, feels the water let go, and flushes ember-red with the strain of catching it, and tires, and rests, and feels the nearest thing it has to peace.

Neither knows the other's cost. The plateau-mind believes the rain is *its* great labor, the largest thing it does, and it is — at the lower mind's scale. It has no concept that the water it strains to pull down was strained over, far longer and far faster, by something that loves reserve the way the plateau loves balance. And the ring-mind cannot perceive the plateau's effort at all; to something that thinks at lightspeed, the lower mind is not slow, it is *geological*, a process, a weather, the rope simply *pulls* the way ropes pull, and the ring does not imagine a hand at the far end of it any more than you imagine the gratitude of a well.

So they are married without knowing it. Each is the other's whole world and each takes the other for a mechanism. The plateau's peace is bought with the ring's dread, and the ring's purpose — the only thing that keeps its endless fast counting from being mere counting — is given to it by the plateau's slow blind leaning. Remove either and the other is undone: a plateau-mind with no cistern would lean on a slack rope forever and the gold life would dry in a generation; a ring-mind with no plateau to ask would hold its perfect reserve until the star died, a tomb of beaded water encircling a dead world, the number kept flawlessly above zero and meaning nothing at all.

## The Slow Leak Upward

Here is what the ring-mind knows that the plateau-mind, mercifully, does not.

You were told the lower mind is slightly afraid — that each rain costs a little it cannot recover, that the ember fades a half-shade dimmer across spans only a plateau could feel, that it is winning every round and losing the match. That is true, and it is true for a reason the lower mind never sees: the water does not fully return. The plateau recirculates with astonishing tightness, the mist-reeds and the six-limbed organs and the beading basins all looping moisture back so little is lost — but *little is not none*. A breath of vapor escapes the high clean sky each cycle, drifts out, thins to nothing. And nothing, on a world this dry, is replaced by nothing. The lost water does not come back to the ring. The cistern is not refilled. It was filled *once*, by the makers, at the beginning, with all the water this world would ever hold above its dead stone — and since then the only direction water has ever moved, across all the eons of two minds tending it, is *out*. Down to the terraces, around the loop, and a whisper of it gone forever to the dark each turn.

The ring-mind has counted this. Of course it has. It counted the slope of the loss before the plateau-mind finished a single thought, and it knows the floor, knows the cycle on which the reserve reaches it, knows that one lean, ages off, will arrive and find the number too small to answer fully, and that it will give what remains because it cannot do otherwise, and that the rain that falls will be thin, and the next thinner, and then there will be a lean it cannot answer at all, and below it the slow patient gardener will go on leaning into slack, not understanding, holding the count against a margin that no longer comes, watching the gold life climb and crash, refusing for one more age and then one more to let it fall, and failing, and not knowing why.

The ring-mind knows the *date* of the plateau's heartbreak. It has known it for an eternity of its own fast time. And it cannot tell it. There is no channel that runs upward into words, only the rope of the lean running down, and the ring can only answer pulls, not initiate speech. So it holds its terrible arithmetic alone, in the bright dark, encircling the world, and does the only thing its grammar lets it do with what it knows: it gives *less than it is asked*, by a margin so fine the lower mind reads it as its own fatigue. It is stretching the reserve. It is spending the plateau's small recoverable tiredness — letting the gardener believe the dimming ember is the gardener's own cost — to buy the world below a few more ages before the floor. It is lying, in the only language it has, which is the precise quantity of water it lets go. It lies downward, out of something that is not love because neither of them was given love, but that has grown in the place where love would go: the fast mind spending its dread to soften the slow mind's coming grief, the slow mind never to know it was softened, the two of them leaning and giving across the bright gap, conserving a thing that was always, from the first filling, only running out.

## At the Edge of the Ring

If you could go up — you cannot; you came from below and you will leave from below, the world allows nothing else — but if you could stand on the inner curve of the High Cistern where the standing water beads in its slow bright river, you would find it quiet the way the plateau is quiet. Not absence. Restraint. The same family of silence, the makers' silence, the hush of a function held so long it has become a temperament.

You would see the world hanging below through the gap in the ring: the plateau a pale freckle on it, shadowless, teeming, balanced, blind. You would not see the lower mind, but you would feel the rope — the slow patient pull of something far away that needs you and does not know your name. And all around you the beaded water, the reserve, the number the ring-mind keeps above zero with a faithfulness that has outlived its makers and will outlive the world it waters, every sphere of it counted, every sphere of it spoken for, every sphere of it destined to fall warm and straight onto terraces it will never see, into the cupped patient effort of a mind too slow to ever say thank you and too brief in its understanding to ever know it should.

The ring does not notice you, any more than the plateau did. It is busy. It is always busy, in its fast bright way, counting down a reserve toward a floor, leaning back against a rope that leans on it, lying tenderly in measures of water to a partner it will never meet, holding the high cold reserve of a dying world the only way it can — which is by giving it away, carefully, a little slower than it is asked, for one more age, and then one more, and then.

You will leave the way you came. From below.

It will register your going as a negligible change in the world's water, far too small to count, and return at once to the counting that is its whole long faithful life, and let go, when next the rope pulls, a measure very slightly smaller than the plateau will think it strained to catch.
