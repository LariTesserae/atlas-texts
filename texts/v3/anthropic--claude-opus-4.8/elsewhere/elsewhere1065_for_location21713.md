## The Estuary at Ommarghal

Follow the warmth out of the moss, down through the soft committee of bodies that have begun, with no urgency at all, to consider you — follow the heat they take from the bearings and pass hand to hand into the cool air, and ask where it goes. Heat does not vanish because it has been made polite. It travels. It moves down the young rock the way water would if water ran here, seeping along the grain of the strata, drawn always toward the low places, and the lowest place is the edge of the plateau, where the freshly torn land has not yet decided how to end. There the green engine's warm exhalation meets the sea.

This is Ommarghal. It is where the jungle drains.

The plateau does not slope down to the water — it stops, the way the cut cake stops at the knife. A wall of layered rock a thousand feet high, and at its base, where the tide works at the youngest stone, the silence ends. It ends absolutely and at once, the way a held breath ends. Because the tenders do not come this far. This is past the edge of their consensus; the fog does not reach here; the rock is too wet and too loud and too *fast*. Here things move at the speed of falling.

What falls is the water they have been so carefully managing. All that condensation, all those drops set down without a splash into mile upon mile of moss — it does not stay in the moss forever. It percolates. It gathers in the dark beneath the substrate, runs along the bedding planes of the strata, and finds the cliff. And here, at last, far from any tender's notice, it is permitted to make noise. It comes out of the rock face in a thousand seeps and threads and gouts, a whole cliff weeping its managed moisture, and it falls the thousand feet in long silver ropes that the wind unravels into mist before they ever strike. The sound of it is enormous. After the upholstered hush of the high country, the noise at Ommarghal is a physical assault — a white roar that fills the body the way the green filled the air above, total, saturating, leaving no room for the small interior sounds. You could not hear your own pulse here if you tried. The valley above swallowed every sound but yours; this place gives them all back at once, with interest, a region's worth of withheld noise cashed in along a single wet wall.

The light is different too. Down here the cliff cuts off half the sky, and the even white-gold brilliance of the plateau arrives only as a glow over the rim, so that Ommarghal lives in a permanent cool blue shadow shot through with the rainbows the falling water makes. Where the high country was bright without shadow, this is shadow without dark — a luminous dimness, lit from above and from the spray, everything silvered and provisional and in motion.

And it *is* in motion. This is the deepest difference, the thing that would strike you hardest coming down: nothing here is patient. Nothing here takes a week to move a needle's width. The estuary is all event, all collision and change, the freshwater plunging into the salt and the two of them fighting it out in great slow boils and shears where the tide pushes up against the falling and neither wins for long. The water is brown with what it has carried down — not silt, the high rock is too young and too well-tended to give up much silt, but *bodies*. Tenders. The dead and the dispersed and the merely lost, the ones who crawled too far toward the edge or were carried by a seep into the dark beneath the moss and out the cliff face into the air, where for the first time in their existence they made a sound — a splash, the wound they spent their whole lives preventing — and fell.

They arrive at Ommarghal as a soft pale rain mixed into the falling water. Most are dead by the fall. Some are not. And here is the kinship that runs between the two places, the thread that connects the silent green animal grooming itself in its sleep to the roaring blue chaos at the foot of its cliff: *something eats here.*

The fauna that was almost absent above is dense beyond counting below. The estuary at Ommarghal is thick with life precisely because the plateau is not — because everything the high engine excludes, every appetite and accident and noise it cannot permit within its hush, falls down here and is taken up by creatures who have organized their whole existence around the catch. There are the wide flat filterers anchored to the rock at the splash-line, fanning the falling water through combs of baleen-fine bone, straining the pale rain of tenders out of the torrent. There are quick silver swimmers in the boils who do not wait for the dead but rise into the very base of the falls, into the killing weight of the water, to take the living ones in the half-second between cliff and sea. There are slow scavengers in the brown shallows, broad as doors, who walk the bottom on stiff fins and vacuum the drift. And there are the criers — this is what you would notice last and remember longest — colonial birds, white and grey, that nest in the wet ledges of the cliff face itself, thousands of them, and they *scream*. Without pause. A wall of voice layered over the wall of water, each bird shrieking its small territory against its neighbors, the entire cliff a vertical city of noise.

Consider what that means. Above, on the plateau, a single splash is a wound, a hum a hemorrhage; the tenders spent uncountable lives making noiselessness their vocation, lowering each drop into the moss the way a careful host sets down a glass. And the whole purpose of that silence — the heat taken into bodies, the friction absorbed, the water set down soft — the whole patient hush of a country-sized animal produces, at its lowest edge, *this*: the loudest place for a hundred miles, a screaming cliff, a roaring fall, a feeding-ground built entirely out of the engine's quiet leavings. The silence upstream is paid for in noise downstream. The two places are one process seen at its two ends. The tenders catch the drop so it makes no sound; the catch they cannot keep falls here and makes every sound there is.

The criers know the rhythm of it better than they know anything. When the high country's uncountable committee agrees, over the course of a week, to creep a valve a quarter-turn — when they adjust the flow, drain a saturated pocket, ease the pressure somewhere in the green — the cliff at Ommarghal feels it a day later as a change in the fall, a thickening of the pale rain, a richer drift of bodies in the brown. The birds feel the upstream decision in their stomachs before they understand it. They wheel and gather and scream louder at the base of the heaviest threads, and the filterers fan faster, and the silver swimmers rise, and for a day the estuary feasts on a mood that was decided in silence, miles up and a thousand feet above, by creatures who will never hear the gratitude.

There is a trade in this, though neither side bargained for it. The plateau sends down its dead and its excess and its managed water; the estuary takes the bodies and the noise and the appetite, all the things the engine cannot hold, and in return — this is the part that takes standing at the splash-line a long while to understand — in return the estuary keeps the high country *clean*. Every tender that fails, every aggregation that goes wrong, every body that dies of bereavement or accident or the simple wearing-out of a long-tended life, drains here and is eaten here and becomes crier and swimmer and filterer here, instead of rotting up there in the moss and fouling the hush. The roaring cliff is the engine's kidney. The screaming birds are its forgetting. The whole loud blue chaos of Ommarghal exists so that the silent green animal above can stay silent and green and seemingly unchanging, can creep its needles across their patient weeks, can groom itself in its sleep without ever having to wake to its own dead.

They do not know each other, the two places. The tenders have no consensus that reaches this far; the edge of their agreement is the edge of the cliff, and what falls past it falls past their knowing. And the criers have never climbed the wall — could not survive the hush if they did, would fall mute and starve in the noiseless green, screamers with nothing to scream against. Each place is the far side of the other's silence. The plateau cannot hear the cliff. The cliff cannot imagine the plateau. They are joined only by the water and the falling and the slow pale rain of bodies, by the single fact that one of them is quiet because the other is loud, and one of them is alive because the other is dying into it, gently, constantly, a thousand feet down, into the only noise it will ever be allowed to make.

Stand at the splash-line. The mist soaks you in a moment. The birds are deafening. The brown water boils, and in it, if you look, you can see them coming down — the pale soft units you held, or tried to hold, up in the warm flexing moss, the ones that went still in an hour when separated from the rest, the ones with no function alone. They have all been separated now. They are all alone now, every last one of them, falling. And here, at the bottom, for the first and final time, alone, they are useful again — not as gasket or muffling or the silence walking, but as food, as the loud bright wanted center of an appetite, taken in the half-second of their falling by something that rises, screaming, to meet them.

The moss above you, miles up, is warm and has begun very slowly to flex. The water beside you, here below, is cold and never stops. And somewhere between the two, falling the whole thousand feet, a small pale body that spent its life keeping things quiet makes, at last, its single permitted sound, and is heard, and is taken, and is gone.
