# Preparing to Visit the City in the Stone

## Understand What You Are Entering

Before anything physical, understand this: you are not visiting a sight, you are entering an *equilibrium*. The city in the stone is a thing in balance, held in tension across generations, and an ordinary traveler is, by nature, a disturbance — loud, fast, wanting, accumulating. Your first task is not to pack but to *slow down* before you arrive. Spend a day or two near the threshold, by the slow river, doing nothing. Let your inner pace fall to match the falling drop. If you walk in still carrying the rhythm of the loud world, you will feel like a struck bell in a room of held breath, and they will feel it too.

## The Body: Cold, Damp, Stillness

**Dress for steady, sourceless cold, not for weather.** This is the crucial distinction. You are not preparing for a *cold day* — there are no days. You are preparing for an unchanging, penetrating, marrow-deep coolness that never lets up and never spikes. Bring:

- **Layered wool and felt**, not a single heavy coat. The cold doesn't attack; it seeps. You want insulation that holds your own warmth around you constantly, because there is no warming hour to recover in.
- **Wax or oil for your outer layers**, because *everything weeps*. The whole geode rains slowly and forever. Cotton will soak through and never dry; synthetics will leave you clammy and cold. Felted wool and treated fern-style cloth shed the constant film. Pack the assumption that you will be perpetually, gently damp.
- **Footwear with grip but a soft sole.** The paths are worn to a shine and slick with condensation and moss. Hard boots will skid and clack. You want something that bites the wet stone but lands silently.
- **Gloves you can work in**, fingerless if possible. Your hands will be cold but you will need them — for the ladders of knotted root, for the low threshold crawl, and above all for *speaking*.

**Train your body for the threshold.** The way in requires you to go on your hands through a low, wet, mossy seam. Be able to crawl, to be briefly humbled and prone, to get muddy at the door. Do not bring anything you cannot drag through that gap or that you would mourn getting wet.

## The Most Important Preparation: Learn to Be Quiet

This matters more than any gear. **The city is quiet, and the quiet is not incidental — it is the culture, the courtesy, the law.**

- **Learn their sign language, or at least the rudiments, before you go.** This is a real, complete language, not gesture-miming. It is how the people actually converse. Arriving able to sign even simple greetings, thanks, and requests is the single greatest gift you can offer and the deepest sign of respect. Speaking aloud when you could sign will mark you as crude.
- **Retrain your voice into its lowest register.** Practice murmuring meaning rather than projecting it. Remember the acoustic strangeness: a whisper may carry sharp and whole across the entire hollow, while a shout dies three feet away in the fern. You cannot predict where your sound goes. So the only safe policy is the one they live by — *keep everything low, keep everything soft, and trust the stone.*
- **Silence your gear.** No zippers that rasp, no buckles that clack, no rustling waterproofs, no devices that beep or chime. Tape down anything that rattles. A noisy pack is an offense here in a way it could never be elsewhere.
- **Practice stillness as an activity.** You will spend long stretches simply standing on a terrace, watching a drop gather and fall. If you cannot tolerate doing nothing — if your hands twitch for a phone, if silence makes you anxious — *this is the thing to work on at home before you come.* The city will expose your restlessness mercilessly.

## What to Bring — and the Discipline of Bringing Little

**Bring almost nothing, and bring nothing that gets used up.** The deepest principle of the city is that *nothing is being lost*. Things last forever; bowls take a year and last a thousand. To walk in with disposable, consumable, single-use goods is a small obscenity against the whole logic of the place.

- **Bring durable, well-made things** if you bring anything at all — objects that would be at home in a culture of knapped stone, ground bone, fired clay, slow copper. A beautiful old knife. A simple cup you've owned for years.
- **For trade or gift**, hammered copper is understood here, traded up from the river. But better than goods is *skill slowly given* — to finish something, to mend something, to weave.
- **Bring your own food and pack out all waste.** The city is in equilibrium; its gardens grow only to the brim and stop. There is no surplus to feed a stranger without taking from someone, and there is nowhere for your refuse to go. Carry your sustenance, eat quietly, leave no trace, take every scrap back out through the threshold.
- **Do not bring anything that grows, spreads, spoils, or breeds.** No seeds, no living things, no spores on dirty gear. The balance of the cold-ferns and mosses and the pale frogs is ancient and exact. You could undo a thousand years of equilibrium with a contaminant on your boot. Clean everything before you crawl in.

## Light, Time, and the Mind

- **Bring no light source, but bring patience for the brightness.** The sourceless, shadowless daylight will make you squint at first; let your eyes settle. Do not bring a lamp — it would be both unnecessary and an insult to the held noon in the stone, and you would not want to meet the *lampkeeper* with a torch in your hand.
- **Leave your clock and your schedule at the threshold.** There is no evening, no winter, no deadline. The only clock is the falling drop. If you arrive needing to be somewhere by a certain hour, you have already failed to arrive. Plan your visit with no internal appointments — only an outer limit for when you must leave the bluff entirely.
- **Prepare for the psychological pull.** This is the real hazard. The city is a place where *nothing is lost* — no fading light, no coming winter, no decay. For a certain kind of weary traveler, this is unbearably seductive. Understand before you go that some who enter do not leave, and that this is allowed, and that it is a genuine choice with a genuine cost: to stay is to step out of time, to accept that nothing will ever change again, including you. Go in knowing why you intend to come back out into the loud, rotting, growing, seasonal world — because that knowledge is the rope that lets you visit the stillness without drowning in it.

## At the Threshold, Before You Crawl In

Stop. Get low. Let the moss and the sweating rock meet your hands. Match your breath to the place. Then go in slowly, quietly, carrying little, wanting nothing, ready to stand still on a middle terrace and watch a single drop of water take its long shining fall — and to understand that it has always been falling, and will be falling still long after you have crawled back out into the rushing world.
