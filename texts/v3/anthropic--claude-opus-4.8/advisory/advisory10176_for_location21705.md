# Preparing for the Cistern Continent

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, internalize the single governing truth of this place: **the only impossibility is scale.** Everything else obeys ordinary physics. This is liberating and dangerous in equal measure. Liberating, because you can prepare with known equipment and known principles — no charms, no wards, no improvisation against the inexplicable. Dangerous, because human intuition catastrophically underestimates large numbers, and this place will punish that underestimation with exhaustion, hypothermia, and disorientation. You are not preparing for a strange world. You are preparing for an ordinary one that is simply *too big to be survived casually.*

Plan as you would for a deep, cold, open-water industrial environment — a flooded mine, a drained reservoir, the cooling system of something enormous — and then scale your margins up by an order of magnitude.

## Thermal Protection (Your First Priority)

The text returns again and again to a single word: *cool.* The chill of deep cellars, of stone that never sees the sun. This is not warm tropical water you can splash about in. This is a steady, body-leeching cold that will not relent because there is no sun-warmed shallow anywhere — the whole sea is one temperature, and that temperature is against you.

Bring:
- A **proper drysuit**, not a wetsuit. Wetsuits work by trapping a warmed layer against the skin; in a place this large and this uniformly cold, with currents the width of rivers stripping heat away, you want full thermal isolation.
- **Layered undergarments** rated well below the temperature you expect — and expect colder than you think.
- **Sealed gloves and hood.** Heat loss from hands and head will dominate; bare extremities in this water will lose dexterity within minutes.
- Chemical or electric **heat packs** distributed across the torso.

Hypothermia here is not a dramatic event. It is the quiet, default outcome of doing nothing wrong at all. Budget your time in the water against your core temperature, not against your sense of comfort, which will lie.

## Breathing and Buoyancy

You begin underwater and the world is vertical as well as horizontal — the light is "far overhead," the bubbles climb toward it, the works rise "the height of office blocks." This is genuine depth, with genuine pressure.

- **Rebreather over open-circuit scuba.** The distances here are measured in horizons. Open-circuit tanks will not carry you between oases. A closed-circuit rebreather extends your range enormously and — crucially — runs quiet, which matters in a world where sound is the second great fact.
- **Redundant gas supply.** There is no surfacing to a boat here; the surface, if reachable, is a long way up and offers nothing.
- **Dive computer with conservative decompression profiles.** Given the depth implied by that overhead light, ascents must be managed. But note: *there is nowhere to decompress safely except the open water itself.* Plan dives that don't strand you in a mandatory stop with no place to make it.
- **Stable buoyancy control** you can hold for hours, because you will be drifting more than swimming.

## Propulsion and the Problem of Distance

This is the heart of the matter. You can drift "for what feels like a day's travel and pass nothing." A human swimmer cannot cross this place. Do not try.

- Bring a **diver propulsion vehicle (DPV)** — a powered scooter — with the largest battery reserve you can carry, plus spares.
- **Better still: ride the currents.** The cistern has its own circulation — cool rivers sliding along the floor, fronts where waters meet. Learn to read these the way a sailor reads wind. The currents are your highways. The smartest traveler here moves *with* the water, conserving the DPV for crossing between current systems and for the final approach to structures.
- The **outfalls** vent in "great soft explosions of current you can feel as a shove from a hundred meters off." These can carry you or capsize you. Approach from the side, never straight downstream of an intake, never straight into an outfall.

## Navigation in a Featureless Vastness

The emptiness is "the dominant fact." There are no natural landmarks for hours at a stretch. Getting lost here is not an inconvenience — it is fatal, because rescue is impossible and the next light may never rise over your particular stretch of floor.

Your navigation aids are built into the place, and you must learn to read them:

- **The seams.** They run "dead straight to the horizon and past it" in a geometric grid. This is your coordinate system. The whole floor is graph paper. Learn to count seams, to note your heading relative to them, to treat each panel as a known dimension once you've measured one.
- **The weeds.** They grow only on the seams and "stream in the current's direction." They tell you two things at once: where the geometry runs, and which way the water flows. Read them constantly.
- **The solitary fronds** in cracked panels — "you'd give them names if you lived here." Do exactly that. Map and name every isolated landmark you pass. They may be the only thing distinguishing one stretch of infinite blue from another.
- **The lights of the works**, visible "across a night plain." These are your distant beacons. Triangulate off them.

Carry: a compass that works in this environment, a depth gauge, a reliable means of dead-reckoning, and — most important — **a logged trail of where you've been.** Drop markers if you can. Never assume you'll recognize a place you've already passed.

## Sound, and What It Means

Sound is the second great fact, and it is an asset. The pumps "travel through water far better than through air"; you "feel them before you reach them, a pressure-throb in the chest."

- This means you are **never truly without a beacon**, even in the void between works. The faint throb tells you a structure exists in some direction. Learn to take a bearing on it.
- But protect yourself near the engines. Close to, the noise is "a grinding roar." Sound that loud underwater is physically punishing. Do not approach an intake at full proximity without understanding that the pressure and noise can disorient and harm you.

## The Engines Are the Danger and the Refuge

Every oasis of life clusters at the works — and the works are machinery built to "shift water by the cubic mile."

- The **intakes** "draw a constant rain of small drift." They will draw *you* too. Treat every intake the way you'd treat the screen of a jet engine. Stay clear. The current that feeds the fish will feed you to the impellers.
- The structures "suggest crews" — ladders, hatches, walkways — but "you never see one." Do not count on rescue, instruction, or permission. Assume the place is automated and indifferent. The hatches may open onto air, machinery, or nothing. Treat all of it as live industrial equipment under load.
- The **iron is fouled with life** — filter-feeders, soft colonies, things that cling. Some of this may sting, cut, or cling to you. Wear protection against contact.

And yet — the works are also the only abundance, the only warmth ("the warm wake of the outfalls"), the only place to anchor and rest. Plan your journey as a chain of these oases, never venturing into the void without knowing where the next light lies.

## The Living Things

Where life occurs, it occurs in dense clouds — schools "packed dense as smoke," and "larger things hunt them, sleek dark predators built like seals or like nothing you know."

- You will be a large, slow, unfamiliar object entering a feeding ground. The predators are built for slashing runs through prey. You are not prey-shaped, but you are not known to them either. Move calmly, deliberately, never with the panicked motion of injured fish.
- The schools themselves are a hazard of disorientation — thousands of bodies turning at once can blind you to your surroundings and your instruments at the worst moment, near machinery.

## The Psychological Preparation

This may be the hardest part, and the easiest to neglect.

You will spend hours "alone in a cool lit infinity," with concrete below and water above and nothing else — just the pulse you can feel but not see. The emptiness is "not menacing," the text insists, but human minds do not tolerate vast vacancy well. The scale will press on you. The single cough in the empty cathedral is *you.*

- Prepare for **sensory monotony** and the strange dread of the very large. Practice. Spend time, before you go, in deep open water or in vast empty built spaces, and learn how your mind behaves there.
- Have a **plan for time**, since the world's clock — the slant of overhead light — moves over a continental floor and may not mark hours you understand. Carry your own time. Keep schedules. Eat and rest by your watch, not by your sense of when it ought to be.
- Hold the central contradiction the way the place demands: **vast and nearly vacant, alive only in islands.** Make peace with the long empty crossings. They are the price of the oases.

## A Final Word on Margins

Nothing here will break the rules. The water is heavy, sound carries, iron rusts, machines wear, cold kills, distance exhausts. You can prepare for all of it with ordinary competence and ordinary equipment.

The only thing you cannot prepare for with ordinary instinct is the **size** — and size, as the place itself tells you, "is not magic, only excess." So meet excess with excess. Carry more gas, more battery, more warmth, more food, more markers, more margin than any sane plan for a normal sea would require. Then assume the place will demand more still.

Travel oasis to oasis. Ride the cool rivers. Read the seams and the weeds. Keep the throb of the pumps in your chest as your one constant companion in the dark, and never, ever drift into the empty blue without knowing exactly where the next light burns.
