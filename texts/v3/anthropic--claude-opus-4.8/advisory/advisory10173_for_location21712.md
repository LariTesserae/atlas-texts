# Preparing for Vel-Anh

## First, Understand What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with the central fact of this place: **it does not change.** Most survival preparation assumes a world that responds — that punishes mistakes and rewards foresight, where consequences accumulate. Vel-Anh has, in some quiet way, switched off that machinery. This sounds gentle. It is not. It is the single most disorienting thing about the place, and it will work on your mind long before the altitude works on your body.

Prepare your body for the mountain. Prepare your mind for the stillness. The second is harder.

---

## The Body: For the Cold and the Thin Air

This part is, mercifully, ordinary. Vel-Anh's hostility is the familiar hostility of high places, and you can ready yourself the way any mountaineer would.

**Acclimatize beforehand.** Spend days, ideally weeks, at altitude on your own side of the multiverse before you cross over. The air here "thins against the lungs," and unacclimatized travelers will move through the first days in the gray fog of altitude sickness — headache, nausea, a dullness that will rob you of the very alertness you need to *notice* what is strange about this place. Do not waste your first impressions vomiting on a slate stair.

**Dress for the cold that never warms.** This is the key insight: the cold here is not seasonal, not diurnal, not a thing that breaks at midday. The sun is bright and far and does not heat the ground. Pack layers for *static* cold — a system you can wear all day without expecting relief. Wool and modern insulation both. A windproof shell, because the wind is one of the few things here that still moves freely. Cover your head; heat leaves there fast in thin air.

**Protect your skin and eyes.** Mountain light at thin altitude is brutal — UV unfiltered, glare off pale stone and beaded grass. Bring good glacier glasses (with side shields) and high-SPF protection for any exposed skin. You will be in bright light for the entire duration of your stay, because — and this matters — **the day does not end.** Plan as if you will never have the relief of darkness to rest your eyes.

**Footwear for fresh, cutting stone.** The scree here is "un-rounded shards that still cut a boot-sole." Bring stiff, thick-soled boots, and bring a spare pair. The terraces themselves are polished and safe, but the country around them is young rock that has not learned to be kind. Trekking poles will help on the switchbacks and save your breath, which the air is already taxing.

**Move slowly, on purpose.** Everyone in Vel-Anh moves slowly because of the air. Match them. Plan your days around a pace half of what you'd choose at home. Carry water and drink constantly — thin dry air dehydrates you without your noticing.

## The Strange Problem of Time and Sleep

Here is where ordinary preparation fails you.

**There is no night.** The shadows do not move. The day has color — morning, noon, evening tones — but the sun does not finally set, and the dark does not travel. You cannot rely on the world to tell you when to sleep, and you cannot rely on darkness to let you.

So you must **bring your own night.** Pack a heavy sleep mask, the best you can find — total blackout. Bring earplugs too, though the place is quiet; what you're really blocking is the *constant suggestion of daytime.* Bring a reliable timepiece and **impose a schedule on yourself by force.** Decide in advance: I sleep these hours, I wake these hours. Without that imposed rhythm, travelers in places-without-night drift, sleep at random, fragment, and within days become unmoored and exhausted. Your discipline is your only clock.

Consider a sleep aid you trust, for the first nights at least. Lying down in unmoving light, your body will resist. Help it.

## The Mind: The Real Preparation

This is what most travelers neglect, and it is what undoes them.

**Bring something that changes.** This is my strongest intuition about Vel-Anh, and the strangest piece of advice I can give you. In a world that holds itself at a single unchanging breath — where walls do not fall, herds do not dwindle, cisterns do not rise or drop — your mind will begin to feel that *it* is the only thing left that moves, and then it will begin to doubt even that. Carry a thing that visibly, undeniably changes. A journal you fill page by page. A plant that grows. A candle that burns down. Something that lets you stand inside this frozen system and say: *I am still passing through time, even if this place is not.* The people of Vel-Anh keep their hands in the "habit of caring" against exactly this. Learn from them. Bring your own habit of change.

**Prepare to mark your own days, because the place won't.** Keep a strict log. Date everything. The lack of night, the lack of weather-drift, the lack of decay — all of it conspires to dissolve your sense of duration. A traveler who does not track time here will not know if they have stayed three days or thirty. This is not a metaphor. Set hard departure conditions before you arrive: *I leave after N sleeps, regardless of how I feel.* Because "how you feel" will stop being a reliable signal.

**Make peace with consequence-lessness — but watch yourself.** Here nothing you do is rewarded or punished by the world. A wall you damage will not crumble; a kindness you do leaves no mark on the system. This is seductive and corrosive. Some travelers, freed from consequence, drift into carelessness or worse. The people of Vel-Anh answer this by doing good work *anyway* — maintenance without reward, care for its own sake. Adopt their ethic before you arrive. Decide that you will behave well not because the place will punish you, but because *you* are the consequence now. You carry your own causality. Don't set it down at the border.

## Practical Kit

- **Food and water** for your full intended stay plus a generous margin — do not assume the unchanging system will feed you; you are an outsider, not part of its held balance. The herds and grass are *theirs*, in equilibrium; take nothing from a count that is meant to hold.
- **A simple, durable trade good or gift.** The people are few, quiet, and slow. Bring something small and well-made that honors a culture whose highest value is good work done for its own sake. Not something flashy — something *crafted.*
- **A way to make warmth and light you control** — a small stove, a lamp — so that you are never wholly dependent on a sun that gives light without heat and never withdraws.
- **First aid weighted toward altitude and cold** — for headache, for nausea, for cuts from sharp stone, for the slow-healing of thin air.

---

## A Last Word of Manner

When you arrive, **observe the fixed band of shade and step around it.** The locals step over that motionless shadow "out of long, unspoken courtesy, the way you would step around a sleeping animal that has decided never to wake." You will not understand why. You do not need to. Match the gesture. In a place this still, manners are one of the few things that remain alive, and the way you treat the unmoving dark will tell the people of Vel-Anh — and tell yourself — what kind of guest you are in a world that has stopped keeping score.

Go slowly. Sleep on your own clock. Carry something that changes. And remember, against all the silence and the stillness and the shadow that will not travel: *you* are the thing that came from a world that moves. Don't lose that. It is the most valuable thing you bring.
