# Preparing to Visit the Undercroft Works

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything practical, sit with the central fact of this place: **it was built for occupants who are gone, and it doesn't need them.** You will be the only thing here with a heartbeat that matters, and the place will not acknowledge you. This is not a hostile environment in the way a desert or a battlefield is hostile. It is *indifferent* in a way that gets into your head. Plan your trip short. The danger here is partly thermal and partly psychological, and the second kind compounds the longer you stay.

Decide your maximum duration *before* you descend, and obey it like a dive timer. Two to three hours. Set a hard alarm. The heat and the strangeness both erode judgment, and you will be tempted to follow the processes "just a little further" to see where they go. They go nowhere you can follow. Don't.

---

## The Heat: Your Primary Adversary

This is the threat that will actually kill you, and it deserves the most preparation.

- **Treat this like a foundry floor, not a sauna.** The radiant heat comes up through the floor grilles and the cracks — it's coming at you from below, which clothing handles poorly. You need **thick-soled, heat-rated boots** (the kind worn near molten metal), not just good treads. Standing still on this floor is like standing on a stove.
- **Wear loose, light-colored, full-coverage clothing** of natural fiber or proper heat-protective synthetics rated for radiant exposure. Cover everything. Bare skin near the floor grilles will burn, and the sharp glassy rock will open you up if you fall.
- **Carry far more water than feels reasonable.** The interior weather will trick you — you'll pass through humid pockets that feel like relief and dry murderous stretches that dehydrate you fast. Electrolytes, not just water. You will not feel yourself sweating in the dry zones because it evaporates instantly. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.
- **Bring a core-temperature monitor** if you can — a wearable that tracks for heat stress. Your subjective sense of "I'm fine" is unreliable in radiant heat. Heat stroke arrives without much warning.
- **Gloves.** The iron plate and pipework will be hot to varying degrees, and you *will* reach out to steady yourself in the dim, layered space. Assume every metal surface is dangerous until proven otherwise.

---

## The Air: The Quiet Problem

The works make their own weather, and the weather is partly the *product* — vapor dispersed into the air you're breathing.

- **Bring a respirator with multi-gas and particulate filtration**, and a small **portable atmospheric monitor** for oxygen, CO₂, hydrogen sulfide, and combustibles. A place built on buried geothermal heat venting steam through fresh volcanic rock can carry sulfur compounds, displaced oxygen in low pockets, and worse. The fact that vapor condenses and runs into channels means there's chemistry happening you can't see.
- **The condensate is not water you can trust.** Don't let it bead on your skin if you can avoid it; don't rest your bare face against the cool seeping walls (tempting as those walls will be). Mineral leaching through young rock means whatever's running in those channels could be acidic or laden with dissolved metals.
- **Watch the low passages and floor-level pockets.** Heavy gases pool. The side passages where the ceiling comes down to your shoulders are exactly where you're most exposed and least able to retreat fast.

---

## The Footing and the Dark

- **The floor will cut you.** Young rock, glassy and sharp-edged, still ticking as it cools. Move deliberately. Every step is a footing decision.
- **The light lies to you.** Two light sources that don't agree — tired yellow electric and the pulsing red from below — produce **doubled shadows pointing different directions.** This wrecks your depth perception and your sense of where edges are. Bring your **own neutral white light**, headlamp-style so your hands stay free, and use it constantly to flatten out those contradictory shadows. Bring a backup and spare power. Getting lost in here with a dead light, in the heat, is the worst-case scenario.
- **Map as you go.** The settlement is dense, comb-like, with shared walls and seams for streets and walkways stacked three or four levels up. It is designed to confuse a body navigating it because it was never meant to be navigated by a stranger. **Drop physical markers** or run a guideline back to your entry point. Don't trust memory in the brown-gold haze.

---

## Navigation and Exit Discipline

- **You came down "the way water comes down" — by gravity, by the open routes.** Note this. Going *up* and *out* will be against that pull, and harder, and you'll be more heat-exhausted than when you arrived. Budget energy for the climb out. Turn back at the halfway point of your endurance, not the end of it.
- **The interior weather changes the terrain.** A passage you walked dry will be dripping an hour later. Don't assume your return route is the same as your entry route. This is why you map, mark, and trail-line.

---

## The Psychological Preparation

This matters as much as the gear.

- **Expect the loneliness to have texture.** The density of construction *promises a crowd.* All this architecture for bodies, and no bodies. Your animal brain will keep expecting someone around the next seam, and the repeated non-arrival of any face is the thing that wears people down. Know this is coming so it doesn't ambush you.
- **The machinery's patience is unsettling by design of circumstance, not intent.** It runs on the planet's heat, needing no hand. It will outlast you the way it outlasted its makers. Do not anthropomorphize it into something watching you — but also don't be surprised when your instincts insist that it is. Let the cycles be cycles.
- **Resist the urge to find the product.** Every process ends in a sealed vessel, a pipe into the rock, the vapor itself. People drive themselves slightly mad following the works to their conclusion. The running may *be* the product. Accept the mystery and leave it intact.

---

## What to Bring, What to Leave

**Bring:** heat-rated boots and gloves, full-coverage heat clothing, multi-gas respirator, atmospheric monitor, core-temp wearable, excessive water and electrolytes, neutral headlamp plus backup, trail line and markers, a hard timer, basic burn first-aid (burns are your most likely injury), and cut-resistant layers.

**Leave:** any expectation of rescue (nothing here will help you), any sample-collection ambition (don't disturb the pale fungal growth — it's the one living tenant and you don't know what it does or what handling it does to you), and any plan to "wait out" a problem. There is no waiting it out. The heat doesn't stop.

---

## The Last Thing

When you're standing against one of the cooler seeping walls in the brown-gold light, with a valve sighing somewhere and the mist coming down the lane — that will feel like a moment to rest. It's the most dangerous moment. That's when you'll want to stay, to watch one more cycle, to understand. **That's your cue to leave.** The place was never for staying. Honor that. Climb back toward the colder air, against gravity, and let the works go on running for whoever or whatever they were really for.

You won't have an answer. No one does. That's the price of admission and the only thing you're guaranteed to leave with.
