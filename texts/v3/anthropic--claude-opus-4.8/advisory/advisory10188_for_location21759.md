# Preparing for the Cloud-Drowned Terraces

## Understand the Central Problem: You Are Visiting a Place Where You Cannot Stop Breathing Water

Everything else is secondary to this. The description tells you the air "has the weight and texture of standing water," that you "move through it as a wading thing moves," that breathing tastes of brine. This is not poetic exaggeration you can shrug off. You are an ordinary human, and your lungs are not built for air this saturated. So the first and most important thing to internalize is this: **your respiratory system is the part of you most at risk, and it will be at risk constantly, from the moment you arrive to the moment you leave.**

Let me build outward from there.

## Respiration and the Throat

Air this thick with suspended water will not behave like the air you know. Two distinct dangers:

1. **Aspiration.** Breathe carelessly through your mouth and you will draw fine droplets straight into your lungs. Over hours this accumulates. The slow drowning of secondary aspiration pneumonia is a real risk in environments far less saturated than this one. You need a **breathing filter** — not a full respirator necessarily, but at minimum a fitted cloth-and-membrane mask that condenses and sheds incoming moisture before it reaches you, with a downward-draining channel so the collected water runs off rather than pooling against your face. A hydrophobic mesh, the kind that wicks and sheds, layered over a breathable membrane.

2. **Cold-wet hypothermia of the airway.** The light is "neither day nor night," weak, never throwing a sharp shadow — meaning little radiant heat. The water is warm enough to be liquid-soft, but a body losing heat to constant evaporative and conductive contact will chill even in mild ambient temperatures. Breathe enough cool saturated air and your core drops. Consider a **heat-exchange element** in your mask that recovers warmth from your exhalation to pre-warm your inhalation.

Practice breathing through your gear *before* you go. Slow, shallow, nasal where possible. Panic-breathing in this place could kill you faster than anything the standing ones might do.

## Skin, Body, and the Endless Wet

"Walk and your skin beads instantly." You will never be dry. Accept this completely and plan as if you will be permanently submerged in a warm bath you cannot leave.

- **Macerated skin.** Skin kept wet indefinitely softens, splits, and grows things. Maceration of the feet, the groin, the armpits, between the fingers — all the warm folds. You need a wetsuit-logic approach: a thin, smooth, **non-absorbent synthetic layer worn against the skin**, not cotton (cotton holds water against you and rots), something that lets you stay wet without letting your skin *soak*. Change it. Bring several. Treat your feet like a diver treats theirs — antifungal powder is useless here (it dissolves), so the answer is barrier and rotation.

- **Footing.** Everything is mossed, "slick and dripping," the moss "squelches and weeps when stepped on." The young rock is smooth, "almost ceramic." This is a place built to make you fall. Soft-soled grip footwear with aggressive siping — the texture of a gecko's foot more than a hiking boot. And a **walking staff**, single point of contact, probing ahead. The terraces have rounded, "half-poured" edges that haven't finished becoming edges — meaning the boundary between *terrace* and *fall* may not be sharply visible in the dim light. A staff finds the edge before your foot does.

## Light

The light is the great quiet danger people underprepare for, because it isn't dramatic — it just *isn't enough*. Pale, dim, sourceless, shadowless. The only sharp light in the whole world is the townsfolk's oil-lamps, "smudges of warm orange," and they "do not give much."

Bring your own light, but bring it **knowing it will betray you**: a strong beam in fog this thick produces backscatter, a glowing wall of nothing directly in front of your eyes. You want a **low, warm, wide light held low** — near the ground, angled forward and down, the way fog lights work on a vehicle. Not a bright headlamp at eye level. And carry redundancy, because in a world this wet your light sources will fail, and the dark in a cloud at altitude with no moon and no stars is a complete and disorienting dark.

## Navigation and the Problem of Sameness

"The horizon falls away on every side into pale nothing." There are no landmarks because there is no weathering — no distinctive canyon, no notable peak, no carved face. The terraces are repeating shelves. The fog limits your sight. **You will get lost with terrible ease, and getting lost here means walking off a rounded edge into the pale.**

- Lay a **physical line** as you move from the settlement — cord, tied off, the way cave divers run a guideline through silt-out. Do not trust memory, do not trust your sense of direction in featureless white.
- The settlement's water-architecture is your friend: water always moves *downward* through the cisterns and cataracts, "terrace to terrace." If lost, **follow the falling water down** and you will be following the logic of the town back toward the town.
- A compass may or may not work — assume it might not. The line is the truth.

## Sound

The world's signature sound is the *drip* — "a million small clocks slightly out of time." There is no wind, no rivers, no rockfall. This means **sound carries strangely and you will hear things approach long before you see them**, and also that you should be quiet, because in a silent world your noise is enormous and the locals live by patience. Listen. The change in the dripping — a sudden local hush, a clumping — may be your only warning that one of the standing ones is near.

## The Standing Ones

This requires the most careful behavioral preparation, because they are the thing here that is genuinely unknowable.

What we are told: man-high coalescences of mist, slow, pathed, pausing at thresholds. They do not speak or threaten. They drink the bloom-flowers. They dissolve "when something disturbed them." No one knows if they are alive. The townsfolk leave them **offerings of cupped catch-leaves full of clean water at the terrace-edges**, possibly to feed them, possibly to keep them gathering *elsewhere* — a meaningful ambiguity. You should treat that ambiguity as a warning.

Rules to adopt:
- **Do not disturb one.** They dissolve when disturbed — but you do not know what disturbing one *costs*, or whether the dissolution is harmless to the thing or to you. A being made of the same hung water you are breathing, that drifts at "the pace of thought" — do not assume it cannot enter you the way the water already does.
- **Mimic the locals exactly.** If they leave water-offerings, you carry catch-leaves of clean water and you leave them too. When in a place whose physics you don't understand, the people who have survived it have encoded their survival in ritual. Copy the ritual without needing to understand it.
- **Yield the path.** They follow paths and pause at thresholds. Do not stand in a threshold. Do not block a path. Step aside, hold still, let it pass. You are the visitor; the slow drifting thing has right of way.

## The Pace — and Why Rushing Is the True Hazard

"Everything moves slowly... a single great patience." This is not just atmosphere; it is **operational guidance.** A world this young, this soft, this saturated, is a world where every fast human instinct is wrong:

- You cannot move fast (you'd fall, aspirate, exhaust your warm air).
- You cannot see fast (the light won't allow it).
- You cannot understand fast (the locals themselves don't fully understand the standing ones).

Match the tempo of the place or it will hurt you. Plan a **short visit measured against your respiratory and thermal endurance**, not against how much you want to see. Set a hard turnaround time — when your line reaches its end, or your breathing gear's heat-exchange begins to falter, or your core temperature notches down — and turn back then, slowly, following the water down.

## A Packing List, Condensed

- Draining condensation-mask with heat exchange; spare membranes
- Several non-absorbent base layers; do not bring cotton
- Geckogrip soft-soled footwear; probing staff
- Low, warm, wide-beam light held near the ground; multiple backups, all rated for total immersion
- Guideline cord, generous length
- High-density, sealed, calorie-dense food (you'll burn heat constantly)
- Catch-leaves or equivalent vessels and a supply of clean water — for the offerings, in imitation of the locals
- A way to track your own core temperature
- Trade goods of dull bronze or worked stone, since that is the local material vocabulary — arrive offering what they value, not your own metals which may mean nothing or seem alien

## The Frame to Hold in Your Mind

You are walking into a world that **has only just begun to exist**, that is still cooling and settling and deciding what it will be. It is warm, patient, half-dark, and drowned. It is not malevolent. Almost nothing here is fast enough to be malevolent. The danger is not predation — it is **the slow, constant, gentle accumulation of water and cold and disorientation**, the way the place will quietly fill you and chill you and turn you around if you let it, at the unhurried pace of everything else.

Survive it the way you'd survive a long dive in cold water in zero visibility: slowly, by the line, breathing carefully, watching your heat, touching nothing you don't understand, and leaving before the place has finished gathering you in.
