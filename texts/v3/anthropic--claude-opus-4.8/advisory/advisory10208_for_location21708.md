# Preparing to Visit the Working Room

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything practical, understand the *temperament* of this place. It is not hostile. It is not haunted, though the absence of life may make your animal brain insist otherwise. It is a machine made of clay and water and light, running its single function in perfect indifference to whether you are there. You are visiting a thing that is *busy*. Prepare yourself to be irrelevant to it — this is oddly restful, but only if you expect it.

## Physical Preparation

**Thermal acclimation.** The water sits at the upper edge of comfort — bathwater that has been warmed further by the light-column. This is more dangerous than it sounds. Warm water does not refresh you; it slowly cooks you. Even if you don't seem to need to breathe here, your body may still register heat-load. Spend a few days before the crossing in genuinely hot baths, sauna sessions, warm springs — train your body not to panic at sustained heat, and to recognize the early signals of overheating (headache, light-headedness, a creeping lethargy that feels pleasant and is not). You will be tempted to *stay*. The warmth and the light will make staying feel like the natural thing. Bring an internal discipline about time.

**Eye preparation.** The light pools rather than dims. It is a clean, shadowless glare with no soft edges to rest your gaze on. This is genuinely tiring — the eye looks for shadow to orient by and finds none. Practice in bright snow-fields or in white-walled rooms under flat overhead light. Consider bringing tinted lenses you can deploy if the glare becomes punishing, but know that the squint passes once your eyes settle. Do not bring anything that creates a hard shadow you'll keep flinching at; in this place a shadow is the strange thing.

**Ear and chest preparation.** This is the detail most travelers underprepare for. The room *roars*. The cataract pouring in from the corner transmits through dense water and you will feel it in your sternum as much as hear it. Sustained low-frequency sound through a fluid medium is profoundly disorienting and, over hours, exhausting — it overrides your sense of quiet, your sense of *thought*. Train with continuous loud water: stand near a real waterfall, a weir, an industrial overflow, for as long as you can tolerate, until the roar becomes a floor you can think on top of rather than a wall pressing on you. Bring nothing to fully block it — silence here would be a lie, and you'll want to leave with the truth of the place. But bring the capacity to *tolerate* it without your nervous system reading it as alarm.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

**The empty-fertile problem.** Everything is right for life — warmth, light, water, structure, even the first thin weed — and yet nothing has come. No fish, nothing in the seams, nothing grazing. Your deep brain will not like this. It is wired to read "good conditions, no life" as a warning: *something keeps things away here.* You must inoculate yourself against this dread in advance, because there is no answer to it inside the room. The room will not explain itself. Decide before you go that you will not need the explanation. Sit with the idea of fertile emptiness — a tended garden no one walks in, a set table in an abandoned house — until it stops feeling like a threat and starts feeling like a fact.

**The pull to stay.** The description ends with someone staying longer than they meant to. Take this seriously as a hazard, not a charm. Warmth, even light, hypnotic rotating current, the white drum of falling water — these together compose a near-perfect lullaby for the human animal. Set an external limit you cannot rationalize away. A genuine timer, ideally one with a physical signal you'll feel (a vibration, a tug) since you may not trust your sense of elapsed time once the current has you turning slowly in its circuit.

## What to Bring

- **A tether or a fixed orientation point.** The slow rotation moves everything — weed, drifting leaf, you. With no shadows and no animals to mark position, you can lose your sense of place entirely. Choose your entry point and *mark it* in your mind, or physically. Know where your way out is before you let the current take you.
- **Minimal, soft-edged equipment.** This room is all angle and crisp chisel-mark and fresh mortar. Bring nothing sharp enough to scar young clay. You are a guest in recent work — the corners are not yet rounded by time, and you should not be the one to round them.
- **A way to feel time passing**, as above.
- **Almost nothing else.** This is a sparse place, generous with its emptiness. Honor that. Travel light. The room is already complete; it does not need your additions.

## On Conduct

Do not look for the drain — you won't find it, and looking will only feed the part of you that needs the room to make sense. Let the weed grow toward the light without your help and without your interference; you are watching the world begin to take a made thing back, and your job is only to witness it, not to hurry it.

Move with the current, not against it. Fighting the rotation is exhausting and pointless; the room is not for crossing but for turning in.

And when your timer pulls at you — go. The room will keep doing the one thing it was made to do whether you stay or not. That is the gift of it and the danger of it both. Carry out with you the memory of warm bright water, working — and leave the room to its work.
