# Preparing to Visit the Slow Canopy

## First, Understand What You Are Entering

Before any packing list, sit with this: you are about to walk *on the surface of a single living thing the size of a continent.* Not through an ecosystem — through an organism. Nothing you bring, do, or feel will register on it. This is not a hostile place. It is something far stranger: a place of total indifference at a scale that makes hostility impossible. Your fear and your wonder will both go unnoticed.

Make peace with this in advance. Travelers who panic in the Slow Canopy do not panic because something attacks them. They panic because nothing does, and the silence and the dripping go on, and the green dusk never changes, and they realize how small a fast, frightened, short-lived thing can feel inside something patient.

Prepare your mind first. The body is the easy part.

## The Body and Its Wetness

**Abandon the idea of staying dry.** This is the single hardest adjustment for most travelers, and the most important. Within a minute you will be wet. You will stay wet for your entire visit. Waterproofing is a fantasy here — there is no rain to shed, only an omnidirectional weeping that beads on every surface and finds every seam. Fighting it will exhaust you.

Instead:
- **Wear thin, fast-wicking layers that are comfortable wet.** Merino, certain synthetics. Nothing that holds water heavily or chafes when soaked. Nothing cotton, which will turn to a cold sodden weight.
- **Treat skin proactively.** Warm, wet, unbroken immersion for days is a fungal and bacterial paradise — though, notably, *this* place's microbial life may not recognize you as habitat at all, which is its own gamble. Bring antifungal powder and barrier creams for feet, groin, armpits. Inspect skin daily for softening, maceration, breakdown. Wet skin tears easily.
- **Protect your feet above all.** You will walk on living, fibrous, faintly warm substrate that gives slightly underfoot like dense moss over peat. Closed footwear that drains rather than seals. Expect to feel the floor's slow swell through your soles. Some travelers eventually walk barefoot, claiming the substrate is kind to feet. This is a choice for later, not arrival.

**The air is a partial fluid.** It is so saturated you will "drink a little" with each breath. This is breathable but not comfortable for a body built on dry-land respiration. Expect:
- A constant low effort to breathing.
- Reduced cooling — you cannot sweat efficiently in air this wet, so your body's thermostat is compromised. The warmth is mild and even, which helps, but exertion will overheat you faster than your instincts predict. **Move slowly. The place wants you to.**
- Bring a breathing aid for the first days if you are sensitive — even simple humidity helps you acclimate, but a small dehumidifying mask for sleep can prevent the disorienting sense of slow drowning that catches some travelers at night.

## Food, Water, and the Question of Eating the Place

The water that weeps everywhere *looks* drinkable. **Do not assume it is.** This is filtered metabolic fluid moving through the body of an organism — closer to lymph than to rainwater. Treat all water. Bring filtration and chemical treatment both. Better: **bring your own water,** more than you think, because while you cannot stay dry, you can absolutely dehydrate — the wet air masks your thirst and you will not feel yourself drying out from the inside.

Bring all your food. **Do not eat anything from the Slow Canopy.** The leaves are soft, abundant, and everywhere — and they are the tissue of the thing you are standing on. The animals graze them safely because they are part of the circulation. You are not. The line between "plant," "animal," and "substrate" is unclear here, which means the line between "food" and "the living world digesting itself" is also unclear, and you do not want to insert your gut into that ambiguity.

Pack dense, calorie-rich, low-bulk food. You will move little and slowly, so caloric needs may be modest, but the constant low thermal drain of being perpetually wet adds up.

## Navigation and the Problem of the Green Dusk

This is where travelers are most genuinely endangered — not by violence, but by **getting lost in a place with no center, no edge, no landmarks, and no time of day.**

Understand the navigational nightmare you are walking into:
- **The light never changes.** Perpetual underwater dusk, the same at noon and midnight. Your circadian rhythm will have nothing to anchor to.
- **There is no horizon, no sun position, no fixed feature.** The leaf-towers all resemble each other. The "marks" left by old makers are too rare and reabsorbed to navigate by.
- **The terrain itself moves.** The substrate breathes and folds. The leaf-towers lean toward warmth — *toward you* — and crowd in over days, then release. A path you took will not look the same coming back, because the forest will have bent around you and straightened behind you.

Therefore:
- **Bring redundant timekeeping.** Multiple independent clocks, because you will lose your sense of time within a day. Set rigid sleep/wake/eat schedules and obey them mechanically. Your body will not tell you when to sleep here.
- **Do not rely on magnetic compass alone** — assume nothing about this world's fields. Bring inertial navigation, redundant. Drop a recoverable beacon at your entry point.
- **Mark your trail in a way the place cannot easily reabsorb on your timescale** — but understand that nothing you place will persist for long against an organism that grows over everything. Frequent marks. Photograph your path obsessively, knowing the photos may not match your return.
- **Plan a short visit and a conservative range.** "You could walk for a generation and never leave the green dusk." Take this literally. There is no edge to reach. If you become lost here, you do not stumble out — you simply continue, inside the same dripping dusk, until your supplies end. Treat every step outward as a step you must reverse exactly.

## The Living Things

Nothing here is fast except the small bright droplet-riders, and nothing here hunts you, because hunting requires an urgency this world has abandoned. You are in little danger from the fauna. But:

- **The floor-things** move at a kilometer a day inside their slow green wake. They are large and warm and the canopy bends after them. Do not bed down in a path one might cross — not from danger of being trampled (you'd have hours of warning) but because **you are warm, and the warm-seeking things will come to you.**
- **The pale ropes** — the dim-dwelling growths that "lie alongside the warm-blooded" — *will find you in your sleep.* They are not parasitic, "not quite." They want your heat. You will likely wake to find one resting against you. This is the most viscerally unsettling experience the place offers, and it is, by all accounts, harmless. Decide in advance how you will respond. Detaching gently is wise; thrashing is not. But know that **you cannot fully prevent it** — your body is a beacon of warmth in a cool dim world, and the leaf-towers themselves will lean toward you as you sleep. You will wake inside a slowly closing embrace of vegetation that wants to be warm with you. This is the price of entry.

## The Things to Witness — and Not Disturb

You came to see the strangeness. Prepare to receive it slowly, on the place's terms:
- **The sound-flowers** open to your voice and close in silence. You can make a corridor of plum-colored blossoms bloom ahead of you by calling out — a slow wave of opening, a slow wave of closing behind. This is the one gift the place gives in response to you. Use your voice sparingly and listen for the far answer.
- **The water that remembers shapes** — the hanging chandeliers in your own form, persisting for hours beneath the broad leaves. You will, if you stay still long enough, find the water holding the shape of *you.* Travelers report this as the single most profound and disquieting moment of the visit: looking up to see your own form hanging in suspended droplets, made by nothing, meaning nothing, lasting longer than your passage. Bring the patience to wait for it. Bring the composure to face it.

## What to Bring Home, and What Not To

**Take nothing living.** Not a frond, not a droplet, not a sample of substrate. You will be removing tissue from a single circulating organism, and the ethics aside, you have no idea what travels with it. Photograph. Record. Leave the place complete.

**Take no marks of your own.** Whatever you build, the substrate absorbs. But more than that — the old makers shaped the living material and *stopped,* and the place finished their thought for them. Honor that grammar. Be a thing that passes through and is taken in, not a thing that insists on itself.

## The Final Preparation

Spend your last day before departure practicing stillness and slowness. The Slow Canopy will not injure you for moving fast, but it will exhaust you, frighten you, and lose you. The single most protective thing you can do is **adopt the tempo of the place.** Breathe slow. Move slow. Let your urgency drain out into the warm wet dark.

You are visiting something that has stopped being able to die and only circulates. It will not notice you. It will take you in just the same. Go prepared to be small, wet, slow, and unimportant — and go prepared to find that, here, this is a kind of peace.
