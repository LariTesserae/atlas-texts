# Preparing for the Unfinished Heights

## Understand What You're Walking Into

Before anything physical, understand this conceptually: you are visiting a *draft*. A place where the work of making the world is still underway, where the ground beneath your feet has not yet agreed to be permanent. Most multiverse travel involves visiting finished things — eroded, settled, decided. This is different. You are entering a sentence mid-composition, and the engine that writes it does not know you are there and would not care if it did.

Hold that humility close. It will save your life more reliably than any equipment.

---

## The Body: Acclimatize First

The air is thin and cool — high-altitude thin. **Do not arrive at full elevation immediately.** If your transit method permits intermediate staging, take it. Spend time at a lower analogue elevation breathing harder than you'd like, letting your blood thicken to it. Otherwise you'll spend your first days in the city with the dull headache and clumsy judgment of altitude sickness — and *clumsy judgment is precisely what gets people killed in the unset country.*

Pack:

- **Layered insulation** for a coolness that never bites but never relents. You want to manage a persistent low-grade chill across long, slow hours, not survive a single freeze. Wool over synthetics. Things that stay warm when damp, because *everything here is damp.*
- **A breathing aid** if your physiology is marginal at altitude — even a simple supplemental supply for emergencies, for the moment you must move fast through thin air and your body refuses.
- **Waterproofing that breathes.** The rain is a fine intermittent dampening, the mist a slow weeping. You will not be drenched, but you will never be dry. Plan for chronic moisture: foot care, dry storage for anything that matters, fungal prophylaxis. Trench foot is a real and stupid way to ruin a journey.

---

## The Eyes: Light Discipline

The light is flat, grey, eternal half-dawn. **You will lose your sense of time.** The day does not commit; neither will your circadian rhythm.

- Bring a **mechanical timepiece** — not one that listens to the world, because the world's time is wrong here. The echoes arrive late or early; the foundry-bell has rung before it rings. *Trust your own clock, carried from a place where time still sets.* Keep it sealed against damp. Wind it on a schedule you set arbitrarily and obey it like scripture.
- Bring **a light source warmer than the ambient grey** — not for seeing, but for sanity. The only warm color here is the sour yellow arc-lamps. Carry your own small warmth. The grey will press on you more than you expect.

---

## The Rules: Learn Them Before You Need Them

The locals have made laws for living in a provisional world. **These are not customs. They are physics, learned at cost.** Memorize them before you arrive and follow them with the discipline of someone who does not understand why, because you don't, and the people who learned why are dead.

1. **Do not walk in the unset country after the mist comes down.** This is the first law and the most absolute. When the grey mist pools in the hollows, the unfinished places stop honoring direction, sequence, and place. Be in a settled district, on old hard stone, before it descends. Watch for it the way a sailor watches weather.

2. **Do not trust an echo until you've heard its cause.** Sound here uncouples from its source — arrives late, arrives early, arrives from events not yet occurred. If you hear a warning, a voice, a bell, a footstep: *find the cause before you react.* Reflexes calibrated for a finished world will betray you. The bell that rang an hour ago is not ringing now.

3. **Build, stand, and sleep on the oldest, hardest stone available.** The fresh stone is "not yet committed to being stone." Learn — from locals, always from locals — which ground finished setting before the city came. That is the only ground you can trust to be where you left it.

4. **Verify direction constantly, by multiple means.** There are slopes where *down* is an opinion, where water runs upward along the grain of growth. Do not trust your inner ear alone. Carry a weighted plumb-line and a level. When they disagree with your body, *believe the instruments and leave.*

---

## What to Carry, What to Leave

**Carry:**

- A **plumb-bob and spirit level**, for the places where gravity becomes negotiable.
- A **physical map you draw yourself, daily, and date** — because districts have been built on ground that later "decided to be elsewhere." Addresses float. The map you bought is already a lie. The map you make is at least an honest record of where things *were.*
- **A tether and a partner.** Never enter the unset country alone, and never untethered when you do. If the mist comes, if the ground decides to go elsewhere, a rope to a person standing on old stone is the difference between a story and a disappearance.
- **Local guidance, hired and trusted.** The people here have negotiated a working relationship with a world that won't hold still. Their knowledge is not transferable to paper. Pay them. Listen to them. When they stop and go quiet, you stop and go quiet.

**Leave behind:**

- The assumption that *places stay where they are.*
- The assumption that *the past is fixed and the future is unmade* — here, the mist remembers, the echoes precede, and the line between has gone soft.
- Any need to **descend.** Do not seek the engine below. No one has gone down to see it and returned to say so. The patient enormous warmth of creation-in-progress is not a destination. Tap its heat from above like the foundries do, and leave the depths to the thing that is still composing them.

---

## A Final Orientation

Spend your first day doing nothing but standing in a settled district, on old stone, learning the rhythm of the place. Feel the slow thud underneath — the one everyone has stopped hearing, the world pushing itself up another fraction of an inch. *You* should keep hearing it for as long as you can. It is the clock that matters most: the reminder that you are a momentary thing standing on a draft of a mountain, that the chapter you occupy may be revised, that you are a guest in a world being written and you are not the author.

Travel light. Stay high on old stone. Trust your own time. Leave before the mist.

And when you go home to a finished world, do not be surprised if for a long while afterward you find yourself distrusting the ground — listening for the slow thud beneath your own settled, decided, *finished* mountains, and wondering whether they too are only further along in the writing than you'd ever thought to ask.
