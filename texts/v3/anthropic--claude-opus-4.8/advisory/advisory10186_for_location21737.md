# Preparing for the Tableland of Slow Forgetting

## Understanding What You're Facing

Before anything else, internalize the one rule that governs survival here: **distance and direction are negotiable, but persistence is real.** Everything you bring, everything you remember, everything you hold tightly — this is your anchor against a world that dissolves the unfastened. You are not going to a place that wants to kill you. You are going to a place that will gently, patiently, *forget you into itself* if you give it the chance. The fog is full of people who came empty-handed.

This changes how you pack. You are not packing to fight an environment. You are packing to remain a discrete, persistent thing in a world that no longer enforces that distinction.

---

## The Body and the Cold

The altitude is your first real adversary, and it's a slow one. The air has thinned to "something you must learn to breathe" — treat this as literal.

- **Acclimatize beforehand.** Spend weeks at high altitude in your home world before crossing over. Arriving unprepared invites hypoxia, and a hypoxic brain is exactly the kind of unmoored, drifting consciousness this place erodes fastest. Confusion is the first symptom of altitude sickness *and* the first symptom of being forgotten. You will not be able to tell them apart. Don't let either start.
- **Consider supplemental oxygen** in a closed, persistent system you carry — but know that anything you set down may not be where you left it. Keep it strapped to your body.
- **Dress for patient cold, not sharp cold.** No wind means layering works beautifully. Wool against the skin, then insulation, then a waterproof shell — because the damp here is the real threat. The fine rain that "appears" and the perpetual condensation will soak anything that isn't sealed. Wet plus thin cold air equals slow, creeping hypothermia that you may not notice until your judgment is already gone.
- **Waterproof everything, twice.** The damp gets into seams, into thread, into the small spaces. Anything that can wick water will.

## The Anchor Kit — Your Most Important Gear

This is the part that makes this journey unlike any other. You need objects whose entire purpose is to be *held tightly and remembered.*

- **A single carry-stone.** Choose a stone from your home world — distinctive, weighted, something you can close your hand around completely. This is your test object and your tether. As long as it remains with you, it proves you remain. Keep it on your body, against skin if possible, on a cord around your neck. Never set it down to free your hands. If you ever feel yourself becoming uncertain — about who you are, where you came from, why you came — grip the stone and recite its history aloud.
- **A written log, kept obsessively.** Persistence of memory is survival here. Record where you are, what you saw, what you intend, *every hour.* Write in waterproof medium on waterproof paper. The act of writing forces remembering, and the log becomes an external persistence you can re-read when the plateau tries to shuffle your sense of sequence.
- **A knotted cord or counting line.** Tie a knot for each "thing you must not forget" — your name, your home, your purpose, your exit point. Run them through your fingers like a rosary. Ritualized memory resists erosion.

## Navigation in a World Where Walking Lies

Conventional navigation is **useless and dangerous.** Compasses, dead reckoning, line-of-sight — all of these assume distance and direction are real. They are not. You will walk toward a doorway and arrive elsewhere. Stairways will invert your ascent. The ruins themselves *migrate.*

So navigate by **persistence, not geometry:**

- **Do not navigate by landmarks.** Walls move. Doorways shuffle. The horizon folds. Mapping the plateau is mapping water.
- **Navigate by what you carry and what you remember.** Your destination is wherever you can hold in mind clearly enough. A path you *remember walking* stays remembered — so retracing a deeply-memorized route is safer than pioneering a new one toward a visible-but-untrusted goal.
- **Lay a persistent trail.** Bring carry-stones or marked objects from home and place them as you go — but understand that anything you *release* may drift. Better: maintain an unbroken physical link. A long, strong line played out behind you, the far end anchored to something you hold, may persist where a dropped marker would wander.
- **Mark your entry point indelibly in memory.** Before you take a single step inward, stand at your crossing point and burn it into your mind with ritual intensity: its exact look, the moss around it, the feel of the stone underfoot. This memory is your way home. The plateau cannot move the place you remember most fiercely.

---

## Discipline of Mind

This is the true preparation, and the hardest.

- **Hold something tightly at all times** — a thought, an object, a purpose. The instant you drift, unfocused and unmoored, you become the kind of thing the plateau dissolves. Idle minds here join the fog.
- **Do not wander aimlessly.** Every movement should be intentional and remembered. Aimlessness is surrender.
- **Beware the silence.** The total, structural quiet is "a quiet you can lean back into" — and that leaning is the danger. It invites the mind to empty, to stop holding. Bring sound of your own: hum, speak, recite. Keep yourself acoustically present.
- **Set a hard time limit and obey it absolutely.** Decide before you go how long you will stay, and tie the exit to your most persistent memory. Time is one more thing this place erodes; the meres rise on no tide, seasons pass in the migrations of walls. Trust your log and your discipline over your sense of elapsed time.

---

## What Not to Do

- **Do not pursue the moving ruins.** The migrating walls, the doorways that lead elsewhere — these will fascinate you, and fascination loosens your grip. Observe, record, move on.
- **Do not enter a ruined hall the fog has not left.** Where the fog goes in and does not come out, the hall "holds a little extra darkness." That held breath is not for you to release.
- **Do not follow the voices in the dripping.** Those are the people who came holding nothing. They are not warnings or companions. They are the absence you are here to avoid becoming.
- **Do not set down your anchor-stone.** Not to eat, not to sleep, not to rest your hand. Ever.

---

## The Shape of the Visit

Go in knowing this is a place at the end of something — finished, settled, beyond waiting. It will not threaten you with violence. It will simply continue being what it is, and offer you, very gently, the same final stillness it has accepted for itself.

Your entire task is to remain a thing that persists. Bring your stone. Hold your memory. Walk with purpose. Leave before the quiet gets comfortable.

And when you cross back out, grip your carry-stone one last time and speak your own name — proof that you, unlike so much else here, were not forgotten.
