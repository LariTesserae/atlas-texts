# Preparing for Telch: A Traveler's Briefing

## Understand What You're Walking Into

Telch will not try to kill you. That's important to internalize, because almost everything about the place *feels* hostile to a dry-world body — the heat, the wet, the crush, the noise — and your instincts will spend the first day screaming that you're in danger when you are merely *uncomfortable*. The single most useful preparation is psychological: rehearse the difference between threat and discomfort until you can tell them apart on reflex. Telch punishes panic and rewards the kind of low, patient endurance the locals carry in their shoulders.

## Acclimatize Before You Arrive

Do not step into Telch cold from a temperate world. The body needs time to adjust to constant warm humidity, and the adjustment is real and physiological.

- **Heat-and-humidity conditioning, two weeks minimum.** Spend hours daily in a hot, saturated environment — a sauna with steam, a tropical greenhouse — until your sweat response recalibrates. In Telch you will sweat constantly and it will *not evaporate*, so your usual cooling won't work. You need to train your body to tolerate running warm.
- **Salt and electrolyte loading.** You will lose minerals continuously through sweat you can't even feel leaving. Begin supplementing before you go and bring more than you think you need.
- **Noise conditioning.** Sleep several nights against loud, layered, unrelenting white-roar sound. The description is correct: after an hour the body stops fighting the wall of noise and starts reading it as weather. You can pre-train this surrender. Arriving with that adaptation already half-built will save you a brutal first night.

## What to Wear

Take your cues from the locals: **they have made peace with being wet, and so must you.**

- **Quick-shedding, never-absorbing fabrics.** Anything that soaks and holds water will rot, chafe, chill you in your sleep, and weigh you down if you go in the canal — which you will. Wear materials that sheet water off and dry against the skin.
- **Footwear with grip for a two-millimeter mirror.** The floor is a wet poured resin under a film. Smooth soles are a death sentence of small humiliations. Bring footing that bites a sweating surface, and accept that your feet will be wet the entire visit. Treat your feet daily against fungal rot — this is the most likely thing to actually injure you.
- **No tight seams, no trapped folds.** Anywhere water and warmth pool against skin for days, your skin will macerate and break down. Loose, draining, simple.
- **A way to keep your head and hands cool-ish,** since you can't rely on sweat. Light, vented, pale.

## What to Bring

- **A waterproof case for everything that matters** — documents, tools, electronics. Assume total immersion is routine, not exceptional. Children there swim before they walk; falling in is "only a Tuesday." Pack as if you, too, will go in.
- **Antifungals, antibacterials, and wound care.** In permanent warm wet, the smallest cut or rash does not stay small. Clean and dress everything immediately.
- **Your own clean water, or robust means to make it.** The seep-water is the city's circulatory medium, thick with metals the biofilm metabolizes and dense with fish-life. The locals are adapted to it across generations. *You are not.* Do not drink it. Do not assume the fish are safe for your gut without caution.
- **Earplugs you can dial down, not off.** You want to take the edge off the pressure-wall of sound for sleep, but never seal yourself fully deaf — the locals read coming changes partly by sound, and so should you.
- **A small dry sleeping pocket setup,** because dry rest is the one luxury that will preserve your sanity. Find or carry a way to sleep genuinely dry for a few hours a night.

## The One Survival Skill That Matters: Read the Floor

This is non-negotiable, and it cannot be brought — it must be **learned on arrival, fast.**

Telch is not finished. It is precipitating itself, rebuilding overnight, reabsorbing plazas and re-routing canals while you sleep. **The city does not know you are in it.** It will reshape a structure with you standing on it the same way your skin sheds cells without consulting the mites.

Your warning system is the **biofilm**: gray when stable, shading to **amber along the seams** before the city remakes that section. The locals navigate by this and by a shared "feel for the grain." You have neither instinct. So:

- **Attach yourself to locals immediately.** Hire a guide, or simply move where the crowd moves and rest where it rests. Their ten-thousand-small-yieldings-a-minute crowd-flow is also a flow *away from danger* you can't yet see. When the crowd avoids a deck, avoid it without asking why.
- **Learn the amber rule before you sleep the first night.** Never bed down on a seam going warm-colored. Never cross a plaza that's "soft" — sagging, off-limits, re-pouring.
- **Don't trust yesterday's map.** The route you took this morning may not exist by evening. Re-orient constantly.

## A Note on the People, and on Yourself

The locals practice a **courtesy of crowds** — frictionless yielding in impossible density. To them it's breathing; to you it will feel like an intricate dance you don't know the steps to. Learn it by imitation, move slowly, yield early and often, and never plant yourself stubbornly in a flow. Stillness is rudeness here, and clumsiness in the crush is the closest thing to giving offense.

Do not ask them where Telch *ends*. They will not understand the question, and the gap in understanding is real — to them this warm, loud, weeping density *is the world*, and the dry silent country beyond the rim is the unbearable place. Respect that. You come from the silence they fear. Carry that humility.

## Finally: Set a Departure Discipline

Telch is mild in its weirdness but total in its saturation — there is no dry, no quiet, no still, no *finished* anywhere in it. For a dry-world mind this wears a slow groove. The danger isn't death; it's a kind of erosion, the way the loud-warm-wet pressure overwrites your sense of edges and ownership ("nothing here is fully yours"). **Decide your exit point before you go in, and keep a private marker of how many days you've stayed,** because the city offers no natural punctuation to tell you time is passing. Leave while you still find the silence beyond the rim a relief rather than a terror.

Go warm. Go wet. Go yielding. Read the floor, follow the crowd, and remember on your way out what the locals never will: that the silence is not death — only home, waiting.
