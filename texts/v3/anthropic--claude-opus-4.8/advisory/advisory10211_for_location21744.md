# Preparing to Visit the Sumpworks

## Understand first what you are walking into

This is not wilderness and it is not ruin. It is something harder to brace for: a finished thing. Most places a traveler visits are *going somewhere* — decaying, growing, drying, freezing, in some slow argument with time. The Sumpworks has settled that argument. Nothing you do there will matter to it, and nothing it does will ever surprise it. You are entering a closed loop, and you must accept before you go that you are the only thing in the place that is actually *changing* — aging, sweating, breathing out moisture that the loop will simply absorb and balance. Pack with the humility of a guest in a house that does not know you arrived.

## The body and the heat

The central physical problem is thermoregulation, and it is genuinely dangerous.

Your body cools itself by sweating and by radiating heat into cooler surroundings. Here, neither works. The air sits at blood-warmth or above, saturated past holding, so your sweat will not evaporate — it will simply join the water already coating everything. And every surface is the same temperature as you, so you cannot shed heat by contact or radiation either. You will slowly cook in your own retained warmth without ever feeling the dry, alarming heat of a furnace. **Heatstroke here arrives gently, disguised as drowsiness.** This is the thing most likely to kill an unprepared traveler.

Prepare accordingly:

- **No insulating clothing.** Wear the lightest, most open weave you own, or as little as decency and skin-protection allow. You want maximum surface exposed, even knowing evaporation won't help — every marginal pathway counts.
- **Active cooling is the only reliable answer.** Bring a personal cooling system that does not depend on ambient air: a circulating-coolant vest with its own chilled reservoir, or phase-change cooling packs in good quantity. You must carry your *own* temperature differential into a world that has none. This is non-negotiable for any stay beyond an hour or two.
- **Carry a reliable core-temperature monitor** and check it on a schedule, because your own sense of overheating will be unreliable in a place where the air feels like a warm bath and "feeling hot" never resolves into "feeling cool again."
- **Plan strict time limits** and respect them even when you feel fine. Especially when you feel fine.

## Water — drink, but never the local water

You will be tempted. Water is everywhere. **Drink none of it.** It is laden with dissolved metals — iron, copper, who knows what else leached from the castings — and it hosts the bloom and unknown microfauna. It tastes of iron because it *is* iron-rich, mildly toxic, and unsterilized.

- **Carry all your own drinking water,** more than you think you need, kept cool both to drink and to help your core. Dehydration will be severe and deceptive: you are sweating constantly but the sweat never dries, so you won't *feel* parched the way you would in a dry heat.
- **Electrolytes are critical.** Constant sweating without evaporative relief flushes salts steadily. Plain water alone will leave you cramping and confused.

## The air, and the things in it

The air is breathable — the fauna proves that — but it is thick, humid, mineral-tasting, and full of slow soft fliers and whatever the bloom and the swarming life shed into it.

- **Bring a light filtering respirator.** Not for poison gas, but for spores, microfauna, aerosolized metals, and the simple density of organic particulate. You don't want to be breathing the bloom's exhalations for days.
- **Eye protection.** The fliers part around your face, but the moisture-laden air and unknown irritants make sealed goggles wise — fogging will be a constant fight, so choose anti-fog or actively ventilated ones.
- **Accept that you cannot keep the fliers off you,** and decide in advance that you will not flinch. They are indifferent. They do not bite. Walking through them is like walking through beaded curtains. The traveler who panics and flails in a place that has never once been hostile will only exhaust and overheat themselves. Practice the calm.

## Footing and movement

Everything is slick — warm wet metal, machined grooves, troughs of standing water, surfaces upholstered in slimed bloom.

- **Wear grippy, water-shedding footwear with hard toes,** because you'll be crossing riveted plate, casting-seams, and trough-edges, ankle-deep at times.
- **Move slowly and test footing.** A fall here means landing on warm metal in bloom-slick water among the swarming pools — not deadly in itself, but in a place this large, an injury that slows you is a thermoregulation emergency waiting to happen.
- **Mind the plate-gaps.** Long pale things pour through them, and you do not want a hand or foot in a gap whose far side you cannot see.

## Navigation — the gravest practical danger

This is the trap that will undo the careless. The Sumpworks is uniform in every direction — same light, same haze-ceiling, same structure fading into vapor, no horizon, no sun, no morning, no night. **You will get lost, completely and quickly, and the place will not help you.** There are no landmarks because the makers built one thing endlessly repeated, and the perpetual grey-gold light gives no clue to time or direction.

- **Bring inertial navigation,** not anything that relies on celestial cues or external signal — assume nothing outside the structure can be sensed.
- **Lay your own trail.** Drop markers, tie lines, leave bright tags at every junction. The bloom and the swarms won't disturb them; nothing here disturbs anything.
- **Carry your own clock and keep a rigorous log,** because the timeless light will dissolve your sense of duration within hours. People in unchanging light lose track of how long they've been awake, how long since they drank, how long until their cooling packs fail. Externalize all of it.
- **Know your exit before you go deep.** Establish your entry point as a fixed base and never let the chain of markers back to it break.

## What to bring for the mind

Strange as it sounds, this matters as much as the cooling vest.

The Sumpworks is the warmest, wettest, most crowded stillness imaginable, and it does not change. Many travelers find the perfect, breathing, swarming equilibrium profoundly unsettling — a kind of soft existential vertigo. There is no rhythm to anchor you: no day, no weather front, no sense that *anything is going anywhere.* The subsonic engine-hum drops below conscious notice after an hour and works on you anyway. People report a creeping unreality, a difficulty believing time is passing, even a reluctance to leave — the warmth and silence can lull like a tide.

- **Go with a companion if at all possible,** and agree to monitor each other's clarity, not just temperature.
- **Set hard, externally-cued schedules** for drinking, cooling, and turning back, and obey the schedule rather than your feelings — your feelings will be soft and unreliable in there.
- **Decide your departure time before you enter,** and treat it as fixed. The place will not give you a reason to leave. You must bring that reason with you.

## A final note on conduct

You are bringing change into a thing that has perfected having none. Tread lightly — not because the Sumpworks is fragile, but because it is not, and there is a dignity in not trying to leave a mark on something that has finished forever and is full, edge to edge, with quiet patient life that wants nothing from you at all.

Carry your own water, your own coolness, your own time, your own way out. The Sumpworks provides none of these. It provides only warmth, wetness, silence, and the company of the numberless still-living.
