# Preparing for the High Cold City

## First, a reckoning with what you cannot bring

Most of your instincts as a traveler are tools for engaging with a place. Here, almost all of them are liabilities. You are accustomed to *looking*, *measuring*, *approaching*, *confirming*. This place punishes all four. So the first preparation is not packing — it is unlearning. Spend time before departure practicing a softer mode of attention: the way you watch a fire, or let your eyes rest on middle distance during a long train ride. You are training a muscle you'll need constantly: the ability to *perceive without fixing*. Meditators call it open awareness. You'll call it survival.

## The body, briefly

The altitude is the only ordinary danger, and it's mild. The air is thin but breathable — "honest," the description says, and that word matters. Bring time, not equipment. Acclimatize before crossing. The temperature is the cool of a held breath, so dress in light layers that don't constrain movement and don't rustle. **The clothing must be quiet.** In a place sealed under silence, the sound of your own jacket will be deafening and, worse, *informative* — it will tell the place exactly where your attention is. Wear soft fabrics, soft soles, nothing that clicks or zips loudly.

Do not bring water in any conspicuous quantity. I don't mean you'll be punished for it — I mean it is the deepest rule of the place that water has been *concluded out of existence*, and carrying a sloshing canteen into a world that has forgotten water is like carrying a lit candle into a held breath. Bring what you need sealed, rigid, internal — a hydration bladder under your clothes, drunk through a tube without ceremony. Do not pour. Do not let it be seen being water.

Bring food that requires no preparation, no fire, no sound of unwrapping. Eat little. This is a finished place; arrive already mostly settled.

## The instruments that will betray you

Leave your measuring tools at home, or expect them to lie. A compass, a rangefinder, a map — all assume that distance and direction keep their promises here. They don't. A doorway is near, then far, then behind you. Your instruments will report numbers and the numbers will be *sincere and wrong*, which is more disorienting than no numbers at all. If you bring anything, bring a single low-stakes tool — a length of cord, say — so that when distances misbehave you have something physical and humble to be wrong about, rather than betting your sense of reality on a precise readout.

**A camera is the most dangerous object you can carry.** Not because it breaks, but because it works. A camera is pure directed attention, and attention freezes the city. Photograph a wall and you will have arrested the very process that makes the place itself. Worse, you may find your photographs disagree with each other and with your memory in ways that erode you. If you must record, record obliquely — point the lens away from what interests you and let the subject live at the frame's edge, where it is most alive and least disturbed.

## The crucial discipline: not-looking

This is the whole art of the visit, so prepare for it deliberately.

The city moves only when unobserved. The occupants stay forever distant the moment you pursue them. The truth of the place lives in your peripheral vision. Therefore your real work is to **navigate by indirection** — to move toward things by not aiming at them, to learn by refusing to confirm.

Practice this before you go. Try crossing a familiar room while attending only to its edges. Try walking toward a goal you never let your gaze land on. It feels impossible and slightly nauseating at first. That nausea is the point — it's the friction between your hunting instinct and a world that closes whenever the hunt begins.

Establish a rule for yourself and keep it like a vow: **when something becomes interesting, look slightly away from it.** The plaza that's rearranging, the occupant on its errand, the wall migrating into new shape — give it the corner of your eye and the back of your attention. It will tell you more that way. The description ends with exactly this instruction, and you should trust it as the local law it is.

## On the occupants

Do not approach them. You cannot reach them anyway — they recede at exactly your speed — and the attempt marks you. Understand the deeper unease here: to them, *you* are the half-real apparition, the thickening in their grey that won't resolve. You are visiting a place where you are the ghost. Carry that humility. You are not the observer here so much as the observed-and-disbelieved. Do not try to communicate. There is no sound to communicate with, and you would only be insisting on your own reality to beings who have, gently, declined to grant it.

If one drifts into your periphery, let it stay there. Let it do its errands. You are sharing the edge of each other's vision — that is the only intimacy this place permits, and it is enough.

## Anchoring yourself

The greatest risk is not physical injury. It's the slow erosion of your own rules to match the place's — the way distances stop meaning, shadows stop agreeing, your own shadow begins to deliberate. Reality here *wears*, and you are made of reality.

So bring an anchor: something small, dense, and yours, with a fixed and stubborn nature. A worn coin. A ring. A stone from somewhere that still believes in water. Keep it in a pocket and touch it when the place starts to soften your edges. It is your private vote for the rules you came in with. When the silence develops its small dry sounds — the tick, the settling, the grain falling near your ear with no grain to be seen — touch the anchor and remind yourself which world you belong to.

Set a hard limit on your stay before you arrive, and keep it by some internal clock, because external time will not behave. *Stay a while*, the place invites — and the invitation is genuine, and that is precisely why you must decide in advance when "a while" ends. A finished place is patient. It has all the time it has stopped needing. You do not.

## Leaving

Plan your exit while you still trust your direction sense — at the very start. Mark your entry not on a map but in your body: the slope of the ground under your feet, the particular curve of the worn stone where you came in. You will not be able to *look* your way back. You will have to *feel* your way back, against a landscape that drifts when you're not watching. Move toward your exit the same way you moved through everything else: obliquely, peripherally, never staring it down.

And when you go, go without confirming. Don't turn at the threshold for a last clear look. The city is most itself in the corner of your eye. Leave it there — alive, settling, shy, concluded — in the place where you have already, wisely, decided not to look.
