# Preparing for Vault-Reach: A Traveler's Guide

## First, Understand What You Are Walking Into

You are going to live underwater. Not visit it, not dive into it for a held breath — *live* in it, for as long as you stay. This single fact restructures everything. The people of Vault-Reach are at home in the water in a way you are not and cannot become in time. They were born to it; their bodies and their grandparents' bodies were shaped by it. You will be a slow, soft, drowning-prone animal among people who are nothing of the kind. Prepare with humility first, and equipment second.

## The Breathing Problem

This is the central problem, and you should not be cavalier about it.

The water is cool and clear and full of life, but you cannot breathe it. The city assumes you can stay submerged for the length of a long swim between trunks — and those swims are *long*, because the city is a constellation, not a crowd. A neighbor is a far swim away. For ordinary lungs, that distance is not crossable on one breath.

So you need a way to breathe that does not depend on a return to the surface — because the surface, here, is a green leaf-membrane two thousand feet up that you must not break. You cannot bob up for air. There is no "up" in the ordinary sense.

Bring a **closed-circuit rebreather**, not open-circuit scuba — the bubbling roar of exhaust gas would be obscene in this resonant, singing quiet, and your bubbles would rise and pool against the underside of the canopy, where they do not belong. A rebreather is silent, it produces no plume, and it lets you hang motionless and listening, which is how you must move here. Bring redundancy: two units, or one unit with bailout. Bring far more gas duration than you think you need, because distances are long and the place rewards lingering.

Learn it cold before you go. Be neutrally buoyant *by instinct*. The whole social life of Vault-Reach happens in three dimensions, hanging at every depth, and a traveler who sinks or rises against their will is a clumsy thing in a place built on stillness.

## Temperature

It is the cold of a stone church in autumn, and you will be immersed in it for the duration. This is the cold that kills slowly and quietly — not a shock, but a long leeching. A wetsuit is not enough for a long stay. Bring a **drysuit** with proper undergarments, and understand that even so, your hands and face — your tools of contact and expression — will grow stiff and stupid over hours. Plan your activity around your heat budget. Warm yourself between excursions; the woven dwellings hold pockets of trapped air, and some are likely warmer.

## The Light, and Your Eyes

The light is real daylight, but strained green and slowed, like a bottle held to a window. It is dim. It is also *long-sighted* — the water is clear enough to see far, and seeing far is how you navigate a sparse place. Bring a mask with the widest field of view you can get, and let your eyes adjust slowly; do not blind yourself with a bright lamp. If you must carry light, carry a warm, low, hooded one for close work only. The shafts from above are the proper light of the world. Learn to read by them. Bright artificial light will make you a stranger and ruin everyone's dark-adapted sight.

## Sound — The Thing You Are Least Prepared For

Everyone imagines underwater silence. Vault-Reach is *loud*. Water carries sound full and far, and the canopy is a resonating chamber. You will feel the leaf-membrane drum in your chest. You will hear the trunks groan under the held weight, the silver shoals ratcheting, and the long booming calls coming up from the dark below.

Two preparations:

First, **do not bring anything that adds noise carelessly.** Your gear must be quiet. Sound is sacred infrastructure here — it is how the scattered people know they are not alone. You would be shouting in a library.

Second, and more important: **learn to listen, and learn their speech before you arrive if you possibly can.** The people speak in long descending sung moans, because song travels in water where words do not. You will not be able to make those sounds with a regulator in your mouth. Think hard about this. You may need a way to produce tones — perhaps a struck object, a plucked cord, something that rings long, since *they* communicate by plucking bridge-ropes and striking hollow trunks. Arrive able to ring a greeting. A bridge-rope plucked rings for a long time; learn what that means before you pluck one.

## Moving Through the City

The architecture is knots — woven cane, lashed branch, vine-cable in long sagging catenaries, dwellings like seed-pods and inverted baskets. It is all *hand-craft*, the technology of patience, soft and old and silted at the lower levels with the falling matter of centuries.

Move gently. Everything is woven and lashed, rounded by slow erosion, settled into equilibrium. You are heavier and clumsier than anything that belongs here. Do not grab the bridges for balance the way a land animal would; learn to move *without* needing handholds, by buoyancy and slow finning. When you must touch the city, touch it the way you'd touch something that took generations to grow.

Bring nothing cast or machined that you can avoid. Their tools are bone, worked shell, fire-hardened cane, ground stone, a little soft hammered metal. Your hard bright modern gear will mark you as outside their whole order of things. Keep it minimal, muted, and close to your body.

## The Long Time-Sense

The currents take a full day to cross the settlement. Nothing is in a hurry. The trees stopped growing taller long ago; the water found its level long ago; even the motions of the place have *learned to be unhurried*. You are arriving into a deep, weathered equilibrium, and your visit is a brief perturbation.

Prepare your *mind* for slowness. Do not arrive with a schedule. Do not arrive intending to "see everything" — the city is mostly empty water and silence and slow shafts of light, and that emptiness is not a lack but the substance of the place, a cathedral that decided to be mostly nave. Plan to hang in the open water between two trunks and see nothing in any direction but drifting motes and the pillars going up into green and down into blue-dark, and let that be the destination, not the transit.

## The Ecology — What to Respect

- **The canopy-grazers** hang upside-down against the leaf-ceiling, cropping it thin enough for light, thick enough to hold the water. They are doing the single most important work in the world. Do not disturb them. Do not go near the membrane carelessly anywhere they are working.
- **The shoals** pour around the trunks in silver sheets and nest in abandoned lower dwellings. They are loud and harmless and beautiful; let them move around you.
- **The deep-dwellers** keep the dark floor and are spoken of *with respect*. Take that cue exactly. You do not go down to the floor. No one has seen it for generations. Whatever discipline the locals have about the dark below, adopt it without question.

## The One Rule Above All

The leaf-membrane holds an ocean in the sky for no reason anyone can name. The people call it *the kindness of the roof.* They tend the grazers, they do not break the membrane, and once a year the elders swim up and lay open hands against the green ceiling and feel the held water hum with the strain of not falling.

**Do not test it.** Do not probe it, sample it, instrument it, or break it to "see what happens." Whatever your traveler's curiosity, whatever your instinct to measure the inexplicable — leave the roof alone. You are a guest under a favor no one understands, in a place that asks only one thing: *that you notice.* Two thousand feet of held water is above you. Behave accordingly.

## A Packing Summary

- Closed-circuit rebreather, redundant, with far more duration than seems necessary; total fluency in its use
- Drysuit and warm undergarments; a heat plan, not just heat gear
- Wide-view mask; minimal, warm, hooded light for close work only
- Something that rings long and clear, to greet and be greeted by
- As much of their language and its sung tones as you can learn beforehand
- Trade goods, if anything: soft hammered metal, the kind of thing that travels up from somewhere — small, useful, humble
- Nothing loud, nothing bright, nothing hard you can avoid, nothing in a hurry

## Last Advice

Go slow. Stay quiet. Hang still in the green water and listen to the city singing to itself across the gaps, and the trunks groaning, and the unseen things calling up from the dark. You have come to a place that has held its shape for so long that even its silences are full and its emptiness is the point. Match it. Be a small, careful, attentive thing in a vast held ocean, and when you leave, leave the roof exactly as kind as you found it.
