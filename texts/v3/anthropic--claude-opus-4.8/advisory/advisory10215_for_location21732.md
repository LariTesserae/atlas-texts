# Preparing to Visit the Lung-Engine

## The First Truth: You Cannot Breathe Here Naturally

Three measures of elevation means you are functionally in the death zone — the air is too thin to sustain a human at rest, let alone one walking and gawking. The cruel joke of this place is that it is a *breathing machine* that nonetheless will not breathe *for you*. The dense warm air it exhales floods the tile floor, but it is the machine's air, on the machine's schedule, and you cannot tie your survival to a ninety-second tide.

**Bring:** A closed-circuit rebreather or a sealed supplied-air system with at least double the redundancy you think you need. Not an open-flow oxygen mask — the ambient pressure is too low for that to be reliable. You want a system that pressurizes against your face independent of what the world is doing. Pack a backup rig and carry it on your body, not in a bag you might set down and lose track of in the hollow.

Acclimatize first, even with the rig. Spend days climbing the pressure ladder somewhere safe before you cross over. Pressurized air does not undo the effects of altitude on a body that has not adjusted; your blood, your sleep, your judgment will all suffer if you arrive unseasoned. **Judgment is the thing you cannot afford to lose** in a place this loud and this empty.

## The Second Truth: It Will Try to Deafen You

The sound is at the top of the scale. This is not poetry — it is a medical hazard. Sustained noise at that level causes permanent hearing damage in minutes and can disorient, nauseate, and panic you long before that. The roar enters the chest; that means it carries enough energy to be a physical force, not merely a sound.

**Bring:** Double hearing protection — molded in-ear plugs *plus* over-ear defenders rated for industrial extremes, worn together. Understand that even this will not make the place quiet. It will make it *survivable*. You will still feel the ninety-second tide in your sternum. Accept that you are entering a world where speech is impossible and plan accordingly.

Because you cannot speak: agree on **hand signals** before you cross over if you travel with others. Establish a clear "I am in distress, we leave now" gesture and drill it until it is reflex. Carry a slate or a lighted text device for anything more complex. Do not rely on shouting. No one will hear you, not even yourself.

## The Third Truth: The Floor Is Old and the Drop Is Real

This world is a single chapel-sized chamber that is mostly *vertical hollow*. The bellows hang far above; the floor falls far below. The riveted brass and ceramic tile have spent ages reaching their final equilibrium, but "equilibrium" is not "safe." Gaskets weep, tiles rattle loose, seams have crept apart. Wet algae films the cupped tiles like paint — and wet algae on aged brass is among the most treacherous footing that exists.

**Bring:**
- Boots with aggressive, siped soles meant for wet metal — the kind used on ships and oil rigs.
- A harness and a way to clip in. Even if there are no obvious anchor points, the copper mains "thick as tree trunks" may serve. Treat the entire visit as work at height, because it is.
- Test every surface before you weight it. Move on the cold pause, when the exhale is not pushing warm wind across you and the moths are still. The ninety-second rhythm is your clock — learn to move *with* it rather than against it.

## The Fourth Truth: The Air Is Warm, Wet, and Not Yours

The machine exhales heat and moisture; the cold pause between breaths plunges you back toward the killing chill of altitude. You will cycle between warm-damp and bitter-cold every ninety seconds. This is exhausting and it is a recipe for both overheating during exertion and dangerous chilling during stillness.

**Bring:** Layered technical clothing that handles moisture — wicking base, insulating mid, a shell that breathes. Expect condensation on everything, including your mask and any lenses. **Bring anti-fog treatment and a way to clear your faceplate one-handed.** A fogged mask in a roaring void above a long drop is how people die in places like this.

## The Fifth Truth: The Living Things Keep Their Distance — Until They Don't

The fauna have arranged themselves into the emptiness. Hand-sized moths with wet-paper wings ride the exhale. Long-legged insect-things graze the pipes upside down. Metal-feathered birds shriek from the dead flues. None of them seems to want you — but you do not know their tempers, their venom, their territorial flues. The birds in particular, descended into the high thin air, may be larger and bolder than you expect, and they nest in the very flues you might be tempted to shelter beside.

**Bring:** A sealed suit with no exposed skin if you can manage it — not for cold alone but because you do not want a hand-sized moth landing on your faceplate during a fragile moment of balance. Do not disturb roosts. Do not reach into flues. Move slowly enough that nothing perceives you as a sudden threat. The place runs on patience; match it.

Do not eat anything. Do not touch the waxy red flowers to your skin. The plants here have evolved for survival, not for your safety, and "deep red and bruise-purple, the colors of high-altitude survival" often means *toxic* in any world.

## The Sixth Truth: The Emptiness Will Work On Your Mind

This is the preparation no equipment list covers. You will be deafened, isolated, unable to speak, breathing borrowed air, cycling between warm and cold, watching pale things ride invisible currents in a hollow that is simultaneously crammed full and utterly vacant. People are not built to hold "watched and alone, both true" for very long. The roaring solitude is a psychological pressure as real as the altitude.

**Prepare by:**
- Limiting your stay. Set a hard time limit *before* you enter, agreed and timed, and obey it even if you feel fine — *especially* if you feel fine, because euphoria at altitude is a warning sign, not a reward.
- Not going alone, if you can help it, but understanding that you cannot speak to the person beside you. You will be alone together. Make peace with this.
- Carrying a small, familiar, tactile object — something to hold that is from your own world and means something. In a place that breathes you and shouts you and dwarfs you, a worn coin or a known stone in your glove is an anchor for the part of you that is still a person.
- Anchoring to the rhythm rather than fighting it. The ninety-second tide is the law here. Let it become your metronome — move on the pause, rest on the breath, count the cycles. Surrender to the schedule of the lungs and the place becomes legible. Resist it and it becomes maddening.

## A Final Word

You are visiting a thing that has forgotten it was ever made, in which everything alive has organized itself around a breath that means nothing and serves no one. There is no host here. No purpose addressed to you. No mystery the light will reveal — the cruel ordinary light shows you everything plainly and explains none of it.

Go to witness, not to understand. Touch as little as possible. Leave the gaskets weeping and the bolts at their final tightness. Take only the memory of a chapel at the top of the air, growing flowers out of an engine, shouting forever that it is alive.

Then descend, while you still remember the words to think with.
