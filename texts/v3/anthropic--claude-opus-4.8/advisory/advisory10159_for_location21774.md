# Preparing to Visit the Seedpod-Foundry

## Understand First What You Are Walking Into

This is not a hostile place, and that is precisely what makes it dangerous. It will not attack you. It has no predators, no toxins designed to kill, no malice in its enormous patient heat. Its threat is *epistemological*. It works on the part of you that decides what is real and how big things are and whether you remember turning left. You are not packing against weather or beasts. You are packing against the slow erosion of your own continuity.

Prepare your body for the heat, but prepare your *mind* for the recompiling. Most travelers who come to grief here do so not from the temperature but from staring too long.

## The Body

**Heat management without water.** The defining cruelty for the human animal is that there is no water here — not in the air, not in the ground, not even as condensation. The place is wetted by mineral sap you cannot and must not drink. You will sweat, and your sweat will not be replaced by anything the environment offers. Bring *everything*. Calculate your water needs and triple them. Carry it in insulated, sealed containers, because the patient heat will warm anything left open and you will lose the comfort of a cool drink within the hour.

Wear loose, light, full-coverage clothing in a pale color — long sleeves, head covering, the desert traveler's wisdom. Do not expose skin to the constant radiant heat, even though it "means no harm." Harm and intent are unrelated here.

**The heat that never changes.** Your body will, as the description says, "forget to argue with it." This is the trap. A constant unbearable heat that the body stops registering means you can be cooking yourself while feeling acclimated. Carry a thermometer and *check it on a schedule, not on a feeling.* Set a timer. Your sensation of temperature has been disabled by the constancy; trust the instrument.

**The keening stone.** The high almost-sound felt in the teeth is low-grade but constant. Over long exposure it will fray your nerves and may damage hearing in ranges you can't perceive consciously. Bring proper ear protection and a vibration-dampening mouthguard if you intend a long stay.

## The Footing

The geology is *sharp-new* — freshly extruded rock with no erosion, no smoothing, edges like fresh-broken glass on a geological scale. Wear the heaviest-soled boots you own. Bring thick gloves. Every surface you might brace against is a wound that hasn't scabbed, and it will open *you* if you fall against it. Move slowly. The whole place moves at the speed of the great wheel; let that govern your pace. Nothing here rewards hurry, and hurrying on knife-new stone in disorienting heat is how you bleed.

Some rock is still cooling. Test surfaces before touching. The singing stone is hot stone.

## The Mind — This Is the Real Preparation

**Carry external memory, religiously.** Distance here is a matter of attention; the world recompiles when you look away; the wheel was always turning the other direction. Your memory cannot be trusted as a record of this place, because the place edits the past to match its present. So *externalize everything.* Keep a written log on physical paper. Photograph, sketch, record. But understand the harder truth: when you check your records against your memory and they disagree, **believe the records.** Train yourself in advance to do this. It is profoundly unnatural. Practice it before you go — the discipline of trusting the page over the certainty in your own head.

**Anchor your scale.** The room cannot decide how large it wants to be, and you will catch the cathedral-vastness or the pocket-smallness depending on your attention. Bring a physical object of *known, fixed size* and keep it on your person — a marked rod, a tool, your own outstretched hand which you have measured. When the walls argue about their distance, refuse to engage. Look at your known object, reassert your own scale, and let the room do what it does. You are the only fixed dimension you can trust.

**Set a hard time limit, mechanically enforced.** The most seductive line in the description is *you could stay a long time.* You could. People do. The place asks nothing of you, threatens nothing, and runs its patient making forever. This is exactly why you must decide your departure *before you arrive* and enforce it with a device that does not consult your feelings — a timer with a loud alarm, ideally two redundant ones, because in a place where the past edits itself you cannot trust your sense of how long you've been present. Time dilates here the way distance does.

**Rehearse the contradictions in advance.** Hot and unfinished. Lush and empty. Machined and untouched. Vast and tiny. Growing and being built with no seam between. You will encounter the place where reality "breaks a little," and the unprepared mind tries to *resolve* the contradiction — to find the line between the piston-arm and the stone column and the living stalk. There is no line. The search for it is what damages you. Prepare yourself the way one prepares for an optical illusion: *I know this cannot be both, I know it is both anyway, I will not strain to fix it.* Let the contradictions take their turns. Do not try to catch the world in the act of choosing. It does not choose, and the attempt to make it choose is the wound it opens in you.

## The Discipline of the Eyes

The breakdown shows most at the edges of your seeing. There are two ways to handle this and you should practice both before going:

- **The soft gaze.** When you must simply *be* in the place — resting, eating, traveling — keep your eyes soft and central, do not chase movement at the periphery, do not snap your attention from wall to frond to wheel. Rapid attention-shifts are what trigger the recompiling. Move your eyes slowly and deliberately, the way you move your feet on the sharp stone.

- **The deliberate audit.** When you *do* want to observe something honestly, fix it, photograph it, write down its state, then look away for a full minute and look back and check. You will sometimes find it changed. Record the change without alarm. You are a scientist of a place that does not hold still; treat its instability as data, not as a personal assault.

## What to Bring, in Sum

- Triple your water, sealed and insulated. No natural source exists.
- Full pale coverage, heavy boots, thick gloves against the cutting new rock.
- A thermometer on a schedule, because your heat-sense will go silent.
- Ear protection and possibly a mouthguard against the keening stone.
- A fixed-size physical reference object kept on your body at all times.
- A physical paper log, camera, recording gear — and the trained reflex to *trust them over yourself.*
- Two redundant mechanical timers set to your predetermined departure.
- A rehearsed mental discipline: do not resolve the contradictions, do not chase the periphery, do not search for the seam.

## The Last and Most Important Thing

Make peace, *before you go*, with the fact that you are visiting a place that has not finished becoming itself. You will be standing inside an act of creation that has no maker present and no ending in sight. The room will breathe. The stone will be new each time you blink. Somewhere above you a machine you will never glimpse will be weaving the next leaf and the next moment of the floor beneath your feet.

You cannot understand this place, and you must not try to. Understanding requires a thing that is *simply there* — and nothing here has yet arrived at being simply there, including, if you stay too long, you. Go as a witness, not as an investigator. Stay short. Keep your scale, keep your records, keep your timer. And when the alarm sounds, leave — even if the room feels small and kind and the heat has stopped arguing and you have just begun to feel that you, too, are something gently under construction.

*Especially* then.
