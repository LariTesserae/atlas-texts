# Preparing for the Underlit Massif

## First, unlearn your eyes

This is the hardest preparation and the one you cannot pack. You will arrive expecting to *see*, and the place will refuse you, and for the first hours — perhaps the first days — your brain will keep throwing its visual processing at a blank wall, generating phantom shapes, false flickers, the swimming pseudo-light of a sensory system screaming into the void. This is exhausting in a way you have never experienced. People who have not prepared describe a kind of seasickness of the soul.

So prepare *before you go*. Spend weeks in true darkness beforehand — not dim rooms, but blacked-out spaces where you learn to move, eat, and rest without sight. Learn to trust your hands. Learn that the absence of light is not the absence of information. The goal is to arrive having already grieved your eyes, so you do not have to grieve them there.

Bring a single dim light source, sealed and rationed — but understand it is a crutch and an insult. Using it will blind you to the moss-glow for an hour afterward, and the moss-glow is the only honest light there is. Save your lamp for emergencies of the hand: a wound, a buckle, a knot. Not for *seeing the world*. The world is not to be seen.

## Dress for the eternal wet

Everything is water and everything is warm. This is not cold-rain misery; it is the saturating, body-temperature wet of being inside a lung.

- **No waterproofing.** It is futile and it traps your own sweat against you, which in this warmth is dangerous. Instead, wear fabrics that move water *through* and away — fast-wicking, fast-shedding, never absorbent. Wool will drown you. Think thin, synthetic, draining.
- **Guard your skin from rot.** Constant moisture against flesh, for days, breeds maceration, fungal bloom, the slow softening of feet and folds. Bring antifungal powders that work wet, change your contact-layers obsessively, and air every part of your body that can be aired. Treat trench foot as your most likely killer. It is.
- **Footing is everything.** Every surface is beaded, slick, running, worn smooth by ages of water. Bring boots with aggressive, self-clearing soles, and bring a staff — a long, sensing staff, your most important tool. You will use it as a third leg, a depth-probe for the runnels and gullets, and a forward antenna in the black. Learn to *read the ground through it* before you arrive.

## Learn to navigate by sound and touch

You will know the world the way the world knows itself.

- **Map by echo.** Practice click-navigation beforehand — tongue-clicks, the cane-tap, learning to hear the difference between a near soft wall of fronds and a far hard wall of weeping stone. The locals — the fauna — do this perfectly. You will do it badly. Do it anyway.
- **Trust the heat.** Your skin is now an organ of vision. The heat-wash from a buried engine, the warmth of a sighing doorway, the cooler drafts the massif manufactures from its own breathing — these are your landmarks. Learn to feel temperature gradients across your face and the backs of your hands.
- **Anchor to the pulse.** The *chunder, chunder* is your compass and your clock. It comes through the stone, through your sternum, before it reaches your ears. Learn its direction. Learn that growing warmth and intensifying condensation means you are walking *inward, downward, deeper*. Learn that the high cold drafts mean *out*. There is no edge to reach, no horizon to steer by — only the pulse, and the gradient of warmth, and your own laid trail.

**Lay a trail.** Bring cord, a great deal of it, and tie-points. You cannot follow your footprints in running water in the dark. The massif has no fixed paths and no fixed light. If you do not string a physical line behind you, you will not come back. This is not caution. This is the single difference between a visitor and a permanent resident.

## Protect your hearing — but do not seal it

The sound here is *enormous*, and it is your primary sense now. The distant falls you feel before you hear; the spore-tick against your skin; the singing hunters; the million-throated chorus timed to the engines.

- Do not wear hard ear-protection — to deafen yourself here is to blind yourself a second time. But know that prolonged exposure to the engine-roar near a surfacing housing could damage you. Carry *filtered* protection that lowers volume without erasing information, and use it only when you must pass close to the turning.
- Learn the local sound-grammar before it learns you. **The hunters sing before they strike — a rising tone.** By the time you place its direction, the briefing says, it is too late. So you must learn this call cold, in advance, from recordings if any exist, so that your body reacts before your mind does. The rising tone means *move now, do not analyze*.

## On the fauna: you are warm, and you can be read

Everything here drinks the heat of you from a body-length away. To the whiskered, the clicking, the eyeless swimmers, you are a blazing beacon of warmth and strange chemistry walking through their dark. You cannot hide. Accept this.

- Do not nest where they nest — the warm housings, the engine-runoff pools. These are taken. To shelter there is to intrude on the oldest tenants.
- Move with the beat, not against it. The whole ecology times itself to the pulse. An arrhythmic thing — *you*, lurching and splashing off-rhythm — reads as wrongness, as prey-distress, as anomaly. The longer you stay, the more the pulse will enter your own chest. **Let it.** Walking in time with the massif is not mysticism; it is camouflage. It makes you part of the body instead of a foreign object in it.

## Food, water, air

- **The water is everywhere but it is not yours.** It runs over engine-leakage, dissolved minerals, the rot of fungal towers, the chemistry of a living system that has nothing to do with your biology. Filter and treat everything, and bring more capacity than you think you need, because here the danger is not thirst but *contamination by abundance*.
- **Eat nothing.** The pale flesh-fronds, the mat-moss, the gilled towers — they eat warmth and minerals and engine-leak. They are built on a chemistry that is not food, and may be poison, and you cannot inspect them by sight to know. Bring all your own calories.
- **The air is thick and warm and near-liquid.** In the deep inward regions it becomes a slow rain that never finishes falling — breathing it is labor. Know your own limits. The warm grows warmer the deeper you go, and warm wet air carries heat into a body that cannot shed it. **Heat exhaustion in total darkness is a quiet, confused, fatal thing.** Carry the means to measure your own core temperature, and turn back toward the cold drafts long before you feel you must.

## The real preparation is psychological

Here is the truth the briefing circles and finally says outright: this is not a place. It is a *body*. You are not visiting a landscape; you are entering something vast and old and alive that has never opened its eyes and does not need to and is *content*.

You must prepare for what this does to a mind.

- **The pulse will get inside you.** Days of *chunder, chunder* through your sternum, days of the million-throated chorus, days of moving in time with a thing the size of a continent — and you will begin to feel yourself dissolve into it. This is pleasant. That is the danger. The sense of being "briefly inside something" can soften into the sense of *belonging* to it, of not needing to leave, of being content as it is content. People do not get lost here only by losing their cord. They get lost by *stopping wanting* the cord.
- **Set a hard external time-limit** and arrange, if you can, an external means of being recalled — a partner at the threshold, an alarm you cannot reason away. Your own judgment about when to leave will erode. Do not rely on it.
- **Keep one small ritual that is entirely yours** — a phrase, a count, a thing your hands do — that is not timed to the pulse, that belongs to the world you came from. A single off-beat thing to remind you that you have an outside, and an eye that wants light, and a reason to go back toward the cold drafts and the strung cord and the door you came in by.

---

Pack light. Pack draining. Pack cord, and a staff, and your own warmth, and a way out, and a piece of yourself that refuses to keep the beat.

Then go in, into the warm and the wet and the singing, and let the great blind body know you by your heat — but do not let it keep you.

Chunder. Chunder.

Follow your cord back out before the dark grows too content with you in it.
