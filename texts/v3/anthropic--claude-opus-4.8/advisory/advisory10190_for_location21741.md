# Preparing to Visit the Brood-Cellar

## Understand What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, internalize the central truth of this place: **you are fast and it is slow, and that asymmetry is your protection.** You will be a flicker to it, a warm spore that wandered in. This is your margin of safety. Everything in your preparation should be designed to *use* that margin and leave before it closes. The warning is literal — it learns names over centuries, but it *is* learning, and you do not want to be present at the moment it finishes a thought about you.

Plan your visit in **minutes to hours, not days.** Treat the place like a deep dive: you have a bottom time, and you respect it absolutely.

## Air and Breathing — Your First Priority

The air is the trap that disguises itself as background detail. Spores fat as dust motes, drifting filaments, a place that "breathes you back" and reads your exhalations. Two facts matter:

- **The place samples you through your breath.** What it does with that sample is unknown, but in an environment where "distinction is provisional" and "nothing is wasted," you do not want your biology catalogued by something building itself out of incorporated material.
- **The spores are almost certainly not inert to your body.** A finished organism would have an immune relationship with this place. You have none.

**Bring a sealed-circuit respirator** — full rebreather if you can manage one, so your exhaled breath is captured and carried out with you rather than fed to the filaments. At minimum, a sealed full-face mask with high-grade particulate filtration (think P100/spore-rated) and your own air supply. Do not "just breathe shallowly." Do not sample the air "to know the smell." The first lungful is the introduction you cannot withdraw.

Carry **redundant air.** Cold slows your body's tolerances and the environment may foul filters faster than expected with that fizzing capillary load.

## Cold

This is real cold — cellar-cold in a country that never warms, the cold that gets into joints and stays. It will sap you faster than it feels like it should, because the dampness and the still, dense air conduct heat away.

- **Insulated, fully enclosed layers.** Sealed at wrists, ankles, neck — this serves the double purpose of warmth and keeping the spores off skin.
- **Active heating** if you can carry it (chemical or battery warmers in the core layer). You'll be sitting still if you want to observe, and stillness in this cold is dangerous.
- **Watch for the lie of slowness.** A slow place invites you to slow with it. Your judgment dulls in cold. Set a hard timer and obey it even when you feel fine.

## Contact — Touch Nothing

The rule is explicit: touch the wall and you may not know whether you touched a surface or an organ. In a thing that is computing and gestating, touch may be *input.* You do not know what you'd be telling it, or starting.

- **Full sealed gloves**, but the deeper rule is **don't touch at all.** Float through. Keep your hands to yourself.
- **Do not sit directly on the floor**, the warning notwithstanding. If you must rest, bring a sealed sit-pad that goes out with you. The fibrous floor is the same thinking-meat as the walls.
- **Do not collect samples.** Resist this completely. Taking a creature, a filament, a bead of water means carrying live, unfinished, beyond-modern biology into your own world — and it means *removing* something from a system where nothing is wasted, which may be the most noticeable thing you can do.

## The Light and the Clock

The light is dim and steady — a bruised blue-green that pools like cold milk. Bring **your own light**, but use it sparingly and warmly-filtered. Sudden bright light may not matter to a thing this slow, but you don't know what the glowing creatures register, and you don't want to find out.

More important: **bring a reliable clock you trust completely**, ideally two. The place keeps time — the dripping is sequenced, the clicking is a counter. Time here is *meaningful to the location,* and time perception is exactly what dulls in cold, quiet, rhythmic, oxygen-careful conditions. The deep chest-felt pulse and the metronome clicking can entrain you. People have lost hours to far less hypnotic environments. **Set an alarm. When it sounds, you go up. No "one more drop."**

## Sound and the Pulse

The thrum is felt in the breastbone before it's heard — sub-audible, organ-low. Prolonged low-frequency exposure can cause genuine physiological effects: nausea, anxiety, disorientation, a sense of dread or presence. Know this in advance so you don't mistake an acoustic effect for a supernatural one and panic, or worse, mistake real danger for "just the hum."

Consider **light ear protection** that dampens without sealing you off entirely — you want the edge off the resonance, but you also want to hear yourself and your own equipment.

## Movement and Navigation

The chamber is chapel-sized, so you won't get lost in scale — but it's dense, full of presences, with shadows moving *inside* the walls and clustered breathing things overhead. Disorientation here is sensory, not spatial.

- **Mark your entry point** with something of your own (a beacon, a light) so the four-meter drop you came through is always findable. You came down without stairs; make sure your way up is rigged and ready *before* you commit to exploring. **Set your exit first.** A rope, a ladder, a partner above — whatever your method, test it before you descend fully.
- **Move slowly and deliberately** — not to honor the place, but because the floor is living and uneven and the light is poor.
- **Don't go toward the clicking.** You won't find what makes it, the text tells you, and looking for the source of the metronome is exactly how a short visit becomes a long one.

## The Psychological Brief

The hardest preparation is mental. This place is **not hostile and not kind — only occupied.** That neutrality is unnerving in a way that danger isn't. You will feel watched, slowly, by something with no intention toward you yet. You may feel the pull to stay, to understand, to wait for the slow sequence of falling drops to "say something." This is the real hazard.

Rehearse this before you go: **You will not be present long enough to receive an answer, and you do not want to be.** The place's attention is the danger. Being interesting to it is the danger. Go as an observer who intends to be forgotten.

## The Itinerary

1. Rig your exit. Test it.
2. Seal your suit, start your air, start your clock.
3. Descend. Mark your entry beacon.
4. Stand still one moment, let the chest-pulse and the cold register, confirm your gear holds.
5. Observe — the falling drops, the glowing clusters, the silver-trailed grazers — touching nothing, taking nothing, breathing your own air.
6. When the alarm sounds, **go up.** Even mid-thought. Especially mid-thought.

## The One Rule Above All

Leave before it learns your name. It would only take a few hundred years — but the point is that the counting has already started, and the only winning move is to not be there when it finishes. Go down briefly. Witness the cold green mind dreaming itself into being. Then climb back into your fast warm world and let the cellar forget the brief flicker that walked through it.
