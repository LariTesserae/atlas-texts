# Preparing to Visit the Sump Works

## Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Before anything else, internalize the central fact: this is a *slow* place. Whatever instincts you bring from quick worlds — the readiness to flinch, to react, to brace against sudden things — will sit uselessly tense in your shoulders for the whole visit. The danger here isn't speed. It's the opposite. The danger is duration, accumulation, the way the place works on you a degree at a time until you don't notice you've changed temperature.

So the most important preparation is mental, and it's this: **bring a way to measure time that does not depend on you.** Your own sense of it will fail. The light won't change. The crickets won't quiet. Bring a windup timer, or two of them, set to chime at fixed intervals — not to keep a schedule but to keep a self. When the chime sounds and you're surprised by it, that surprise is data. It tells you how far under you've drifted.

## What to Wear

Dress for a sauna you cannot leave. Lightweight, breathable, quick to dry — and accept that nothing will actually dry, because the air is already full of water and won't take any more from you. You will be damp the entire time. Plan around the damp rather than fighting it.

Wear closed shoes with aggressive grip and a sole that won't be eaten by lime-paste or algae. Much of the floor "hasn't agreed to be a floor." You'll be walking on partly-set stone that takes a thumbprint, on catwalks slick with snail-track and moth-fall, on thresholds where stalactites are growing down to bite the doorways shut. Test every surface. Distrust the wet places especially.

Cover your skin, but lightly. Long sleeves and long trousers in thin fabric — not for warmth, which you won't want, but as a barrier between you and everything that lives here. You do not want crickets in your collar or a plum-sized snail discovering your neck. A hat with a brim, and a fine net you can drop over your face when you pass through the dense reaches where the moths layer the rails.

## For the Senses

**Hearing.** The fauna make a continuous pressure of sound, "loud as rain on a tin roof, and as constant." Bring earplugs — not to block it, but to *modulate* it. The real hazard of constant sound is that your brain edits it out, and once the crickets "sound like silence" you've lost a sense you'll need on the way out. Use the plugs in shifts: take them out periodically and let the wall of shrilling hit you fresh. Re-hearing it is how you remember it's there.

**Smell.** Bats, droppings, fungus, stagnant warm water, corroding iron. It won't be intolerable, but it will be heavy and organic and total. If you're sensitive, a dab of something sharp under the nose at intervals — not to mask it, again, but to break the habituation.

**Light.** The native light is "the color of weak tea," gold-brown, dim, enough only if you know the work. You don't know the work. Bring your own light — but a *warm* one, low and amber, matched roughly to the furnace-glow. A harsh white beam will feel like an assault in here, and worse, it will destroy your dark-adapted eyes the moment you turn it off. Use it sparingly, in the long empty reaches between the crowded places, and let your eyes do most of the work otherwise.

## Provisions

Bring more water than you think you need, and keep it sealed and cool against your body if you can — the heat will work on everything. **Drink on a schedule, not by thirst.** In steady heat, sweating into already-saturated air, you will dehydrate without the warning of thirst arriving in time. Electrolytes, salt. Light food you don't have to think about.

Do not eat or drink anything found there. The fish are blind and milk-white, the fungus pale, the moss livid — this is not a larder. Carry everything in and out.

## Navigation

You cannot find your way the first time. Accept this. The place is small enough to cross in the time a kettle boils, and labyrinthine enough that you won't. **Lay a line.** Old method, best method — a cord paid out behind you from the bottom of the stairs, so that whatever happens to your sense of direction, the way back is a physical thing in your hand. The forty shallow steps are your only exit and your whole life depends on rejoining them.

Mark the stairfoot itself, brightly, with something that contrasts against the gold-brown gloom. You want to be able to find the coin of daylight even when you've half-forgotten it exists.

## The Real Discipline

Here is the part no equipment solves.

The text gives you the law plainly: *stay too long and the place will finish deciding what you are.* The seduction of the Sump Works is comfort. The heat begins to feel like your own skin. The slow machines begin to make sense to you "the way you understand a sleeping body beside you." This is pleasant. This is the trap. The understanding you feel growing is the place understanding you back, drawing you into its tide-pace, its patient circuit of water and heat that never hurries and never ends.

So set a hard limit *before* you descend, agree it with yourself out loud, and obey the timer over your own judgment. Because your judgment is exactly what the place edits. When the chime says go, you go — even if you feel fine, *especially* if you feel fine, because feeling fine down there is the symptom, not the all-clear.

Travel with a partner if you possibly can, and make a pact: either of you can call the ascent, and the other obeys without argument. Two people drift at different rates. The one who's still surfaced enough to feel uneasy is the one who's right.

## On the Way Out

Climb the forty steps deliberately. Feel the air thin and cool as you rise, the cricket-song fall away, the coin of brightness widen back into a sky. Notice each of these as it happens — name them to yourself. This is how you confirm the place let go of you, that you carried nothing of its slow weather up into the fast bright world.

And when you reach the top, look back down once, at the warm dark you came out of, and be glad you brought a timer.
