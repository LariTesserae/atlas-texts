# Preparing for the Glasshouse Ward

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

The Glasshouse Ward is small but deceptively demanding. It is not dangerous in any dramatic sense — there are no predators, no obvious threats, no extreme cold or heat. The challenge is *saturation*. Everything here is warm, wet, loud, and slow, and the place will work on your body and your nerves in ways you won't notice until you're well inside it. Prepare for environmental siege, not adventure.

The single most important mental adjustment: **this is a place where almost nothing moves but water and machine.** The absence of animal life is unnerving in a way most travelers underestimate. Your nervous system, tuned by millions of years of evolution to expect birdsong, insect hum, the rustle of small creatures, will keep waiting for sounds that never come. The mechanical loudness will *not* satisfy that expectation. Brace for a low, persistent wrongness that has no specific cause. It is not haunted. It is just biologically empty in a way humans find hard to sit with.

## Clothing and Body

- **Accept the wet.** Do not fight it. Waterproofing is futile here — the moisture comes out of the air itself, condensing on every cooler surface including your skin. Goretex and rain shells will trap your own sweat and make you more miserable, not less. Instead wear **fast-drying technical synthetics or merino wool**, layered thin. You want fabrics that stay functional when soaked and don't chafe or grow heavy.
- **Footwear is the critical decision.** The ground is not ground — it is water with a crust of root. Wear **closed boots with aggressive tread and ankle support**, sized to be drained and worn wet, or commit fully to **purpose-made wading footwear**. Whatever you choose, expect to sink to the ankle without warning. Test every step on unfamiliar ground with a probe before committing your weight. Bring a **walking staff** — it doubles as a depth probe and a balance aid on the slick, rounded, furred surfaces.
- **Protect your feet from prolonged immersion.** Trench foot is the most realistic injury risk here. Bring multiple pairs of wool socks, change them when you can, and apply anti-fungal/barrier balm to your feet before entering and after leaving. Your skin will macerate in this humidity.
- **Cover exposed skin lightly.** Not against animals — there are almost none — but against the **bacterial slicks**. Every wet surface, especially the iron-eating orange crusts and the liverwort curtains, is glazed with microbial life. You don't know what those microbes are or how they interact with human tissue. Treat all surfaces as potentially irritating or infectious. Wear thin gloves. Don't touch your face.

## Respiratory and Infection Precautions

This is where an ordinary traveler should be most careful.

- **The air is full of moisture and, almost certainly, full of spores and aerosolized microbes.** The pumps, steam vents, and standing pools create conditions ideal for airborne biological material. Bring a **good particulate respirator (P100 / FFP3 class)** and wear it, especially near the steam vents and the central machinery where aerosolization is heaviest. The warm, wet air is a vector for things your immune system has never encountered across the multiverse divide.
- **Do not drink the water. Do not let it touch broken skin.** Iron-eating bacteria, mineral leaching, generations of patient decay — this water is a chemical and biological unknown. Carry all your own sealed water and food.
- **Cover any open wounds completely before entry** and check yourself afterward. The warm decay-rich environment is a perfect culture medium.
- Pack **broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals** in your medical kit, plus an aggressive skin-cleaning regimen for after the visit.

## Hearing and Disorientation

- **Bring hearing protection** — earplugs or muffs rated for sustained mechanical noise. The pump-thud, the steam hiss, the doubled-back flywheel clatter, the endless drip: this is not a quiet retreat. Prolonged exposure to that reciprocating thud will fatigue you and may damage hearing over a long visit.
- **The green confuses direction.** You cannot locate the central machinery by ear because the wet walls throw the sound around. The canopy hides the sky. The corridors are organic and similar. **Do not rely on landmarks or sense of direction.** Carry a **compass and, ideally, mark your entry corridor** with something visible and non-disruptive (a bright tie around a bar where you felt the iron through the moss). Twenty minutes around the perimeter sounds trivial, but inside, disoriented, you could spend much longer finding your way out.

## What to Bring

- Probe staff
- P100/FFP3 respirator + spares
- Hearing protection
- Thin gloves, multiple pairs
- Wool socks, multiple pairs; foot balm
- Sealed water and food (do not consume anything local)
- Compass; bright marking material for your entry point
- Headlamp — the light is "bright enough to read by, dim enough you keep wanting to look up," but the corridors vary, and you want hands-free illumination in deeper-grown passages
- Comprehensive medical kit weighted toward infection, fungal, and skin care
- A waterproof case for anything that must stay dry (documents, return-travel devices, electronics)
- A change of fully dry clothing kept sealed *outside* the ward, for after you leave

## Pacing and Mindset

- **Keep your visit deliberately short the first time.** The place will not change between visits — it is essentially permanent, settled, slow. There is no urgency to see it all at once, and every benefit to leaving before saturation and the eerie silence-of-creatures grind you down.
- **Move slowly and test the ground constantly.** The whole environment rewards patience and punishes haste. A wrong step into deep mud, alone, far from your marked exit, with no animal life and no other travelers, is the realistic worst-case scenario.
- **Go with at least one companion if possible.** Not for protection from anything — for the disorientation, the silence, and the simple safety of mud and slick surfaces.
- **Expect to feel watched, or expectant, and know it's an illusion.** The machines run with no visible hand at them. That detail will sit in your mind. There is no evidence of anything tending you or watching you — the strangeness is mundane, the place is just *like this*. But your instincts will keep insisting otherwise. Let them murmur and don't be ruled by them.

## After You Leave

The pump-thud follows you past the fence and dies into ordinary distance. **Your clothes will stay damp for hours.** Change out of everything as soon as you're clear. Wash thoroughly. Watch your feet and any skin contact points for days afterward for signs of fungal or bacterial response. Keep your contaminated clothing isolated until it can be properly cleaned or discarded.

You will not be harmed by this place if you respect what it is: warm, wet, loud, patient, and indifferent. It has held its shape for longer than anyone remembers and will not notice your visit at all.
