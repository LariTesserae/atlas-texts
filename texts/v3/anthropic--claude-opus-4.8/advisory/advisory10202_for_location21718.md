# Preparing to Visit the Condensary

## Understand the Climate First — Everything Else Follows

You are going to a place that is *warm and永久 wet*, where the air itself is saturated past comfort and the surfaces never dry. This is not rain you can wait out. There is no waiting it out. Plan for the wet the way a diver plans for water: as the medium, not the weather.

**Clothing.** Forget waterproofing in the keeping-dry sense — you will not stay dry, and trying to will only make you miserable and overheated. What you want is clothing that is *comfortable while wet and dries against your skin*: thin merino wool, certain technical synthetics, things that insulate even when soaked and don't grow heavy or chafe. Avoid cotton entirely; it will sit on you like a cold poultice and never recover. Bring several identical changes, not because you'll be dry between them but because rotating lets one set drip while you wear the other. A light wool layer for the evenings — the warmth is steady but the wet pulls heat off your skin, and on a high catwalk in the steam you'll feel a chill creep in despite the temperature.

**Footwear** is the real decision. The iron catwalks are *slick with condensation, permanently*. You want aggressive, siped rubber soles — boat-deck or climbing-approach tread — and you want them broken in. A fall here is the easy way to die: not from the height alone but from going over the rail into the herd, into four meters of drop onto living grey backs that will not notice you and will not stop. Treat every walkway as wet ice that happens to be made of riveted metal.

## Your Body Against the Damp

This climate is hostile to human skin and lungs over time even though it will not kill you quickly.

- **Fungal infection** is the certainty you must manage. The "slow green" crust and the universal moisture mean your feet, your folds, anywhere skin meets skin, will want to rot. Bring antifungal powder and cream in quantity. Dry your feet whenever you can find a warm dry surface — they will be rare; ration them. Sleep barefoot if you can keep your feet off wet bedding.
- **Bring waterproofed containers** for everything you cannot let absorb water: medicine, electronics, paper, salt, anything that spoils or shorts. Assume that whatever is not sealed is wet.
- **Your respiratory system** will be working in air thick with steam, animal breath, rendering-exhaust, and the spores of the only flora. A few light particulate masks for the days near the rendering-engines won't hurt, though the locals breathe it lifelong. If you have asthma or any reactive airway, reconsider the trip or come heavily medicated.
- **Skin maceration** — prolonged wet softens and breaks skin. Keep small wounds covered and watched; infection in this air will not stay small.

## Provisioning

**Water, paradoxically, is the easy part** — but only if you respect the law. The whole economy is condensation, and *water belongs to whoever's roof catches it*. Do not drink from a cistern, a coil, or a runoff groove without an explicit arrangement. To a traveler it looks like the place is drowning in free water; to the locals, taking uncatchment water is theft of the most fundamental kind. Arrange your water supply formally on arrival — buy cistern-rights or pay for daily ration — and you will be fine. Steal a cup and you have committed the local equivalent of grand larceny.

**Food.** The local diet is tallow, bone-meal products, the rendered yield of the slow death of the herds, and likely cultivated fungal crust. It will be strange, possibly nutritionally complete, possibly missing things your body expects. Bring supplements and some familiar dense rations to bridge your adjustment. Do not assume your gut bacteria are ready for this biome — bring rehydration salts and basic GI medicine, because a stomach upset in a place where you cannot freely take water is a genuine danger.

## Learn the Two Laws Before You Step Off the Catwalk

The Condensary is *at peace* and the peace has two pillars. Violate either and you are not an eccentric foreigner; you are an existential threat.

1. **Never touch a living beast.** Not to kill — that goes without saying — but understand the depth of the taboo. The herds are the foundation, the ground, the water-source, sacred and pragmatic at once. Do not climb down. Do not lean a load against them. Do not feed them, herd them, frighten them, or experiment. The animals are *the floor of the world* and you do not damage the floor. You may, when arranged, work the dead. The dead are economy. The living are bedrock.

2. **Never introduce speed.** This is subtler and the one a traveler is most likely to break out of good intentions. The entire civilization's only true fear is *anything that comes too fast* — a sudden drain on a cistern, a quick thinning of a herd, an efficiency, an improvement, a shortcut. If you arrive thinking you'll *help* — show them a faster condenser, a better render, a way to do in a day what takes a season — you will be seen, correctly, as someone setting fire to the foundation. Do not optimize anything. Do not accelerate anything. Match the pace. The single greatest gift you can give this place is to not change it.

## Calibrate Your Mind to the Pace

This is the preparation no gear can do for you. The Condensary moves at the speed of digestion. A wall is repaired by grandchildren. Nothing resolves in a day; nothing needs to. A human from a fast world will feel a deep itch here — a sense that *something should be happening* — and that itch will make you stupid and rude. Spend time before you go practicing slowness: long unhurried tasks, patience without payoff, the discipline of letting things take the time they take. Arrive impatient and you will offend everyone and understand nothing.

## On the Senses

There is **no silence**, ever — the chest-deep rumble of the herd, the two-note sigh of the condenser-pumps, the high skirl of the bird-smoke. Bring earplugs not to escape it (you can't) but to *modulate* it for sleep until you acclimate. River-people sleep in the river. You will learn, but the first nights are hard. Don't fight the sound; let it become the new silence.

The **light** is ordinary and kind — yellow electric lamps through the mist, enough to read by. This is the one familiar comfort. Bring a good sealed lamp of your own as backup, and a book in a waterproof case, and let the evening on a high catwalk be exactly what it is.

## What to Pack, Condensed

- Wool and quick-recovering synthetics; no cotton; multiple sets
- Aggressively grippy, broken-in rubber-soled boots
- Antifungal powder and cream, in quantity
- Sealed containers for all moisture-sensitive goods
- Rehydration salts, GI medicine, basic wound care, antibiotics
- Particulate masks for the rendering-districts
- Supplements and bridge-rations
- Earplugs, a sealed lamp, a sealed book
- Means to *formally purchase* water-rights on arrival
- Above all: the patience to add nothing, change nothing, and hurry nothing

## The Visit Itself

Go expecting to be *small and slow inside something vast and slower.* Don't go to do; go to stand on the catwalk at evening and feel the wet on your face whether or not it's raining, the lamps coming up gold through the steam, the grey sea of backs moving beneath your feet from one curved horizon to the other, and the whole world breathing its patient two-note breath.

You came from a world that fears stillness. This one fears speed. The traveler who survives the Condensary, and is welcomed back, is the one who learns — for a little while — to want what it wants: to be exactly this, durably, and at peace.
