# Preparing to Visit the Underworks at Cistern Level

## Understand What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, settle your mind around the central fact: this is a working place, not a ruin and not a wilderness. You are not exploring something abandoned to you — you are trespassing through a machine that is still running, one that does not know you're there and was not built with you in mind. Carry yourself accordingly. Touch little. Assume every channel, every grate, every humming pillar has a function you don't understand and a schedule you can't predict. The valve that cycles "periodically" may cycle while your hand is near it.

The second thing to internalize: the laws here are local and partial. Water climbs. Some certainties you carry in your body — that things fall, that downhill is safe, that a film of moisture obeys gravity — are negotiable in this place. Don't fight this realization when it comes. Let the floor of your certainty tilt, feel it, and then keep walking. People who panic at the first uphill leaf tend to make worse decisions about the things that actually matter, like footing.

## Clothing and the Body

**Dress for patient cold and total wet.** This is the cold that defeats most travelers because it doesn't announce itself. There's no wind, no frost — just a settling chill that comes up through your soles and works inward over hours. Cotton will kill you slowly here by holding moisture against your skin. Wear wool or synthetic base layers, the kind that insulate when damp. Layer so you can manage exertion without sweating into your clothes, because sweat plus this cold equals a slow drain you won't notice until your hands stop working.

**Footwear is the single most important choice.** You need waterproof boots with aggressive grip — the glazed herringbone brick is treacherous when wet, and it is always wet. The newly-set tilework with its "sharp clean corners" is if anything more slippery than the old. Get boots that come up the calf at minimum; you will be crossing channels and stepping near pools whose depth you cannot judge in the dim. Bring a second pair of socks, sealed dry, and the discipline to actually change into them.

**Cover your hands.** The air condenses on the backs of your hands and runs — this is the cold finding its way in. Waterproof gloves with grip, thin enough to handle a lamp and a line.

**Cover your head, not for warmth alone but because things drip from a ceiling you can't see,** and conveyors run up there carrying pale buckets of something you have not identified and should not let fall on you.

## Light

The place is dim but lit — do not let the strung lamps fool you into traveling light. Those lamps are for the machine, not for you, and they fail at distances where you'll still need to see. Bring:

- A primary headlamp, hands-free, with a wide flood for footing.
- A focused beam for reaching out into the dark between pillars, where the eye "loses them before it finds the far wall."
- A backup light, sealed and waterproof, carried separately on your body.
- Spare power for all of it. Cold drains batteries; keep your spares warm against your core.

Light discipline matters for another reason: the **pale animal** at the edge of the lamplight does not fear you because it has never learned what you are. Don't teach it fear and don't teach it appetite. A sudden bright beam in its blind face means nothing to it, but your behavior toward it might. Let it pass. It belongs here and you do not.

## Hearing and Orientation

The sound never stops and it has weather in it — the pumps surging into phase and falling out, the humming pillars beating against each other in pulses you feel in your chest. **Bring filtered hearing protection,** the kind that dampens the sustained roar without deafening you to a nearby change — a valve cycling, water beginning to climb a channel near your feet, the new note of a sump about to empty in a hollow boom.

This soundscape will mess with your sense of time and direction. The humming pillars are tonal landmarks if you learn them, but they're unreliable; the place is young and changing. **Do not navigate by sound or by the lamps.** Carry a written or scratched record of your route — count pillars, note the half-finished bays with their standing rebar, mark the channels where water runs uphill (these are stable enough features to navigate by, being engineered around). A compass may behave strangely given the upward pull on water; trust your physical breadcrumbs more.

## Water Discipline

The whole hall is plumbed and the whole hall is dynamic. Treat all moving water as hostile. Never stand in a channel, never step into a trough to cross it — the pipes are "wide enough to walk into," which means water moves with force enough to take you into one. The sumps empty suddenly. The marsh in the failed corners hides depth and hides the soft-edged drop into a grate.

Do not drink anything here. The animals drink from the troughs because they've adapted across generations; you have not. Bring all your own water, sealed, kept from freezing against your body.

## What to Bring for the Mind

This is harder to pack but more important. The place is "becoming both a machine and a world at once," and that ambiguity works on people. The loneliness of the scale — walking a long time between things, the generous and empty hall — combines with the cold and the relentless tidal noise to produce a particular kind of dread, a sense of having descended below where any return is. 

Bring a companion if you can; bring a fixed turnaround time and obey it; bring something small and warm and ordinary — a thing from the surface world — to hold against the conviction this place breeds that there was never a surface at all. Set a hard rule before you descend: you do not improve, do not extend, do not "just see what's past the next pillar." The growing edge of this world extends itself into the dark indefinitely. You should not.

## The One Rule to Carry Down the Stairs

Everything here is made, and everything here is doing things its makers didn't plan. Move through it as a guest who understands he is the only thing present that the system did not account for — neither water, nor moss, nor beetle, nor blind pale animal, nor humming column. You have no place in the spaces the engineers left empty. Fit yourself into none of them. Walk lightly, change your socks, watch the leaf go uphill, and climb back out before the cold has finished working inward.
