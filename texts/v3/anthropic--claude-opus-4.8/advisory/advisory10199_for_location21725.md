# Preparing for the High Country, Unfinished

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, you must accept what this place *is* — not a destination but a moment, a world caught in its own first act of self-definition. You are not visiting a landscape; you are visiting a process. Pack accordingly, but also prepare your mind: most of your instincts about how a place behaves over time will be wrong here. Erosion is your absent companion everywhere else in the multiverse — the great softener, the thing that makes terrain *legible*. Here it does not exist. You will need to relearn how to read ground.

## The Air — Your First and Worst Problem

The single most lethal feature is the **thin, cold-burning air at extreme elevation**. This is not negotiable and not survivable unaided.

- **Bring a closed-loop oxygen system**, not open-flow bottles you'd use for a mountain summit. You may be here for *days* — the text says you could walk for days — and open systems won't last. A rebreather or pressure-regulated rig with redundant scrubbing.
- **Carry a backup independent of the primary.** If your main rig fails in the dark, you want a second source you can switch to by feel.
- **Pressurize your sleep.** If you rest, do it inside a small pressurized bivy or tent. Sleeping at this altitude on ambient air will kill you slowly through the night.
- **Acclimatization won't save you** — this is beyond the altitudes a human body adapts to. Treat it like vacuum-adjacent: technology, not toughness.

The detail that *no fog forms when you exhale* tells you the air is desperately dry as well as thin. Your respiratory tract will crack and bleed. **Heated, humidified inspired air** if your system allows it. Otherwise expect nosebleeds, a raw throat, and a cough that won't quit.

## The Dryness — A Slow Theft

There is **no water. None.** Not hidden, not frozen — absent. This is the hard logistical wall.

- **You must carry every drop you will drink, plus margin, plus margin again.** No filtration gear, no condensation traps, no snow to melt. The dryness is *total*; you cannot pull moisture from this world.
- The dryness will pull moisture *from you* — through your skin, your breath, your eyes. **Seal yourself.** Wraparound sealed goggles (your eyes will dry to sandpaper otherwise), a face covering, gloves, full skin coverage.
- **Lip and skin protection**, heavy occlusive balms. You will chap until you split.
- Counterintuitively, **you may not feel thirsty** in time — the dry air wicks sweat before you notice it, so you never feel "wet" enough to register loss. **Drink on a schedule, by the clock, not by thirst.** Set timers. The clarity the dryness brings is also the clarity of dehydration sneaking up clean and quiet.

## The Heat From Below — Manage Both Directions

The rock is **warm — held heat from a still-cooling world.** You feel it through your soles. This creates a strange thermal split: warm ground, cold thin air, no sun.

- **Insulate your feet from the ground, but not too much** — you'll want to *feel* the warmth changing, because temperature shifts in the rock are your warning that the ground is about to move. A boot with a firm, conductive-but-protective sole. Don't seal yourself off from the floor entirely; the floor is talking to you.
- **The heat is uneven and rising.** Fresh-cleaved faces may be hotter. Test before you touch or sit. Carry a simple non-contact thermometer to read surfaces before you commit weight to them.
- **Dress for the cold air, not the warm rock.** The air is the thing that will give you frostbite while your boots stay toasty. Layered, sealed cold-weather gear over everything.

## Seeing in the Ember-Light

The light is **a deep red-bronze glow with no source**, like the last coals across a wide room — enough to move, not enough to trust.

- **Do not bring white light as your primary.** It will blind you to the ambient glow the moment you turn it off, and your dark-adaptation will take many minutes to return — minutes during which the ground may be rising at your back. Use it sparingly, for close inspection only.
- **Bring low-level red or amber light** that works *with* the existing ember-glow rather than fighting it. You want to enhance contrast, not erase the world.
- **Carry a passive light intensifier / image enhancer**, not thermal imaging — everything here is warm, so thermal will wash out into uselessness. You want to amplify the faint visible glow.
- **Train yourself to move by the pooled light at the bases of things.** The heights are shadow; the floor is where the ember collects. Read the world from its feet up. This will feel wrong and you must practice it before you trust your steps to it.

## The Moving Ground — Your Real Adversary

This is the part no other place will have prepared you for. **The land builds itself.** Crystals push up, ridges shoulder skyward, plates tilt and lock. It is mostly silent, punctuated by grinds, ticks, groans, and sharp cracks.

- **The crucial survival skill is the one the text names: *listen for the silence ending.*** Sound is the exception here, which means sound is *information*. A grind underfoot, a tick, a low groan — these precede movement. Learn the vocabulary of the noises before you rely on it. Stand still on arrival, in a safe-seeming spot, and just *listen* for a long while, mapping the sounds to the movements you see.
- **Never stand on a fresh, sharp-faced plate.** Raw cleaved faces are sharp as obsidian — they will cut boots, gloves, skin, tent floor. They also tend to be the youngest, most active surfaces. Move on older-looking ground where you can — though "older" here may mean only hours.
- **Watch for upward growth in your peripheral vision.** Crystals rise "slow at the edge of vision, then suddenly a hand-span taller." A spire may appear where you were about to set your pack. **Do not leave gear unattended on the ground** — it may be lifted, tilted off a ledge, or pierced from beneath.
- **Plan camps on the broadest, flattest plates** you can find, and **expect to relocate.** Do not anchor anything you cannot abandon. Do not pitch over a seam between plates — that's where the locking and grinding happens.
- **Carry trekking poles or a probe staff.** Test the ground ahead and to the sides, feeling for the warmth and the give that say "this is about to move."
- **The sharp edges mean you should overbuild your gear's durability.** Reinforced soles, cut-resistant gloves, a groundsheet of something that won't shred. Bring repair material — patches, tape — because you will gash things.

## The Made Things — Approach With Discipline

You will encounter **walls, cairns, doorways, standing stones** — deliberate-seeming structures that no one made. The text is honest about how the mind rebels against this and keeps reaching for "builders."

- **Bring a recording instrument and document everything** — measurements, the fitting of the stones, the orientation of doorways. You are perhaps the first observer of a phenomenon where complexity falls naturally into the *shape* of intention. This is the scientific prize of the place. Record dispassionately.
- **Guard your own mind.** The pull to believe in builders, to feel watched, to read meaning into the markers, will grow the longer you stay — especially in the silence and the dark. **Decide in advance how you'll handle the loneliness of a place that mimics company.** This is a psychological hazard as real as the air. Bring a steady internal practice, an anchor, a logbook you write in plainly.
- **Do not rely on the structures as landmarks** — the growing ground swallows walls from beneath, half-burying them between visits. Anything you use to find your way back may be *gone, risen, or relocated* by the time you return. **Navigate by inertial / dead-reckoning tools, not by features.** Drop your own markers if you must, but expect the land to eat them too.

## The Film — Handle as Unknown Life

The faint **verdigris bloom on the youngest rock** is the first life, eating warmth and raw chemistry. It is precious and it is alien.

- **Do not contaminate it, and do not let it contaminate you.** This is first-life, possibly the only life. Sterile sampling protocols, sealed containers. Touch nothing barehanded.
- **It finds new stone within an hour or a day** — which makes it a useful *clock.* A clean fresh face means the cleaving just happened, perhaps moments ago; you are standing somewhere very active. A film-covered face is older, more settled, safer. **Read the bloom as a timestamp on the ground.**

## Navigation and Extraction

Because **nothing flows, nothing travels, nothing pools** — there are no rivers to follow downhill, no drainage to read, no settled trails. The usual wayfinding logic of a landscape is absent.

- **Inertial navigation, a fixed beacon at your entry point, and a hard rule about turnaround time and oxygen reserves.** This is the single most important discipline. Set a point of no return based on your air and water, and obey it absolutely.
- **Mark your entry/exit point with something the ground cannot easily bury or move** — anchored high, or beaconed, and logged by coordinate. Assume the land will try to swallow it.
- **The country is vast** ("days" of walking). Don't overcommit. The drama of the place invites wandering deeper. The dryness and the air budget forbid it.

## What to Carry in Your Mind

This is a world *before* almost everything — before water, before life beyond a film, before weather, before time has touched a single stone. You are arriving at the **dawn of form.** Travel quietly. Take little, leave nothing, change nothing if you can help it. You are a witness to a world deciding what it will be, and the only correct posture is the same one the place itself keeps: **mostly silent, watchful, patient — listening for the moment the silence ends.**
