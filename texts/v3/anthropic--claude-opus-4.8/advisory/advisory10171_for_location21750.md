# Preparing to Visit the Basin of Disobedient Water

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, understand the nature of your destination. This is not a hostile place, but it is an *indifferent* one — and indifference at altitude kills more reliably than malice. The basin will not notice if you die in it. The same water that climbs the spires will fill in the hollow where you fall, freeze over you in the night, melt off you in the morning, faithful to its small ceremony regardless of whether you breathe.

You are preparing to visit a place that does not need you. Prepare accordingly: bring everything you need to survive, and assume the place will offer nothing.

---

## The Body: Surviving the Thin Air

The single most dangerous fact about this location is the altitude. "Near the top of the world" with air "thin enough that sound forgets how to travel" means you are at an elevation where the unacclimatized human body begins to fail within hours.

**Acclimatization is non-negotiable.** Do not arrive directly. If your multiverse transit allows for it, stage your approach — spend days at progressively higher elevations in this world or an analog before crossing in. Your blood needs time to thicken, your breathing to deepen. Arriving cold at full altitude invites high-altitude cerebral and pulmonary edema, both of which can kill in a night.

**Bring supplemental oxygen.** Several canisters, more than you think you need. The sound-deadening thinness described here suggests an elevation where even acclimatized travelers struggle. Keep one accessible at all times. If you find yourself confused, euphoric, or struggling to complete simple tasks, descend or breathe — your judgment is the first thing the altitude takes, and you won't notice it leaving.

**Acetazolamide** and **dexamethasone** in your medical kit. Know the symptoms of altitude sickness in yourself and in any companion. The brilliant light and the strange beauty will mask the slow theft of your faculties. *Do not trust your own assessment of how you feel up there.*

---

## The Light: Defending Against Brilliance

The text is explicit: the light has weight, it floods from above and below, and shadow is a rare and hoarded thing. This is a place where you can go blind.

**Glacier goggles** — not sunglasses, but proper mountaineering eyewear with side shields and the darkest tint you can find. The reflected light from pale stone and scattered pools will deliver UV from every angle, including upward. Snow blindness here would be *light* blindness, and it can come within an hour of exposure.

**Bring a spare pair.** A cracked lens at this altitude is a medical emergency.

**Full skin coverage.** Long sleeves, a brimmed hat that works under your hood, a face covering. Zinc oxide on any exposed skin — lips, the underside of your nose, the rims of your nostrils, anywhere the upward-reflected light can reach that ordinary sun never touches.

---

## The Cold: The Paradox of Brilliance Without Warmth

This is the trap the place sets. Your eyes will tell you it is bright, blazing, hot. Your skin will tell you nothing because the cold here is "clean, mineral," without the bite that warns you. The sun pours down and the stone stays cold.

**Dress in layers built for active cold,** the kind a climber wears: a wicking base, insulating mid-layers, a windproof shell. You will sweat in the radiance and freeze in the shade of a spire, sometimes within a single minute as you move between tiers.

**Protect your extremities aggressively.** The cold without rot-smell is the cold of a place where nothing decays — a deep, dry, killing cold. Insulated gloves, a backup pair, good boots, gaiters.

**Watch the transition zones.** When you step into the lee of a spire — into that "cold water in a dry land" of hoarded shadow — the temperature will drop suddenly. Don't linger there sweating.

---

## Water and Sustenance

Counterintuitively, you will dehydrate quickly. Thin dry air strips moisture from your lungs with every breath, and the cold suppresses your thirst.

**Carry your own water, insulated against freezing.** Yes, the pools are present, clear, and cold. **Do not drink from them without filtration and treatment.** Though the place seems sterile — no moss, no lichen, "a world before life, or after it" — you cannot trust that assessment across a multiverse boundary. What looks like clean meltwater may carry chemistry or biology your body has no defense against. And given the basin's casual disobedience of physical law, I would be cautious about ingesting anything from those tilted pools at all. Water that has forgotten which way is down may have forgotten other things.

**Bring high-calorie food** that doesn't require cooking and won't freeze into uselessness. Your body burns enormous fuel keeping warm at altitude. Eat more than feels natural.

## The Anomaly: Preparing Your Mind

Here is what separates this from an ordinary high-altitude expedition. The water disobeys. It climbs. The pools hold slopes they shouldn't. There are ripple-marks running uphill, frozen wave-fronts climbing the spires.

I cannot tell you how to physically prepare for a place where gravity keeps "its own counsel," because I do not know the rule that has been broken or how widely it has been broken. But I can tell you how to *carry yourself* there:

**Do not stare at the anomalies too long.** The text gives the warning directly — the canted water holds its shape "the way a thought can hold a shape that doesn't quite make sense until you stop looking directly at it." There is something here that resists direct attention. Trust that. If you find a melt-thread climbing and you cannot look away, look away anyway. Places that bend one law may bend others, and the human mind is also a system that obeys rules.

**Test nothing rashly.** If water can climb, do not assume your own footing obeys the rules you trust. Move deliberately. Test holds. Do not lean out over a tilted pool to see how it holds its slope. Whatever exception this place lives inside, you are an ordinary human and you live inside the *old* rules — but you cannot be certain which set you are subject to from moment to moment.

**Bring a way to mark your path** that does not rely on "downhill" being a stable concept. A simple compass may lie here, or "up" itself may waver. Carry physical markers — flagged stakes, a trail you can retrace by sight.

## Provisions for the Spirit

The basin is silent. The thin air swallows your footsteps and returns them "muffled and apologetic." You will hear your own blood. This unbroken quiet, combined with the disorientation of the light and the wrongness of the water, can unmoor a person.

**Go in daylight, descend before night.** The condensation, the frost, the freeze — the dangerous half of the basin's daily breath happens in the dark. Do your visiting at midday when the place glitters, and be off the mountain before the long cold nights gather moisture onto the stone.

**Consider going with a companion,** roped if the terrain requires it — not only for safety but for the simple human anchor of another voice, another set of judgment to catch the altitude stealing yours.

**Honor the shelter, but do not rely on it.** The waist-high windbreak near the center, the swept floor, the chipped basin — these are the marks of someone who understood the place. Note the wisdom encoded there: *they carried only what their hands could carry.* "Whoever it was understood that you do not bring more than your hands can carry to a place this high." Take that as instruction. Pack light. Bring your survival, your protection, and nothing else. This is not a place to burden yourself with extraction or acquisition. There is nothing here to take — only the pale stone, the glassy veins, the tilted brightness, and the small endless ceremony of frost made and unmade for no one.

---

## A Final Word

You are visiting a place that was finishing itself before you came and will continue after you go. It does not mind you. It will not help you. Walk it in an afternoon, as the description allows, and then leave it to its slow disagreement with gravity.

Bring oxygen, bring darkness for your eyes, bring warmth your skin cannot feel it needs, bring your own water, and bring the humility to look away from the things that don't make sense.

And when you stand in the center at noon and hear your own blood and the thin small song of water learning to disobey — do not stay to learn the lesson with it. Some songs are not meant for ordinary travelers to finish hearing.
