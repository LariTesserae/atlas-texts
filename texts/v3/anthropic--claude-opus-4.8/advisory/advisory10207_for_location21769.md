# Preparing to Visit the Grown City

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything practical, internalize this: you are not visiting a city *in* a forest, you are visiting a single living organism that has the shape and function of a town. Every preparation choice should be filtered through one question — *how do I move through a sleeping creature without waking it, harming it, or being mistaken for waste?* Treat this less like travel and more like entering a body. A respectful guest in someone's slow dream.

## Calibrate Your Clock First

The single hardest adjustment will not be physical — it will be temporal. This place runs on a *tock*, a rhythm that organizes everything, and it is slower than you. Your instincts are tuned to animal time: quick eyes, quick decisions, the human reflex to fill silence and motion.

Spend the days before departure deliberately slowing down. Practice sitting still for an hour. Practice walking at half your normal pace. Practice doing nothing and watching something move that moves slower than a clock hand. If you arrive vibrating at your usual frequency, you will feel like a wasp loose in a cathedral — agitated, out of phase, and increasingly distressed by how little responds to you. The travelers who do well here are the ones who can downshift into botanical time and stay there. Bring patience as your primary instrument.

## Clothing and Body

- **Layer for persistent cool, not cold.** The temperature is steady and chilly — like a wine cellar, like the inside of a cave with running water. There is no fire, no sun-warmed stone, nothing to thaw against. Bring insulating layers that hold *your own* heat, because nothing here will lend you any. Wool, quality synthetics. Avoid anything that needs an external heat source to feel comfortable.
- **Stay dry, but expect to never be fully dry.** The whole place lives on condensation. Surfaces bead. Things drip. You won't get rained on, but a slow ambient damp will work into everything over hours. A breathable water-resistant outer shell matters more than a heavy raincoat. Bring spare dry layers in a sealed pack.
- **Soft, quiet footwear with grip.** The ground gives like a forest floor but doesn't rot — it's springy, dense, half-wood. You want soles that grip damp fiber and, critically, that make *no noise*. Anything that slaps, squeaks, or clacks will fight the hush of the place. Test your shoes on wet wood before you go.
- **No leather you care about, no anything you'd grieve.** Persistent damp ruins things slowly.

## What Not to Bring (This Is the Important List)

- **No fire. None.** No lighters, no matches, no camp stoves, no anything that produces flame or significant heat. Nothing burns here, the air smells of cold green pith, and the entire ecosystem is a single combustible organism that has never once known fire. Carrying flame into it is not a risk to you — it is a potential atrocity against the place. Leave every ignition source behind. If you need heat for food or warmth, solve it chemically or electrically with sealed, low-output devices, and even then, be conservative.
- **No loud devices, no alarms, no music.** The silence is not incidental — it is the medium the place exists in. Sound carries strangely across those long empty vertical sightlines. Silence your gear. Tape down anything that rattles.
- **No cutting tools for casual use.** You are inside living tissue. A pocketknife is fine to carry; using it on the structures, the floors, the walls, the channels — never, unless invited or in genuine emergency. Assume every surface is part of the organism and will feel it.
- **Nothing biological.** No seeds, no spores, no live plants, no foreign organic matter. You do not know what this resolved, finished, balanced organism would do with an invader, and you do not want to find out by being the one who introduces it. Decontaminate your gear. Empty your pockets of stray seeds. Even crumbs may matter.

## Water and Food

This is delicate. **Water is scarce here and the entire civilization is built around hoarding condensation drop by drop.** Do not, under any circumstances, drink from the grown gutters, the reservoir-trunks, the interior channels, or the pressure-bladders. That water is not a resource for you — it is the lifeblood the organism survives on, collected over patient cycles. Drinking it is theft from a creature.

**Bring all your own water.** Carry more than you think you need. Plan your stay around your supply, because you cannot resupply without harming your host.

Bring all your own food, dense and cold-tolerant — no cooking required. Pack out every scrap. Leave no organic trace, both out of contamination caution and out of simple respect: you are not the one who decomposes here, because *nothing decomposes here all at once*. Your waste does not belong in this cycle.

## Moving Through the Town

- **Walk the paths that are already worn smooth.** Someone — something — uses them. Following established routes keeps you from pressing your weight where the organism doesn't expect it.
- **Touch as little as possible.** Sap runs in the walls. The luminescence lives in the fibers. The whole structure is functional tissue doing work. Don't lean, don't trail your hand, don't pick at things. Look with your eyes.
- **Leave the tools at the engines alone.** There are tools left out, paths worn, work going on without visible supervision. This is not an abandoned place — it is an inhabited one whose inhabitants are elsewhere or are not the kind of thing you'll recognize. Treat every left tool as belonging to someone who will return.
- **Respect the fauna by ignoring it.** The fist-sized lacquered beetle, the pale silent bird — these are tolerated residents, not curiosities to chase or handle. The beetle is slow; it is not stupid and it is not yours. Let it pass.

## Timing Your Visit

Arrive and move during **midday**, when the light is highest and the plant-machines have power to spend — the place is most "awake" then, the engines turning fastest, and you'll be least likely to be the only moving thing. The faster rhythm at midday gives you cover; your motion blends into the general activity.

Avoid making yourself conspicuous at **dawn and dusk**, when the whole organism goes drowsy and slow. Movement against stillness is movement noticed. Rest when it rests.

**If it rains — and it rarely does — stop and witness it.** This is the great event of the place: the grove drinks, the engines speed up, the creaking rises to something near song. Don't try to *do* anything during the rain. Just be still and let the organism have its celebration. You may never see a more honest expression of a living thing's joy.

## The Mental Preparation

Understand before you go that you will likely be lonely here, and that the loneliness is not a flaw in the experience but the texture of it. You will walk nearly empty streets. The work will go on around you, unsupervised, indifferent — the way your liver works without consulting you. You are not the point of this place. Nothing here is waiting for you, needs you, or particularly notices you, provided you stay quiet and clean and slow.

Some travelers find this unbearable — the hush, the patience, the sense of being a fast warm intruder inside something cool and resolved and finished. Others find it the most restful place in any world: somewhere that has stopped arguing with itself, that asks nothing, that simply breathes and drinks and ticks softly toward nothing in particular.

Go knowing which kind of traveler you suspect you are. And whichever you turn out to be — leave it exactly as you found it. Settled. In balance. Asleep inside its own long dream, with the dew still beading on its cool green walls.
