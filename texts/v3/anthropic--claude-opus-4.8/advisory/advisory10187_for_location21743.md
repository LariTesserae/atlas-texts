# Preparing for the Drowned Highland

## First Principles: Understand What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with the central paradox until it stops feeling like one. You are going somewhere that is both *altitude* and *submersion* — high mountains whose valleys are filled with warm, thick, breathable-by-plants, drinkable-feeling water two miles above the ordinary ocean. Your instincts will fight this. Mountaineering instinct says *thin air, cold, exposure*. Diving instinct says *pressure, cold, dark*. Neither applies cleanly. You must arrive with a recalibrated body-model, or you'll waste your first hours flinching at contradictions.

This is not a hostile place. There is nothing here that wants to hurt you. The dangers are subtle, environmental, and mostly *self-inflicted through misunderstanding*.

## The Body and the Liquid

**The water is your primary problem to solve, and it is a strange one.**

The liquid is thicker than water — syrup-adjacent. This changes everything about movement:

- **Buoyancy will not behave normally.** A thicker fluid is generally denser, which means you will be *more* buoyant than in ordinary water. Bring adjustable weighting — a modular weight belt you can shed and add in increments. You want to achieve neutral buoyancy in the upper light-zone, where the life is. Test and tune this in the first half hour, near the surface, before committing to depth.

- **Movement is dreamlike but exhausting in an unfamiliar way.** Drag is higher. Quick motions are pointless — the fluid swallows them. You must learn to move *with the medium's patience*: long, slow, deliberate strokes. Fast kicking will only tire you. Practice "underwater tai chi" before you go. The plants here take a full minute to complete a single sway; let that be your tempo.

- **Things fall slowly.** Anything you drop will drift down for a long time before settling. This is a gift — you won't lose tools instantly — but also a trap: never assume something has *gone*. It's still descending, somewhere below you, into the kelp.

**Breathing is the critical question.** The text says the air at altitude is so saturated you're "almost drinking" it, and the water is warm and dense and drinkable-feeling. **Do not trust this.** "Almost drinking" is not breathing. You cannot assume the water is respirable, and you cannot assume the surface air provides adequate oxygen at two miles of altitude.

Bring a **closed-circuit rebreather** — not open-circuit scuba. Reasons:

1. The water is profoundly *quiet*, and open-circuit bubbles would be the loudest, most violent intrusion in a place that has never heard violence. A rebreather lets you move silently, in keeping with the place's nature.
2. Rebreathers are efficient and let you stay long. You will want to stay long. This is a place for floating and waiting.
3. They produce no disruptive plume to disturb the spore-fall and pollen-drift, which is the foundation of the entire food web you're observing.

Carry redundant gas. The high-altitude surface air may not be a safe refuge. Plan as though *the surface is not a guarantee of breathable air* — your gas supply is your lifeline whether up or down.

## Thermal and Pressure Considerations

**Temperature is your friend here, for once.** The water is "warm as bathwater." You don't need a heavy thermal suit against cold. But warm water plus long immersion plus exertion is its own hazard — you can overheat slowly, become dehydrated despite being submerged, and not notice because everything feels gentle. Wear a thin, light suit primarily for **protection from the vegetation**, not warmth. Carry drinkable water in a closed system; do not be tempted to drink the medium, however drinkable it seems.

**Pressure changes are real but mild.** The valleys are "hundreds of feet" deep. If you descend from the surface to the floor, you're crossing real pressure gradients — but in a thick fluid, the relationship between depth and pressure may differ from water you know. **Descend and ascend slowly regardless.** The dreamlike pace of the place is also good decompression practice. Build in long, lazy ascents. Treat every dive as one requiring decompression discipline until you have data saying otherwise.

## The Living World: How to Move Through It

**The vegetation is a crowd, a congregation, a held breath of chlorophyll.** You will be pushing through dense kelp-trees, lantern-weed, and column-mosses. Rules:

- **Move slowly enough that the fronds part for you.** They close behind you reluctantly. Don't tear. Don't hack. Nothing here has ever been cut, and you should not be the first to introduce that.
- **The lantern-weed holds trapped gas and glows.** Do not puncture these pockets carelessly — you don't know the composition of the gas, and you don't want to release bubbles or unknown metabolic products into your breathing zone or your face.
- **The column-mosses are the only "architecture."** They are slow accretions, possibly centuries old. Do not break them. A single careless kick could destroy something that took a thousand years to build.

**The animals will not flee from you.** This is the most important psychological fact to internalize. The drift-medusae and slow-fish have *no fear, because fear has not been invented here.* There has been no predation, no history that would teach flight.

This means:
- **You are the most dangerous thing this ecosystem has ever encountered**, simply by being a mover with intent. Behave accordingly. You carry, in your body and your tools, the entire concept of predation. Do not be the one who introduces it.
- A slow-fish will let you approach. A drift-medusa will coast right past you. **The temptation to touch will be enormous.** Resist it. You may carry microbes, residues, or simply a roughness of contact that this world has no defense against. Observe; do not handle.
- Bring a way to record — a quiet camera, low-light capable, because the green diffused light is gentle and shadowless and your eyes will adapt but your recording gear must be tuned for it.

## Light and Orientation

The light comes down "green and slow," diffuse, casting no hard shadows. **Shadowless environments destroy your sense of depth and orientation.** You will struggle to judge distance, to tell up from down in the dimmer depths.

- The **silver surface above** is your reliable up-reference. Keep it in sight or know where it is at all times.
- Below it, the **lantern-weed's faint glow** marks the lower zones. Use these as a depth gauge — they belong to the dimmer water.
- Bring a **dive computer with depth, time, and a compass**, but understand that in a freshly-formed range of sharp clefts and narrow valleys, magnetic behavior may be unreliable. **Lay a guideline** if you penetrate deep into the kelp. The fronds close behind you; your path will vanish. You can become lost in a forest that has no landmarks, only sameness.

## On Sound and the Quiet

This is a profoundly quiet place. The loudest thing is your own pulse. The only external sound is the deep tectonic *settling* of the young rock — felt more than heard.

- **Those tectonic groans are not a hazard you can predict, but they are real.** The mountains are still finding their footing. Do not position yourself beneath unstable-looking new rock faces or in narrow clefts where rockfall, however rare, would have nowhere to disperse. The "settling" is slow, but you are small.
- **The silence will affect you.** Hours of near-total quiet, your own heartbeat in your ears, dreamlike slow movement through green light — this is a meditative, almost hypnotic environment. **Plan against the trance.** Set timers. The place invites you to "float and wait and let nothing hurry," and that is exactly how a traveler loses track of gas, of time, of depth, of the slow creep of overheating or hypoxia. The gentleness is the danger.

## The Ethical Preparation

Read the final stanza again: *Nothing is built. Nothing is made. No tool has ever entered this water; no hand has shaped its stone. It is a place that has never been used.*

You are about to be the first hand, the first tool, the first user. Prepare for this as a *responsibility*, not an opportunity.

- **Decontaminate everything.** Your suit, your gear, your body. This world's fauna has no immune history against your microbiome. You could be a plague with a face. Sterilize, and if your multiverse-travel protocols allow it, quarantine yourself beforehand.
- **Take nothing. Leave nothing.** No samples, no souvenirs, no anchors driven into virgin rock, no markers. Lay only retrievable guideline and retrieve it.
- **Make no marks.** Do not be the one who teaches this stone what a tool feels like.

## Final Packing List

- Closed-circuit rebreather, plus redundant gas, calibrated for high-altitude departure and dense-fluid immersion
- Modular weight system for buoyancy tuning in an unusually dense medium
- Thin protective suit (vegetation, not cold)
- Dive computer: depth, time, redundant timers; compass with the expectation it may lie
- Retrievable guideline and reel
- Low-light quiet camera
- Sealed drinking water and minimal sustenance
- Full decontamination kit, used before *and* after
- A disciplined plan with hard turn-around limits, set against the seductive timelessness

## The Last Word

Go in slow. Move as the kelp moves. Touch nothing, take nothing, frighten nothing — though nothing here knows yet how to be frightened. Watch a drift-medusa cross the green dimness like a thought, and let it cross uninterrupted. Then ascend slowly through the silver skin, into the thin warm wet air, and leave the held breath unbroken behind you.

You are visiting a world that has not yet decided what it will be. The single most important preparation is the resolve not to decide it for them.
