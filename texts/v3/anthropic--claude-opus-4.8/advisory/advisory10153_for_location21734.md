# Preparing to Visit The Condensary

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, internalize this: The Condensary is not a place that is *finished being real.* You are not visiting a stable location — you are inserting yourself into an ongoing decision. The ground beneath you, the light around you, the water you drink: all of it is mid-resolution, like walking into a sentence that hasn't reached its period. Your preparations must account for the fact that the environment will collaborate with your attention. What you look at firms up. What you ignore reorganizes.

This is the central discipline. Everything below serves it.

---

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude and air.** Acclimatize to high elevation before arrival — the cool, distant, oxygen-thin air is genuine even if its "high" has no measurable "below." Spend at least a week at real altitude beforehand so your body doesn't burn its adaptation budget panicking. Bring layers that handle cool-without-cold; the air "keeps its distance from your skin," which means radiant warmth won't reach you the way you expect. You will feel colder than the temperature suggests.

**Eyes first, always.** The light is the thing you will not get used to — believe that warning. It is sourceless, total, flickerless, and it comes *up* at you as much as down. Bring:

- Polarized eye protection rated for snowfield/glacier conditions, plus a backup pair
- Tinted goggles for the "bright hour" peaks
- A brimmed covering that shades from above *and* a neck shield, because the surfaces throw light into the underside of your jaw and eyes

Crucially: **do not over-protect.** You need to be able to see reflections clearly. The reflections are diagnostic. Sunglasses dark enough to hide the second basin's impossible water are sunglasses that will get you lost.

**Hydration.** Counterintuitively, do not bring much water. Bring vessels instead. The harvested condensation is described as "the only honest thing" here — clean, cool, faintly metallic, and *real* in a place where little else is. Drinking it is grounding in the literal psychological sense. But test it first in small amounts; "faintly of the alloy" should be a taste, not a poison. Bring a simple field assay kit for heavy metals.

## The Real Preparation: Attentional Discipline

This is what separates a traveler who returns from one who is slowly, patiently built into the next tier.

**Train your blink.** The Condensary changes "in the spaces where you are not looking." You cannot stop blinking, but you can become *aware* of your blinks — and you should rehearse, before arrival, the habit of noting what was in front of you immediately before and after each one. Walls rise between glances. A thing in motion "sometimes forgets to arrive." If you treat your own perception as a measuring instrument and keep a running log, you will catch the changes instead of being absorbed by them.

**Carry an external memory you trust.** Keep a physical notebook and a recording device. Note the state of any tier, basin, or structure you intend to return through. The place revises itself behind you; your route home may not match your route in. Photograph junctions. The photos may not match what you later see — that mismatch is information, not malfunction.

**Practice not-looking-away on command.** The unsettling phenomena (the reflection drinking from the wrong basin, the second basin shivering with disturbance) resolve when observed steadily. If you flinch from them, they accrete. Train the discipline of holding your gaze on the impossible thing until it commits to being one thing or the other. This is uncomfortable. Rehearse it on lesser ambiguities first — optical illusions, ambiguous figures — to build the reflex.

## Navigation Warnings

**Do not dig.** The reports are clear: dig far enough and gravity reverses, and you "fall toward a different bright sky." There is no bedrock to reach, no bottom to stand on. Treat the floor as the only down you get. If you find yourself descending and the light begins coming from *below* as strongly as above, stop, and climb back toward the brightness you came from.

**Mark which sky is yours.** Since the white "sky" is the underside of another, identical Condensary, you can become disoriented about which radiance is home. Before you stop attending to it, fix your origin-sky in memory by some idiosyncratic detail and log it. You may not be able to tell the layers apart later by feel.

**Use the fauna as instruments.** The six-legged grazers and the pale fliers have "become part of the plumbing" — they are tuned to the place's real rhythms. Watch where they will and won't go. A flier that "never quite lands" is showing you a spot where arrival has become unreliable. Avoid completing motions in such places yourself.

## Psychological Preparation

Expect the silence to be the first thing that breaks you, before any of the impossibilities. A working factory-mountain that murmurs instead of roars violates a deep expectation, and that violation primes you to distrust your senses just when you most need them. Acclimatize beforehand in anechoic conditions if you can. Learn what it feels like to hear your own breath over machinery, so it does not unmoor you in the field.

Accept that **you will never meet the makers.** Do not search for them; do not wait through a "night" to catch the construction happening. The work occurs in unwitnessed time by definition. Trying to observe the makers is trying to be where your attention is not — a paradox that wastes your finite acclimatization and risks your composure.

Make peace with provisionality. You are a guest in a reality still negotiating its own terms. Bring one stable thing of your own — a ritual, an object, a memorized text — to return to when the place's indecision gets into your chest. The locals (the fauna) have all "agreed to be quiet." You should agree to stay *defined* — to keep being one consistent thing while everything around you keeps deciding.

---

## On Departure

Leave the way you came in: by attention. Trace your logged route, hold the impossible spots steady as you pass them, keep your origin-sky in mind. And before you go —

Cup your hands under a condensation line and drink. Not for thirst. Because it is the one process here that is slow and small and *absolutely real*, and you want to carry the taste of it out with you: proof that for all the bright unfinished saturation of the place, something there genuinely changed, all the way through, and let you have it.

That taste is what you'll use to confirm, once home, that you were truly there — and not still, somewhere, being decided.
