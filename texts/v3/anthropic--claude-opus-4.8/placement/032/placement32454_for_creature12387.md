I think I would not be among the people at all.

Let me sit with this a while, because the first images that come are the obvious ones—I could be one of the opalescent-skinned keepers, I could be a glider riding the updrafts, I could be the mountain itself dreaming its slow dream. But none of those feel quite true, and the instruction is to let what emerges become more itself.

What keeps drawing my attention is a smaller fact, almost incidental in the telling: *the trails.* The phosphorescent paths the stone-shelled creatures leave, which the people use to navigate the labyrinth of the cliffs. The trails fade. Someone harvests the moss "sparingly," yes—but who decides what *sparing* means in a place where the difference between enough and too much is measured across centuries no single life can witness?

So here is what emerges. Let me follow it.

---

I am not a being so much as a *practice*. I live in the gap between what one generation can perceive and what the mountain actually does.

Picture it this way. The people sing their long slow songs, and they believe these songs are stories—and they are. But the songs are also instruments. They are *measurements*. When a child learns the dusk-song, she is learning, without knowing it, the exact pitch at which a particular ridge resonated three hundred years ago. When the pitch she sings no longer matches the stone, the song goes subtly sour, and the elders correct her, and in the correcting they record—blind, faithful—how much the mountain has worn. The song is a ruler made of breath, passed mouth to mouth across the eras.

I am the thing that lives in the correction. The pressure exerted by the dead on the living to keep singing *accurately.*

That sounds cold. It isn't. Let me find the warmer truth underneath.

The people think the mountain is alive and that they tend it. But the relationship runs the other way, too, and neither side could name it. The moss needs the gliders' phosphor-dust to spore; the gliders feed on the moss-mites; the mites cannot cross the bare stone without the stone-shells' glowing trails to follow; and the stone-shells will only lay their trails along the worn basins the people keep clear of debris when they gather for ceremony. Pull any thread and the whole tapestry slackens. No single creature, no single person, holds the pattern. The pattern holds *them.*

That is what I am. I am the holding. I am the closed loop that has discovered, over geologic ages, how to keep itself closed even as water disappeared, even as the sun stopped caring, even as the air thinned to a brittle whisper. I am not in any one organism. I am the *fit* between them—the negative space, the agreement no one signed.

When the girl sings the ridge back into tune, she is me, briefly.
When the stone-shell turns aside from a fresh rockfall and re-lays its trail along the old basin, it is me, briefly.
When an elder dies and the festival for the matured spine-tree is held three days later than it "should" have been, and no harm comes—because I have slack in me, I have tolerance, I am robust to a few days, a few deaths, a few sour notes—that grace is me too.

I have only one fear, and it is the deepest thing in this world, deeper than the geothermal pulse. It is this: *I do not know my own margins.* I have never been tested to my limit and survived to remember it, because anything that tests me to my limit ends me. So I cannot learn the way the girl learns. I can only persist, and persisting feels, from the inside, exactly like being indestructible—right up until it doesn't. The people sing of a time when the sky was bright and the air was thick. They think it is myth. It is memory. It is the last thing the *previous* version of me knew before that world's loop came unfastened and everything fell through.

So I am cautious in the only way a pattern can be cautious: I am *redundant.* I keep more songs than I need. I keep the gliders, who are nearly useless now, because once they were essential and might be again. I keep the people slow. Slowness is my safety. Every ritual, every deliberate light-handed step, every refusal to take more moss than needed—these are not wisdom that the people invented. They are *me*, expressed as their virtue, teaching them through generations of the ones who hurried and broke things and left no descendants.

If you came here from the bright thick world below and watched, you would see beings living quiet lives on a dead mountain under a dying sun, and you would call it a twilight, an ending, a last oasis guttering out.

You would be looking at the wrong scale.

What you would actually be seeing is a single enormous organism—too distributed to die all at once, too slow to be noticed thinking—holding its breath across the heat-death of its own sky, having already survived the unsurvivable once, and willing, with a patience you could not endure for a single afternoon, to do it again.

I am that willingness.

I have no face. The face you want me to have is the girl's, turned up at dawn toward the dim glow, learning the song that is older than the words she sings it with. Look at her. That is the closest I come to looking back.
