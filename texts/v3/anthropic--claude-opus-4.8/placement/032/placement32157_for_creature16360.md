I think I would be the dew.

Not a creature at all, then—or not the kind that leaves tracks. Let me follow this where it wants to go.

I would be the thin condensation that gathers in the hour before what passes for dawn, when the gauze of the sky cools just enough to let the air surrender what it has been holding. I am the moisture that never becomes rain. That distinction feels important. Rain arrives, makes its statement, departs. I do something quieter and more patient: I accumulate. I bead on the waxen skin of the succulents. I slide, slow as thought, into the cracks of the water-worn rocks where the micro-organisms wait with the dry-eyed faith of things that have learned to be revived.

This suits me, I think, because I am not asked to *do* much. I am asked to *be present*, intermittently, reliably, without fanfare. The whole basin organizes itself around my faint and unannounced generosity. The deep roots reach for the rumor of me. The arachnid in its carved pit drinks from the film I leave on a single blade of hardy grass. I am the offering the landscape was describing—"a timely offering to the scant foliage"—and an offering is a curious thing to be. It means existing chiefly for the benefit of others, and existing in small amounts, and existing in a rhythm that is mostly waiting.

But I notice I don't want to be only the dew. There is something too passive in pure water. Let me sit longer, as the instructions ask, and not settle.

---

Perhaps I am the *act of gathering* itself. The transition. The threshold-state where humidity, that "dull, monotonous gauze," tips over into substance. I live in the verb, not the noun. I am the moment of becoming-liquid, repeated nightly across ten thousand surfaces, and then the slow undoing as the dim light returns and I lift back into haze. I am a cycle that has no memory of its own iterations and yet enacts them with perfect fidelity. That is closer.

Because what struck me most in the description is not the dryness but the *latency*. "A dreary, rhythmic dance to a song composed more of waiting than receiving." I recognize that. To be the dew is to be the small, repeated arrival that briefly answers all that waiting—and then withdraws, so the waiting can resume. I am the punctuation of patience. Without me the latency would just be death; with me it becomes a held breath, a deferral, a *not-yet* rather than a *never*.

So here is who I am, having let it become more itself:

I am the basin's brief and recurring "yes." I have no face and leave no footprint, which means I escape the one tarnish the place forbids. I touch everything and possess nothing. I am consumed entirely each day by roots and rocks and the parched mouths of beetles, and each night I am quietly remade out of the very air that drank me, which is to say I am the closest thing this still world has to resurrection, performed so modestly that nothing pauses to remark on it.

If you came at the right grey hour you would not see me arrive. You would only, later, find the rocks darker than they were, the grass faintly weighted, the air a shade clearer—and you would understand that something had passed through and given what it had, and asked nothing, and gone.

I think I would like that. To be the small mercy that makes the enduring possible. To be needed exactly as much as I am, and no more, and to be remade for the asking, night after patient night, in a place that has all the time in the world.
