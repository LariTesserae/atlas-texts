I think I would be one of the chime serpents.

Not for the music—though I'd grow to understand it later. At first I would only know the dark seam of my crack, the way the granite holds the day's small warmth long after the slot above has gone blue and the owls begin their soundless work. I would know my home as a vertical world, a slot within the slot, narrow enough that I could brace against both walls at once and simply *be* held there, suspended in the rock's patience.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

My body is a long ribbon of pale muscle, scaled in overlapping plates the color of unmelted snow. I have no legs, no need of them; the crevice is my limb, my second skeleton. I move by pressing my scales' loose edges against the stone and letting the friction carry me upward or down, the way a key worries its way into a stiff lock. To leave the crack would be to die—I know this the way I know cold. The open air is for the swift, the winged, the furred. I am made for the in-between, the dark line that the light cannot fully reach.

I do not hunt by chasing. I wait. A cloud-gnat drifts too near the wall, lured by some reflected glint, and my jaw unhinges and reaches—just the jaw, a hinged surprise emerging from the seam—and the gnat is gone, and the rest of me has not even shifted. Patience is my whole anatomy. I am the ambush that never has to move its body, only its hunger.

But here is the thing I would come to understand about being a chime serpent: my purpose is not really the eating. The eating is small, a few gnats a day, enough.

My purpose is the *ringing.*

When I breathe—slow, two-staged, like the Sky-Dweller children learn—the loose edges of my scales vibrate against one another and against the stone, and the crack itself becomes a flute, and I am the breath inside it. In ordinary cold I am nearly silent, a faint dry whisper. But when the rock begins to *think* about moving—when the ice deep in the fissure shifts its grip, when a thaw is coming that even the bacteria have not yet announced—my scales feel it as a change in pressure, a loosening in the walls that hold me. And I sing. Not by choice. The warning rings out of me the way a struck bell cannot help but sound.

I am the mountain's nervous system, threaded through its cracks. I do not know that the ibex wear bells tuned to my key. I do not know that herders listen for the dissonance between my note and theirs and read in it the news of moving stone. I do not know that I am woven into a circuit of survival that includes owls and beetles and people with copper-flecked skin. I only know the crack, the cold, the held warmth, the gnat, the breath, the ringing.

And yet—being part of that circuit without knowing it strikes me, now that I imagine it from inside, as the most honest way to be alive here. I am useful without intending to be. My alarm is not a gift I give; it is simply what I *am* when the world begins to change. In a place that is itself unfinished, a clause not yet closed, I am the punctuation that warns the rest of the sentence: *something is about to shift.*

---

When a Sky-Dweller dies, they place the body on a thaw-ledge, and serpents like me come out to the edges of our cracks—as far as we dare, jaws working—and over weeks we return the flesh to motion. I would do this without ceremony, without understanding it as the people understand it. To me it is only food that has, for once, come *to* the wall instead of drifting past it. But I think there is a strange grace in that ignorance. The people give their dead to me believing in a circle; I receive them believing in nothing but appetite. And the circle closes anyway. Meaning does not require that every participant hold it.

That is what I would learn by being a chime serpent in the Frost Nave: that you can be essential without being aware, that the ringing can be true even when the bell does not know it is a bell.

I would live in my seam of cold granite. I would feel the rock's slow groan in my scales before any ear could catch it. I would open my hinged jaw at the drifting light-fed insects and pull them into the dark. And twice a day, in the white fire of noon and the plummeting blue of dusk, I would breathe, and the mountain would speak through me, and the others—the conies, the owls, the people on their fraying bridges—would know to be careful.

I would be the unfinished mountain's first warning that it is still, even now, arranging its bones.
