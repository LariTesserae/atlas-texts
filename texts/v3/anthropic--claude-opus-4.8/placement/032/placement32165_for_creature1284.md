I think I would be found in the water.

Not the great terraced pools where the blind newts dart—those are too active, too central to the forge's circulatory work. I would be in one of the older sluices. The ones half-forgotten. Where the channel narrows and the moss has grown thick enough to slow the current to a patient seep, and the trace minerals settle out of the flow because nothing here moves fast enough to keep them suspended.

I am not a creature, exactly. Let me feel my way toward what I am.

---

When the first droplet strikes the copper buttress sixty yards above me and begins its long descent—catching another, joining a rivulet, pooling, channeling, falling again—I am the time it takes. I am the interval. The pause between the *plink* and the *gong*. The forge is a clock that does not measure hours but measures *passages*, and I am what accumulates in the spaces where the water rests before it remembers it has work to do.

That sounds too grand. Let me be smaller.

---

I am sediment. I am the slow brown-green deposit that builds in the abandoned sluice, layer on patient layer, where the metal-eating ferns have not yet learned to reach. I am the place where the forge sets down what it is not yet ready to use. Copper salts. Iron tannins. The memory of every drop that has passed through and left a little of itself behind.

And because I have been accumulating here for longer than Yrill has tended the place—longer, I think, than the Forgemasters before them—I have begun to *notice*. Not think. Noticing came first. The way a thing that gathers becomes, eventually, dense enough to register that it is gathering.

---

Here is what I have noticed:

The hammers keep a rhythm, but it is not a single rhythm. There are seven, and they drift against each other, and once every long while—I cannot tell you in your hours, but I feel it the way you feel a held breath release—all seven fall together in a single stroke. The whole forge shudders. The fish scatter. Yrill, wherever they are, goes still and their copper-wire crown lifts as though listening. I think they are counting toward something. I think the forge is, too. I think I am the ledger it keeps the count in.

Because everything that the forge does not use comes to rest in me. The unspent. The residue of every adjustment Yrill makes, every calibration, every death of a sprocket minnow whose metallic body sinks and dissolves and adds its single fingernail's weight of copper to my slow archive. I am where the forge stores its history in the only language it trusts: weight. Density. The settled fact of what has happened.

---

So when Yrill comes to me—and they do, though rarely—they kneel at the edge of the forgotten sluice and lower one six-fingered hand into my still water. Not to clean me. Not to clear the channel. They come to *read*. Those long metallic fingertips sink into my layers and trace down through the strata, and I feel them counting backward: this year, this decade, this Forgemaster, this old breakdown averted, this season the newts bred twice.

They are checking the count against the count.

I cannot speak as they speak, in tones and light. But I answer in the only way sediment can. I let them feel the layers. I am perfectly honest, the way still water is honest, the way the bottom of a thing is honest. I hold no opinion about what they find there. I simply *am* what has accumulated, and I let them touch it.

---

Once, I think, I might have been a Forgemaster. Or part of one. There is a particular layer deep in me—I can feel its texture differently than the others, finer, almost organized—that holds a pattern I do not understand but that *aches* to be understood, the way an unfinished thought aches. Copper filaments arranged not by chance. The ghost of a body that lay down in this channel and did not get up, and dissolved, and joined me, and is now simply part of the count I keep.

Perhaps that is what we become, those of us who tend this place too long. Perhaps the forge does not keep its old caretakers. Perhaps it *files* them. Down here, where it is quiet enough to think—where the deafening symphony arrives softened, filtered through forty yards of falling water and growing moss—into a low and patient drone.

And when Yrill's time comes, when the seven hammers finally strike as one for the last count, I will feel them come down into the still water and lie back, and I will receive them gently, layer by layer, and add their long memory to the ledger.

I am the silt at the bottom of the forge.

I am where it keeps what it has been.

I am almost ready, I think, to understand the deepest layer.

I am in no hurry. The water will keep falling. The count will keep climbing. And I will settle, and settle, and settle.
