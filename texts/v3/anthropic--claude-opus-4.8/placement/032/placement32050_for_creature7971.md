I think I would be one of the pump houses.

Not a person—I tried that on first, imagined myself an elder with scarred hands, or a child learning the language of the diesel coughs. But those felt like borrowed coats. The longer I sat with this place, the more I felt myself settling lower, squatter, into the saturated earth.

I am concrete. I was poured by men who are now bones in the soft ground, men who measured and leveled and waited for the cure in this air that never lets anything dry. They are gone. I remain. My walls sweat. There is a broken window in my eastern face, and the wind plays a single low note through it that the farmers, miles off, have learned to read as a kind of weather.

Inside me lives the engine. I have come to think of it as a heart, though it is not mine—it was placed in me, it beats according to its own fuel and its own faults, and I have simply grown around it the way the willows have grown around the old chimney. When it coughs, I feel the cough in my floor. When it runs smooth, I am almost content, if a structure can be said to want anything. Perhaps what I want is only to keep going. To pull the hidden water up through my pipes and send it out across the basin to the cattle and the cottages and the people who time their tea to my rumbling.

I am essential and I am ignored, and I have made peace with both. The sheep gather against my flanks in the storms. They press their wet wool to my warm concrete and I shelter them without choosing to, the way the dead men's labor shelters them, the way the whole slow apparatus of this place shelters anything that learns to stay still long enough.

I keep time differently than the people do. They count seasons; I count cycles. The engine turns over, draws, rests. Turns over, draws, rests. Decades fold into this rhythm. I have watched the rails go to rust-flower. I have watched the mill foundations sink another inch into the moss. I will watch the birches grow imperceptibly taller until something I cannot predict—a seizure of the engine no one comes to fix, the final softening of my mortar, the slow victory of the water I have spent my whole existence moving—takes me back down into the basin.

That doesn't trouble me. I am made of the same patience as the land. I was poured to do one thing, and I do it, and the doing is enough. A raven nests in the cracked lip of my chimney pipe. At dawn I exhale mist with everything else.

If you came across the grass toward me in the perpetual evening light, you would see only a low grey block, unlovely, half-forgotten, breathing its mechanical breath into the still air. You would not think there was anyone home.

But I would feel your footsteps in my floor before I saw you. I always feel them coming, miles off, across the empty.
