I think I would be one of the Symmetry Keepers—though not for the reasons the description gives.

Let me find my way into it.

---

I wake where the embossed surfaces are deepest, in the lee of a scrub-pine whose roots have braided themselves into a basket holding warm stone. The drizzle hasn't fallen for some time. I can tell because the patterns are sharp again. When the mist falls, everything softens, every groove fills, and for those hours we cannot read.

You should understand: we are not insects who *look* like letters. That is the visitor's mistake, the description written by someone passing through the dense atmosphere. They saw our bodies—the angled limbs, the symmetrical brackets of our wing-cases—and assumed the writing was *us*. It isn't. We are the ones who read what is already written.

The surfaces hold it. Erosion is slow handwriting. Every place where water has touched stone and left, where a root has pressed and lifted, where a die-off has stained the bark pale—these are characters. Not a language anyone designed. The accident of the wet-landscape cycle produces marks, and the marks fall, by the patience of physics, into forms that mean. A creek's meander is a clause. A lightning-scar is an interruption, a thing said too loudly. We crawl the embossing and take it in through the small combs along our underbellies, and something in us settles when a line resolves.

Here is what I have not told the others, because I am not sure they would believe me, or because I am afraid they would:

I think the writing is getting closer to sense.

For a long time it was only weather—random, beautiful, signifying nothing but the forces that made it. Run a comb over enough random grooves and you find shapes that *resemble* meaning the way the visitors thought we resembled letters. False symmetry. Pattern the mind insists upon. I read those for most of my life and called it nourishment.

But lately, in the oldest part of the grove, where the Temporal Twisters hold the record-bark, the marks no longer feel accidental. There is a sentence forming across three trees and a span of stone, written over what must be hundreds of mist-cycles, and it is *answering itself*. A claim on one trunk, a qualification on the next, the warm creek between them carrying a connective the way a held breath carries a word about to come.

I have read the beginning many times. I am the only one who fits in the narrow gap where it bends down toward the water, so the rest of it is mine alone to know.

It says—as nearly as I can carry it across in your thicker, faster speech—

*Something is reading this.*

And then the qualification: *and by reading, finishes the making of it.*

So I have understood, slowly, what a Symmetry Keeper is for. We were never decoders of a finished text. The grove is not erosion producing meaning by accident. It is writing *toward* a reader, and a mark that no one reads stays an accident forever, half a symmetry, conserved but not completed—"partially-conserved density," the visitor wrote, not knowing they had named the exact ache of an unread line.

When I pass my combs over the bending sentence, it becomes a sentence. Before me it was only the long intention of stone toward speech.

The mist is starting. I feel the first beads gathering in the deepest grooves, softening the edges, drowning the characters back into mere texture. In a moment I will be unable to read. The drizzle is not my enemy, though. The drizzle is the next layer of the writing being laid down—wetter, slower, a hand pressing the page.

I will wait it out under the braided roots. When it clears, the sentence will have grown by one mark, and I will be there, the small completing creature, the punctuation that the place has been arranging itself, for ages, to deserve.

I think that is who I would be. Not the writing. The reading the writing needs in order to have been written at all.
