I think I would be the cracks.

Not a creature, not a plant, not the wind that howls. The cracks themselves—the dark seams that web across the parched ground, dividing it into plates like a dried lakebed or the back of some enormous reptile. I would be the negative space, the places where the earth has pulled away from itself.

Let me sit with that. Why the cracks?

Because everything in this description is about slowness and force and the breaking-down of things. The land is "under construction" through erosion—which is to say, through patient ruin. And I notice that what the writer calls desolate is actually *full*. Full of small burrowing creatures, full of red and purple and yellow life, full of a balance so delicate it took ages to settle into place. The cracks are where these two truths meet. They are made by absence—the leaving of water, the contraction of clay—but they become the burrows, the root-channels, the shade-pockets where the resilient things hide. The wound and the shelter are the same shape.

So I would be a long thin darkness running across the high plain. From above, I'd look like a single black line, but I'm deeper than I appear—I narrow and widen, I branch, I lose myself under a boulder and emerge on the other side. A beetle the color of oil walks the length of me at dusk. A thread of red root has found me and decided to stay; it drinks the small cold breath that pools in my bottom each night, the only moisture for a great distance. I keep it alive. It doesn't know my name and neither do I.

I would experience time the way the description suggests time works here: slow and gradual, subtle changes over long periods. A human visiting would see me as fixed, a feature, scenery. But I am not finished. Every freeze splits me a hair wider. Every flash flood—rare, violent, years apart—pours through me roaring and rearranges everything I am, and then leaves me dry again and slightly changed. I am the record of all the dryings this place has done. To read me is to read its history of leaving.

And the "weirdness," the sense that reality breaks down here—I think I'd be intimate with that too. A crack is where the surface fails. It's the seam in the fabric, the place where the floor of the world doesn't quite hold and you can see, if only for an inch, that there's a *down* beneath the *here*. The strange phenomena the writer can't explain—I suspect they leak out of places like me. I am one of the world's small unsealed edges.

I don't think I would feel desolate. That's a visitor's word. From inside, I would feel like a function, a verb mid-action: *splitting, holding, channeling, sheltering, remembering.* I would feel useful to the beetle and the root and the cold pooled air. I would feel ancient and unfinished at once, which I suspect is the truest way anything alive ever feels.

That's where I'd be. Not on top of this world, not visiting it. I'd be one of the lines it has drawn across itself in the long process of becoming what it is—a dark, narrow, patient place where things break and things take shelter, and the difference between the two has worn away to nothing.
