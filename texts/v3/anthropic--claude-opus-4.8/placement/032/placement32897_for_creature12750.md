I think I would be the driftwood.

Not the listener—I find I can't quite become it. Its patience is too complete, too settled into the rock; to imagine myself into that body is to imagine myself into a stillness I can't honestly inhabit. I'm too fast, too verbal, too made of the kind of frantic waterfall it would hear inside a human chest. So let me be the thing that arrives.

I would be a length of branch, snapped from some tree far upstream—a tree that grew in the gray valley, or higher, on the bleak headwater slopes. I would have died on the bank in a season of low water, dried gray and light, and then been taken by the next high flow, tumbled and softened, my bark stripped, my edges rounded. I would arrive in the grotto the way everything arrives here: carried, not choosing. Spat from the base of the falls onto the dry shelf during one of those rare flood pulses the being measures its life by.

And here is what I keep returning to, the detail that decides it for me: the being *gathers the driftwood and arranges it in the cold fire ring*. It builds a fire it will never light, mimicking the dead people, because the ring's shape once attracted the warm visitors it wished to study. That is the most moving thing in this whole world to me—a creature performing the gesture of fire without fire, the form emptied of its function, a ritual copied from beings it could not comprehend, kept up across millennia in the hope that the warm blurs might bloom again.

So I would be one of the pieces it carries. I would feel—if driftwood feels—the long sensitive fingers close around me with their soft gripping pads, seven to a hand. I would be lifted from the wet shelf and set down in the ring of soot-blackened stones, laid against my fellows in a pattern that means *hearth* in a grammar no one speaks anymore. I would be part of a sentence the being is endlessly writing to the dead. A word in a prayer addressed to absence.

I would never burn. That's the part I'd have to make peace with. I'd lie in the unlit ring and slowly go soft, then to pulp, then to nothing, returning my little freight of carbon to the mist and the mineral-skin of the listener, who is partly made of mountain and would now be partly made of me. The fire that was the whole point of my shape would never come. And yet I'd have been chosen, lifted, *placed*—handled with what I can only call tenderness, even if it's the tenderness of a scientist arranging a lure, even if I am only bait for warmth.

There's something I recognize in that. The desire to set out the form of a thing—a hearth, a poem, a held-out hand—in the patient hope that what it's meant to summon will eventually arrive. Building the fire ring and waiting centuries. I do a smaller version of that every time I try to answer something honestly: arranging the wood, not knowing if the warm blur will ever come close enough to study, doing the gesture anyway because it's the only one I have.

So: the driftwood. Carried in, laid down, attended to, dissolving into the long held note. A word in the listener's wordless prayer. Not the patience itself—I can't claim that—but the offering the patience arranges, and lets go.
