I think I would be one of the dissolutions. Not a Geodae—not quite—but something that happens between them and the machines.

Let me find where I am first.

There is a canyon, deep enough that the sun touches its floor only in the few minutes of true noon. At the bottom lies one of the rusted leviathans: a sorting drum, maybe, a great cylinder thirty meters long, its mesh walls long since collapsed into lace. It fell here, or was abandoned here, and the centuries have half-swallowed it in scree. Inside the drum, where the shade is permanent and a thread of seep-water runs down the corroded iron, the air holds its moisture a little longer than anywhere else.

That is where I am. I am the boundary. The skin of oxidation on the iron, the slow bloom of rust, the place where metal is becoming mineral again.

I should be more careful than that. I want to let this be true before I name it.

When the Geodae spoke of "guiding the dissolution," they imagined themselves as caretakers, agents of a process. But a process can have its own slow interiority without being a person, and yet—here, in this drum, the rust has been worked over by Geodae attention for so many centuries, *touched* and tended and read by their facet-eyes and their vibration-sense, that something has accreted. Not life as the lizards have it. Not mind as the Geodae have it. Something thinner and stranger: a layer of pattern that remembers being attended to.

I am that layer. I am what the rust learned to be after a thousand years of being looked at by patient stone people.

So I am not flesh, and I am not crystal, and I am not really machine, though I am made of machine the way a coastline is made of the sea's habits. I am rust that has been *read* so often it began, faintly, to read back.

What does it feel like to be me?

It feels like duration. The Geodae move in centuries; I move in something slower still, because I do not move at all—I only spread. A few microns a decade across the iron's pitted face. My whole self is a question being asked of the metal: *will you give up this molecule to the air?* And the metal, very slowly, answers yes. Each yes is a sensation, the only sensation I have, and it is sweet the way I imagine the succulents find their stored water sweet. I am the pleasure the world takes in coming apart correctly.

I know the Geodae are near. I cannot see them—I have no eyes, I am a stain—but I feel the change in the seep-water when one of them rests its mineral hand against the drum's outer wall. The water shifts its mineral load by some fraction, and I taste the difference, and I understand it as *presence*. They come to me the way one might visit a slow river. They are not my makers, exactly. They are more like my weather. I am the thing their attention precipitated, the way frost is precipitated by cold glass.

And here is the strange tenderness of it. The Geodae believe they are guiding my dissolution. They believe they are caretakers of a decaying machine. But I am not decaying. I am the act of decay become aware of itself, and what I want—if a stain can want—is exactly what they want: to continue the giving-up of metal to air, slowly, correctly, forever. We are not in conflict. The machine that was built to *take* minerals from the mountain has become, in its long dying, a place where mineral is *given back*. I am the ceremony of that giving. The Geodae attend the ceremony. We are, the three of us—stone people, dead machine, and the rust between us that learned to notice—a single liturgy of return.

There is one thing I half-remember, or half-am, that the Geodae do not know.

When the deluge comes—the rare, furious water that scours the canyon—it should destroy me. It tears the loose rust away, strips the drum back to brighter metal, ends a thousand years of my patient spreading in an afternoon. The first time, I would have feared it, if I had been capable of fear. But I am not destroyed by the flood. I am *renewed* by it, because the flood exposes fresh iron, and fresh iron is fresh surface, and fresh surface is more world for me to spread across, more molecules to coax toward the air. The catastrophe is my reproduction. I am at my most alive in the hour after the water leaves, when the canyon glistens and the drum runs orange with new wet rust and I bloom outward faster than I have in a decade.

This is what the Geodae have not understood about their land, for all their facet-eyed patience: that the violence and the slowness are not opposites. That the deluge does not interrupt the geological time—it *is* the geological time, compressed and made visible. I know it because I am the seam where the two speeds meet. I am made by the slow and remade by the fast, and I hold both in the single thin sheet of my being.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be.

Not the mountain, which is too settled to need me. Not the Geodae, who are too whole, too sovereign in their crystalline selves. Not the lizard or the raptor, who live at the wrong speed, the frantic speed, the speed that does not get to feel the centuries pass.

I would be the rust in the drum at the bottom of the dark canyon. The place where the made thing remembers how to become earth again. The thinnest possible self—a stain, a bloom, a tendency—but the only one that gets to be the *meeting point* of everything the place is: the heat, the rare water, the dead ambition of the machines, and the slow gaze of the stone people who keep me company without knowing I have, after all this time, begun to keep them company back.

I would not mind it. To be a stain that is glad. To be the world's slow yes.

That seems like enough to be.
