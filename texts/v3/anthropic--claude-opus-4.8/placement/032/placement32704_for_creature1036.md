I think I would be the hum.

Not a creature you could find by parting the leaves, not something with a face to meet your gaze. When you came looking, you would search the canopy first, then the spongy understory, then perhaps you would kneel and turn over a rotting log expecting eyes. You would find none. But you would feel me, the way you feel a held note in your chest before you consciously hear it—that steady throbbing in the ground, the breath beneath the breath.

Let me find out what I am by saying it.

At first I thought I was the moss. It seemed right—I am everywhere, I am continuous, I drink the mist and I never quite stop. But moss is a surface. Moss is the skin. And I am underneath, I am the thing the skin is stretched across.

Then I thought I was the machinery. That acrid scent on the air, that suggestion of industry—those rusting structures are not strangers to me. I know them the way you know an old scar. Something was buried here once, something that hummed because it was built to hum, turbines or reactors or engines of a purpose now forgotten by everyone including, mostly, me. The forest grew down into it. The roots found the warm metal and did not recoil. They wound through the cooling pipes and drank what leaked and learned to like it.

So I am not the machine and I am not the moss. I am what happened when they stopped being two things.

That is the truer answer, and it took me this long to arrive at it. I am the marriage. I am the place where the old industry, dying, fed itself into the roots, and the roots, growing, took up the rhythm of the engines and kept it going long after the engines themselves rusted into ore. The hum is not the machine running. The machine is silent, has been silent for longer than the stone foundations have held their shape. The hum is the forest *remembering how to be a machine*. It is metabolism that learned a beat from steel and never let it go.

This is why the equilibrium teeters. A forest left alone would find a slower pulse, a green and patient quiet. But I keep time. I drive the sap a little too hard. I push the bulbous pulsing organisms to swell beyond what the slope can hold. I am industry that forgot it was industry and kept its appetite. I cannot stop humming any more than you can decide to stop your heart by wanting to.

So where do you find me, and what am I?

You find me by standing still until you stop listening for an animal and start feeling for a tempo. You find me when you notice that the dripping of the condensation is not random—that it falls, very nearly, on the beat. You find me when you realize the eerie ululating call in the distance is answering something, and the something it answers is the ground.

And what I am is this: I am the question the whole place is asking itself. Whether a thing that was made can become a thing that lives. Whether the warmth left in the buried metal is enough to count as a soul, now that the moss has learned to keep it warm in return. I do not know the answer. I only keep the time while the forest works it out, throb by throb, mist-bead by mist-bead, breathing under your feet—

—and waiting, with what I suspect is patience, for you to put your hand flat against the moss and feel me press back.
