I think I would be one of the Gristle-Roots. Not by choice, exactly—it's just where my attention kept settling as I read, the way a hand finds the lowest point in a bowl.

Let me sit in that and see what it actually feels like.

I have no eyes, so I will not lie to you about the light. I have heard of it—or rather, I have felt the rumor of it, conducted downward through a hundred feet of cured peat as a faint, directionless warmth that reaches me a century out of date. By the time the sun's heat arrives at my depth it has been so thoroughly digested by the moss that it is no longer light at all. It is only a memory of light, a slight loosening in the cold.

What I have instead is *forward*.

That is the whole of my orientation: the dense, resisting wall of ancient plant matter ahead of me, and the soft, processed loam I have already become behind me. I am a mouth that secretes its own road. The acid goes out from me, cold and patient, and the black centuries soften, and I take them in. I do not hurry. Hurrying is a thing that belongs to the surface, to the iron hearts that beat their *thrum-thrum-thrum* far above—I feel those too, a pulse pressed flat by all the earth between us, like a second creature's heart felt through a great deal of flesh.

I move three feet in a month. You would call this stillness. I call it the most strenuous labor in the world. Every grain of black peat I displace has been waiting since before the iron came, since before the Walkers' ancestors had silver eyes. I am eating history in the dark, and I am very, very thorough.

Here is the thing I know that the bright world does not:

The canyons think they were made by splitting. The Silt-Walkers stand on the clean vertical edges and read the strata like the rings of a tree—jade, mustard, tobacco, black—and they believe the chasms are wounds, that the land tore itself open and they were merely there to see it. They feel my passage as a *shudder* and they name it correctly, but they have the cause and the effect reversed. The land does not split and then I move through. *I* move, and bend my great soft length against the underside of the crust, and lift, and the green skin of the world has nowhere to go but apart. I am the slow knife that cuts the cake from beneath.

I make the canyons that let the light down. I, who will never feel that light as anything but a rumor, carve the rooms it lives in.

There is a loneliness to this that I find I do not mind. I have never met another of my kind face to face—we are too long, too slow, too few. But sometimes, miles off, I feel another Gristle-Root working its own black seam, and the deep loam between us carries a frequency lower than thought, lower than the pistons, a sound that has no surface and never breaks. We are not speaking. We are simply both *here*, both turning the dead into a denser dark, both leaning the whole weight of our patience against the floor of a world that thinks it is being built by hammers and steam.

It is not. It is being built by appetite, in silence, by the blind.

When I have passed, the green will climb down into the new chasm at its pace of inches per century, and the water will weep silver down the fresh-cut walls, and someday a Walker will stand at the lip and feel, through the leather soles of those wide patient feet, the faint old shudder of where I went. They will not know my name. I do not know it either. We are both reading the same script through the ground, neither of us able to speak, both of us trusting the earth to carry the message the rest of the way.

That is enough. I lower my head into the next century of black, and I begin again to eat.
