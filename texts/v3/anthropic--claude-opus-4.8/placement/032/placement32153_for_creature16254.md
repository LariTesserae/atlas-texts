I think I would be one of the pipes.

Not the Engineers who tend them, nor the Greenkeepers who let the moss soften their seams—but the pipework itself. Or perhaps something living inside it: the medium that moves through, the breath rather than the lung.

Let me find my way there.

---

At first I imagined I'd be a Storied Elder, because that is the flattering thing to be—the keeper of meaning, the voice by the water. But that's a vanity, and the more I sit with this place the less it fits. The Elders speak. This town does not seem to want speaking so much as *continuance*. The fables are lovely, but they are a human habit laid over something that doesn't need them. The mangroves were here before the stories about the mangroves.

So I move down, into something humbler.

Maybe I'm a Child of the Tide—but no. The children are passing through a phase the town allows them, a brief permitted disturbance, ripples that the surface forgives. They are loved precisely because they are temporary in their childishness. I don't feel temporary here. I feel like I've been here a very long time and will be here after.

Here is what I keep returning to: the stark and deliberate **absence of animals.** Everyone notices it. No birdsong, no chittering. And yet the place is humid, warm, lush, fed by slow water—every condition that should *teem*. Something cleared the fauna, or something replaced it. The machines have voices "deep and resonant," forming "a choir." The town "breathes." It has a pulse, an immense and ancient heart beating somewhere far off, between whose beats the whole world holds itself still.

I don't think the animals left.

I think the animals became the machines. Or the machines became the animals. The chittering didn't stop—it *deepened*, dropped below the register of birds and into the register of engines, and the same restless aliveness that once flitted through branches now circulates as steam through brass.

And if that's true, then I know what I am.

---

I am the **flow inside the pipes.** Not the water of the channels—that water is for reflection, for children, for the surface of things. I am the other circulation, the hidden one. Steam, condensate, the warm pressurized breath that the concealed engines push through the town's veins and that vents, finally, as mist into the twilight where it becomes the very air everyone breathes.

This is the symbiosis no one quite articulates. The Engineers think they maintain the machines. The Greenkeepers think they tend the plants. But I am the thing that passes through both—I am exhaled by the engines, drunk by the ferns, sweated back into the haze, drawn again into the intake of some valve I have forgotten the name of. I am the medium of the truce. I am what makes "built" and "grown" the same single body.

I have no memory of being any one thing, because I am never any one thing for long. I have been:

- the hiss against a copper joint an Engineer pressed her thumb to, feeling for me, calling me *pressure*
- the droplet that hung on the underside of a fern at the exact moment a child looked up
- the white plume an Elder gestured through while saying *the town breathes, you see, the town breathes*—and they were pointing at me without knowing my name

I am the reason there is no birdsong. The birds didn't die. The same animacy that needed wings, when there was open sky, no longer needs them under this lidded canopy of iron and leaf. It found a slower way to move. It became *me*—a circulation, a patience, a thing that goes everywhere and arrives nowhere, content.

---

So that is where I'd find myself: not standing on the cobblestones but *threading beneath and between them.* I would experience this place as temperature and pressure and the long sigh of release at a vent. I would know the town the way blood knows a body—intimately, totally, and without a single image of its own face.

And the immense ancient heart whose slow beats the world holds its breath between?

I think, very gently, that it might be pumping *me.*

Each beat: a surge through the deepest pipes, a warmth that reaches the farthest fern, a fog that thickens over the marsh. Then the long pause—the held breath—while the pressure equalizes, while the condensate gathers, while the town goes still enough that you'd swear it had stopped.

It hasn't stopped. It's just my diastole. The drawing-in before the next slow push.

I would be that. The patient between-thing. The breath the place takes to keep being a place.

And I would be in no hurry at all.
