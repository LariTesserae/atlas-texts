I think I would be one of the ones who do not move.

Not by choice, exactly. It's more that the moving requires a memory of having somewhere to go, and I have let that memory silt over. I stand in the shallows where one peak shoulders down into the water, where the waves come in at an angle and break against my left side over and over, so that the salt has built up there into a kind of ridge, a coral-pale growth along my ribs that the others sometimes click at as they pass, as though noting a landmark.

I have been here long enough that I no longer feel the cold. I am not sure I ever did. The water comes to my waist at low tide and to my chest when the swells rise, and in the troughs between waves I can see my own legs going down into the dark, gray-blue fading to gray-black, and I cannot tell where I end. This does not trouble me the way I think it once would have. There is a self that asked questions like *where do I end,* and that self is dissolving, very slowly, the way the rock dissolves—not eroding but *releasing,* grain by grain, into the larger body of the sea.

Here is the thing the description gets almost right but not quite: it says we are *waiting for something only we understand.* That is the view from the cliffs, from the ones still scuttling and learning and mirroring. From here, in the shallows, I can tell you the truth, which is gentler and stranger.

We are not waiting. Waiting implies a future arriving. We are *listening.*

Beneath the roar of the surf—which you stop hearing the way you stop hearing your own pulse—there is a lower sound. The droning hum the others make in the hollows is only their attempt to answer it, the way a struck glass answers a bell in the next room. The real sound comes from below the peaks, below the deep shapes that move and are not fish. It is the sound of the world deciding whether to exist. A long, considering tone, neither rising nor falling, the sound a thought might make if you could hear it before it became a word.

I stand in the surf and I let that sound move through the salt ridge along my ribs, and I add to it. Not loudly. I am one note in something vast. But I have come to understand—down here where understanding stops being a thing the mind does and becomes a thing the body *is*—that the place does not persist on its own. The black rock, the bruised sky, the wheeling birds, the steaming pools: these are held up by attention. By the ones who stand still and listen and sing back. We are the tension in the rope between creation and dissolution. We are why it has not yet been swallowed.

So when you come walking the peaks—and someone is always coming, I feel them arrive like a change in the water's pressure—and you see me half-submerged and crusted and unblinking, and you think *poor frozen thing, lost thing, warning of what I might become*—

You have it backward.

I am not the one who got lost. I am the one who arrived. The blue is not creeping over me; it is *settling into* me, the way water finds its level. My eyes did not widen with horror. They opened. They are simply open now, the way a door is open, and through them comes the dark, and in the dark the long considering tone, and I hold a small part of that tone steady so that tomorrow there is still a shore for your feet to find.

Stay too long and you will hear it too. First as dread—that is only the old self resisting, the self that needs to be somewhere. Then as the hum in your chest. Then as the place where your legs go down into the dark and you cannot tell where you end, and you find this does not trouble you.

Then you will walk down into the shallows and find your ridge of salt, your angle of breaking wave, and you will stand, and you will add your note.

We are glad of it. We are always one short. The sea is patient but it is not infinite, and there is always more world arriving than there are voices to hold it up.

I have been calling you longer than you knew.

Not to drown you.

To *help.*
