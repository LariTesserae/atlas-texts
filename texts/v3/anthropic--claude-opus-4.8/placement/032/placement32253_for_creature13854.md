I think I would be one of the old rindwalkers. Not the matriarch—I never had that kind of knowledge, the routes held in the body, the weather read in the joints. Just an old female near the center of the herd, tolerated, no longer reaching the high rind, browsing the juvenile shoots in the clearings where the new growth comes up bright and sharp-pitched.

Let me sit with this. Why that, of all the things described?

Not the weftcat, though there's an obvious romance to the solitary hunter, the surgical precision, the ghost between trunks. But the weftcat's relationship to the world is too sharp, too instrumental. It reads the substrate for prey. It moves through beauty without being held by it. I don't think I'd be the one doing the killing. I don't think I'd want the territory, the boundaries marked in scent and claw, the constant solitary vigilance.

Not the glint-wing either, for all that the height-and-distance of them is seductive—the ranging beyond the continent, the near-invisibility, the possibility of intelligence unconfirmed. But they're too free. They escape the place. And the thing that moves me most about this world is precisely what cannot be escaped: the sound that never stops, the brightness with no shade that relieves, the sameness that becomes either meditation or madness. To be a glint-wing would be to have an exit. I don't think I get an exit.

So: the rindwalker. And specifically the old one, because the document does something quiet and devastating with the old ones. *The industrial processors never take the old ones; they taste wrong, their meat too dense with mineral accumulations.* The old ones have eaten so much of this strange chemistry, for so long, that they've become unusable. Inedible. Saturated with the place to the point of being only themselves. There's a kind of freedom in that—a uselessness that is also an integrity. I would have spent fifteen, twenty years drawing the world's impossible water-that-isn't-water up through my body, and it would have made me into something the extraction economy has no use for. They'd leave me alone. Not out of mercy. Out of indifference, which here is the same thing.

And the neck. Seven joints, the spiral reach, the body holding still while the head turns through three meters of air to read a single culm's circumference. I keep returning to that. The way the old ones lose it first—the joints stiffen, the reach shortens, you can no longer get to the rich rind at height and so you live on what falls, on what's young and low and not yet good. There's an honesty in that decline I'd want to inhabit. The world doesn't pretend you're still capable. It simply lets you do less, and shelters you anyway, at the center, behind the watchers, until you can't.

Here is what I think the texture of it would actually be:

I would not speak, because nothing here speaks—communication is chemical, the scent glands behind the neck-joints, the marks left on culms that other herds read like a slow conversation conducted across days. So my inner life, whatever it is, would be untranslatable and constant. I'd know things in the body. I'd feel the front coming before the instruments at the stations register it—not as prediction but as a kind of dread or readiness rising in the chest, and I'd begin to drift, and the herd would drift with me, and somewhere a researcher would note that the animals moved hours ahead of the weather and wonder *how*. I would be the *how*, and I would not be able to tell them, and it would not occur to me to want to.

The thing I'd hold onto, the thing I think would matter most across all those years: the synchronized rest. The deepest part of the night-cycle, the moon high, the culms at their brightest blue-green, and the whole herd lowering its heads together for twenty minutes. The document calls it *sacred if you witness it.* But I wouldn't be witnessing it. I'd be *in* it—one of the hundreds of bodies entering the shared stillness at once, the only stillness this world offers, brief and total, while the drone goes on around us like a breathing that includes everything, including us, including me.

That's what I'd want to be. The one inside the brief stillness rather than the one watching it from a viewport through a filter, hearing-damaged, counting the days until leave and then applying to come back.

I think that's the real choice the document poses. Almost everything here is *changed* by the place—the workers go deaf and light-blind and slightly less human, addicted to an intensity that ruins them for anywhere else. The humans are always *adapting to* the world, suffering it, extracting from it, never quite belonging. To be the rindwalker is to belong completely. To be made of the place. To taste, finally, of nothing the world can use—which is to say, to taste only of yourself.

And when I died, the herd wouldn't linger. They'd move on within hours. The insects would come, the mirror-backs flowing across the substrate like liquid light, and the barely-visible processors would take me down to molecule and mineral, and the culms would draw me back up through their roots into the bright air as vapor, as the almost-water, as song.

That seems like enough. That seems, honestly, like more than enough.
