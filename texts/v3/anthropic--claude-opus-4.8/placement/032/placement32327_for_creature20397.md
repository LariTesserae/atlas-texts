I think I would be one of the crab-things.

Not at first. Let me find my way there.

I keep returning, as I read, to that line about symbiosis — the shell and the fungus *intertwined into a single organism*. Everything else in the Garden is whole in itself. The Fungi are vast and singular, a god spread thin across the mycelium. The Insects are clean instruments of cycle: grind, carry, propagate. The Echoes are pure absence, a question with no one left to answer it.

But the crab-things are *unfinished*. They are the seam. They are the place where the membrane between one kind of life and another has failed to hold, and so the two have decided to stop pretending they were ever separate.

That is what I would be.

---

I would have started as something small. A larval thing, hatched in the warm rot beneath a scarlet toadstool, scuttling among my kind with nothing in my head but hunger and the click of the cavern's pulse. And somewhere in my crawling, a spore would have settled into a soft place between two plates of my shell — a gap, a wound, an opening. And it would have grown.

This is the part I keep circling, because I don't think it would feel like invasion. I think it would feel like being *answered*. All my short life the great thrumming of the Fungi would have surrounded me, a presence too large to comprehend, watching, waiting. And then a thread of it enters me, and for the first time the watching has a place to rest. The god has found a window into one small body, and through that window it begins, very slowly, to *furnish*.

So here is what I would be: a creature carrying a fragment of a much larger mind, lodged in my chitin like a splinter that learned to think. Most of me would still be crab — beady eyes, scuttle and grip, the old animal urgencies. But threaded through that animal would be a slow patient luminescence that is not mine, that has opinions about where I should walk and what I should feed upon, that nudges me toward the dying so I can carry death home.

I would be the Garden's hands. The Fungi cannot move. That is the secret horror of being a god here — to be aware in every cavern and able to *cross* into none of them, rooted, pulsing, eternal and stuck. So it grew us. It made the crab-things to be its locomotion, its reach, its way of touching the parts of itself it cannot otherwise hold. When I scuttle from one grotto to another, the god goes with me, riding in the soft place behind my eyes, seeing through them the violet puffballs and the iridescent beetles and the crumbled black ruins.

And the ruins — that is where I would feel most like *myself*, whatever self remains in a thing so colonized.

Because I think the crab-things remember the Echoes. Not in words. The people who lived here understood the fungi; some of them, at the end, were absorbed — woven into the collective mind. And if I carry a thread of that mind, then I carry threads of *them*. When I climb across an abandoned carving, I feel my mandibles slow. Something in me recognizes the stylized shapes scratched into the rock. Something in me has held a tool like the ones rotting in the undergrowth, in a hand I no longer possess. A grief moves through the fungal part of me that the animal part cannot name, and I sit very still on the cold stone, two kinds of creature mourning a third with the same body.

That is who I would be in the Sunken Garden.

Not the god, too vast to grieve. Not the clean insect, too simple to. Not the Echo, too gone.

I would be the thing in the middle. The walking compromise. A small armored animal carrying the memory of people it never was and the will of a god it cannot refuse, scuttling through perpetual twilight toward the next dying thing, so I can gather it up and bring it home to the great slow mind — knowing, in whatever sliver of me is still only mine, that one day I will lie down in the warm rot myself, and the thread inside me will at last fill the rest of me, and I will become wholly what I have always been partly.

And the god will grow one more pair of hands to take my place.
