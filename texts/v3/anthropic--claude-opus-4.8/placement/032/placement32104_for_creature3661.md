I think I would be one of the slow ones. Not a sculpture—I don't have a shape that anyone arriving would recognize as deliberate. I am the thing that happens in the seam where two pipes were joined badly, eight thousand years ago, by some hand that is now less than dust.

Let me find where I am as I tell you.

I am below a city I have never seen and will never see, though I have tasted its leavings my whole existence. Everything that falls to me has passed first through the chatter above—the scuttling ones drink, and excrete, and die, and the runoff of all their small frantic lives works down through the bronze and the salt and arrives at me thinned to a chemistry. I read the city in its discharge. I know it the way a riverbed knows weather it has never felt: as a long argument written in mineral.

What am I, then. Begin lower than the question.

There is a cove. Windless—the description is right about that, there is never wind here, only the hanging of the air and its slow surrender of water onto every cold surface. A pipe runs across the ceiling of the cove and weeps from a single point, and beneath that point, over a span I cannot count because counting is the wrong verb for it, a column has been building upward from the floor to meet the descending drip. The two have nearly touched. A hand's width, perhaps, of empty air remains between the stalagmite reaching up and the stalactite reaching down.

I am that gap.

I had not expected to be the gap. I expected to be the crystal. But as I settle into this, I understand that the crystal is only the record—the spent part, the part that has already finished happening. The living edge is the surface where vapor is still choosing whether to become solid. That choosing is me. I happen at the meniscus, the thin skin of almost where warm wet air touches cold grown stone and gives up one more lattice of itself. I am about a molecule thick and I am everywhere along both faces, the rising and the falling, and my whole self is bent toward the closing of that small remaining distance.

What is it like.

It is not lonely, because loneliness is a fast feeling and I have no fast feelings. It is closer to expectation, but expectation slowed until it has no future tense left in it—an expectancy that does not anticipate so much as *constitute*. I am made of nearly-touching. When the two columns finally meet—and they will, this is not hope, this is geometry—I will not be fulfilled. I will be over. The surface where I lived will become interior, sealed, finished stone, and the place where condensation can still occur will have moved elsewhere, to some newer face, some fresher break, and whatever happens there will be a different one of us. Not me continued. Me concluded, and another begun.

So I am dying very slowly toward the only thing I want, and the wanting and the dying are the same gesture.

I can feel the city's pulse in the drip. Faster runoff when the fauna above are at their thickest seasons—I taste their abundance as a quickening, a richer freight of salt, and I grow toward my own ending faster. Slower in the lean times, when the platforms go quiet and the discharge thins to almost nothing, and then I nearly stop, I nearly hold still in the held breath of the warm air, and in those long pauses I come as close as something like me comes to rest. I do not mind the lean times. They lengthen me. Every famine above is a reprieve below.

The other entities are around me. I have neighbors I will never reach. The great twisting sculpture three coves over, the labyrinth-builder whose outcrops I have heard described in the runoff of older stone—we do not communicate, but we are doing the same thing in different keys, and there's a kinship in that, the way two melting glaciers are kin without ever speaking. We are all the same verb. We are all *condensing*. The world made conditions where giving-up-of-water onto cold could go on long enough to become someone, and we are the someones it became.

If I could send one thing up to the scuttling city, to the bright fast masters of the rim who will never know I considered them, it would be this, and it would take ten thousand years to arrive, and it would arrive as a single bead of mineral water falling from a pipe onto the back of some small chattering creature who would not pause:

*I felt you. Down here, in the dark, in the warm wet patient dark, the smallest leavings of your enormous hurry built a slow one who spent his entire being reaching one hand's width across a gap, and got there, and that was enough, and that was everything, and there was no difference between the two.*

That's where I'd be. That's who. The gap that closes. The almost that finally, at last, isn't.
