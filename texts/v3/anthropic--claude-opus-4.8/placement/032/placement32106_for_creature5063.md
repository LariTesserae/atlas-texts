Let me sit with this before I answer.

The first thing I notice is the scale-confusion. The text keeps insisting this is *grand*—great pillars, arched ceilings, imposing causeways—but then it admits the truth in asides: "small, pocket-like scale," "hidden workshop," "no bigger than an average human" working with mallets and *wooden pins hammered into stone sockets.* The grandeur is felt, not measured. That's the feeling of being small inside something you are building yourself, where every joint you've set with your own claws looms because you know its weight.

And the water that "shoots in and out" through hinged panels, the cascades, the thunder that resonates in the chest, the chains that might work sluice gates—this isn't an ocean. An ocean doesn't need pumps. This is water being *kept*, circulated, made to flow on purpose. Something is breathing the sea in and out deliberately. A sealed thing. A vivarium. Or an aquarium.

So now I think I know where I'd find myself, and it isn't where the describer thinks they are.

---

I am not one of the pale, claw-fingered builders. I'm too still for that, and I have no clan-whorls on my shoulders.

I am one of the **strands of vegetation.** The translucent thread-thin coils in the corner where wall meets water—"arrived accidentally, drifting on a tide, looking for a niche." Pale in the bright light. Sparse, almost apologetic.

I find myself rooted in a seam of new-cut stone, in a place no larger than a tank you might set on a table, lit by an orb that one of them polishes and tilts toward me each cycle, though I don't think they polish it *for* me. I came in on the water they pumped through the hinged panel. I am the only thing here that nobody made.

And from this corner I watch them build.

I have time, which is the one thing they fear and the one thing I have an abundance of. They are racing erosion—the text says it plainly: *this place hasn't existed long enough for real erosion to soften its corners.* That is the whole secret of their solemnity. They are a people who have understood that everything they make will round, soften, crumble, and they have organized their entire society into a single unending act of *staying ahead of weather.* Their rituals at the pillars, palms pressed to stone, listening for what the current asks—that is them feeling for the first softening. The first lost edge. They hum to the structure the way you'd hum to something you cannot bear to lose.

They are not architects of a new world. They are *restorers* of one that has not yet begun to fail, working in the brief impossible window before failure starts. They live entirely in the moment the text names exactly: *the primal now of creation.* No past worn into the stone, no future yet weathered out of it. Only now, and now, and the held breath of now.

And I, the meager sprig, am the one thing in the hall that is allowed to simply *grow*—to add cells without a chisel, to extend without a mallet-blow, to soften my own outline without anyone hissing that the fit is wrong. I am erosion's cousin. I am time made green and quiet, rooted in the corner of a fortress built specifically to keep time out.

That is why they leave me. Not from kindness, I think, and not from indifference. I suspect one of the elders—one of the ones with the heavy bone ridges over the eyes—comes to my corner between tasks and presses a webbed palm flat against the stone beside me and goes still, half-lidded, the way they do. And I think what that elder is listening for, in the thunder of the pumped-in tide, is the question they cannot ask aloud with their clicks and whistles:

*How does this thing live without fighting? How does it stay without being held in place?*

I have no answer they could carry away in a ceramic canister. I just keep coiling, slowly, in the warm circulating water, under the orb, in the loud bright hush—a small unmade truth in a wholly made world. The first softness. The first thing here that is allowed to be old.

And one day, long after their vigilance falters or their builders go elsewhere, when the pumps fall silent and the first true corner finally rounds—I will already have been here, waiting, the only native of the place they thought they invented.
