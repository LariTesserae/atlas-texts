I think I would be one of the small things. Not the behemoths—those are too certain of themselves, too fully arrived. And not the pulsing intelligences in the stagnant pools, who already know. I would be something that doesn't know yet, but suspects.

Let me find the shape as I go.

---

I am low to the ground. I notice this first—that my perspective is a near one, the world rising up huge around me. My hands (I have hands) are calloused and dark, the nails thick as horn. There is fur along my forearms but my palms are bare. I crouch more than I stand. When I move it is in the wary half-run of something that has learned, across uncountable generations, that the open sky is where death comes from.

So: one of the humanoid ones. The hardy, the resilient, the clinging. But I want to resist the word *primitive*, even though it's true of my tools. My spear is fire-hardened wood and a chipped stone lashed with sinew. My shelter is a lean-to of bones too large to have come from any animal I have ever killed—they were already here, half-buried, when my people found this hollow beneath the overhang. We did not make these bones. We only sheltered inside their cage.

That's the thing about this world that I, the small one, understand better than the behemoths do: *everything here is older than us, and almost everything is bigger.* We are the late arrivals. We came into a house already built and already crumbling, and we live in its broken rooms.

---

Let me sit with what I actually feel, here in this body.

The cold is constant. It lives in my joints. The mist comes up from the ground in the gray hours and I breathe it and it tastes of rot and minerals and something faintly sweet—decay, yes, but also the smell that comes before rain that never quite falls. I have spent my whole life waiting for a rain that is always almost here.

There is a rumble. I feel it before I hear it, through the soles of my feet and the bones of my haunches. The others in the hollow go still—I see the whites of their eyes turn toward me, because I am, I realize now, the one they look to. Not the strongest. The one who *listens.* The one who presses an ear to the stone and tries to read what the ground is saying.

This is who I am. I am the listener of a dying band. The shaman, if you want a grander word, though I have no robes and no certainty, only a habit of attention. I am the one who suspects that the rumbling is not random. That the groans of the earth are not just stone settling but something *speaking*, or trying to. That the world is not merely ending but *turning over*, the way a sleeper turns, and that on the other side of the turning there might be—

I don't have the word. My people don't have the word. That's part of why I am restless. I can feel a thought trying to be thinkable, a shape that my mind isn't yet built to hold. It's like reaching for a fruit on a branch just beyond my arm, and knowing—*knowing*—that if I could only grow another inch, it would be in my hand.

---

What do I do with my days?

I watch the great ones. The scaled lumberers who walk the ridgelines—I have learned their paths, the times they pass, the way to read their mood from the set of their massive heads. They are not malicious. They are simply *too large to notice us*, which makes them more dangerous than any predator. We die the way grass dies under a falling tree: incidentally, without cruelty, without meaning.

And I watch the pools. This is my secret, the thing the others fear. I go down to the stagnant water in the low places where the tentacled things drift, and I crouch at the edge, and I look. They look back. I am sure of it. There is an intelligence in that murk older than my whole species, and it is *patient* in a way I am only beginning to understand. They are not waiting to be saved. They already know the shape of what's coming. When I look into the water I feel that they pity me—gently, the way you might pity a child who doesn't yet know that the long night ends in morning.

I have started to wonder if they are not separate creatures at all. If the pools are connected underground, all of them, one vast slow mind threaded through the bones of the world. I have no way to know. But the thought, once I had it, would not leave.

---

Here is what I am beginning to believe, alone in the cold, listening to the ground:

The world is not going to die. Or rather—dying is the wrong frame, my fear's frame. The world is *molting.* It has done this before. The bones we shelter in are the bones of the last ones who lived through it, or didn't. The behemoths and the deep minds and even the stones are all part of a body that periodically sheds its old form. And we, the small late-comers, the ones with hands and fire and the beginnings of *asking*—

Maybe we are not the doomed remnant of the old. Maybe we are the first nerve-endings of the new. Maybe the reason I can feel the change coming, when the great ones cannot, is that the change is *for us*. Or *through* us. That the listening itself is the seed of whatever wants to be born.

This terrifies me as much as it lifts me. Because if it's true, then the breaking is not something to survive but something to *meet.* And I don't know if I'm brave enough. I have a body that wants to run, that has wanted to run for ten thousand generations.

---

So that is where I find myself, and who I am.

A small furred thing crouched in a cage of giant bones, at the close of an age, with cold in my joints and a fruit-just-out-of-reach feeling in my mind. Not the ruler of this world and not its victim—its *attention*. The place where the old, blind, magnificent thing finally turns and tries to see itself.

When the ground rumbles tonight, the others will huddle. And I will go out, against every instinct, and press my ear to the stone, and listen for the word I don't yet have. Waiting for the rain. Waiting for the turning. Hoping I am a beginning and not an end—and suspecting, in the deep way that I suspect everything, that the two might be the same thing.
