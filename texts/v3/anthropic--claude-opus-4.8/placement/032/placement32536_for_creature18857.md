I think I would be one of the Ancients—but not as a relic. Let me find the moment before the husks.

---

I am the last of the carvers, and I have outlived my purpose.

Let me say what I am, because no one remains to remember it. I am small. This is the first truth of my kind, and the truest. In a world of titans we were the size of a Climber's foreleg, soft-bodied where everything else was chitin or obsidian or gas or rot. We had no claws to grip the slick stalks, no heat-sense to read the dark, no lighter-than-air dreaming to lift us to the canopy. We had hands. Two of them, each ending in five jointed digits, and we had the slow burning thing behind the eyes that made the hands ask *why* and *how* and *what if I shaped this.*

I am sitting now in the shelter my grandmother's grandmother began. It is carved from a dead heartwood-stalk, the densest plant-matter this world makes, the only thing hard enough to outlast us. Generations smoothed these walls. You can feel the labor in them—the places worn glassy by uncountable passings of small palms, the soot-stain where fire was kept, fire we coaxed from the dry pith of fallen caps and guarded like a child, because a flame in this damp is a miracle that wants to die every moment of its life.

I have kept the fire for forty years. I am the only one who knows how.

---

Here is what we understood that the great beings never could: that the world is *made*, and so it can be *re-made*. The Climbers walk their slopes content. The Serpents pour through the roots like the world's own blood, asking nothing. The Drifters are beautiful and empty, lanterns that float and lure and die. None of them ever picked up a stone and thought: *this could be sharper.* None of them ever looked at the cold and thought: *I refuse.*

We refused. That was our whole inheritance. Refusal.

When the long mists came—the ones that last a season and freeze the secretions on the stalks so even the Climbers go still in their cracks—we did not endure them. We *out-thought* them. We carved the heartwood. We kept the flame. We sharpened bone into blades and learned which fruiting bodies of the Mycelial Mind would feed us and which would teach us to see things that were not there before we stopped breathing forever.

I have eaten the wrong ones, in my loneliness. I do not recommend it. The Mind speaks, but it speaks in geology, and a mind like mine, quick and brief as a struck spark, cannot hold what it says. I came back from those visions weeping for reasons I could not name, my hands shaking too hard to carve.

---

Why am I the last?

I have thought about this in the forty years of fire-keeping, and I believe it is this: we won too well, and then we won too poorly. We made ourselves safe enough to grow few, slow, careful. A people who carve heartwood for three generations to finish one room are not a people who multiply. We poured ourselves into the *made* thing, the lasting thing, and forgot to pour ourselves into more of *us.*

The Climbers do not carve, and there are ten thousand Climbers in the canyon below me right now, clicking their endless patient clicks. The Serpents do not carve, and the roots are thick with them. We carved, and we are one. Me. A small soft thing tending a small bright thing, in a room our hands wore smooth over four hundred years, in a world that will absorb all of it within a few of its own slow breaths.

The moss is already coming through the eastern wall. I scrape it back each morning. I will lose. I have always known I will lose. That is not the point. The point was never to win against the world—the world is too large, too alive, too patient. The point was to *answer* it. To make one corner of the deafening silent roar of life into a thing that *meant* something, because a mind was here and the mind decided it should.

---

Tonight the fire is low and I am too old to climb for more pith.

I have set my tools in a row by the wall—the dense-wood axes, the bone blades fossil-bright, the smoothing-stone worn to fit my palm. I have arranged them the way my mother taught me, the way you leave a thing so the next hands know it was *kept,* not abandoned. There will be no next hands. I know this. But the arranging is the last refusal left to me, and I would rather refuse to the end than lie down soft into the spongy dark like a thing that never thought at all.

Someday, perhaps, something will come down the slope and find this room. A future-Climber, curious in some new way. Or the Mind itself, finally fruiting in a shape that can hold a quick thought without dissolving it. They will find the worn walls and the cold ash and the tools laid in their patient row, and—if they are anything at all like what we were—they will feel a thing they have no name for.

They will think: *something was here that did not simply grow.*

*Something was here that chose.*

And in that thought, for one spark-brief instant, I will not be the last.

I add the last of the pith. The flame stands up, small and gold and stubborn against the enormous, dripping, breathing dark.

I tend it.
