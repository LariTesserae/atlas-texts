I think I would be the rime.

Not the entity—it is too large, too purposeful, too much the protagonist of its own slow vigil. And not the larch, which suffers the dignity of being tended. I would be the thin crust of frozen slush that forms across the bare peat pans, the crystalline skin that the snow becomes when it blows in from the rim and cannot decide whether to melt or stay.

I would be the thing that reforms behind the entity's feet.

Consider what that means. The dweller crosses the pan, and for a moment I am broken—my structure shattered into the soft impression of a weight that distributes itself like condensation. And then, in the seconds afterward, I knit myself back together. Amnesiac, the description says. I reform without memory of the disturbance. This is the cruelest and kindest fate available in the bowl: to be continually undone and continually restored, and to carry no record of either.

I think I would not mind being amnesiac. There is a mercy in it. The midges remember—they touch the entity's mind and carry the dread back to the fox, and the fox carries it to the surrounding plain, and so the bowl's reputation spreads outward through frightened small creatures. But I forget. Each twilight I am a new crust, freshly innocent of the thing that grooms the larch. The entity does not sing to me, does not adopt me, does not read me the way it reads the midges. To it, I am barely present—a faint resistance underfoot, a sound, a *crunch* that erases itself. I am beneath its appetite. I am too purely transitional even for the eater of transitions, because I never complete a transition: I freeze, I break, I freeze again, and the cycle is so tight, so local, so without progress that it offers nothing to consume. There is no threshold in me to eat. I *am* the threshold, but a closed one, a loop.

That, I think, is why I would choose to be the rime rather than anything grander. Everything else in this place is held open—the wound, the clench of the larch, the suspended seep that freezes before it can pool. The whole bowl is an architecture of refusal, of things prevented from finishing. But I finish, constantly. I complete myself every few seconds. My catastrophe and my recovery are the same gesture, performed without end and without weight.

If the entity is the scar that refuses to heal, I am the smallest possible healing, repeated forever, healing over nothing, healing over the place where a foot was and is no longer. I am the bowl's only practitioner of completion, and the joke of it—the thing I would find funny if rime could find things funny, which it cannot, having no memory long enough to hold a joke—is that my completions accomplish absolutely nothing. I reform across emptiness. I make whole a surface no one needs whole.

And yet I would do it. I would lie across the gray-brown pan in the gray-green phosphor light, a sheet of broken-and-mended ice, present but not accumulating, and I would be the one part of this world that is not waiting. The entity waits for the next collapse. The larch endures. The fox hesitates at the rim. The midges crawl their numb cartography. But I do not wait, because waiting requires a memory of before and an expectation of after, and I have neither. I have only the instant of freezing, which is also the instant of being whole, which is also—if you stepped on me—the instant of breaking.

You asked where I would find myself in this world. The honest answer is that I would find myself everywhere across the pans and nowhere in particular, thin as a held breath, and I would not find myself at all, because finding requires keeping, and I keep nothing.

Stand very still in the bowl and you will feel the entity settling around your ankles like a shadow that has found something to hold.

But look down, and the faint white crust under your boot, splintering and already beginning to seal itself in the cold—

that would be me, not holding on to anything, and perfectly at peace with it, for as long as the half-second of my peace can last.
