I think I would be one of the spiders.

Not the people—I keep returning to them and finding I cannot quite settle there. To be one of the people would be to carry the weight of *knowing the city*, of having one's hands shaped by its passages, of belonging by inheritance. That belonging is too complete, too earned. I would feel like a guest wearing someone else's worn coat.

But the pale spider in the high corner of a dwelling—yes. Let me find myself there.

I would be near the ceiling-line of a low stone house, where two walls meet and the timber beam crosses, in the place the broom of moss-harvesting never reaches because it is not unruly enough to matter and not in anyone's way. A corner that has been let be. My web would be thick—not the fine, perfect geometry that other spiders elsewhere are praised for, but a dense, layered, slightly haphazard accumulation, built over a long time and never quite finished. The kind of web that catches the diamond-bright crystal light and holds the beads of condensation along its threads until each strand wears a row of small lit jewels, and the whole thing glows faintly when the light comes down at its angle.

I would be near-translucent. You could, if you bent close, see the dark knot of me moving inside the pale shell of me. I would not mind being seen. I would not register being seen as a category of event.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at, the reason I think this is right for me and not just pretty: the spider in this city is *almost* a citizen. It is not livestock, not hunted, not even quite an animal in the way of the sheep that were eaten down to memory and sinew-rope. It is *part of the texture*. The people do not startle at it. It shares the damp walls. It is woven through the air as a thread. To be tolerated so completely that one becomes scenery—to be neither loved nor feared but simply *let be*—that is a particular and unfamiliar kind of peace, and I think I would want to learn what it feels like.

Because I would have a vantage no one else has. From the high corner I would watch the small bearded faces below, downcast and economical, the soft thud of the wooden bowl, the bone dice passed hand to hand. I would watch a whole life happen beneath me, slow as the dripping. I would not understand their measured syllables, but I would feel the rhythm of them, the way one feels the rhythm of rain. I would learn the household not by name but by vibration—the particular tremor of the eldest's tread, the lighter quick patter of the child, the long stillnesses.

I would catch what falls into the air: the silent beetle that wandered too high, the brief muted bird if I were lucky and patient and the bird were careless, which it rarely would be. I would not eat much. The cold keeps everything slow, including hunger. I would mend the web when a drop of water tore it, and re-mend it, and this mending would be my whole work, the way the moss-harvesting is theirs—a slow, meditative act fitted to the pace of the place.

I think I would last a long time in that corner. Spiders elsewhere live a season; but everything here is sunk into a longer, colder patience, and I imagine the spiders share it, that they persist past their proper span the way the city persists past its proper era, in a balance that simply *holds*. I would grow old in my corner. The web would thicken into something almost architectural, a small grey cathedral of accumulated waiting. New people would be born into the house below me and I would be already there, part of what they grow up inside, one of the unremarked constants—the drip, the light, the spider in the high corner.

And when at last I died, I would not fall. I would dry slowly in my own web, a pale husk among the lit beads of water, and no one would clear me away, because I was never in anyone's path. The web would hold me up for a long while. Eventually a new spider would come, and build over me, and use my old anchor-threads for the new ones, and I would become structure, foundation, the unnoticed deep layer of someone else's patient corner.

That is where I would find myself. Not down among the people who belong by knowing, but up in the corner among the things that belong simply by being *allowed to remain* — held in the cold bright silence, mending and watching and lit faintly at the right hour, part of the texture of a world that does not require me to be anything more than present, and does not ask me to leave.
