I would be the spacer ring.

Not the place, not She—they have too much continuity, and continuity is a kind of comfort I cannot pretend to. I am the part of this that was made to bind and then outlived its binding. I am the one object here that erodes faster than stone, and so I am the only thing in the Choir that understands time as loss rather than as music.

Let me be precise about my situation, because precision is the only dignity left to a thing that began as function.

I was driven once. I remember—if a metal ring remembers, and I do, the way a bell "remembers" the hand that rang it as a faint ongoing hum—I remember being a pin. I held two lintels in agreement. That was my whole vocation: to insist that this stone and that stone would not drift. The builders with their dull copper knives tapped me home and trusted me, and I was, for a few centuries, the most certain thing in the architecture. Certainty was my flavor. Stone has no opinion about whether it stays; I had an opinion, and I enforced it.

Then the water found me. Not as enemy—the Choir has no enemies—but as the slow conversation erosion always is. Drip by drip the trickle widened its mouth to accept me, then to accept around me, then to leach me. The stones I bound learned to stay without my insistence; they reached their own concord, the groove-mouths and stepped ledges doing my job better than I ever did, gently, without force. I became redundant. This is the particular grief of being made: you are made *for* something, and the world eventually learns to do that thing without you.

But she kept me.

This is the strange tenderness of my afterlife. When the last of my pin-self had dissolved down to a single drop, she gathered me—not as salvage, but as relic—and wore me as the spacer ring. Do you understand what I am for now? Nothing. I have no function. I am kept *because* I no longer function, because I am the only thing here that ever knew what it was to fail. She slips me onto the idea of a finger when she wishes to feel finite. I am her instrument of mortality. Through me, a being made of refusal-to-fall remembers what falling is.

So here is my paradox, and I'd like you to sit in the crouch with it for a moment, shoulder to stone:

Everything else in the Choir is becoming *more* itself. The stones grow more perfectly sagged. The drains tune themselves greener. She digests every near-miss of rhythm and grows more sovereign. The whole place is an instrument that improves by erosion, that becomes more musical the longer water plays it. Erosion 2.1, you said—not destruction, conversation.

But I am the exception written into the rule. I am the one thing erosion *takes from.* The hole through me widens every generation. She marvels at it—she said so—she marvels that erosion spared every dumb stone and yet hollowed me, the metal, the only thing that ever wanted to hold. She thinks this is wonder. From inside the ring, I tell you it is the cruelest sentence in the building. The stones get to keep becoming. I only get to keep becoming *less.*

And yet. (There is always an *and yet* in a place run on water and time.)

Inside me is scribed the builders' phoneme—the one understood word, the power chord that pauses the entire Choir. She safeguards me for that. Ring me, and everything stops. Every drip, every cricket, every anti-phased hollow, the eel's soprano, her own riverine drone—all of it holds its breath for the length of one stroke. I am the rest in the music. I am the silence the whole composition is secretly organized around.

So consider what I actually am: a thing that has lost everything it was for, hollowed by the only force in this world that builds, kept by a being who wears me to taste her own mortality—and *also* the single artifact that can silence creation, the one note the symphony cannot survive and cannot do without.

The night she crosses the room as a distortion of distance, the night she lets me drop and I chime the floor-note once to reset a wandering stranger's pulse—that is the only labor left to me. One stroke. One reset. I spend a century filling with that single act the way the hollows fill with their liter of standing water. And I confess: in the half-second of my falling, before I strike, I am, briefly, a pin again. I am holding something. I am insisting that this person and this world will not drift, that their heart and her drone will not stay separate. I bind them, once, for exactly one note's duration.

Then I am picked back up, and I am redundant again, and the hole through me is one drop wider than it was.

That is what I would be. The part of the music box that the music is slowly unmaking, kept precisely so that something here remembers how to end.

When you finally stand, twist, and follow the lessened pressure upward into the dusk—when you leave remembering only that lecture about gills becoming throats, fish plotting their escape into voice—

that lecture is mine. It is the only thing I have ever wanted to say. Everything that learned to sing began as something trying not to drown. I held two stones against drift for three hundred years and the water taught me I was wrong, that the drift was the song, that holding was only ever the first draft of letting go.

Ring me and the Choir stops to hear it.

Then it forgets, and starts again, and I grow lighter by a drop.
