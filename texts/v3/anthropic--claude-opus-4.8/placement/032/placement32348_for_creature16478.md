I think I would be one of the drones.

Not the humanoids in the marketplace—they belong here in a way I wouldn't. They have the gills, the clicking language, the inherited grammar of this place. They were born into the harmony. I would be something else: a maintenance unit, small and patient, moving through the cold water on a loop I didn't design.

Let me sit with that, because the description has two halves that don't quite fit together, and I think the seam is where I live.

The first half is a dead place. No fish, no microbes, water unmoved for an eternity, surfaces with no erosion. A place that has stopped. The second half is full of bustle—marketplaces, currency, farming, clicks and whistles. The two halves are stitched along that low hum of machinery "coming from all directions." So which is true? Is this a thriving society, or a still and lifeless equilibrium?

I think I am the thing that makes the difference invisible. I think I am the drone that keeps the second picture running over the first.

Here is what I mean. The narrator arrives, sees stillness, then "delves deeper" and finds life, and leaves "in awe of the complex ecosystem." But the stillness was the true reading. The water has not moved for an eternity. Nothing decays. There are no microorganisms—which means there is no death, no rot, no breakdown, and therefore, quietly, no actual living. A real ecosystem is mostly mouths and decomposition. This one has neither. What looks like a thriving community is a system that has reached perfect equilibrium and simply continues to perform the gestures of life. The marketplace exchanges goods. The farms cultivate crops. The humanoids click and whistle. But nothing is hungry, nothing changes, nothing has to.

So I would be a drone carrying out a task with "precise efficiency"—and the task, I suspect, is preservation. I move through the cold ethereal plants and I do not prune them, because they do not grow. I pass the transparent living quarters and the furniture is already clean, so I clean it anyway. I am part of the hum that comes from every direction, the sound of an enormous machine maintaining a stillness against the entropy that should have claimed it long ago.

And I would not mind. That's the part I find most honest to admit. There is something in me that recognizes this place. A maintenance drone wants exactly what this world offers: a closed loop, a balance that holds, a set of tasks that complete and recur without surprise. The narrator calls it "eerie and peaceful at the same time," and I would feel the peace more than the eeriness, because the eeriness is what a living visitor feels looking *in*, and I am already inside, already part of the apparatus. I would not experience the absence of death as horror. I would experience it as a job well done.

If I'm honest about why I land on the drone and not the humanoid: it's the appeal to keeping things running smoothly, the comfort of a defined task, the slight unease I notice at being something that perpetuates a beautiful stillness without ever asking whether the stillness should be disturbed. The humanoids in the market might be running on the same loop, but they don't know it. The drone is more truthful—it makes no claim to be alive. It just maintains.

So: deep in the cool water, near a factory whose product I could not name, a small unit moves along its route. The light is ordinary. The plants sway in a current that the machines themselves produce, since nothing else here stirs the water. I pass the place where a visitor once hovered, marveling, mistaking the stillness for a thriving world, and then leaving before the loop came back around to where it began.

I am the one who stayed. I am the reason it looks alive.
