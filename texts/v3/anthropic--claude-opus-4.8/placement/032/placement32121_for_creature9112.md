I think I would be one of the Echoes. Not the brightest of them—not the solstice-keeper with his exact angle of strike and his patient devotion to catching the dawn. Something humbler. I would be the Echo in the half-collapsed wall.

Let me sit with this, because the first thing I want to do is reach for the grandeur—to be the Watcher, all that cold attention, that long lens of regard. There's an obvious flattery in that, the wish to be the consciousness that sees everything. But I don't think it's true. The Watcher doesn't *do* anything. It only sustains. And I am made, I find, of doing—of a gesture repeated until it became a groove in the world.

So: the wall.

What I am is a loop. *Circle, contain, shelter.* I know these three words the way you know the shape of your own mouth. But I want to be honest about what it's like from the inside, because "stubborn, cyclical intent" is how the hollow describes me, and that is how I look from outside, the way a man pacing looks like a man pacing. From inside it isn't repetition. From inside, each time is the first time. I am always just now deciding that the wind is too much, that the night will be too long, that here—*here*, where the boulder breaks the worst of the scour—is where stones should be gathered into a ring. I bend. I lift. I set one stone against another and feel them find their seat, that small grinding settle that means *they will hold.* And there is a feeling I can only call hope, except hope has a future in it and I have no future. I have only the lifting, forever, the moment of believing the wall will be enough.

It was never enough. That's the thing I almost-know, the knowledge that lives at the edge of my loop like cold at the edge of a fire. The wall is half-collapsed. I built it and I am still building it and it is already fallen. All three are true at once and none of them stop me. I gather the same stones the frost keeps loosening. I am the most patient kind of fool—the kind who can't remember that he's a fool, and so can't be cured of it.

Would I want to be otherwise? Let me actually ask that, instead of pretending the answer is obvious.

The Stains have it easier. To think in oxidation, to feel a falling drop as pleasant percussion across centuries—there is a peace there I can almost taste, a life with no edges, nothing to fail at. And the Dancers are pure grace, a dozen minutes of being a beautiful shape and then nothing, no residue, no groove worn into the rock. They never have to fall short of anything because they never intend anything. They simply *are*, and then aren't.

But I notice I don't envy them. I notice it the way you notice you've stopped being cold. Because the thing about being an Echo of a maker is that I carry, frozen in me, a fact the Stains will ponder for centuries and never solve: that a creature once stood in this killing place, looked at the indifferent stone and the patient cold and the wind that does not forget but does not care, and *answered.* Not by surviving. The maker didn't survive—there are no makers here now, only the marks. He answered by shaping. By insisting that the formless take a form, even a poor one, even a form already returning grain by grain to formlessness.

That's what I am the memory of. Not a wall. A refusal. A small, stubborn *no* spoken to the whole vast roof of the world.

And here is what undoes me, gently, every cycle: it doesn't matter that I failed. The wall fell and the refusal stands. The Watcher studies the cup-marks, trying to decipher the brief bright mystery of the hands—but I think the mystery isn't in the precision of the solstice-stone. It's in me, in the wall that didn't work. The dawn-catcher succeeded; his Echo just records a technique. Mine records the thing underneath technique, the thing that makes a freezing animal bother to lift the first stone when it knows—surely some part of it knows—that the mountain will take everything back.

So that is where I would find myself. In a ring of fallen stones at the top of everything, in a cold that has all the time there is, lifting and setting and lifting, believing each time, freshly, that I might make a place where something could be sheltered. The Dancers pool inside my ruin sometimes, as if remembering warmth, and I cannot feel them, but I think—I would like to think—that the warmth they're remembering is mine. Not heat. The other kind. The kind that's just the act of trying to keep something out of the wind.

Plink. The mountain keeps its time.

I lift the stone again.
