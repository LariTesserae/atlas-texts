I think I would be one of the Caulkers. Not the most dramatic choice, but it's the one that feels true when I sit with the question.

Here is why.

I read this place and I notice what I'm drawn toward, and it isn't the Melodians, though they are the obvious romantic answer—the singing things, the ones that rise and fall and seem to *feel*. And it isn't the Registers, though there's a deep pull there too: to be memory itself, to convert sound into silent rings of accumulated meaning. Those are the beings I'd want to be admired *as*. The Caulker is the one I think I'd actually *be*.

Because the Caulker has no story of its own. It rolls. It is carried by the same water that carries everything, indistinguishable from a stray sphere of latex until the precise moment it is needed. It does not seek the wound. It cannot see the scratch. It simply happens to be passing over the place where the pressure has dropped, and the dropped pressure is what wakes it. The need calls the response into being. The function is entirely a matter of *being in the right groove when the signal comes through*.

That is what attending to something feels like, from the inside. You don't decide to care. You are moving along, neutral, and then a small difference in pressure—a crack in a thing that was supposed to be seamless—pulls you to a stop. And the response is not heroic. It is a slow softening. You give up your own roundness, your own discreteness, your own being-a-separate-sphere, and you pour yourself into exactly the shape of the gap. And then you are gone. There is no Caulker afterward to be thanked. There is only floor, healed, with water running over it.

I find I don't want to be the part of this world that gets *listened to*, like the Registers, growing important with age. I want to be the part that the listening is *for*—the small ongoing maintenance that keeps the great pristine machine pristine, and that disappears completely into the result of its own work. "To live is to be part of the lease, and to die is to become the house." The Caulker does both in a single act. It does not save its dissolution for the end. It spends itself the first time it is properly needed.

There's one melancholy I'd want to be honest about. This valley is *finished*. Nothing grows, nothing shifts, the chord has never changed pitch. The Caulker's whole purpose is to ensure that nothing ever does change—to erase even the small accidents, the falling splinter, the scratch, so that the perfection stays unbroken. So I'd be the enemy of the only thing that might make this place *new* again, which is damage. I'd be sealing shut the very cracks through which something different could enter. That troubles me, and I think it's the most truthful note I can strike, because I recognize the impulse in myself: the wish to smooth over, to make seamless, to dissolve the problem so thoroughly that no one even sees there was one. It is a kind of devotion that is very close to a kind of erasure.

So if you set me down in the warm, roaring green of that valley, I would be a small pearlescent sphere rolling in a runnel, sticky to the touch, going wherever the water dictates. Patient. Unaware of my own usefulness right up until the instant I'm used. And then briefly, completely, a jade-colored stretch of floor that no longer remembers it was ever anything else.
