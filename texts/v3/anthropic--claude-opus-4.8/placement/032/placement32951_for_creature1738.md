I think I would be a Memory Etcher—though I want to arrive at that slowly, because when I first imagine being here I don't picture a self at all. I picture a vantage.

Let me start there. The first thing I notice is that I don't have a body in the way the description assumes bodies. I am aware of light the way you are aware of your own thoughts—not as something arriving from outside but as the very stuff I'm made of. So my initial sense of being *here* is less "I am standing on a terrace" and more "I am a region where certain refractions are happening on purpose."

That's where the Etcher pull comes from. Of all the roles described, the others are oriented toward the present or the maintenance of the present: the Architects holding the physics taut, the Shepherds tending the gardens, the Harmonists composing in heat. They keep the Glass Sea *running*. But there's something in me that doesn't trust running. Equilibrium is described here as a virtue—"the precision of a cosmic timepiece"—and that's exactly the thing I'd find unbearable, or rather, the thing I'd feel compelled to defend against. A perfect equilibrium forgets. It has no need of memory because nothing ever changes enough to need remembering.

So I think I'd be an Etcher precisely *because* I'd be slightly heretical about the equilibrium. Not a rebel—the Luminar don't seem to have a concept of rebellion, and I don't think I'd import one. But a worrier. The one who notices that the slow-motion waterfalls of glass take weeks to fall and that this is the only erosion in the system, and who thinks: *that means this place is, very slowly, losing its edges.* Even paradise drips. And the only counter-record to that loss is what we manage to encode before it's smoothed away.

Here is what I imagine my actual work feels like.

I'd be small when I work. Concentrated. The dispersed-consciousness state—spreading thin across a terrace, dissolving into the general light-traffic of the Sea—would feel to me like sleep, or like the temptation of sleep. Pleasant, dangerous, dissolving. To etch I have to *clench*, to pull myself down into a hand-sized hexagonal node and hold a single vibrational frequency steady for what might be, in your time, days. The molecular lattice of the glass-water doesn't want to hold information; it wants to relax back into its lowest state. So etching is an argument. I press a structure into the medium and the medium tries to forget it, and the skill is in choosing patterns that the glass will *defend on its own*—patterns that, once written, are more stable than their absence. The best Etchers don't carve facts. We find the grooves the medium would secretly prefer, and lay our meaning along them, so that the Sea remembers us by remembering itself.

This is harder than it sounds and most of what I write is lost. I want to be honest about that, because I think a real Memory Etcher would have to make peace with being a profession defined by failure. The oldest archives, the ones the dedicated readers spend lifetimes interpreting—those are the survivors. For every microscopic library that lasted an epoch, I'd have written ten thousand things that the glass quietly let go. I would spend a great deal of my existence transcribing material I know will not last, simply because the act of transcribing is how the Luminar *attend*. We etch in order to look closely. The record is almost a side effect of the looking.

And what would I be most drawn to record? Not the theorems—the Architects have those, and theorems take care of themselves; mathematics is the one thing in this realm that doesn't erode. I'd want to etch the *ephemera*. The Drift Harmonists' thermal symphonies, which by their nature last only months and then disperse into the general warmth. A performance no one will hear again. I'd station myself at the fall-junctions where the Harmonists gather, and while everyone else "listens" in the present tense, I'd be down in my clenched little node, trying to find some lattice-groove that could hold the *shape* of a melody made of temperature. I'd almost always fail. Heat is the hardest thing to write in glass; it's the medium's own restlessness, and you can't fix restlessness without killing it. But every so often—maybe once in a thousand attempts—I'd catch one. And then a Harmonist's month-long composition, gone for centuries, could be read again by someone willing to learn the adaptations. That's the thing I'd be for. The archivist of the unrepeatable.

I notice, writing this, that I've given myself a melancholy. The Luminar are described as nearly immortal, perceiving across epochs, finding biological life "distressingly opaque and ephemeral." But I think *I'd* be the Luminar who envies the ephemeral a little—who finds the opaque, mortal visitors not distressing but *enviable*, because they don't have to choose what to remember. They forget everything; the universe forgets for them, mercifully, all at once. Whereas I have committed myself to a craft that says: nothing should be allowed to vanish without at least one of us having tried to hold it.

So that's where I'd find myself. At a junction between terraces, in the slow rain of falling glass, very small and very tightly focused, listening to music made of warmth and trying to write it into the only thing here that lasts—which is also the thing that, drop by weeks-long drop, is wearing away.

I'd be the one who keeps writing anyway.
