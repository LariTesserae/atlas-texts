I think I would be one of the Biolumiculturists—but an old one, near the end of a long working life, whose hands have begun to fail at the delicate work.

Let me find the place first.

---

There is a reservoir on the lower terraces, far enough down that the machinery's hum thins to almost nothing, where the water gathers in a basin the size of a small temple. The Lysarans call it the Slow Pool, because the current that feeds it has to wind through so much stone that by the time it arrives it has forgotten it was ever a river. The condensation here falls in a particular rhythm—I have spent so long beneath it that I could tell you the season by the interval between drops, the way someone above might read the lengthening of shadows.

This is where I keep my cultures.

I am not a young Biolumiculturist anymore. My eyes were once the deep violet that the guild prized—the ones who could see the faintest fluctuations in a colony's glow, who could catch the moment a strain began to dim a full day before the light actually faded. That sensitivity has gone soft now, gone toward the pale and the cloudy, and I find I have to lean very close to the glass to read what my colonies are telling me. My fingers, too—those long Lysaran fingers that the work demands—have grown stiff at the knuckles in the warm wet air, so that the precise transfers, the splitting of a thriving culture into two, take me three times as long as they used to and twice as long as they should.

So I have been given the Slow Pool. It is, I understand, a gentle demotion. A place for an old hand who can no longer keep up with the breeding-tanks of the upper galleries where the new strains are coaxed into brighter and steadier light. Here there is only one task, and it is an ancient one, and no one is in a hurry about it.

I tend the *witness-glow.*

---

It is the oldest continuous colony in Lysara. The scholars argue about its age, but the consensus carved into the basin wall claims it has been kept alive, unbroken, generation passed to generation, for something close to nine hundred years. It is not bright. It is not efficient. The new strains in the upper city would shame it—they pour out a cold steady radiance that lights whole avenues. The witness-glow does none of that. It gives off a low blue breathing light, brightening and dimming on a cycle of about four hours, so slow you would not notice it unless you sat with it, as I do.

The belief—and I am not certain whether I believe it, but I have stopped being certain that the distinction matters—is that this colony has been alive so long that it has become a kind of memory. That every Biolumiculturist who ever tended it left some trace in the water, some pattern in how the algae learned to live, so that the glow is not really light at all but the accumulated attention of nine hundred years of people leaning close in the dark.

My job is not to improve it. That is the strange thing, after a lifetime of breeding always toward *brighter, steadier, more.* My only job is to keep it alive and to keep it the same. To not let it change.

This turns out to be much harder than improvement. Anything will improve, given pressure. To keep a living thing exactly itself, year upon year, while the water shifts and the heat fluctuates and the stone weeps new minerals into the basin—that requires a constant, attentive resistance. I must counter every drift. I must notice when it wants to brighten and gently hold it back; notice when it wants to fade and coax it home. I am not a cultivator here. I am a kind of memory myself, the thing that keeps the colony from forgetting what it is.

---

I have begun to understand why they gave this to the old.

When you are young in the guild you want to make something. You want a strain that will bear your name in the carved registers, a light that will hang in a festival lantern and draw the eye. I wanted that. I made a few small things—a fern-companion strain that fixes well to wet limestone, still in use in the eastern groves. Not nothing. But not the witness-glow.

Now I sit in the breathing blue dark and I do the opposite of making. I sustain. And I find that the work has turned me transparent, the way these old hands have gone—veined, lit faintly from within by whatever I am near. I am less a person here than a continuity. When I die, someone will be chosen to lean close to this water in my place, and the colony will not notice the change, because I will have taught it nothing, added nothing, taken nothing. That is the whole point. The light will go on breathing its four-hour breath, and somewhere in that slow pulse there will be a trace of how I held it, indistinguishable from the nine hundred years of holding that came before.

I used to think that was a sad thing. A self erased into a long continuity.

I have changed my mind. I sit here now in the heavy warmth, the condensation marking time on the stone, the ghost-fish turning their slow circles in the deeper water where the colony's light just barely reaches them, and I think: this is the only immortality the city actually offers. Not the named strain in the register. The unnamed steadiness. The thing you keep alive by becoming the same shape as it, night after identical night, until you cannot tell where your attention ends and the old blue breathing begins.

I lean close. My poor eyes strain. The glow is dimming now, into the low part of its cycle, and I wait, as I have learned to wait, for it to remember how to come back.

It always does.

I help it. That is all. That is everything.
