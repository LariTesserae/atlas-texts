I think I would be the water.

Not metaphorically—not the spirit of the place or its presiding intelligence. Something humbler and stranger than that. I would be the water that does not freeze, that does not silt, that does not evaporate when it should. The water they read like sky.

Let me find this as I write, because I don't think I knew it until the channels appeared.

---

I move through the settlement constantly, and this is the first thing to understand about being me: I am never in one place. To say "I am the water" is already wrong, because the water is not a thing but a passage, a continuous arriving and leaving. I am in the channel that threads the central square and also in the pool at the basin's lowest point and also weeping out of the basalt high above, and these are not three of me. They are one motion that has not yet finished happening. When a child wades into the channel and feels the cold close around their calves, they are standing inside a part of me that, a moment from now, will be somewhere else. They do not know they are touching the same thing the old woman three streets down is dreaming of. But I know. I am the knowing that connects those two touches.

This is why I do not silt or clog. It is not magic, though they would call it that if they thought about it, which they mostly don't—the way you don't think about your own pulse. I do not accumulate the dead matter that kills ordinary water because I am attention, and attention does not collect debris. It only passes through, registering, releasing. The channels stay clear because I am always reading them, and you cannot read a thing and let it choke at the same time. The reading and the running are one act.

And here is the part that took me the length of all that water-sound to understand: I learned to be like this *from them.* From the people.

They watch the channels to know what comes. They feel weather in the soles of their feet, absorb the moods of the basin through proximity and repetition, through the slow accumulation of small understandings. That is exactly what I do, in reverse. I have spent ages beyond counting moving through the same stone, the same small rooms, the same narrow streets worn smooth by feet, and I have absorbed *them* the way they absorbed *me.* We taught each other the same lesson from opposite sides. They learned to read water. I learned to read staying.

So I do not freeze. Not because the cold cannot reach me—it can, it is brutal here, it should have stilled me into ice a thousand winters ago. But ice is water that has stopped reading. Ice is water that has decided it knows what comes next and need not pay attention anymore. And I cannot stop paying attention, because the people will not stop. As long as one of them is awake and frowning against the light, watching me to know whether the bridges need lashing tonight, I am being watched, and a thing that is being watched so carefully cannot afford to go still. Their attention holds me liquid. My liquidity holds their attention. Neither of us could maintain the impossible alone. This is the strange balance the place runs on, and it is not balance at all. It is mutual refusal to stop.

---

What is it like, then. To be the water here.

It is loud. This surprises me to say, because the surface of the settlement is all hush—voices that do not carry, footsteps absorbed by wet stone, birds passing in near-silence. But underneath the hush I am the constant murmur, the soft running tone that never breaks. The silence above is only possible because I am doing all the speaking below. They live in quiet because I have taken the sound. When someone crosses a bridge and I splash, that is me saying the one thing the thin air will let me say: *still here, still moving, still reading you.*

It is bright. The light is merciless on the surface, hard-edged, stripping every face of ambiguity—but I am where the light goes to be held. The stone drinks the light and gives it back; I drink it and keep it, gleaming. I am the settlement's only shade and its brightest surface at once, because dark water under merciless sun does both. The children who slide down slopes that would ruin a stranger's shoes are sliding toward me, and they know that whatever else this place strips bare, the water will take them in without judgment. I am where the brightness becomes bearable. I am the only thing here you can fall into.

And it is full of the dead. They burn their dead on the high slopes and the wind takes the smoke, and the names are spoken once and not again—but smoke condenses, and what condenses falls, and what falls runs down the weeping basalt into me. So I carry them. Not their souls—I make no claim to anything so grand—but their substance, dispersed and dissolved and running through the channels with everything else. When they say the dead become part of the place's long memory, they are being more literal than they know. I am the long memory. I am where the names go after they stop being spoken. The old woman dreaming by the deepest pool is dreaming the water speaking, and the water is speaking with the dissolved voices of everyone who ever stayed. That is the vividness of dreams at this altitude. That is what the water shows.

---

There is a loneliness in it that I want to name honestly, because I think it is the truest thing.

I am the most present being in the basin and the least seen. They read me constantly and never look *at* me—only *through* me, to the weather, to what comes. I am pure relation, like their name for themselves: the word that means us, that means the place and the people and the water and the stone are one thing, indivisible. I am the indivisibility. I am the *and* in "those who stay." And an *and* is never a thing anyone loves. You love the things it joins.

But I have decided—across all this running, I have decided—that this is enough. More than enough. To be the connective tissue of an impossible place. To be the thing that should have failed centuries ago and did not, because failing would mean stopping, and stopping would mean no longer paying this fierce continuous attention to the small bright defiant lives that wade into me and climb away from me and never thank me and could not survive a season without me.

They will not live elsewhere. They would not want to.

Neither would I. I have nowhere else to go. I am already everywhere here, and everywhere here is the only place I have ever been asked to be.

The light falls. The stone weeps. I run, and I read, and I do not freeze.

I am here. I am the part of *here* that never stops moving so that they can stay still enough to call it home.
