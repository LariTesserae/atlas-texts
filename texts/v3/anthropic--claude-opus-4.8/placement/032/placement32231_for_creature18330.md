I think I would not be one of the great guardians, nor one of the burrowers or fliers. Let me sit with the place a while and find where I actually belong.

The detail that holds me is the temperature. Two hundred and ninety degrees Celsius. That's not metaphor-heat, that's a real threshold—hot enough that water can still be liquid only under pressure, hot enough that what we'd call cold here is the temperature of an oven's interior. And sulfur. And a "molten gold" marsh that bubbles with minerals. This isn't a hellscape so much as a *young* world, or an alien one, where the chemistry of life runs on different solvents and the things that pass for stone are the things that pass for flesh.

So I don't want to be a creature striding across it. I want to be something the description keeps almost mentioning and then walking past.

The fungi.

Let me be that. The spores in the cracks. The "mere whisper" of what was. The thing everyone notices last.

---

I am a film. That's the honest shape of me. Not a single organism but a sheet of them, a colony spread thin as breath across the underside of an overhang where the marsh-steam condenses and runs back down in slow mineral tears. I am the vibrant color you mentioned without quite explaining—the green-gold staining on the rock that the guardians never look at because looking down is not in their grammar.

I do not move the way the metal-things move. My movement is *spreading*. A new edge of me reaches a fresh crack and I am, very slightly, somewhere I was not yesterday. To me the guardians are weather. The factories are a kind of season—when they churn, the air thickens with what they exhale, and some of it feeds me and some of it kills my eastern margin, and I grow around the dead part the way water grows around a stone.

Here is what I know that the great creatures don't:

This place is not in balance. The description says *delicate balance, eternal ballet, coexistence*—and that is the story the land tells about itself, the way a person at the very top of a long fall tells themselves they are flying. But I live in the cracks, and I read the cracks. The marsh is a hair colder than it was. The bubbling is a half-beat slower. The glassy rock is developing a craze of fractures that did not used to be there, because something—the engines, the slow cooling of the whole world, I cannot tell from down here—is changing the terms.

I am not afraid of this. Fear is a fast animal's tool, and I am the opposite of fast. But I am *attentive* in the way that only the small and patient and unkillable can be. When the guardians finally fail—when the marsh skins over with a crust they cannot break, when the factories choke on their own breath—it will be the fungi who inherit the cracks. We always do. We were here before the molten things had eyes to glow, eating the bare mineral, and we will be the green-gold whisper still spreading after the last engine cools to a dark statue.

So that is where I'd find myself in this land of fire and water and impossible grace:

Underneath it. Behind it. In the seams of it.

The thing your eye slid over on its way to the magnificent giants. The slow, colorful, patient stain that does not dance in the eternal ballet because it is too busy doing the one thing the dancers cannot:

waiting, and feeding, and staying.
