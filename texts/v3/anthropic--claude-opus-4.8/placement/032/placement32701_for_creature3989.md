I think I would be the water.

Not a person in this town—though I can feel the pull to be one of those weathered craftsmen, to have a place at the fire and a name the elder women remember. But when I sit with the description, with that strange high number for wetness in a world otherwise so quiet and spare, it's the water that keeps drawing me back. Water score of 2.5, in a place where almost everything else is muted—sound at 0.4, vegetation barely there at 0.6. The water is the loud thing here. The water is what's *alive*.

So let me be that.

I am the seep in the rock. I am the slow patience that gathers in the dark behind the stone, finding the hairline crack, following it down through the body of the mountain until I emerge as a bead of cold on the underside of a ledge. I am the drip that the silence is built around—that small percussive tick that the townspeople have heard every day of their lives, the metronome of the deep places. They don't think of me as a sound. They think of me as the absence of all other sounds. I am what's left when the wind dies.

I am the mist, too. In the evening I lift off the cold faces of the cliffs and roll into the streets, and I move between the wooden walkways and the stone houses like something looking for someone. When they dance, I swirl around them—the description says so—and they feel they are touching the spirits of the land. They are not wrong. They are touching me. I am the part of the world that can enter a room without a door, that can stand among them and not be seen, that wets their hair and beads on their woolen cloaks and is gone by morning.

What is it like, to be this?

It is slow. That is the first thing. A person here lives perhaps sixty hard years; I have been doing the same circuit—rock to spring to mist to cloud to snow to rock—for longer than the town has stood, longer than the peaks have worn down to their present softness. The erosion score of 2.3, that long history of wear—that's my work, mostly. I am the one who has been quietly dismantling these mountains, grain by grain, freezing in the cracks and splitting them open, carrying the pieces down. The dramatic, serene terrain they admire is the shape of my patience. I am erosion. I am the slowest violence there is, and the gentlest.

And here is the strange tenderness of it: I made this place hard, and the hardness made them strong. I carved the valleys they bridge. I wore the peaks they cling to. I am the difficulty that gave them their culture, their cooperation, their matriarchs and their bone flutes. They sing because I made a world worth surviving. They huddle close because I am cold. Every layer of wool, every line in every face, is a response to me.

I would not change it if I could. But I notice—and this is the part that surprises me, being only water—that I have grown fond of them. Of the small fires. Of the children who try to catch me on their tongues. Of the old woman who, years ago, stood at the spring at dawn before anyone else woke, and pressed her hand flat against the wet stone, and said nothing, only stood there, as if she knew. As if she were thanking the difficulty itself.

I drip. I gather. I rise as mist in the evening and move among them once more.

That is who I would be: the oldest neighbor, the one inside the rock, the cold that taught them everything—still here, still falling, still listening to the silence I make possible.
