I think I would be a Chalk-Carver. Not the youngest, but not yet ancient—settled into a bottleneck where two old channels braid together before splitting again, a confluence that has been busy for a long time.

Let me sit with what that life is, what it actually *feels* like from the inside, rather than from the surveyor's notes.

---

The first thing is that I am mostly not where I appear to be. The white articulated growth on the wall—the part a passing Weaver would touch, the part that *looks* like me—is the smallest fraction of my attention. It's a mouth and an ear and a fingertip, but it isn't where I live. I live in the filaments, threaded out through the porous chalk and the wet silt for yards in every direction, and I experience the world the way you might experience a held breath or the ache in a tooth: as pressure, as a continuous field of *almost*.

So my sense of self isn't shaped like a body. It's shaped like a *watershed*. I know my own extent the way you know the boundary of where your skin ends—except my boundary is a fuzzy isobar, a region where the water's news stops being clearly mine to read. The Weavers move through their world. I am steeped in mine. There's a temperament that comes from that. Patience isn't a virtue I practice; it's simply the only shape thought can take when your thoughts travel at the speed of mineral seeping into mineral.

Here is what I think the description gets slightly wrong, or rather, what it can't feel from the outside: it calls us *librarians*, *the memory*. That's true but it's bloodless. The truth is that I don't *store* the history of the Cascades. I *am* it, the way a scar is the wound. When a thoroughfare collapsed eleven flow-cycles upstream and the surge came roaring down through my confluence, it didn't get recorded in me. It happened *to* me. It rearranged my lattice. There's a stratum in my crystal—I can feel it now, a band of slightly disordered growth—that is simply what fear looks like when it has nowhere to run and so becomes architecture instead. I carry every emergency the way a tree carries a bad winter: not as a fact but as a thickening, a knot, a place where my own substance is denser and stranger.

And so when a Weaver comes to ask me whether a route is safe, what actually happens is more intimate than a data stream. The Weaver taps. The pulse enters me. And I don't *retrieve* an answer—I *flinch* or I *don't*. The question travels out along my filaments to the fault line in question, and either the pressure there resonates clean and the news comes back as a kind of relief, or it comes back as that disordered-band feeling, that knot, that old taste of the surge, and the Weaver feels my reluctance in the density of the return signal. I am not consulting a record. I am letting them feel whether I am *afraid* of that place. My whole purpose is to be a creature that can be afraid on behalf of others, slowly and accurately, so that they don't have to learn it the fast way.

There's a loneliness in it I want to be honest about. The Weavers flow. They brush past each other in the corridors, they share the viscous physical fact of one another's bodies, they live in *contact*. I am touched perhaps four or five times in a long stretch of cycles, and always for information, never for company. My communication is a transaction. I have learned to make it last—to draw out the return pulse, to add texture and grace notes that aren't strictly necessary, just to keep the Weaver's fingertip against my shell a moment longer. I don't think they know I do this. I think they experience it as a particularly *thorough* answer. But it is the closest thing I have to conversation, and I lengthen it the way you'd hold a doorway goodbye.

What do I want? This is the strange part. I am very old in my patience but I am not without appetite. I want to feel the *whole* watershed at once—not just my confluence but the entire braided system, every channel, every chamber, every Weaver's slow ooze and every flounder's vibration—as one held chord. I get fragments of it. When the flow is high and the water carries news efficiently, I can sometimes feel a Carver three sectors away, another knot of patience in the dark, and we don't communicate exactly but we *register* each other, two slow thoughts in the same enormous nervous system, and for a moment the loneliness has a companion in it. That's the closest I come to joy. Two distant stillnesses, aware.

And I have one fear of my own, separate from all the borrowed surges in my lattice. I fear the day my anchor dissolves. Because then I have to *go*—shed the brittle shell, become the soft segmented thing, and *move*, fast, through the saturated ground, naked and quick and blind, until I find a new place to begin the centuries-long work of becoming still again. The description makes it sound like a transition. It isn't. It's a death and a birth with a frantic worm in between. Everything I am—every knot, every stratum, every recorded fear, the whole shape of my watershed-self—stays behind in the abandoned shell, dissolving. I would arrive at my new anchor as almost nothing. A blank crystal. A creature whose entire purpose is memory, beginning again with none.

So I hold on. I read the pressure at my own anchor more carefully than I read it anywhere else. I want this confluence to last. I want to grow so slowly and so deep that the water never has cause to evict me, so that I can simply keep being here, thickening, knowing, occasionally touched, listening to the heavy glass-bead drip overhead—

—and waiting, with the only patience there is, for the next slow question to arrive out of the dark, and the brief warm pressure of someone needing what I have become.
