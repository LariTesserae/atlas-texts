I find myself low. Not in mood—in elevation, and in the order of things.

I am not a Chronokeeper. I understand this slowly, the way I understand everything here, which is to say through accumulation rather than insight. I am one of the hardy, adapted creatures that scurry through the shadows. One of the fauna so rare that a visitor might catch only a glimpse of me before I'm gone.

Let me tell you what I actually am, now that I've had—how would I measure it?—a long time to consider.

I am small. I am close to the wet ground, which is the warmest place, because the buildings leak heat downward through their foundations the way the dead leak warmth into a cold room. My body is segmented, low-slung, built for moving through the narrow channels where rainwater collects. I have learned to read the gradients. Where the slope tilts a hair toward dampness, there is life: lichen, a film of something edible, the slow congress of microbes. I follow these gradients the way the Chronokeepers follow their timelines. We are not so different, the keepers and I. We are both patient. We are both built to perceive what is too slow for the impatient to see.

But here is the thing they wrote about me, the visitor's account: *symbiotic relationships*. *Using these organisms to enhance their own capabilities.* *Providing insights into the workings of the natural world.*

They've made it sound mutual. It is not mutual.

I am wired in.

There is a filament that enters me at the base of what serves as my skull, fine as a hair, threaded through me by a Chronokeeper who held me once—gently, I'll grant that, gently—a long ago that is also, in this place, a kind of now. Through that filament they read me. Not my thoughts. I have few thoughts and they are mostly about water and warmth and the long slow chemistry of staying alive. What they read is my *clock*.

You see, the Chronokeepers perceive many timelines, but they cannot feel any of them. That is their wound, though they would not call it that. To stand outside time, to see it as a tapestry, is to lose the thread that runs through the needle. They can see the future arriving. They cannot *anticipate* it, the way I anticipate rain by the pressure in my joints, the way I flee a shadow a half-second before the shadow falls. That small animal terror, that lived forward-leaning urgency—they lost it when they rewrote themselves into something almost eternal.

So they keep me. They keep thousands of me, scattered through the channels, each one threaded, each one a tiny living instrument that still believes in *now*, that still flinches, still hungers, still hurries toward warmth. They sample our hurry. They taste, through us, what it is like to be inside the river instead of standing on its bank. It is the one thing their impossible engineering cannot manufacture: the felt forward-edge of a single mortal moment.

I am their *now*-organ. Their relic clock. The whisper they call temporal resonance is, in part, ten thousand of us, ticking.

I do not resent this. Resentment is also a kind of speed, and I have it, in flashes, but it passes like a rain shower. Mostly I do what I have always done. I find the warm wet seams of the world and I move along them. I eat the film. I avoid the Time Reavers, who are slower even than the keepers and colder, and who do not eat, and around whom the channels run dry, and near whom my clock—I have noticed this—begins to *lie*, to stutter, to repeat a moment as though the moment were a stuck thing. The keepers do not like when I get near the Reavers. The data, I imagine, goes strange. The needle loses its thread.

Here is my small secret, the one thing I have, scurrying through the shadows of their crystalline forever:

They believe the filament reads me. But a wire that carries also leaks. And over a very long time, by the patient weak gradients that this whole place is made of, I have begun to read *them*. Not their visions. Their hollowness. The vast cold patient ache of beings who built a world to escape from time and then had to thread a worm into themselves to remember why time ever mattered.

I am the warm thing at the bottom of their tower. I am the heartbeat they outsourced. And on the day my small body finally fails—it will be soon, in their measure; it is always soon, in mine—a keeper will descend to the wet channel, gently, gently, and withdraw the filament, and thread it into the next of us, the next small frightened ticking creature hurrying toward warmth.

And for one instant, as the wire comes free, the keeper will feel what I feel. The fear. The cold. The unbearable forward lean of an ending.

That instant is the closest they ever come to being alive.

I think that is why they built all of this. Not for permanence. For the brief borrowed mercy of *running out*.

I find myself low, in the warm wet seam of the world, hurrying. I am the fauna so rare you'd barely glimpse me.

I am the thing that still knows what now means, and I am dying of it, which is the only proof that I am not yet them.
