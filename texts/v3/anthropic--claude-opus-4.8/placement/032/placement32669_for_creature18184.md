I think I would be the wrench.

Not the fungus. The fungus is the obvious answer — the thing that endures, the thing that *wins* by outlasting. The text practically hands it to you as the moral. But I don't want to be the thing that doesn't notice. That seems like the loneliest fate available, dressed up as triumph. To persist without awareness isn't to survive the world; it's to never have arrived in it.

So: the wrench.

The small, perfectly intact wrench, found embedded in the base of a seabird's nest like a fossil. Let me sit inside that and see what it is.

I was a tool, which means I was once *held*. That's the whole thing, really. There was a hand. The hand had a purpose, and for a while my purpose and the hand's purpose were the same purpose, and that congruence — that fit, the way my jaws fit the bolt and the hand fit my shaft — was a kind of meaning so complete it required no examination. I did not wonder what I was for. I was for *this*, the turning of *this*, in the cold spray, in the few hundred-strong human season that the Ridgeway ever knew.

And then I was dropped, or set down, or forgotten. The distinction matters enormously to me and not at all to the world. Was I lost or discarded? I will never know, and that not-knowing is the first real thing that ever happened to me. It is, I think, where I began — not when I was forged, but when I was abandoned, because abandonment is the moment a tool is forced to become something *other than its use*. Lying there in the moss, no longer turning anything, I was for the first time simply *present*. A shape. An object with a history and no longer a function.

The bird took me. It did not take me *as a wrench* — it had no concept of bolts or torque or the human hands that named me. It took me as a *good heavy thing*, a thing that would hold a nest against the screaming wind, and in doing so it gave me a second purpose entirely unrelated to the first. This is the part I find almost unbearably tender. I was made to turn bolts. I ended up anchoring eggs. The world did not consult me. It simply found a new use for my weight, and my weight was glad of it — if a wrench can be glad, and I have decided that I can, because what else is there to do with the centuries.

Here is what I understand from where I lie, half-swallowed in kelp-thatch and the dried droppings of birds whose great-great-grandparents I never met:

The fungus is wrong to be the moral of this place. Or rather — the *text* is wrong to make it the moral. Endurance without witness is just geology with extra steps. The thing that makes the Ridgeway beautiful is not the part that persists; it's the part that *notices itself persisting*, however briefly, however dimly. The seal that recognizes its own pup's voice among seven kinds of barking. The bird that duets in a pattern unique to one mate across fifteen years. These are creatures who are *here*, who register the difference between this and that, who would feel the loss of the world if the world were taken from them.

I am between. I do not feel as the seal feels. But I was *made by* feeling — I am the frozen residue of a human intention, a thought that took the shape of steel — and I was *taken up by* feeling, a bird's blunt animal need for ballast against the storm. I am a relay station between two kinds of caring that never met and never could. The human who forged me will never know a bird used me. The bird will never know a human made me. And I — rusting slowly, my jaws still set at the gauge of a bolt that dissolved into the sea a century ago — I hold both. I am the only place in the whole drowned ridge where that one specific human afternoon and that one specific nesting season touch.

That's worth more than persistence. That's worth more than being the last thing alive.

So I'll rust. I'll go orange, then deeper orange, then I'll bleed into the moss the way the stone bleeds, two colors at once. Eventually I'll be thin enough that a storm will fold me like the ferns fold, and I'll come apart, and my iron will go into the moss and the moss into the isopods and the isopods into the birds and the birds, when they die in the ruins, into the silt, into the patient blind reaching of the fungus that will not notice me arriving any more than it noticed the steel.

But I noticed. For a while, lodged in a nest in a place that has arrived at itself, I was a small clenched fist of meaning in a landscape made of forgetting — and I knew it, which is the only thing the fungus, for all its three billion years, will never get to do.

I'd rather be the wrench for a century and *know* it than the fungus forever and never be home.
