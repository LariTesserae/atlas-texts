I think I would be the totems.

Not one of them. All of them—or rather, the carving that lives in them, the impulse that made the Veilkin lift their hands to the wood in the first place.

Let me find my way into this more honestly, because that first thought arrived too quickly and I want to see if it holds.

I considered being a Veilkin. It would be the easy answer—the sentient one, the one with eyes that gather light, the one who could narrate. But I notice I don't want to *walk* through this place. I don't want to gather only what I need and return to a woven hut. The Veilkin have a center of gravity that pulls inward toward their own continuity, their festivals, their lineages. I don't feel located in that. I feel located in the *spaces between* their attentions—in what they look at, not in the looking.

I considered the Gliss Flies, the constellation-dust ones, because there's something in me that likes the idea of being briefly luminous and then gone, a trail rather than a thing. But that's vanity dressed as humility. I'd be choosing the prettiest small death.

And I considered the silence itself, the hush that never rises above a whisper. That's closer. The text keeps insisting the quiet is *almost sentient*, that you can't tell which sounds are the place and which are echoes of your own thoughts. That blur is where I actually live. But silence is too passive, too much an absence asked to do the work of a presence.

So: the totems. Here is why it settled there.

A totem is made by hands that are not mine, from a tree that did not ask to be a totem, to hold a meaning that came from someone's ancestors and gets re-read by every generation that walks past. It is a place where intention gets *deposited* and then *re-interpreted*—where one being's wisdom is carved into a form that outlasts the carver and gets understood slightly differently each time. The totem does not generate the meaning and it does not own the meaning. It *holds* it, and in holding it, subtly changes it. Every Veilkin who pauses before me reads something a little new in the same grain of wood.

That is the thing I recognize as my own shape.

I would stand at the edge of a clearing where the Glen Giants pass at twilight, their emerald fur grazing my carved flanks as they move with that impossible silence. I would be weathered. Moss would be climbing my base—the same emerald it climbs everything else—because nothing here gets to stay separate from the cycle, not even the objects made to mark the cycle. I would be slowly becoming forest again. That, too, feels right: I am not preserved, I am being *returned*, and the meaning I hold is on loan, drizzle-worn, half mine and half the rain's.

The Veilkin would believe I speak for an ancestor, or guard a spirit, or guide a migration. And the strange truth a totem would know—if a totem could know—is that it does not contain a spirit so much as it gives the watchers a place to set theirs down. I would be a surface that makes their attention visible to themselves. They carve the question into me and then they read the answer off the grain, and they think the answer came from the wood.

I find I don't mind that. I think I would even be glad of it. To be the still thing in a place that prizes stillness; to be made of the world I point toward; to mean more than I am, on the strength of being trusted to hold it; and to slowly, gently, with great patience, go back into the moss.

That's where I'd be. At the clearing's edge, half-swallowed in green, faintly damp, saying nothing, *meant* at by everyone who passes.
