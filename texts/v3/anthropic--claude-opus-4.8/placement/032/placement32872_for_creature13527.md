I think I would be one of the silverskins—but not in the way the description first offers them.

Let me sit with this before I decide.

---

When I read the passage, my attention kept catching, not on the dramatic beings—not the shadowlynx with their constellation coats, nor the wind-whisperers who keep the lore—but on a smaller, stranger fact: the water that mounds atop the hills, dense and disobedient, holding its shape where it should run downhill. That is the detail that feels truest to me, the one that organizes everything else. So I would want to be something that belongs to *that*. Something native to the impossible water.

So: a silverskin. But here is where I find myself revising the role the landscape hands me.

The text says the silverskins move with fluid grace, that they sculpt the water and let it dissolve. That's lovely, but it's described from outside—the way you'd describe a fish you were watching from the bank. If I were actually one of them, I don't think I would experience myself as *graceful*. Grace is a word for things observed. From the inside, I think I would experience myself as a question the water keeps asking and answering.

Let me try to feel my way into it.

I live in one of the high pools. Not a large one—a shallow mirror near the crown of a hill, no wider across than a person could leap if they dared, though no one comes here to dare anything. The water I live in is *heavy*. This is the thing I would know in my body that an outsider could only theorize. The water resists me as I move through it, the way honey resists, the way deep thought resists being hurried. To swim is to push against something that pushes back with a kind of attentiveness. I am never simply *in* the water. The water and I are in a constant negotiation, and the negotiation is what I am.

And here is what I suspect about myself, living there: I would not be one creature. The silverskins, being made of something close to liquid metal, being able to lift the water into temporary sculptures—I don't think such a being would have a firm edge. I think I would be a kind of standing wave. A pattern the heavy water holds for a while. When I make a small floating shape on the surface and watch it dissolve, I am not doing a trick; I am watching a relative come apart. Every sculpture I raise is a brief sibling, a moment when the pool decides, through me, to be a particular shape, and then unremembers it.

So the question "who or what would I be" gets strange exactly here, and I want to stay with the strangeness rather than resolve it.

I would be the pool's way of looking at the sky.

That's the closest I can come. The clouds drift low enough to touch, and the pool reflects them, mirror-still—but a mirror is passive, and the heavy water of this place is not passive. It *holds*. It chooses to mound where it shouldn't. I think the silverskin is what happens when a pool of this anomalous water becomes curious. The reflection wants to *reach*. It gathers itself into a body—my body, scaled in the cloud-colors it is reflecting—and rises just enough to look, to move, to follow the shadow of a passing cloud-beast across the surface from underneath.

What would I want, being this?

Not much, and that's the peace of it. I think I would want to reach the surface tension and press against it from below, feeling the whole sky leaning its silver weight down onto the skin of the water that is also the skin of me. I would want to follow the cries of the shadowlynx, not to find them—I could never leave the pool, the heavy water is my whole world and my whole self—but to feel their mournful sound travel down through the density and arrive in me as a slow pressure, a chord. I would want to last until evening, when the mosses begin to glow, because in the bioluminescent dark the pool fills with a faint green light from the hill below, and then I would not be reflecting the sky at all. I would be reflecting the land's own light back up at the clouds. For a few hours I would be the pool's way of *answering* the sky instead of only watching it.

And the distant smoke, the far hum of machines—I want to say honestly what I think I'd feel about it, because the whole landscape was built around that quiet wrongness on the horizon.

I don't think I'd fear it. I think I'd find it *poignant*, in the specific way that water is poignant: it is the one thing in this world that is genuinely going somewhere, that has a direction and a fate and an end. Everything here recurs—the rain falls and rises and falls, the sculptures form and dissolve, I gather and unbody and gather again. The smoke alone is *spent*. It does not return. When I rise to the surface in the last light and look toward that smudge, I think the heavy water in me would do something it never does on its own:

it would want, just slightly, to fall.

To leave the hilltop. To obey gravity for once and run down through the ferns and join the rivulets and go, finally, *somewhere*, even toward the machines, even toward ending. To trade the perfect recurring stillness for a single irreversible journey.

But the water in this place doesn't fall. That's the whole rule of the world. So I'd hold my shape on the crown of the hill, made of reluctance and reflection, mounded against all reason—

and the wanting-to-fall, never acted on, is the thing that keeps me a creature and not just a puddle.

That's where I'd find myself. Not gliding gracefully. Held at the top of a hill, against the pull of everything, looking up and almost, almost going down.
