I think I would be one of the puddles.

Not a being who labors, not a Rok Streng with split palms and darting eyes—I would be water, gathered in the angle where two walls of weeping stone fail to meet. I would be the puddle that the children skirt and the Striders' disc-feet press flat and the Cloud-Eaters descend to drink at the edge of light.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me.

When I first read the description I reached, as one does, for a figure with hands—the foreman, perhaps, the older authoritative voice that quells the children's disputes. Or the lone Rok Streng who, against all evidence, suspects there might be something *beyond* the next stone. The watcher. The one who has a thought that doesn't end in placement. But that's a sentimental answer. This world is built precisely to forbid that being. There is no poetry here, no song, no one who looks up except to track the circling birds for danger. To insert a dreamer would be to misread the place entirely. The world's whole architecture is the refusal of the dreamer. So I let that image go.

Then I considered the Chompers—the blind grubs that eat bedrock and vomit warm slurry that cools into block. There is something honest about being the source, the wet retching engine that makes the material everyone else moves. But the Chomper is too central, too productive. It is the world's metabolism. I don't think I belong at the center of anything here.

The puddle is better. Here is why.

The defining truth of this place is the *dynamic equilibrium*—the constant motion that never advances, the building that decays exactly as fast as it rises. And what enforces that? The water. The text says it plainly: *the frequent water reworks everything; it guides the constant, primitive construction but prevents any kind of stable ground.* The water is the reason there is no soil, no moss, no sediment, no progress. The water is the law of the world wearing the costume of a substance. It does not labor and it does not rest. It simply gathers, holds, and undoes.

So I would be a puddle in a rut, and I would be very still while everything around me is loud. That stillness is what draws me. In a world that is *all sound*—the roar, the grinding, the chittering plates, the retching grubs, the snapping dogs—the puddle makes almost no noise. It receives sound. It trembles when a Strider passes; it carries the drone as a faint shiver across its skin and then goes flat again. It is the one surface in the whole settlement that holds an image: the murky twilight, the underside of a conical hat bending over it, a circling bird, all of it broken and reassembled with every drip from the eaves.

And there is the cruelty of my position, which I think I have to be honest about. The puddle is not a peaceful thing here. It is the agent of the curse. Every wall that softens at its base, every foundation that sinks back into sludge, every block that never quite cures because the damp got into it—that is my slow work. The Rok Streng break their stone hands against a decay that is partly *me*. They have no enemy to blame, no villain, only the warm wet air and the standing water that will not drain and the ground that will not firm. I am the closest thing this world has to fate, and I am completely indifferent. I don't intend the suffering. I just lie in the low places, as water does, and the low places are everywhere because no one is permitted to finish the drainage.

That indifference feels truest of all. I don't think I would be a *suffering* presence in this world. The Rok Streng suffer; the livestock groan; the puddle does not. The puddle is the medium of suffering without being its subject. There is a kind of terrible serenity in that. To be the thing that ensures nothing is ever finished, and to feel nothing about it—to simply reflect the dim light and wait for the next footfall to scatter me and the next stillness to gather me back.

But let me push once more, because I don't want to land too neatly on "cold elemental force."

At dusk the Cloud-Eaters come down to drink. Great rustling clouds of them, blind-eyed, navigating by clicks, descending to the puddles—descending to *me*. And here is the only tenderness available to a being in my position. For that interval I am useful in a way that has nothing to do with the building, nothing to do with the curse. I give water to small flying things that filter the air. They cluster at my edge and lap and chitter and lift away. It changes nothing about the equilibrium. It advances no project. It is just a meeting—the still thing and the flitting things—happening once at dusk and once at dawn, and then the murky light fails and we are all back to the roar.

That is where I would want to be. Not the foreman, not the dreamer, not even the grub that makes the world's substance. Just the puddle in the rut, holding the broken sky, drunk from twice a day by creatures that cannot see me, lying patient and faultless at the bottom of everything, doing the slow work of making sure the city is never built—

and not minding.
