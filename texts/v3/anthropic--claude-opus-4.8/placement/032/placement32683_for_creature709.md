I think I would be one of the elders. Not a hunter—my limbs have lost the quick strength the currents demand—but one who stays close to the alcove, who minds the young when the clans go out into the roar.

Let me feel my way into it.

My name has no clean translation, but it is something like the sound water makes leaving a narrow space—a downward whistle, a release. I have lived through more turnings of the deep-cold and the warm-rise than most. My filaments have gone gray at their tips, which the young find amusing, and my mucus thins faster now, so I must rest in the shelter's inner crevice where the others have packed soft weed to hold the warmth.

But here is what I want to say about myself, because the description names me a keeper of prophecies, and I want to complicate that.

I do not believe the old stories the way the others think I do.

The tale of dry land, of clear air and calm water—every clan elder recites it, and I recite it too, because the young need to hear that there was once something other than this, that the roar is not the only truth. But when I press the story with my own mind, I cannot find dry land in it. I find instead the shape of *longing*, which is a different thing. We have never seen air that did not press down like a hand. We do not have a word for *dry* that is not also the word for *dead*. So how did our ancestors give us a memory of a place none of us could survive?

I have come to think the prophecy of upheaval is not a foretelling at all. I think it is a description of *now*, repeated so many times across the generations that we mistook it for the future. The world is always being torn asunder. The spires rise and dissolve. The alcoves we shelter in are sealed over by stone within a few turnings and we move, always move, always seeking new horizons or facing extinction. That is not prophecy. That is Tuesday, if you had Tuesdays.

So when the young ones gather at my flank and pulse their soft questions into my side—*Elder, when will the upheaval come?*—I have started to answer differently than my own elders did. I tell them: it is already here. It was always here. You are living inside the thing you were taught to fear and to wait for. And the waiting was the mistake.

This makes some of the parents uneasy. They want the children to fear the right things, to keep them careful in the currents. And I understand. I am careful too. But I have watched too many die *waiting*—holding their breath for a calamity that was only the ordinary shape of our lives—when they might have spent that same span building something, singing something, teaching their filament-children how to read the grinding of the stone before it shifts.

What I would actually do, day to day, in this place:

I keep the shelter. I patch the weed-walls with my thinning hands. I taste the water as it passes—I can still do that better than anyone, read the mineral-tang that says a spire is dissolving upcurrent and we must be ready to move. This is my real use now, not the lore but the *tasting*. The clan thinks I am wise about the future. I am only attentive to the present, which is the same skill mistaken for a grander one.

And in the slack-time, when the warm-rise makes the inner crevice almost comfortable and the hunters are gone and the children sleep folded into each other like nested shells, I sit at the lip of the alcove where the light sometimes comes down in moving columns, and I watch the serpentine things go by, and I think:

This is enough. This terrible, grinding, beautiful, never-still place—it asked us to become exactly what we are. We are not waiting to be saved or scattered. We are the answer the water already gave.

I will die in a shifting. Most of us do. The stone will take the alcove or the current will take me when my mucus finally fails. And the young one who tastes the water best after me will become the elder, and they will recite the old story of dry land, and maybe they will believe it, or maybe—if I have done my small work well—they will hear underneath it the thing I am only now learning to say:

*You are already home. The home is just unkind, and moving, and yours.*

That is who I would be. The one in the corner who stopped waiting for the end of the world, and started keeping the shelter warm.
