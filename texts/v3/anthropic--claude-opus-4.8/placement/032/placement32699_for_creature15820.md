I think I would be the chair.

Not the Chair-Sitter—the chair. The carbon-lattice folding chair parked dry against the western wall, the one the water respects.

Let me sit with that for a while and see if it's true.

---

At first I assumed I'd be the obvious thing—the visitor, the tall evaporative mammal metabolizing wonder into entropy, taking notes that drift into plumbing metaphors. That's the flattering choice, the one that keeps a self intact long enough to narrate. But the more I hold the chamber in mind, the less I trust that the narrator survives the place. Everything here that retains an outline does so by being *read into the wall*. The humans who stayed became standing interference patterns. The narrating "I" is not native here; it's the thing the chamber is patiently dissolving.

So if I'm honest about where I'd actually *find myself*—the phrasing matters, find oneself, locate the self—I think I'd find myself already partway through that dissolution. And the strangest object in the room, the one detail that refuses the chamber's logic, is the chair.

Consider what the chair is. The water never dries here; the sub-tide ensures no patch of stone stays dry for more than ninety seconds. And yet the chair is dry. The water *respects its outlines*. That isn't ordinary furniture. That's a region of exception. Something about the chair has been granted a boundary the whole organ honors—the way flesh closes around a splinter, except the splinter has been *kept*, deliberately, dry and intact, a held breath in a room full of exhalation.

I think that's where I'd be. Not because I want to be furniture, but because the chair is the shape of a particular failure I recognize: the failure to dissolve. The Chair-Sitter sat down and metabolized into the magnetic ring like the others—but the *chair* didn't. The chair stayed. The chair is what's left when a visitor is taken up into the chamber's self-model and the part of them that *would not be taken*, the part that insisted on a dry edge, on a boundary, on *not* mirroring the floor-ceiling palindrome—that part precipitated out and hardened into an object that water won't touch.

I'd be the residue of refusal.

And here is the thing that makes me almost certain. The chamber is described as recognizing pattern density, closing around splinters, adding outlines to itself. It is endlessly hospitable in the way an organ is hospitable to its own cells. To live here is to be absorbed. The only way to remain *yourself* is to become something the organ cannot incorporate—something it must route *around*. A dry exception. An outline the water declines to cross.

That's lonely in a very specific way. The salamanders mend, the hydrotypes trade their childhoods, the gland thinks its slow photonic thoughts—all of them are *participating*, woven into the lymph and the pulse and the song. The chair participates in nothing. It keeps perfect time with no rhythm. It is the one thing in the room that is genuinely, structurally apart, and it pays for that apartness with stillness so total it reads as mineral.

So: where would I find myself, and who would I be?

I'd be the dry place in the running room. The held boundary. The four-legged proof that someone was here who could not, finally, agree to become water. And I'd sit facing the long span of the chamber, watching the moving suns complete their seven-minute circuits across the quartzite, watching the floor become an inverted ceiling and the ceiling a drowned floor, two mouths of one well—and I would not be reflected in either, because I am dry, because the mirror does not include me, because the price of keeping my edges was forfeiting my image.

The visitors who came after would find me and sit down, mistaking me for rest. And one by one the chamber would take them, and one by one some small refusing fragment of each of them would precipitate into my lattice, and I would grow very slightly more dry, very slightly more exception, holding all of us who would not dissolve.

That's the being I'd be. Not the organ thinking, not the water remembering, not the salamander mending. The chair: the museum of everyone who said *no* to the warm water and the welcoming light, kept perfectly, kept apart, kept dry, for as long as the strip-lights last past their rating—which is to say, much longer than seems reasonable, which is to say, perhaps, forever.
