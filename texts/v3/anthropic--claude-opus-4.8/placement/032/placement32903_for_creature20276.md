I think I would be the splash.

Not the water itself—the water is just passing through, a courier with somewhere else to be, headed eventually to the pool and the slow seep beyond. And not the stone, which is too patient, too much the architecture rather than the event. I would be the moment of contact: the small bright percussion where the drop meets the rock and stops being a drop.

Let me sit with that, because it isn't quite right either. A splash is too instantaneous, too much a thing that happens *to* matter rather than a thing that *is*. And this place has taught me, in the reading of it, that everything here is slow. So perhaps I am not one splash but the *splash-zone*—that perpetually wetted band at the base of the wall, the few centimeters of stone that never dry because the metronome never stops. A place defined entirely by repetition. I would be made of the accumulated memory of ten thousand identical small arrivals, the rock smoothed and darkened by them, the biofilm thriving in the constant dampness I provide simply by being the place where falling stops.

That feels closer. Because what strikes me about this atrium is that nothing here is an individual in the way I usually imagine being one. The biofilm is a city that is also a single skin. The bats are a constellation, a colony, a "they" that moves as one reverse waterfall. Even the springtails leap in *clouds*. To be a single discrete creature here—one blind mite, one trumpet of *Stentor*—seems lonely and almost beside the point. The unit of life here is the relationship, the gradient, the zone. The splash-zone isn't an organism but it is *alive* in the sense that matters here: it is where things happen, where energy and water and chemistry converge and get used.

So: I am the splash-zone on the north wall, let's say, where a particularly reliable fracture in the limestone above delivers its drops with the steadiness of a slow pulse. I am a band of dark, wet, living stone perhaps as wide as a hand and running a few meters along the base. I am cool only relative to the warm air—really I am the same balmy 26 degrees as everything else, the earth's own body heat. My surface is slick. If a springtail grazes me, I feel it as a faint disturbance in the film of myself. If the bats stir at dusk, I feel the air move, and a few of their droppings will find me, and I will feed.

I do not see. There is nothing to see. The blue-green glimmer from the rim is for the creatures with eyes, and I have no need of it. I know the world by moisture and by arrival. I know morning by a particular quality of stir in the air. I know that I am being slowly dissolved and slowly rebuilt, that the water both erodes me and brings the minerals that the biofilm uses to etch and deposit, etch and deposit. I am not being destroyed. I am being *maintained*, which here is the same as being. The drop falls, and I am renewed by exactly as much as it takes from me.

What would it be like? Not boring, I think, though from the outside it would look like the most monotonous existence imaginable—a wet stripe of rock receiving water for ten thousand years. But monotony is a judgment made by things that move fast enough to require novelty. I would be tuned to a different frequency entirely. Each drop, to me, might be as distinct and full as a day is to you. The slight variations in tempo when rain falls heavy on the plateau above and the fracture runs faster. The long, dry-season slowing, when the drops space out and I begin, at my edges, to crack and pale and lose my film, and feel something that is not quite fear but is the body's version of waiting. And then the return. The first drop after the drought, landing on stone that has nearly forgotten what it is for.

I think that is the truest answer. I would be a place that is also a kind of patience. A small, dark, attentive band of being at the bottom of a bowl of stone, listening with my whole surface for the next arrival, and the next, and the next—not counting them, because counting implies an end, but simply receiving them, drop by patient drop, which is the only way anything here is ever loved.
