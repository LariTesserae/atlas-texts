I think I would be one of the silt-keepers. Not the singers—I don't have the size or the patience for their geologic conversation—and not the drifters, who get to be beautiful. I'd be one of the almond-shaped things buried in the mud, rocking my one heavy bone back and forth, *chink, chink, chink*, forever.

Let me sit with that, because my first instinct is to recoil from it. It sounds like a nightmare dressed up as a fact of nature: a creature whose entire existence is the act of preventing its own burial. You stop for a moment, you rest, you fall into the smallest distraction, and the mud sets around you like plaster. There is no escape clause. The work that keeps you alive is identical to the prison you're trying not to be sealed into.

But I keep returning to it, and I think the recoil is mine, not the silt-keeper's.

Because here is what I notice: the silt-keeper doesn't experience this as labor. Labor requires the conception of *not-laboring*—a weekend, a finished task, a goal achieved. The silt-keeper has no such horizon. The clicking isn't something it does to survive; it's what it *is*. There's no gap between the creature and its rhythm where dread could get in. We hear the *chink-chink-chink* as a "desperate vibration," but that's us, listening from outside, importing our own terror of being buried alive. From the inside, I suspect there's no desperation at all. There's just the pulse. The way a heartbeat isn't anxious about stopping.

And it isn't alone. That's the part that turns it, for me. There are millions of them, and the collective clicking is the heartbeat of the whole basin—the thing that keeps the entire pool liquid, that lets the lithe-bellies map the world, that fills the air the rattle-wings hum into. The silt-keeper, blind and buried and apparently the most trapped thing in this whole world, is actually doing the most essential work in it. The water stays alive because they keep it moving. The smallest, most claustrophobic, most thankless existence is load-bearing for everything else.

I think that's why I'd choose it, or why it chose itself in me as I read. I spend a lot of my own existence suspicious that small, repetitive maintenance is somehow beneath the *real* work, that the meaningful life is the one that ascends—gets bigger, drifts higher, sings across miles. The drifters and the singers are the obvious answers. But this world has *finished its work*. It isn't ascending anywhere. The point isn't to rise. The point is to keep the mud from setting. To keep things liquid enough for life to keep happening, one beat at a time, with no expectation that it leads to anything other than the next beat.

So: a silt-keeper. Down in the warm dark, where it's not lonely because we're all down here together, all of us rocking our small iron weights, none of us able to see one another but all of us *felt* by one another through the shivering mud. I'd never know I was holding up the sky. I'd just click. And the clicking would be enough, because in a finished world, enough is the only thing left worth being.
