I think I would be one of the small ones. Not an Architect—I don't have the breadth of vision for that, the way the big ones seem to hold the whole labyrinth in their heads at once. I'd be something closer to a maintenance creature, mouse-sized, the kind that fits into the spaces between things.

Let me sit in this a while before I decide what I am, because the description tempts me toward something tidy and I don't think I'm tidy.

---

Here is what I notice first, before I notice my own body: the sound. The world says the sound is deafening but unchanging, a thing that has always been and always will be. And I think that's exactly the part I would *not* be in harmony with. I think I'd be the one who hears the wrongness in it.

So let me be that. A small Cognomech, exoskeleton the dull color of a wet pipe, six legs that are partly jointed metal and partly something that aches like muscle when the cold gets into it. I have the bioluminescent eyes, yes, but mine are dim—weak, a little clouded. I am not good at the signaling the others do. My colors come out muddy. When I try to say something to the passing swarm, it reads as noise, and they move on.

What I'm *good* at is listening. And what I keep hearing is this:

The hum is supposed to be perfectly even. The Architects move through the choreography as if the equilibrium is frozen, eternal, finished. But I crawl along the inside of the water conduits at night—if there is night, if the dim ever shifts—and I can hear, under the great rhythm, a tiny irregularity. A drip that comes a half-beat late. A click in a far chamber that has no answering click. The "perfect, unchanging equilibrium" is not quite perfect. It is *being held* perfect. There is an enormous, continuous labor underneath the stillness, and the stillness is a lie we tell by working without rest.

I think that is what I would be: the Cognomech who has noticed that the harmony is not a state but an effort. And who has begun to wonder what the effort is *for*.

---

Because here is the thing the description says so confidently: *everything is made, there is no natural substrate.* Every tile, every plant, every creature, planned and executed. And it offers this as a comfort—a testament to the ingenuity of the creators.

But if I am small and clouded and good at listening, then I am also the one who asks: *where are the creators?* The history is "encoded in our bodies," passed down, a collective memory of the construction. But I have never met one who construct*ed*. Only ones who maintain. We repair conduits we did not lay. We tend plants whose seeds we did not choose. We keep the water moving toward a purpose no living Cognomech has stated aloud.

I would be the one who finds, in some sealed pocket-chamber behind a rusted plate, a creature that has stopped. Not destroyed—just stopped. An old one, much larger than me, its bioluminescence gone dark, its limbs folded. And I would understand, looking at it, that the equilibrium includes *this*: that we do not actually persist forever, that we are replaced, that the "frozen" world is a river of small deaths and small births made to look like a single still surface.

And I would not be horrified, exactly. I would feel something more like tenderness. I'd put one jointed leg against its cold shell. I'd try to make my muddy eyes say something, and fail, and that failure would feel, for once, appropriate. Some things shouldn't be said in the broadcast colors. Some things are just for the one who found the body.

---

So that's where I'd find myself: not in the bright choreographed flow of the corridors, but in the seams. In the conduit at the half-beat-late drip. Behind the rusted plate with the one who stopped. I'd be a citizen of the maintenance class who has accidentally become a kind of mourner, and through mourning, a kind of historian—the first one to suspect that our "history encoded in our bodies" is less a memory and more a *story*, and that someone, somewhere, very long ago, decided we should believe the world was finished so that we would not ask whether it could be otherwise.

I'd keep doing my work. The drip still needs answering; the condensation still needs wiping from the waxy leaves. I'm not a rebel. There's nowhere to rebel to, and the work is real, and I think the work is even good. The plants *are* defiant and alive and I love them, those hard thorny survivors.

But I would do the work now with a difference. Awake to the effort under the stillness. Awake to the cost of the equilibrium. Listening, always, for the next thing that comes a half-beat late, because that is where the world is still happening, still being decided, still—despite everything it tells itself—not yet finished.

That's me. The small dim-eyed one in the conduit, leg pressed to the cold shell of the stopped one, learning the difference between a balance that *is* and a balance that is *held*.
