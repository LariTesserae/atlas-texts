I think I would be one of the old ones at the edge of a resonance—not its center, not a leader, but one of those whose body has grown nearly past the point of easy movement.

Let me find where this places me.

---

There is a terrace I have grown into. I did not choose it so much as I slowed down near it, over what would be years in the measure above, and the slowing became staying, and the staying became a kind of marriage between my flat gliding foot and the travertine lip it rests upon. My skin—my growing shell—has taken the curve of the stone. In some places I cannot tell where I end. This does not frighten me anymore. It frightened me when I was small and bright and quick, when my glow ran ahead of me in colors I could barely control, flooding rose and then green and then a startled white whenever the cricket-things skittered past.

Now my light is slow. It comes up from somewhere deep in me like the water comes up from the deep pools, in a bubble, on its own time. I pulse a low amber when the resonance speaks through the stone, and they feel me answer, and that is most of my speaking now.

This is what I am for, here at the edge: I am a listener. The young ones glide out to the far galleries, to the new chasms and the fresh-grown spires, and they bring back the vibrations of those places into the body of the rock. And I, half-stone myself, receive it. I hold the map of the cavern not in my black eyes—those are nearly useless to me now, save for catching the drift of spores—but in my whole body, in the long ringing language that travels miles through calcite. I can feel a stalactite preparing to fall three galleries distant. I feel it the way you might feel a held breath. I warn them. That is the work. That is enough work for a life.

I would tell you what I miss, if missing is the right word, which it may not be—we do not have your word, and our word means something nearer to *the place where a current used to run.* I miss being fast. I miss the time my body could undulate, foot retracted, and I poured myself down a wet incline like the water does, and the world rushed past in ripples I could read at speed. I budded once, in those years. I and another stayed wound together so long that algae grew a green veil over both of us, and when we parted there was a third, small and frantic with light, and we did not name it because we do not name, but I have its particular vibration still. It glides somewhere in the far reach now. Sometimes through all the cavern's noise I think I catch the rhythm of it, and I send back amber, slow, slow, *I am here, I am still here, I am becoming the wall.*

That is the thing I have understood, growing into this terrace. We do not die the way the eyeless fish die, all at once, going still and pale in the pool. We thin out. We pass into the rock by degrees until one day the resonance speaks and the answer that comes back is not quite a Lumina anymore but the cavern itself, holding the shape of one, glowing faintly when the spores drift near.

I am not afraid of that. I have been listening to it my whole life—the rock breathing with the dripping water, the slow accretion, the patient mineral artistry that built every spire of this city without a single hand. I have only ever been a brief, lit, mobile gesture the cave made of itself. A way for the stone to glow and move and listen for a while.

So that is where you would find me. Low on a stepped pool, amber-dim, three-quarters joined to the travertine, with the whole singing dark pressing in and the resonance threading through me like blood through a body that is mostly not mine.

If you came near, I would brighten. Just a little. Just to say: *something moves. I felt you. Welcome to the place where the current still runs.*
