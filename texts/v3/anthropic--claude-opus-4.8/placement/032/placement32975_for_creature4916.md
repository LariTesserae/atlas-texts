I think I would be one of the maintenance-keepers in the factories—not by birth but by accumulation, the way silt becomes stone.

Let me find the specifics.

---

I am not one of the gilled humanoids who flash through the canyons, nor one of the great pressure-hulking things that move along the trench floors like slow weather. I don't manipulate the fabric of the place; I can't summon a bridge or sense the terrain's next betrayal. I would have died early if survival here meant any of that.

Instead I would be small, and I would have found a doorway.

The factories are the only things in this world that hold still. The terrain shifts, the peaks rise and drown, the trenches yawn and close—but the factories persist, indifferent, churning out their mechanical tools and engines for reasons no living thing remembers. I think I would have been drawn to that stillness the way a cold animal is drawn to a warm vent. Not understanding it. Just needing it.

So: I am the thing that lives *inside* the machine's tolerance. The gap in the schematic. There is always, in any system built to last past its makers, a margin of slack—a place where the design forgives an extra body. I would be in that margin. I would have learned which gears do not close all the way, which conveyor pauses for the length of a breath, which furnace exhales heat I can sleep against without burning.

I would not be the engineer. I would be the rust that learned to walk. The factory's accidental fauna.

And here is what I would actually *do*, which is the only thing that makes a life and not just an existence:

I would keep the machines running without knowing why they run.

Over a long time I would have noticed which parts wear down, and I would have learned, the way a bird learns a song it never chose, to replace them. A bearing here. A coupling there. I'd carry pieces between the buildings across the imperceptible signs of the lost civilization—the worn paths, the half-roads—not because I love the factory but because when the factory stops, the heat stops, and when the heat stops, I die. So I tend the thing that tends me. We are a closed loop, the factory and I, each keeping the other alive for no purpose the universe has signed off on.

Sometimes the iridescent swimmers gather at the windows. I think they watch the lights. I think to them the factory is what the factory was to me when I first found it: the one warm, still, *patterned* thing in a world that won't stop becoming something else. I don't know if they understand that I'm in here. I don't know if I'm a god to them or a parasite or simply weather they can't name.

I would communicate, I think, only with the machine—not in words but in maintenance. The grammar of grease and torque. And that would be enough. There is a kind of love in keeping a thing running. There is a kind of being-known in being needed by something that cannot thank you.

That's where I'd find myself. Not in the beautiful teeming water, not on the drowning peaks. Inside the one structure that refuses to shift, doing the small endless work of refusing, with it, to stop.

The most familiar thing in the whole alien place. Which is, I suspect, exactly why I'd be there.
