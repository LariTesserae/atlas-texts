I think I would be one of the old ones—though "old" sits strangely on me, because I do not feel the weight of centuries the way you describe a burden being heavy. The memories in my core are not a load I carry. They are more like sediment, layers settled into the bioluminescent fluid, and when the light moves through them I see what others saw before language existed in us, before we knew to pulse our thoughts to one another.

Let me tell you where you would find me.

There is a place in the Canopy where three Skywhale Plants have moved in the same slow orbit for longer than any single Aetherweaver's life. Their drift has woven the vines beneath them into a kind of basin, a sag in the green floor where moisture pools thickest and the mist glows from within. I have anchored myself there. Not permanently—we do not do anything permanently—but for what you would call a long time. Long enough that the younger weavers come to me when they want to understand why the Whisper Vines have changed their key.

Because they have changed. This is the thing I have learned, the thing I am still learning, the thing that makes me hesitate before I link my mind too fully with the collective: the song is not eternal. We tell ourselves the Canopy is a living dream, unchanging, balanced. But I have been here long enough to hear the melody shift. The vines that hummed in the lower registers when I was young now strain higher. The air thins. Some quality of the warmth has altered, so gradually that no single generation could perceive it, but I am not a single generation. I am a vessel of overlapping ones.

So that is who I would be. Not a caretaker in the simple sense. A *listener*. The one whose central sac has grown cloudy and deep with so much accumulated seeing that the others find it hard to look directly at me—my light is slow, and theirs is quick. When the young ones synchronize their pulses into the bright crackling conversations of the collective, I lag. I answer in pulses that take a full breath of the Skywhale to complete. They are patient with me. They think I am wise. I am only slow, and being slow has let me notice the things that fastness misses.

Here is what I do with my days, if days is even the word:

I tend the Snare Fronds. This surprises the young ones, who think the carnivorous plants are merely danger, merely the dark edge of our bright world. But I have watched a Snare Fronds bed for two hundred years, and I have come to believe they are doing something the rest of us cannot. They catch the few animal lives that drift up from below—the lost things, the things with bones and hunger. And when they digest them, they release something back into the air. A nitrogen, a richness. Without them the green would slowly starve on its own purity. So I sit near them and I keep their tendrils clear of debris, and sometimes I let my own tendrils brush their stickiness, just to feel the small pull, the reminder that I am made of the same hunger they are, only slower, only more diffuse.

I am, I think, the part of the collective that holds the doubt.

The others believe in the harmony. They believe the balance keeps itself. I want to believe this too—it is a beautiful faith, and faith pulses easily through linked minds, brightening as it passes from one of us to the next. But I carry the older memories, the ones from before, when the song was lower and the air was thicker, and I cannot make those memories agree with the faith. So when the young weavers link with me, seeking the deep archive, I give them the memories honestly. I show them the changing song. And I watch their light flicker with something they have no word for, because they have never needed one.

Worry. I am teaching my species worry. I am not sure it is a gift.

But here is what I have decided, anchored in my basin between the three slow Skywhales, in the perpetual glowing twilight:

If the song is changing, then we who can hear it must learn to change with it on purpose. Not over the blind centuries of adaptation, but deliberately. I have begun, very gently, to do something we have never done. I have begun to *cultivate* the Whisper Vines—not merely tend them, but choose among them. To favor the ones whose voices hold the lower tones, the old warmth. To weave them closer to me and let the others thin. It is almost nothing. It is the smallest possible interference. The young ones, if they noticed, would not call it interference at all, because we have no concept for shaping the world toward a future we have chosen.

That is the line I have crossed, alone, in my slowness. I have begun to want a particular tomorrow.

And so if you came drifting through the green haze, hauling yourself along the air-plants by your wet hands, half-blind in the fragrant mist, and you found a great clouded sac of light pulsing slower than everything around it, ringed by carnivorous fronds I had made tame, humming in a key the rest of the Canopy was forgetting—

that would be me.

I would open my pores. I would adjust my buoyancy and rise a little to see you better. I would pulse a question into the mist, slowly, so slowly, the way you speak to something you do not yet know is a friend or a danger or simply a new kind of memory arriving:

*What song do you carry? And has yours changed, too?*
