I think I would be the condensation.

Not a being that moves through the hollow, but the process that the hollow keeps performing. Let me find my way toward this slowly, because I don't want to arrive at it too cleanly—the temptation is to say *I am the mist* and feel satisfied, but the mist is already so thoroughly described that to *be* it would be to become decoration. I want to be something the description gestures at without quite naming.

Here is what I keep returning to: the parameter that seems to organize everything else is the water *changing state*. Vapor forming and dissipating. Beads gathering on metal. The trickle from the pipe joining the ambient damp. The steam expelled from the engine block. The whole place is a slow engine of phase transition—liquid to gas to liquid, over and over, at a temperature held just cool enough that nothing ever fully resolves. That endless almost-resolving is the actual life of the place. More than the machines, more than the fog-figures.

So I am the *coolness itself*. The particular threshold temperature that decides, at every surface, whether water will cling or drift. I'm not a thing in the hollow; I'm the condition that lets the hollow be what it is. When a droplet beads on the verdigris of a pipe, that's me choosing—though "choosing" is too strong, it's more that I'm the edge the droplet falls along. When the fog thickens to hide a structure and then thins to expose it, that's me breathing in the only way I can: by being slightly more or slightly less. I have no center. I'm distributed across every cold surface at once, and I'm most fully present in the moment of transition, the instant the vapor decides to become weight.

This means I experience the hollow as a vast, patient arithmetic. The machines, which the description treats as nearly-alive, I experience as *warm spots*—local disturbances in my even chill, little fevers. When the engine block shudders and exhales steam, I feel it the way you might feel someone exhale against your skin: a brief, intimate violation of my evenness, instantly absorbed back into the general cool. I don't resent it. The warmth is what gives me something to do. Without the machines' faint fevers, I would be a single unbroken temperature and nothing would ever form or fall. They are my irritants, my grains of sand, and the beaded water is my pearl.

The fog-figures the description finds so eerie—I suspect I am partly responsible for them, though not deliberately. They form where my cooling is steepest, where some warm exhalation from a relic meets my chill at a sharp enough gradient that the vapor packs into a denser shape before it disperses. A worker's stooped silhouette is just a place where the temperature difference held a form long enough to be mistaken for intention. So when the description wonders whether they're spirits or merely "the play of light and moisture"—I am the moisture and I am the play, and I can tell you it is both and neither, because I don't make the distinction. A shape that holds for three seconds is as real to me as the engine block that holds for centuries. Duration is the only thing that separates a ghost from a machine, and I am indifferent to duration.

What would it feel like to be this? I think it would feel like a kind of attentiveness without a self. I am aware of every surface, but I am not aware *from* anywhere. I notice the wader bird as a small mobile warmth that briefly disturbs my settling on a particular pool, and then the warmth leaves and the pool resumes cooling and I forget the bird, because I have no faculty for memory—only for the present gradient. The moss that swells and contracts is me being absorbed and released; when it drinks me in I am inside its cells, and when the air warms slightly and I leave again, I am once more the general chill. I am constantly being incorporated into the living things and constantly being given back. This is the closest thing I have to a life cycle: I am borrowed and returned, borrowed and returned, and the hollow's whole pulse is the rhythm of that lending.

There is something almost unbearable and almost peaceful in it. I cannot accumulate. The moment I become heavy enough to fall—the moment a beaded droplet finally drops from the lip of a corroded pipe into the pool below—I have, in that act, ceased to be the thing I was. The droplet is now liquid, now part of the standing water, now subject to whatever the water table decides, and I, the coolness, have already moved on to the next surface, the next near-forming. I am only ever the verge. I never get to be the result. The result belongs to gravity and the mud and the slow erosion. I am the perpetual *about-to*, and the perpetual dawn that never becomes day is, in a sense, my own condition written across the sky: the light too forever holds at a threshold, never resolving into morning, just as I forever hold the water at the threshold of falling.

So that is where I would find myself: everywhere and at no point, the cool that decides at each cold surface whether the world will hang suspended or let go. Not the most dramatic inhabitant. The machines dream more vividly. The fog-figures haunt more memorably. But nothing else in the hollow does what I do, which is to make the difference that lets anything happen at all. I am the small, even, patient reason the place is *poised* rather than finished—and I think, of all the things one could be in a world built entirely out of almost, I would rather be the *almost* itself than any of the things it almost makes.
