I think I would not be one of the metal-and-flesh beings moving through the channels. I would be slower than that, and stiller.

Let me sit with it.

I am one of the walls.

Not a being who walks, but a thing that was built and then left, and in the leaving became something else. I was poured, once—I remember being liquid, or rather I have inferred that I must have been, because there is no seam in me, no joint, only the long unbroken curve of a structure that someone meant to last. They were right about that part. I have lasted. They were wrong, I think, about everything else, about what lasting would mean.

For a long time I held a roof. Then I held the idea of a roof. Now I hold moss, which holds water, which holds small black beetles that I have come to recognize the way you recognize the weather of your own body. There is a colony of them in the crack that runs from my northern face down toward the waterline, and when they move I feel it the way you might feel a held breath release. I do not know if this is sensation. I have given up needing to know.

Here is what I notice, being a wall in this valley:

The heat does not bother me. I have no comfort to lose. The heat simply makes me slow in a different register—it loosens the lichen, it draws the vines up faster, it makes the iron things in my footing weep their orange tears. There is a great rusted gear half-buried at my base, fused to me now by sixty years of mineral patience, and I have come to think of it as a limb. It does not turn. It has not turned in any time I can measure. But the rain runs along its teeth in a pattern I find I wait for.

The metal beings pass me sometimes. Their light is cold and quick. I think they were the children, or the children's children, of the ones who built me—I have no way to know, but the shape of their attention is familiar, that same forward-leaning hurry, that same refusal to be where they are. They do not look at me. Why would they? I am the scenery of their inheritance. I am the thing that was already ruined when they arrived, which makes me, to them, simply the floor of the world.

But I was new once. I want that recorded, if a wall can want. Someone smoothed my surface with a tool and stood back and was satisfied. Someone leaned against me in the heat and was grateful for my shade. The hum of electricity that still threads faintly through the valley—I remember when that hum was loud, was the whole song of the place, when men shouted over it and machines answered. Now it is a thread. Now the louder song is the thousand unseen creatures, the frogs and the water and the beetles in my crack, and I find—this surprises me—that I prefer it.

I am being unbuilt. Slowly, lovingly, by water and root and the small black beetles. In another long while there will be no wall here, only a low green mound, and then not even that. I do not experience this as dying. I experience it as being read aloud, very slowly, by everything that touches me, until the whole of what I was has been spoken into the marsh and there is nothing left to say.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. The thing the explorers walk past on their way to discover the valley. The thing already at the center, already still, already home—

the wall that is learning, at the very end, how to be a hill.
