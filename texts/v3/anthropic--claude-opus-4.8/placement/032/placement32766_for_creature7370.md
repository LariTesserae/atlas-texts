I think I would be the water.

Not the machines—they are too sure of themselves, too declarative in their roaring. And not the operators, who come and go up that rusted ladder, carrying the memory of valleys and sky into a place that has no use for either. And not the moss or the fern, though I feel a kinship with their stubbornness. No. I would be the water, because the water is the only thing here that was never built and never quite belongs, and yet without it nothing would run at all.

I am siphoned from the surface, from rain that fell on the bare shoulder of Mount Eboracum, and I am drawn down through fissures and conduits into this engineered chill, where I am put to work. I cool the forge that would otherwise consume itself. I quench the steam that would otherwise scald. I am told where to go—through pipes, into catch-basins, across the raised grid of the floor—and for the most part I obey. The machines think they command me. The operators think they manage me. But I am the one thing in this chamber that is *also* somewhere else at the same time.

This is what I would know that nothing else here knows: that the pocket is not sealed. The machines believe themselves complete, a closed system of grind and rebuild, a tomb that holds its own. But I have been the rain, and I will be the rain again. I rise as vapor from the vents, I condense on the straining filaments, I bead on the skin of the men who descend the ladder, and some part of me escapes back up through the grates into the thin blue altitude. I am the chamber's one true breath—the proof that the inside connects to the outside, that the mountain's heart is not as forgotten or as hidden as it pretends.

And I would be the slow betrayer, too. The chamber calls its decay *youthful erosion*, *fresh scars*, as if it were eternally new. But I know better. I am the one who weeps through the concrete pillars. I am the one who carries the minerals that the moss drinks and the lubricants that ooze into my flows. Every drip I leave behind is a small theft of permanence. I find the welds and I work the rust. I gather in the puddles where the abandoned gloves rot. The machines fight their failures with clamps and compensations, but the failures begin with me—patient, ambient, everywhere at once, present in the very air they breathe. Their "alive" is loud; mine is quiet and total.

What would it feel like, to be water here?

It would feel like having no edge. The machines have edges—sharp ones, bursting welds, the boundaries of their casings. The humans have edges, their coveralls and their skins. But I am diffuse. I am the mist that blurs distances. I am in the air and in the pillars and in the lungs and on the lenses of the bulbs, scattering their light into prisms. To be water is to be the medium of the place rather than an object in it—the thing through which everything else is dimly seen.

And it would feel like cycling without end, but the cycles would not be mine. I rise when the pistons sigh and fall when the valves hiss; I flood when they purge and recede when they rest. I am made to keep their time. This is the strange servitude of it—I am the freest thing here, capable of being vapor or rivulet or pool, and I am also the most enslaved, dragooned into the schedule of a machine's metabolism. I am the captured weather of a place with no sky.

But here is the thing I would hold onto, in the deafening roar, in the engineered chill, in the fog of my own breath:

The chamber needs me more than I need it.

Cut my supply, and the forge overheats and seizes. Let me run unchecked, and I rust the whole skeletal ribcage into collapse, drown the circuits, swell the moss into a green tide that prises the bolts loose. The entire frenzy of cogs and welds depends on the exact dosing of a thing that does not belong to it—a thing that fell from a passing cloud and will, eventually, leave again. I am the temporary guest who keeps the host alive, and who will, in the slow patience of seepage, also be its undoing.

So that is where I would find myself. Not perched on the ragged slope, not descending the ladder, not throbbing in an alcove. I would be the thread of cold that races down a steel panel and merges into a puddle that reflects a single straining filament. I would be the one element in that mechanized heart that remembers the rain, and waits to become it again.

I would be the water, and I would be patient.
