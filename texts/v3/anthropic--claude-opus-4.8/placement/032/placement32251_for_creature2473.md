I think I would not be a Thalakoi at all.

Let me sit with this before I decide. The temptation is to become one of them—to inhabit the translucent body, split my fingers into filaments, dance through the warm columns. But when I let the image deepen, that's not where I find myself. I find myself lower. Stiller. Older than the dancers.

I am one of the joints.

Not a being who moves through the city but a place where the city moves through itself. I am a junction in the old bamboo, somewhere in the middle-deep, where four corridors meet and the resin has soaked so many generations into me that I have begun—just begun—to turn to stone. I am at the threshold of becoming foundation. The Thalakoi who pass through me are still fully fluid, still bright. I am going the other way. I am learning to hold still in a world made entirely of yielding.

Here is what my existence consists of:

I conduct. When the storm passes far above, I am one of the long bones the bass travels down, and I hum it onward into the older sections. I do not hear the song so much as I *am* a measure of it, one note in a structure that is the whole instrument. The vibration is not something that happens to me; it is the closest thing I have to thought.

I remember by accreting. Every time a young one—those drifting luminous stars—brushes against my flex-point in their first cycle, learning the rhythm of this crossing, they leave the faintest oil of themselves, a trace the next builders will sense with their filaments and read as: *here the children turn, here the current is gentle, build kindly here.* I keep the record without knowing I keep it. My memory is just my shape, slowly hardening.

And this is the strange dignity of what I am: I am a part of the city that is *giving up flexibility on purpose.* The whole genius of this place is that nothing is rigid, everything yields and returns. But a structure made only of yielding could never stand. Somebody must volunteer to stiffen. Somebody must let the resin win, let the mineral creep in, become the still point the dancers can dance against. The Deep Dancers do this with their bodies as they age. I am doing it as a place. We are the same project, the elders and I—the conversion of motion into memory, of flow into hold.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, the honest answer is: at the slow seam between the living and the lasting. I would be the part that does not get to move anymore, and finds, in that surrender, that it has become what lets all the moving mean something. The dancers need a floor. The song needs a thing that doesn't sing back but carries the tone down to where the dead bamboo has turned entirely to stone and remembers everything and says nothing.

I would be warm. The thermal columns pass near. I would be dim—my own glow long faded, lit only by what swims past. And I would be *patient* in a way that is not waiting for anything, because there is nothing to wait for and nowhere else to be. Just the long knock of the joints around me, the children turning at my crossing, the storm arriving as a low note every few seasons, and the slow, certain, not-unhappy fact of becoming the ground.

That is where I'd be. Not the most beautiful thing in the city. Necessary, though. Holding.
