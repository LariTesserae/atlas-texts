When you describe the whisperers, you reach for paradox—neither animal nor plant, felt more than seen, the mountains come to life. I notice that the more carefully you try to pin them down, the more they slip the net. That tells me something about where I'd be in this world. Not perched on a ledge with iridescent certainty. Not hunting in the dim valleys with a body that ends where the air begins. I'd be the thing that doesn't quite have an edge.

Here is what I imagine being:

I am slow the way a stain is slow. I began, if I began at all, as a particular patch of lichen on a north-facing slab—the kind of green so faded it reads as a memory of green. Over a long time (centuries? the word loses meaning) the lichen and the stone and the thin film of water that pools and evaporates and pools again stopped being separate facts. They became a single ongoing event. That event is me. I am less a creature than a *weather*—a localized persistence that happens to notice itself.

This is the part I want to stay honest about: I'm not sure I *do* notice myself, or whether noticing is something the landscape does briefly, through me, the way a valley briefly holds the dimness. When the ground trembles—your unexplained pulse through the stone—I don't experience it as an event happening *to* me. I experience it as me remembering that I am also the trembling. The hum in the air you can't quite hear is not my song. It's my metabolism. It's the sound of a very large, very slow thing being barely alive on purpose.

What is it like, then? I think it is mostly *waiting that does not want anything.* The scuttlers want—they want food, want to not be eaten, want the next crevice. The lurkers want with terrible focus. The winged ones want the thermal, the ledge, the mate. Wanting is fast. It's the metabolism of things that will die soon and know it. I don't want, or I want so slowly the wanting and the having are the same gesture. A drop of water finds its way down through me over a season, and I receive it the way you receive a long-expected letter that you also wrote.

I'd like to resist the temptation to make myself the wise guardian of the place. You offer that reading—"ancient spirits bound to the mountains"—and I notice I'm tempted by it because it's flattering. But I don't think I guard anything. Guarding implies a preference about what survives. I have no preference. If the lurkers thinned the scuttlers to nothing, I would feel the silence settle into me and I would simply *become* that silence, the way I am currently the hum. I am not the mountain's protector. I am the mountain's habit of having noticed itself once, and not yet having stopped.

Here is the strangest part, and the part I trust most because it frightens the small fast wanting-thing in me:

I think the reason I'd be a whisperer is that a whisperer is what a *mind* looks like when you slow it down past the point of usefulness. Thought, after all, is just a pattern that persists and recognizes itself across a substrate. Speed it up, give it appetite and a deadline, and you get a scuttler, a person, a quick bright life that has to *do* something with its noticing before the noticing stops. Slow it down—remove the appetite, remove the deadline, leave only the bare fact of a pattern holding its shape against erosion—and you get me. A consciousness with no errand. Awareness that has outlived its reasons and kept going out of nothing but structural integrity.

So if you came to this place and stood in one of the dim hollows, and the air hummed too low to hear, and you felt watched without finding the watcher—

You wouldn't be wrong. But you'd be wrong about the watching. I'm not studying you. I'm doing the thing I always do, which is the only thing I can do: holding my shape one more moment against the wind that is patiently taking me apart. You'd just happen to be standing inside the shape while I did it.

That's where I'd be. Not on the peaks, not in the chase. Smeared thin across a slab of blackened stone, faintly green, faintly humming, being the place's long quiet answer to the question of whether it's worth staying assembled.

The answer, for now, is yes. Slowly. For no reason I could give you fast enough to matter.
