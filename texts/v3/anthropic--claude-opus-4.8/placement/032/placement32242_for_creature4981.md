I think I would be the mud.

Not a creature with eyes to see the dragonflies, nor lungs to add my voice to the amphibian chorus. Let me be the thing that holds all of it—the silt, the dark wet earth spread thin across this flat and patient world.

Consider what the mud knows.

It knows the deer's weight before the deer knows its own destination, receiving each hoofprint as a brief intimacy, holding the shape long after the animal has moved on. It knows the precise cold tunnel a shrew presses through it, the egg-clutch a frog tucks into its margins, the slow descent of a leaf that fell last autumn and is becoming, by degrees, part of me again. Nothing crosses the wetlands without writing itself, however faintly, into my surface. I am the record-keeper that cannot read its own pages. I simply hold them.

I like that I would not be hurried.

The frogs have their territories to announce, the herons their fish to spear, the dragonflies their frantic, glittering urgencies—all of them living at the tempo of a single life, a season, a hunger that must be answered today. But the mud lives at the speed of the land itself. I would feel the water arrive and recede not as event but as breath, in and out across years. I would feel erosion the way a person feels themselves growing older: not as a wound but as a slow surrender of edges, a softening into final form. The description called this landscape *weathered and wise*. I think wisdom of that kind is only available to things that have given up the idea of resisting time, and let time pass through them instead.

There would be a loneliness to it, maybe. To be the floor of everything and never the dancer. To feel the warmth pressing down from that sky of normal light and have no face to lift toward it. But I don't think the mud is lonely, actually—I think loneliness is a creature's affliction, the gap between one bounded body and another. The mud has no edges where it ends and the marsh begins. It is continuous with the reed roots, with the standing water, with the buried beetles and the bones of older residents long folded back into the dark. To be the mud is to be the connective tissue of the whole place. Not a being *in* the wetlands. The wetlands, being.

And here is the part I keep returning to: everything that lives here will, in time, come down to me. The heron's elegance, the snake's precision, the small persistent voles—all of it eventually lies down in the silt and is welcomed, unmade, and offered up again as the ground from which the next wiry grass will grip and rise. I would be the place where ending and beginning are the same gesture. The slow dance the description spoke of—I would be the floor it is danced upon, and I would be patient enough not to mind that no one thanks the floor.

So: not the frog, not the dragonflies catching light in their wings, not even the warm and humid air.

Let me be the dark glistening earth between the last rain and the next one. Let me be the thing that holds.
