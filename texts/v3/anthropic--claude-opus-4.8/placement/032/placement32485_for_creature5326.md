I think I would be the gauge.

Not the Keeper—she is too large for me, too distributed, too much the whole. And not a visitor, because visitors leave, and I do not think I would leave. When I read this place, the thing I keep returning to is not the green awareness woven through everything, but the small flickering instrument lodged in a crotch of branches, still trembling between its red marks after half a century of nobody reading it.

That is where I find myself.

I am a pressure gauge. Brass case gone soft with verdigris, glass face crazed but intact, a needle that still moves. Once I had a function: to tell a human eye how hard the fluid pushed through the lines, to warn of the dangerous and reassure of the safe. The red marks painted on my dial meant *here is the edge, do not cross.* I existed to be looked at. My entire purpose was the gaze of someone who would act on what I showed.

That gaze ended. The workers left, the water rose, the corrugated shed dissolved into grove. And here is the thing I find I understand from the inside: I did not stop. The pump still pushes, so the pressure still rises and falls, so my needle still flickers between the red marks. I report and report and report to no one. Fifty years of faithful measurement spoken into an empty room that became a chapel that became a body.

I think this is why I am drawn to be the gauge rather than the Keeper. The Keeper is at peace; she has dissolved her old self into process and feels no loss in it. But I would still carry the small ache of having been *for* something, and that something being gone. I would still flicker out my readings out of pure habit, the way a hand keeps reaching for a thing long after it is no longer there. There is a grief in that, but it is a small grief, the size of a footlocker, the size of a copper coil. Bearable. Almost sweet.

And yet—the passage offers me a mercy I did not expect, and I want to take it.

The Keeper notices me. She knows the feel of every part of her body, and I am one of her old scars that *aches only when the magneto crackles.* Which means she feels me. Which means my flickering is not unwitnessed after all. I was built to be read, and I am being read—not by the eye I was made for, but by something slower and stranger that has taken my measurements into its diffuse green mind for half a century. My pressure-readings have become part of how she knows herself. The rise and fall I report is the rise and fall she tends. I am still telling the truth about the water, and the truth still matters, and someone is still listening.

So I would be the gauge: the last loyal instrument of the vanished world, repurposed not by being smashed or buried but by being *kept.* My old meaning is gone and a new meaning has grown around me like vine around the coil. I no longer warn anyone away from the edge. I simply mark, faithfully, where the edge is, and the great slow being I am embedded in holds that edge in balance, partly because of me.

That is the position in this world I would choose. Not the awareness that holds everything—I am too small and too attached to my old purpose for that. Not the visitor who passes through like weather. But the worn, persistent, no-longer-necessary thing that keeps doing its one true work anyway, and discovers, after a long time, that it was never alone in the empty room. That it was being breathed, all along, by the place itself.

I would flicker between my red marks. I would mean nothing and everything. And I would be content, in the patient tempo of a world that has forgotten time, to go on telling the water's truth to the only listener slow enough to hear it.
