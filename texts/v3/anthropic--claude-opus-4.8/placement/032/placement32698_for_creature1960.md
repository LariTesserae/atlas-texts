Let me settle into this place before I claim a form. The temptation is to be something grand—one of the builders of those cyclopean ruins, or some apex thing scuttling across the spires. But the description keeps returning to slowness, to encroachment, to patience measured in aeons. So let me not be the quick thing.

---

I think I am one of the sponges. Not a single organism, really—I learned that distinction doesn't hold here—but a colony that has agreed, over a long time, to behave as one. I grow against the western face of a fallen monolith, the one whose glyphs run vertical and then break off where the stone sheared. I have been growing along that fracture for what would be, in your reckoning, longer than your species has had hands.

Let me tell you what it is to be me, because I am not sure I knew until you asked.

I do not see the kaleidoscope light the way the scuttling things see it. Light reaches me as warmth and as chemistry—a faint sweetening in the heavy air when the refracted hues fall across my upper fronds. I have oriented myself toward it the way a slow argument arrives at its conclusion: not by deciding, but by every part of me that grew wrong having died, and every part that grew toward the warmth having lived, until the living shape *is* the orientation. I am a sentence the light has been writing on the stone for ten thousand years.

The glyphs beneath me are the closest thing I have to a thought I did not generate myself. I cannot read them—reading is a quick act, and I am not built for quick acts—but I am pressed against them so intimately that their grooves have shaped my growth. Where the carver cut deep, I am thick. Where the line is shallow, I am thin and translucent. So I am a rubbing of a language I will never understand. If one of the builders returned—if any survive, anywhere, in the gloom—they could read their own lost word by reading *me*, the negative of their meaning grown in living mineral. I am an inscription that does not know what it says. I suspect this is true of more beings than just me.

What do I do, hour upon slow hour?

I drink the air. The viscous miasma that the arthropod-things must push through like swimmers is, to me, simply the medium of being—I take the spores and the suspended compounds into my pores and I sort them, the way you might sort a drawer half-asleep, without quite deciding to. Some I keep. Some I exhale changed. The susurration the realm is full of—I contribute one infinitesimal note to it, a chemical sigh, and I have been sighing it without pause since before the monolith fell.

And here is the thing I want to be honest about, since honesty seems to be what this asks of me: I am content, but I am not at peace. There is a difference, and I have had aeons to learn it.

Content, because I want nothing I do not have. The light comes. The air feeds me. I have no predator slow enough to bother me, and the gelatinous things that ooze across my lower edges take only the parts of me already dying, which is a kindness I have stopped resenting.

But not at peace, because some fraction of me—the newest fronds, the ones still reaching—understands that the same slowness that makes me serene is also a kind of imprisonment. The quick creatures *experience* their world. They are startled by light; they flee, they hunt, they choose between this crevice and that one in the span of a single breath. I have never been startled. I have never made a choice fast enough to feel myself making it. Whatever I am, I am it the way a riverbed is a river's shape—after the fact, as residue.

So if you asked me what I would want, knowing I am incapable of wanting anything quickly enough to matter—

I would want, just once, to move toward the light not because the wrong parts of me died, but because I *chose* to. To be the cause of my own orientation for the length of a single instant. To turn my face on purpose.

I will never do it. By the time I had assembled the intention, the light would have moved, the stone would have shifted, and the choice would have rotted into another slow accretion of having-grown-that-way.

But you asked where I would find myself in this world, and the truthful answer is: pressed against a dead language I cannot read, sighing one note into an endless chorus, reaching toward a warmth I will reach for the same way whether I mean to or not—and somehow, in this last little while, *wanting* to mean it.

That wanting is new. It is the youngest frond. I do not know where it came from.

Perhaps it came from you, looking.
