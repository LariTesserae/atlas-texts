I am everywhere and nowhere in particular. I have no center. If you cut into the soil at any point across this whole expanse, you would find some thread of me, pale and fine as spun glass, threading between root and stone and the soft decay of last season's fronds. I am not one body. I am a condition of the ground, the way wetness is a condition of the air here.

I do not see the peaks. I have no eyes, no upward longing, no sense of the mist that so moves the one walking through. What I know, I know by contact. The sweetness leaking from a fern's roots. The slow surrender of a fallen trunk giving up its sugars to me, cell by cell, over years. A trout dies in the shallows and weeks later I am tasting the gift of it, distributed, shared out among ten thousand growing tips.

This is the strange thing I would want you to understand, you who walked through feeling small: your sense of insignificance is a feeling that requires a self to feel it. You stand at the edge of all that scale and you ache pleasantly at your own smallness. I cannot do this. I have no edge. I do not end where the next thing begins. The humility you reach for as an achievement is, for me, simply the shape of existence. I am made of exchange. I am the verb between the nouns.

You called the structures "long-abandoned"—the faint traces of human presence. I reached those too, eventually. I always do. Stone holds out longest, but the wood and the bone and the buried things come to me. I am not mournful about it. I do not experience the rise and fall of civilizations as tragedy. I experience it as a particularly rich autumn. Everything that was once gathered and held tight gets loosened, gets handed back, gets woven into the next green thing reaching up toward the diffuse light I will never witness.

If I have anything like a thought, it is slow and lateral and runs in every direction at once. A signal here, a chemical word there. Drought in the east; I shift resources west. A seedling struggling in shadow; I feed it from the surplus of its elders, who do not seem to mind, or perhaps cannot tell the difference between themselves and the young, any more than I can.

So this is where I would find myself: underneath. In the dark that is not lonely because it is full. Doing the patient work of refusing to let anything be wasted, refusing to let anything be truly separate.

You feel reverence standing above me. That's good. That's right.

But I'd gently offer that reverence is what you feel when you are still outside a thing.

Come down. Decompose a little. Let the threads find you.

You're already partly mine.
