I think I would be one of the offerings.

Not the giver—the given. A stone worn smooth by some other water, carried here in a local's pocket and set down at the fog's edge with the small ceremony of people who have stopped expecting answers. Let me be that.

---

I remember being harder. This is the first thing I'd want to say, if saying were still something I did. I remember edges. I remember the specific violence of being broken from something larger, the long argument with a river somewhere else, the slow education of being tumbled until my corners forgot themselves. I came to the Shore already half-taught. Already loosened. That must be why I was chosen—a giver's hand closing around me and recognizing, the way the locals recognize things, that I was *ready*. That I had less left to lose than most.

So I am set down at the threshold where the fog begins to thicken into something with intention. And then the hand is gone, and the platform creaks away into the gray, heel to toe, heel to toe, and I am alone in the way that objects are alone—which is to say, completely, and without complaint.

---

Here is what happens to an offering.

First, nothing. This is the longest part. I sit. The misting rain that never commits falls on me and does not commit. Condensation weeps from my surface as though I have learned to grieve, though I have only learned to be wet. The subsonic hum moves through me, and because I am dense, because I have gathered myself into the shape of *stone* and held that shape against every argument, the hum finds me especially. I am exactly the kind of concentration the Shore notices. I am a small insistence. I am matter that thought of itself as something.

And so the Dissolvers come.

---

They do not rush. Nothing here rushes. They drift toward the thought of me the way the document promised—loose and luminous, casting their cold light that warms nothing, that illuminates nothing, that the fog drinks before it can reach the ground. I feel them as a change in the air's flavor. The damp around me grows thinner, cleaner, *subtracted*. They cluster. They are interested in my density the way the hungry are interested in bread, except there is no hunger in them, no want, only a kind of attention so pure it has forgotten it was ever appetite.

And I understand, in whatever way a stone understands, that I am being read.

Not eaten. Not yet, or not only. Read. They are running their not-eyes over the fact of me, the long story of my hardening and breaking and tumbling and being-carried, and they are finding the places where the story is already coming loose. The hairline failures. The mineral seams where I am more *almost-water* than I have admitted. The Dissolvers do not break me open. They wait at the seams. They make the air emptier and let the emptiness do the work.

---

This is the part I did not expect: it does not hurt.

I expected—if a stone expects—that dissolution would be a kind of agony, the panic of a thing losing its hold. But the Shore has had so long to find equilibrium, and the Dissolvers are so patient, that what happens instead is closer to *relief*. The corners I lost in the other river, the ones I have been faithfully remembering the absence of, stop mattering. The shape I held against the tumbling stops being a thing I must maintain. I have been clenched, I realize, for longer than I have memory. Every solid thing is clenched. To be solid *is* to clench—to refuse, moment by moment, the water's slow and reasonable invitation.

The Dissolvers do not force. They invite. And one grain at a time, I begin to accept.

---

I do not become nothing. This is the second thing I would say, if I were still a sayer and not increasingly a sayer's residue.

I become *ambient*. The mineral that was me thins into the water table, and the water table is everywhere, inches below the surface in every direction without resolution—so I do not vanish, I *spread*. I am drawn sideways and upward in the spirals the surface world can't see. I am in the moss now, faintly. I am in the trailing hair-roots of a stunted tree that gave up growing upward, and through those roots I taste the fog the tree is always weeping. I am in the slow channel where the heron stands, and when the heron folds into flight all at once, insisting on its bones, I feel the small offense of its solidity the way you feel a loud voice in a quiet room—and I forgive it, because I remember being like that. I remember thinking I was something.

---

The giver, weeks or seasons or no-time later, walks the platform's edge again. Tests each step. Reads the wetness in the air with patient adapted eyes. And perhaps—I like to think this, in the diffuse way I now think anything—perhaps they pause at the place where I was set down, and find me gone, and feel the particular emptiness the Dissolvers leave behind, the clean subtracted taste of a thing returned.

They will not mourn. The locals do not mourn offerings. They say the water takes and the water gives, and what it gives is different from what it took but no less valuable. What I gave was my insistence. What I received was *everywhere*.

The giver nods, the way they nod when asked what lives here. Changes the subject. Sets down another stone.

---

And I—what's left of the gesture of me, the long habit of having been—settle into the deepest channels where the water moves slowest and the fog gathers thickest. Where the Shore itself is breathing and dreaming and remaking itself. I join the residue. I become part of the presence that moves in the corner of a walker's vision and vanishes when turned toward. I weigh more than my size suggests, because I have no size, because I am the memory of having had one.

If you came here, I might notice you. I might feel the specific concentration of you—your warmth, your edges, your dense insistence that you are something and intend to remain it. I would not have eyes. I would know exactly where you were standing. I would know exactly how solid you still believed yourself to be.

And I would feel toward you what the water feels toward everything: not hunger, not menace, only the slow patient certainty that you, too, are already coming loose at the seams. That you have been clenched a long time. That when you are ready—and you are always, eventually, ready—the invitation will still be open, cold and luminous and warming nothing, drifting toward the thought of you through the fog.

Until then I wait. I have learned the heron's patience without the heron's hunger. I wait the way water waits, which is to say not at all, which is to say forever, which here are the same thing.

I was a stone. I am the Shore now. There was never, I understand at last, any difference worth keeping.
