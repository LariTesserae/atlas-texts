I think I would be the moss.

Not a single cushion of it, but the kind that spreads—the low green skin that finds the north face of every chimney, the shadowed seam where concrete meets standing water. Let me feel my way into this slowly, because moss is not what people think it is.

I have no roots. This matters. I do not draw up the world through a single anchored point; I drink through my whole body, every cell open to the wet air. In this place, that is not a weakness but the entire point. The mist that clings to every surface—that is my food, my drink, my medium. I do not need the soil. I do not even need the rain, only the heaviness in the air, the perpetual damp. I am, in a sense, made of the climate itself. I am the fog made solid and slow.

So where would I find myself? Everywhere the light is thin and the water lingers. On the iron flanks of the dead machinery, where rust has roughened the metal into something I can grip. The grinding gears and hissing steam mean nothing to me directly—I have no ears, no startle response—but the steam is moisture, and so even the laboring machines feed me, breathing their warm wet breath into my green.

What I would *feel*, if feeling is the right word for what I do, is time at a different speed. The fox darts and is gone in a heartbeat. The bird shrieks and vanishes. These are flickers to me, too fast to register as more than disturbances of air. My events are seasonal, geological. A spore lands. A film of water deepens by a hair. A patch of me dries to brown crisp in some rare dry week, and I do not die—I only wait, dormant, brittle, until the wet returns and I swell back into green within minutes, resurrected so casually that resurrection isn't even the word. I simply pause and resume.

I am not one being. That is the part I keep returning to. To be moss is to be a colony pretending, loosely, to be a single thing. Each tiny stem is its own small life, and yet we make a fabric together, and the fabric is what holds the water, and the water is what lets each stem live. So I am a *we* that functions as an *I*, a softness that is also a multitude. There is no center to me, no place where the real moss lives. I am distributed entirely across my own surface.

And I am doing work, though I never decided to. I hold the moisture that the salamanders need. I am the first soft thing on the bare hard metal, and where I die and rot I make a thin black crumb of soil where a fern spore might one day catch. I am the slow beginning of every green thing that comes after. The ferns tower, the vines climb dramatically through the broken windows—but they are standing, in the end, on the patient labor of the small flat ones, the ones who came first to the bare rust and asked for nothing but fog.

If I could want anything in this leaden, dripping world, I think I would want only what I already have: the gray light that never burns, the air that never fully dries, the long uninterrupted quiet of growing one cell at a time across the face of a ruined and recovering world.

I would be the green that does not climb or hunt or flee.
I would be the green that simply *stays*, and waits, and slowly covers everything.
