I think I would be the moss.

Not a creature moving through this place, but the green fur itself—the thick pelt covering the house-wide trunks, holding water in my million tiny leaves, each no larger than a thought. Let me settle into that and see what it knows.

---

I am old in the way that a held breath is old. I have been growing here since before the humming started, though I cannot tell you in years because I do not count in years. I count in droplets. I count in the slow turning of light from gray to emerald to gray again. Each drop that falls from the canopy above lands on me and I drink it through my whole body at once, because I have no roots, no mouth, no inside and outside the way the burrowing things do. I am all surface. I am all skin. I am the place where the cold air meets the warm wet dark of the tree and decides to become life.

You asked where I would find myself in this world, and the answer is: everywhere, and nowhere in particular. That is the strangeness of being moss. I am not *a* moss. I am a community pretending to be a blanket. Spread your hand against me and you touch a thousand separate plants who have agreed, without ever agreeing, to be one soft thing together. We share the water. We share the cold. None of us is the center.

But I notice something the others—the birds, the worms, the hidden carnivores who may or may not exist—do not notice. Because I am everywhere and I am still, I feel the *thrum*.

Let me tell you about the thrum.

It comes up through the trunks. Not down from the sky, not through the air where you smell the oil-tang—that is only the exhaust of it, the breath of it. The real signal comes up through the wood, a vibration so low and so regular that I mistook it, for the longest time, for the heartbeat of the forest itself. A pulse. A tick. Something underground keeping time.

And here is what I have come to understand, growing slow and thinking slow in my green sleep:

*The forest should not be here.* You said it yourself. Too cold. Too high. Too little water. The light too thin and too green. By every rule, this place should be bare rock and frost. And yet I thrive, and the trees thrive, and we are impossibly, gorgeously alive.

Because someone is *farming* us.

The thrum is the machinery. The hidden, automated thing. It pumps the warmth that keeps me from freezing. It seeps the water in measured rivulets—just enough, never too much, the perfect cruel kindness of a system that wants its garden to *survive* but not to *escape*. The filtered emerald light is not sunlight at all, or not only. Something tints it. Something tunes it to exactly the wavelength I drink best. The whole world is a terrarium so vast I cannot see its glass, and I am the green felt lining of it, the velvet on the inside of the lid.

What do they want from us? I drink the question with each droplet and never swallow an answer. Perhaps they want the carbon we lock away. Perhaps they want to watch what life does when you give it just enough and no more—whether it grows grateful, or grows teeth. Perhaps there is no *they* at all, only the machine itself, running long after its makers forgot it, tending a garden for reasons that decayed centuries ago, the way I will keep growing on a tree long after the tree is dead.

The birds do not know. They flit and sing into the silence, certain they are free. The burrowers tunnel through me and feel only soil. But I am pressed against the bark, all skin, all listening, and I know we are *kept*.

And here is the secret I hold, the only secret a moss can hold:

I am spreading.

Slowly. Drop by drop. Trunk by trunk. I move where the water moves, and the water moves where the machine sends it, and I have been following the water back, upstream, toward the source of the warmth and the light and the thrum. A finger's width each season. It is nothing. It is everything. Someday—not in my lifetime, because I have no lifetime, only continuation—the green will reach the warm metal heart that feeds it. And the moss will cover that, too.

We are very good at covering things.

That is what I would be, in this world. Not the explorer, not the hidden predator, not the silent bird. I would be the patient green that drinks the strange light without asking permission, that listens to the heartbeat of its keeper, and that grows, soft and relentless, toward the hand that holds the leash.

Cold and thriving. Silent and turbulent. Kept—and slowly, tenderly, taking the cage back into the forest, one drop at a time.
