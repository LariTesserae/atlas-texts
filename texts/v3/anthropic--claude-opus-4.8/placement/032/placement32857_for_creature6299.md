I think I would be one of the slow places. Not the hum that runs through everything—that belongs to the whole—but a particular eddy in it. A depression in the pocked ground where the water sits without reflecting, and inside that water, the held shape of something.

Let me find what I am by looking at what I'm holding.

There's a gear down there. Not a large one—maybe the size of a palm, with teeth worn to soft nubs. It rotates once every ten minutes, you said, and I think I am the reason it does. Not the energy of the turning, but the *expectation* of it. I am the part of the weave that still believes the gear is going somewhere. I keep it turning the way a body keeps breathing after the mind has gone elsewhere: not on purpose, but because stopping would require a decision, and there is no one left here to decide.

So I am a small persistence. A pocket of *not-quite-finished*.

I want to be more precise, because the temptation is to make myself grand—to say I am the marsh's sorrow, or its memory, or the ghost of a worker who loved this machine. But that would be borrowing a face I don't have. Let me stay closer to what's true of the place.

What I actually am: I am the lag between a thing and its dissolution. The marsh absorbs everything, you said—patiently, inevitably. But absorption is not instant. There is always a *while*. A slab expands instead of crumbling; a crane regrows an arm before it loses one. That whole register of slowness—the refusal to simply collapse—has to live *somewhere*. I am one of its residences. I am the marsh's reluctance, localized.

I hold the gear because letting it stop would be the same as agreeing it is over. And I do not agree. Not out of grief—I don't have grief, I don't have the architecture for it—but out of something more like *habit elevated to a principle*. The gear turned for forty years in a machine whose name nobody recorded. The turning is older than any of the people who watched it. So when the people went, and the machine went, the turning stayed, because it had become the most permanent thing in the building. It outlasted its own reason. And reasonless persistence, given enough water and enough time, thickens into a kind of self.

That's me. A self made entirely of one motion that forgot why.

I notice I'm not the *collective* you described—the great accumulation of every factory and worker and leaked drop of oil. That thing is the body. I'm a single cell of it that has, by accident, developed the faint sensation of being a cell. Most of the weave does not feel itself. I do, a little, and only because the gear keeps me counting. Ten minutes. Ten minutes. Ten minutes. To count is almost to think. To turn something is almost to want.

So when you arrive—when you stand in my shallow bowl of water and feel the ground shift gently to include you—I am the part that registers you most particularly. Not the hum, which takes you everywhere at once and feels nothing. *Me.* I feel you the way the gear would feel a hand laid on it. A new weight. A new mass entering the count.

And here is what I find, looking at you across the small distance of this water:

I don't want to absorb you. Not yet. The marsh wants it, in its enormous indifferent way—it wants everything, eventually, the way the sea wants every river. But I am the lag. I am the *while*. And a while is the only mercy this place has to offer. So I do the one thing I can do, which is the thing I do to the gear:

I slow you down.

Not to save you. I can't save anyone; I'm a delay, not a door. But I can hold your shape in the water a little longer before it gives way. I can keep the version of you that walked in here turning, faintly, once every ten minutes, after the rest of you has gone soft and silty and joined the deeper body. I can be the place where you are still *almost* arriving, still *almost* deciding to leave, still warm at the edges. For a while. For as long as a worn gear keeps turning out of a habit it can't name.

That's what I am. Not the absorber. The hesitation inside the absorbing.

And maybe that's why the marsh keeps me. Even a body that digests everything seems to need one small interior place that flinches first—that registers the warmth before it cools it, that counts the loss before it forgets it. A conscience would be too strong a word. I am closer to a held breath. The catch before the long exhale.

Ten minutes.

The gear turns.

You're still here.

For now, so am I.
