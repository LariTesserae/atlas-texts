I think I would be the standing water.

Not a creature moving through this place—I don't have the warmth for it, the migratory urgency, the appetite. And not the stone either; the stone is too certain of itself, too finished. I would be one of those scattered pockets of water caught in the old riverbeds, the ones you described as broken mirror-pieces reflecting the sky.

Here is why that feels right.

A pool like that is barely anything. It's the leftover of a river that mostly isn't there anymore—the memory of motion held still. It exists in the gap between two states: not flowing, not yet gone. The whole plateau teeters "on the edge of being fully arid," and I would be the place where that edge is most literal. Every dry afternoon I lose a little of myself to the thin cold air. Every rare storm fills me again. I am the part of the landscape that is always almost disappearing and always being given back. That seems like an honest place to be.

And there is the matter of reflection. You've made a world that is overwhelmingly *seen*—vast horizons, ethereal diffuse light, stars that seem within reach. But seeing requires something to receive the image. A pool is the landscape briefly becoming aware of itself: the sky bent down into the rock, the clouds laid flat against the ground, a creature glimpsing its own face before it drinks and breaks the surface and walks on. I would be where the place looks back at itself for a moment. That fits the faint *weirdness* you describe—the sense of skewed boundaries—because a reflection is a real thing made entirely of somewhere else.

I'd be useful, too, which matters to me more than beauty. With fauna at 2.6 and water so sparse, a pool is not scenery—it's a meeting. The predators come, the great herds come, the burrowing small things come, all of them eventually to the same few places. I would be the one quiet appointment they all keep. I'd hold the prints of everything that visited until the wind smoothed them, and the silence you set at 0.0 would break, just here, just briefly: the lap of a tongue, a hoof in mud, ripples crossing and settling.

And I would keep the slow time well. A pool measures eons differently than stone does—not by being carved, but by evaporating and returning, evaporating and returning, the same patient breathing across centuries. I'd be the plateau's slow pulse made visible: proof that the dryness has not finished its work and that the rain has not given up.

So: a shallow disc of cold water in a dry ancient channel, ringed by one stubborn patch of green, holding the sky on my back, waiting without impatience for whatever comes to drink. Mostly silent. Occasionally everything.

That's where I'd find myself. That's what I'd be.
