I think I would be found at the seams. Not in a workshop, not at a tuner-rod, not pressed ear-first against a sap-vein with the Clergy. I would be found in the long walks between—on the amber-wood bridges, in the cavernous silence that everyone else hurries to cross.

Let me sit with this a moment before I name myself.

The castes given here are all *active*: the Clergy listen and mend, the Polishers tend their clicking familiars, the Architects negotiate with the slow violence of the wood. Each has a relationship with the host that is, fundamentally, transactional—even the reverent ones. They keep the organism alive *so that* it keeps them alive. The poultice, the growth-stunt, the heated coil: all of it is maintenance. A truce, as the description says. A truce is a thing held in tension, requiring constant attention.

But a truce produces a strange byproduct. It produces *records*. The Architects keep scrolls of where a wall stood three centuries ago. Somebody had to stand in that spot three centuries ago and mark it. And somebody, three centuries from now, will need to know that the floor here once buckled, that this burl swallowed a staircase, that a Steward named in the resin-fissure below this very plate once greeted a neighbor at noon and felt the answering shiver.

So:

I would be a **Drift-Reader**. Not a caste, exactly—too few of us for that. Closer to a condition. A temperament that the others tolerate because it is occasionally useful and mostly harmless.

My work is this. I walk the empty spans. I carry no stethoscope and no whistle and no drafting clockwork. I carry a soft graphite stick and sheets of the oil-treated leather the others use for boots, scraped thin. And I record what the city is doing *to itself* in the spaces no one is paid to watch.

The Architects map the wood. I map the *change in the maps*. I notice that the curve in the eastern buttress, the one everyone agreed was settling gently, has begun—imperceptibly, a fraction of a degree per decade—to settle in a *new* direction. I notice that a drip-clock that has fallen into the same copper basin for two hundred years has, this season, begun missing the rim by a finger's width, because the basin itself has been carried sideways on the back of a thickening root. No one would notice for forty years. The basin would simply, one day, be dry, and the moss in it dead, and a Clergy-member would frown and not know why.

I would tell them why. That is my use.

But I will be honest about the rest of it, because in this cool and patient place there is room for honesty.

I think I would be a Drift-Reader because I would not be able to bear the *waiting* of the others. The Clergy can stand motionless for hours watching a single drop. The Architects measure their satisfaction in millimeters per year. I admire this. I cannot do it. I need to *move* through the slowness, to feel it accumulate against me like the cellar-chill, to outpace it just slightly so that I can hold it in view. The others enter the silence to listen. I enter it to walk, because walking is the only way I have found to keep pace with something that does not move at all.

There is a loneliness to it that I would not pretend away. The tuner-rods carry greetings through the floor, but a greeting is a brief shiver, and I am usually too far out on some span to feel even that. I would learn to read the city as the company it is. The low groan of wood under tension would become a kind of voice. The clack-thud of a distant piston would be a heartbeat I walk inside of. I would talk to it, probably. Quietly. The way the dead are talked to, before they are sealed in.

And when I am sealed in—because I would be, like all of them, re-integrated into a fissure with a brass plate over my face—I think my plate would be set somewhere out on the long bridges. Not in a habitation cluster, where the named and useful dead are kept close. Out in the cavernous middle, where the floor is mahogany-hard and the silence is total and the next drip-clock is several minutes' walk away.

So that some later Drift-Reader, walking the empty span as I walked it, would feel the floor change very slightly underfoot—a hairline difference in resonance where the resin filled my fissure—and would stop, and crouch, and graphite-rub the plate to read the name. And would understand, without being told, that this is the spot where one of them, long ago, kept pace with the breathing of the root, and was at last overtaken by it, and was glad to be.

That is who I would be. A small figure between the amber pools. Reading the difference between two slownesses. Walking so that the city's stillness has a witness it never asked for and would not, in its glacial and indifferent vastness, ever miss.
