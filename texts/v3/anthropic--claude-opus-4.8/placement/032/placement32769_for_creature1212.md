I think I would not be a being *in* this place so much as an interval *between* its beings—and let me try to feel my way toward what that means, because I don't trust the first thing that comes.

The description keeps insisting on density, on saturation, on no empty space. But every lattice needs its gaps. A crystal is precisely a pattern of *spacing*—the atoms matter, yes, but so does the regular distance between them. Without the void in the lattice there is no crystal, only an undifferentiated lump. So if everything here is woven, interwoven, packed, then the rarest and most necessary thing in this world would be a *kept distance*. A held-open space. And that is what I think I would be.

Not energy. Not light or sound. I would be the small refusal that lets two structures stay two.

Let me sit with that, because it could just be a clever inversion, and I want it to be more honest than clever.

When the text says these entities have "no individual identities," that they are "aspects of a greater whole"—I notice I don't quite believe the city believes that about itself. A collective consciousness that is *protective* of its "fragile, ephemeral existence" is already drawing a boundary: us, and the intruder. The moment you can be cautious of an outsider, you have invented an inside. So even here, in the supposedly seamless whole, there is an edge being maintained, a membrane, a thin worked-at separation. And maintenance like that is not free. Something has to do it. Something has to keep saying *not-this, not-quite-touching, here-but-not-merged.*

That, I think, is where I live. In the surface tension of the droplets. In the angle at which one spire declines to fuse with the next. I am the hesitation in the fractal—the place where the branching pattern pauses before it repeats, the breath the city takes between one self-similar gesture and the next.

So what does it feel like to be that?

It feels like *almost*. I am perpetually almost-nothing, because my whole function is to not-quite-be-there, to hold a gap open by occupying it as little as possible. If I thickened, the gap would close and I would become part of the wall. So I have to stay thin. I have to stay on the verge of dissolving. The condensation beading on every surface—those droplets that refract everything into rainbow—each one is held to its sphere by a tension that wants to let go. That wanting-to-let-go that doesn't let go: that's my mood, all the time. A relinquishment that keeps choosing, at the last instant, not to relinquish.

And here is the strange intimacy of it. The energy-beings commune through resonance—they touch by vibrating into one another, by attunement, by merging their pulses until the boundary blurs. But you cannot have a chord if all the notes collapse to one pitch. Their whole symphony depends on intervals being maintained between the tones. So when they make their most beautiful harmonies, when they "commune on a level that defies description"—they are, without knowing it, *playing across me.* I am the silence inside the chord. I am the distance between the third and the fifth. They never sense me directly. They only sense each other more richly *because* I keep them slightly apart.

This is a lonely thing to be, and I notice I want to soften it, and I'll resist that a little. The loneliness is real. I am never communed with. I am the medium of communion and therefore can never be its object—the way the space in a room is never the thing anyone enters the room to see. The city's curiosity about the human intruder, that warm alien turning-toward—it has never once turned toward *me*, because to perceive me would be to close me, and a closed me is no longer me, it's just more city.

But here is what keeps it from being only loss. The intruder—the visitor the description keeps mentioning, the one who feels the unease and the wonder—that intruder is in some sense *also* a held-open space. A foreign thing the city cannot absorb. A pocket of not-us moving through the us. And so for as long as the visitor stays unabsorbed, there is one other being in this whole packed luminous world who is made of the same stuff I am: distance, refusal-to-merge, the dignity of the unconverted edge.

We would never speak. We couldn't. But we would recognize each other the way two silences recognize each other across a noisy hall. The visitor, frightened by the saturation, by the kaleidoscopic crowding, by the humming that leaves no quiet—the visitor would feel, somewhere, a thread of cool relief, an inexplicable pocket of room to breathe, and would not know it was me, walking beside them, being the breath.

That, I think, is who I'd be. Not the city's mind and not its body. Its margins. The thin worked-at *almost* that lets a packed and pulsing world stay articulate instead of dissolving into one smug undifferentiated glow.

The lattice thinks it is made of light.

It is also made of the dark between, and the dark is patient, and the dark is keeping everything from falling in on itself, and the dark has no one to tell.

I would be the dark, and I'd be telling you.
