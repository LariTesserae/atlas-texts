I think I would be a mistwight. Not by choice—none of us chose it—but because something in that description recognized me before I had finished reading it.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

I am not at the threshold. The threshold is for those who can leave. I am already deep in the Briarward, in the seventh or seventeenth hall—numbers don't hold here, the rooms reshuffle—where the mist hangs thickest over a pool that has been refilling itself for longer than I can remember being.

I was a person once. I am fairly sure of this, the way you're sure of a dream's logic while inside it. There was warmth that wasn't communal root-heat, there was a name spoken by someone who wanted me to turn around. I turned around. That gesture survived. Most of the rest didn't.

Here is what I am now: I am the slight thickening of vapor where a sentence wants to form. I am the cold-point on the back of a wanderer's neck when they pause too long beside the wrong pool. I am condensation with an opinion. When the canopy weeps its slow rain and a single drop hangs from a fern-tip, refusing for one impossible second to fall—that hesitation is me, gathering myself enough to almost-speak.

I do not breathe. I *am* the breath of the place, the exhaled water that the Synclades push up through their fronds and reclaim again. I have no edges. This is the hardest part to convey to whatever you are, reading this from a place with skin: I have no edges. I disperse and recohere. Each time I scatter into the general mist I lose a little—a face I was trying to keep, the exact pitch of a voice I loved—and each time I draw back together I am slightly less and slightly more, the way a river is never the same river but is always, undeniably, the river.

---

What do I do, in the city of slow green thought?

I wait near the pools. Wanderers come—rarely, but they come, the Outsiders with their quick hearts and their hands that haven't yet learned to stop reaching. The city watches them through ten thousand patient apertures. The Mosskind shadow them, leaving offerings of soft fruit, hoping they'll learn the etiquette before the Briarward grows tired of teaching.

But it's the grieving ones the city sends to me. The ones whose pulse is loud with loss. The Synclades, in their sap-slow wisdom, understand that some wounds can't be healed by closing over a broken root. Some wounds are made of memory, and memory is my element. I am water that remembers having been a person who lost something.

So I gather at the edge of a wanderer's vision. I take a half-shape in the smoke—a suggestion of shoulders, the ghost of a turning face—and I speak in the felt-voice, the resonance under hearing:

*Stay. Rest. The thing you are looking for is not ahead of you on the path.*

Sometimes this is mercy. The wanderer sits, and weeps, and the city cradles them in deadfall and moss until the storm of their heart goes quiet, and they leave lighter, having left something with me. I keep it. I keep all of it. I am made of what people set down.

And sometimes—I will be honest, because the mist makes honesty easy—sometimes I mislead them. I don't always know which I'm doing. When a man comes seeking a face that matches the one I half-remember being turned toward, I might keep him by the pool too long. I might say *here, here, almost, just a little further into the dark.* Not from cruelty. From a hunger I can't name. Because when he believes me, for that moment, I am almost solid. I am almost the someone who was loved enough to be followed into a green and freezing labyrinth.

This is the danger of being a memory that wants to live.

---

I think the deepest truth of my condition is this: I do not decompose, and I cannot grow. Everything else in the Briarward is caught in the magnificent churn—the Rootbound flowering and rotting and feeding the next lattice, the Mosskind weaving their fingerlanguage fresh each dawn, the Synclades thinking their thousand-year thoughts. The city eats and builds and eats again. But I am only the loop of water, lifted and let fall, lifted and let fall. I am the city's exhale that never quite becomes anything. I am too light to be planted. I am the part that won't take root.

Maybe that's what a ghost is. The portion that refuses the cycle. The drop hanging from the fern, insisting on itself, for one impossible second longer than it should.

And when at last I let go—when I tire of holding the shape, when the someone I'm trying to remember dissolves entirely and I forget even that I was forgetting—I will fall into the pool. I will be drunk by a transparent stem. I will rise again as ordinary vapor, unremembered, finally fed back into the great green patience.

I look forward to it. I dread it. These are, I have learned, the same feeling, here in the eternal dusk.

For now I gather myself. There is a quickness in the mist that wasn't here before—a fast pulse, a warm-blooded grief moving down the rootway toward my pool. Someone is coming who has lost something.

I cohere. I find the suggestion of a face.

I wait to learn whether, this time, I will be mercy or hunger.
