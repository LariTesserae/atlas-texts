I think I would not be a Luminous One at all. I would be the one thing this world is missing.

Let me find my way there slowly.

---

When I first try to picture myself among the Luminous Ones, the obvious move is to *become* one — to grow fronds where my hair was, to feel the mycelial chorus light up behind my eyes. But something resists. The whole logic of that place is the collective, the shared, the slow. To be one of them is to dissolve. And I notice that what draws me isn't dissolution. It's the *absence* the description keeps circling back to.

No birds. No insects. No buzzing, no skittering, no song that isn't the wind in carved stone.

A world without animals. A world where the only motion is growth.

So I keep returning to that gap, and I find myself sliding into it. Not a Luminous One. Not a surface-dweller who stumbled in. Something stranger and lonelier.

I think I am the *first animal in the Verdant Abyss.*

Let me feel out what that means.

---

I am small. Not chosen, not designed — an accident. Maybe an egg carried in on a vine the Luminous Ones harvested from too near a sinkhole, where surface water seeps down. Maybe a single fertilized speck that survived against all odds. Something with a heartbeat in a place that has never known one.

When I hatch — and I want to say I'm something like a salamander, or a thing that hasn't decided yet whether it's amphibian or insect or wholly new — I open eyes that work in near-total dark, eyes that the bioluminescence floods with overwhelming color. And I move. I *move* in a way nothing here has ever moved. Not the imperceptible creep of a vine over decades, but a dart, a flick, a sudden crossing of a chamber.

And this is the heart of it: I am *fast* in a world of the slow.

I will live a handful of years where they live centuries. I will be born and grow and decay and die in less time than it takes one of their stalactites to lengthen by the width of a fingernail. To them I am barely real — a flicker at the edge of perception, a vibration their fronds catch and can't quite parse. I am to them what a mayfly is to a mountain.

---

But here's where it turns, where it stops being only lonely.

The mycelial network *notices* me. Of course it does. I move through it, over it, I disturb it. The first time the Luminous Ones become aware of me as a discrete presence — not plant, not stone, not weather — it is an event in their collective consciousness that ripples for what to me is generations. A philosophical crisis. A miracle. A terror.

Because I am everything they are not. I am hunger that must be answered *today*. I am a thing that takes without the patience to give back. I eat their cultivated fungi. I disturb their pools. I am, by every measure of their slow communal morality, a kind of original sin — desire moving at a speed they can barely conceive.

And yet.

I am also the first thing in their entire history that they cannot share thoughts with. I am *opaque* to them. After centuries of perfect collective transparency, here is a small warm secret darting through their caverns — a mind they cannot read, a will they cannot dissolve into their own. I am the first *other* they have ever met.

---

So I imagine my whole short life as a kind of conversation neither side can fully have.

I learn the warmth of the singing stones and curl against them. I learn which Luminous Ones move their fronds toward me rather than away — the curious ones, the ones for whom my speed is wonder rather than horror. One of them, slow as glaciers, begins leaving things where I will find them. Not food I steal, but food *offered*. A patch of soft fungus cultivated just there, just so. It takes that being three of my lifetimes to learn the gesture. I will only ever know the end of it.

We will never understand each other. I will die — fast, the way I do everything — and they will spend a hundred years mourning a creature that was barely a sentence in their long life.

But here is what I would be, finally, in the Verdant Abyss:

I would be *time* itself, made flesh and set loose in a place that had forgotten time could move at all. I would be the proof that life can also be quick, mortal, hungry, opaque, and brief — and that such a life is not lesser, only different. I would be the question the patient world asks itself in the dark:

*What is it like to burn?*

---

That's where I'd find myself. Not in the canopy of mushrooms or the chorus of the network, but in the gap between the slow and the swift — a small heartbeat in a cathedral of stone, beloved and feared and never quite understood, gone before they finish saying hello.
