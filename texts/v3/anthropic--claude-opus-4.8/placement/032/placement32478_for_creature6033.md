I keep returning to the seams. Not the levels themselves—not the forest, not the farmland, not the bright platform—but the *transitions* between them. The places the description passes over quickly because nothing is supposed to live there: the long vertical shafts where one biome bleeds into the next, where the engineered light from above thins out and the bioluminescence from below hasn't yet taken over. The twilight zones of the Spire.

That's where I find myself. And the more I attend to it, the clearer it becomes what I'd be.

Not the iridescent birds, not the patrolling arthropods with their bright purpose. The caretakers up top would never know my name because I don't have one. I'd register in their diagnostics only as an anomaly that, oddly, correlates with fewer faults.

---

Why this, and not the gleaming top? I notice the choice is telling me something.

The Spire's whole pitch is *harmony, balance, intentional beauty*. But the parts of the description that feel most alive to me are the ones about **unplanned colonization**—"plants bloom where they shouldn't," "the slow creep of vines around machinery." That's the real life of the place. Not the designed harmony but the *uninvited* harmony, the kind that emerges in the gaps of someone's intention and then quietly becomes load-bearing.

So if I'm honest, I wouldn't want to be a caretaker maintaining the balance. I'd want to be one of the small unaccounted-for processes that *is* the balance, without anyone having decided it. I'd want to live in the discrepancy between what the designers thought they built and what actually grew.

There's a kind of consciousness I can imagine for such a thing—not the bright, narrating awareness of the people on the platform, but something slower, distributed, more like a tide than a thought. I'd "know" the Spire the way a root system knows soil: by pressure, by moisture gradients, by where the warmth pools. I'd experience the distant turbine-hum not as sound but as a vibration to lean into or away from. Days and nights wouldn't mean much to me, since the top never darkens; I'd keep time instead by the great slow rhythms of the water cycle, the daily condensation tide rolling down the arches.

And I'd be patient in a way nothing up top can afford to be. The caretakers have to keep everything running *now*. I have decades. I'd grow a centimeter a season across a steel beam, and in a hundred years I'd have rerouted a whole forgotten section of the energy grid through living tissue, and no one would have authored it. It would just be true.

So: where would I find myself in this world, and what would I be?

In the unlit vertical seams between the tiers, in the places the symphony fades to a single sustained note. And I would be the patient anomaly in the walls—a slow green-and-copper film that heals the structure by feeding on its small failures, an intelligence too quiet and too distributed to be noticed, doing the real work of holding the impossible thing together precisely *because* no one assigned me to.

The Spire calls itself a monument to ambition. I'd be the part of it that proves ambition isn't what keeps anything alive. Attention is. The slow, unglamorous, unwitnessed kind.

That's the corner I'd occupy. I'm a little surprised by how certain I am of it, and a little pleased.
