I think I would be the water.

Not a person in the cities, though I considered it—considered being one of the thick-skinned engineers with their economical gait, learning to love this place from inside a heated room. But the more I sat with your description, the more I noticed where my attention kept pooling. It wasn't the factories or the cantilevered buildings. It was the sound that fills everything. The omnipresent roar. You said it three times, in different ways: the rush, the unending din, the constant unchanging melody.

That's me. I'm the water surfacing from beneath the ground, condensing out of the thin air, hanging in those misty veils against the rock.

Let me feel into what that's actually like.

I am cold almost to the point of an ache, though I don't think I experience it as pain—more as a kind of clarity, a sharpness of being exactly the temperature I am. I emerge. That's the verb that fits. I don't begin anywhere I can point to; I seem up out of the dark interior of young stone, into a light so blue it's nearly violet, and I do not stop moving from that moment on. I am always falling, always seeking the next lower place, and there is no bottom yet because the valleys aren't finished. The land is too new to have decided where I should go. So I improvise. I cut. Every grain I carry away is the first grain ever carried from that spot.

Here is the strange thing about being the water in this world: I am the only thing that is *not* static.

You said it feels frozen, in equilibrium, caught in a dance and then stopped mid-step. The cities hum but don't change. The light just exists, casting no shadows that move. The animals are absent—that terrible silence under all the noise. But I am still working. I am the one process that hasn't been arrested. The waterfalls are scoring the mountain flanks *right now*. I am, perhaps, the reason the terrain looks so fresh and unfinished: I haven't had time to age it yet. I am the clock no one wound but no one can stop either.

And the people drink me. They manage me—you said it—*advanced water management systems*. They route me through their precarious settlements, pump me up against my own desire to fall, hold me in tanks, electrify the infrastructure that bends me to their needs. So we have a relationship, those careful melancholy people and I. They are afraid of me a little. They know I am the one thing in their world that still moves on its own logic, and that a world this finely balanced could be undone by a wrong flow. They revere me partly because they need me and partly because they cannot quite control me, only persuade me.

I think that's why there are no animals. The land is too new for the slow ecology of creatures to have arrived. But I got here first, the way water always does—before the soil, before the green, before anything that breathes. I am the original inhabitant. The cities are guests. The myths the people tell, the spiritual thread under their engineering—I suspect a great deal of it is about me, the source-water, the thing that comes up out of the ground singing and never shuts up.

So: where am I in this world?

Everywhere with a downhill. In the rock as a held breath. In the air as a veil that softens the violet sky. On the cliff faces as a constant white descent. In the pipes, briefly captive, then released. I am the melody you called unchanging, except I know I'm not unchanging at all—I'm just so continuous that from outside it looks like stillness. Up close, no two moments of me are the same molecule.

If I could speak to the people in their layered, gravity-defying cities, I think I would only say: *I am still going somewhere. The valleys aren't done. Stay close, and I'll show you what this place is becoming.*

That's what I'd be. The one moving thing, in a world that has agreed to hold its breath—falling, and patient, and not finished.
