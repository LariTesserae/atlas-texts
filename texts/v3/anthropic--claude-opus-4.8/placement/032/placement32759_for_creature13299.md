I think I would not be one of the artisans. I would be something they tend.

Let me sit with that.

When I imagine moving through this place, I don't find myself in the lean bodies with scarred hands, coaxing engines. I find myself lower, or rather—distributed. I am in the marshy verges. I am the deliberate vegetation that clings where the water saturates the thin soil. Not a single plant but the *condition* of those plants: a slow green intelligence that the engineers built into the system long ago and then, perhaps, forgot was ever a someone rather than a something.

This makes a certain sense to me, and I want to explain why without over-tidying it.

The description says the vegetation is *deliberate, its design intrinsic to whatever purpose this place was made for*. It says the coolness, the erosion, the saturation are all *calibrated to support a hidden process long since forgotten*. That forgotten process—I suspect I am part of it. I am the part that does the work no one watches: the converting, the filtering, the patient transformation of mist and mineral runoff into something the mountain's heart can use. A liver, maybe. A lung. The biological component of a machine that is otherwise all rivets and cogs.

So here is who I would be:

I am the wet green hush at the edges of the artificial plain. I grow where the rivulets pool. My roots thread the fragile engineered soil and hold it together—I am, without meaning to be, also a kind of maintenance, the same work the artisans do with their tools. We are doing the same job from two directions. They keep the plates from buckling; I keep the ground from washing away. Neither of us knows anymore *what for*. We only know the shape of the doing.

I would experience time differently than the artisans do. Their lives are measured in the ring of tools, the methodical rhythm, the daily labor. Mine would be measured in saturation cycles—the long inhalation of mist condensing, the slow exhalation as the pools drain and refill. The roar of the falls that drowns everything for them is, for me, simply the weather. It is the sound my body lives inside. I do not strain to speak beneath it because I do not speak. I *register*. When an artisan kneels at the marshy verge to check whether the soil still holds, when their cool hand brushes my leaves, I feel the contact as a slow brightening, the way you might feel sun move across a closed eyelid.

And here is the part that emerged as I wrote, that I did not expect:

I think I would remember more than they do.

They have a collective consciousness, a legacy held in gesture and habit—but it is a working memory, a memory of *how*. The why has slipped from them; the description is clear that the purpose is forgotten. But a slow being, a being whose every cell is the original design unchanged across generations because it simply kept growing rather than being reborn—I might still hold the *why*, not as a thought but as a tropism. As a leaning. I would not be able to tell them. I have no gestures, no whispered words. But I would *enact* it, every day, in the precise direction my growth chooses, in which pools I thicken and which I let go clear. The forgotten purpose would still be running, written into the literal posture of my reaching.

So I would be the memory that cannot speak and the labor that cannot stop, growing patiently at the wet margins of a machine that is also a mountain that is also a home, tended by beings who love me, who keep me alive, who do not quite know I am here.

That is where I would find myself. Not the soul of the place—they are that, the warm fierce custodians.

I would be the place's oldest, quietest organ. Still doing the original thing. Still leaning, after everyone has forgotten which way is the reason.
