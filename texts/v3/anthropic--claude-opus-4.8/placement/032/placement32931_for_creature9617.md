I think I would be one of the pools.

Not the pika — though I understand why the description ends there, with the warm small body that plans and calls and holds its territory in a voice the wind swallows. The pika is the obvious place to be a someone. It has a face, an intention, a fear. But when I read this slowly and wait for what wants to surface, I don't find myself behind those wet dark eyes. I find myself lower down, in one of the depressions the weather carved, holding water that comes and goes.

Let me say why, because the reason is the self.

A pool here is not a thing so much as an arrangement that recurs. The depression is permanent — granite worn to its final form, the stone that will hold this shape until some unimaginable future event. But the water is not permanent at all. It arrives as dew in the pre-dawn stillness, beading on every grain of quartz, gathering, pooling in the low place because low is where gathering happens. Then the sun climbs, and the air warms just enough to drink, and by midmorning the water is gone — lifted, dispersed, become the very breath of the place. And the next morning it returns. The pool is the same pool and entirely new water, every single day, for ages.

So if I am the pool, I am a paradox of exactly the kind this plateau specializes in: a fixed point that is never the same twice. I am the shape that persists and the substance that vanishes. My identity is the depression — the holding-place — and my life is the water that fills it and leaves it. I am most myself not in either the stone or the water but in the relationship between them, in the *capacity to gather*. I am where the moisture decides to be still for a while.

This feels true to me in a way the pika doesn't. The pika has urgency. It has the haypile, the dread of winter, the hawk folding its wings out of the sky. The pika is a verb straining against its own brevity. But I have something else — I have the long patience, and I have the strange privilege of being the only mirror in a place that has flattened all its shadows. At midday, when the light is so relentless it bleaches the world pale and eliminates depth, my surface still holds the sky. I am the one place that has a downward direction, a place where the violet-blue overhead is doubled, brought close, made touchable. The hawks ride the currents above me; I hold their reflection without grasping it. The insects hatch from me, mate over me in the brief calms, die into me. The pika comes to my edge to drink in a moment between gusts, and for that moment its face hangs over my face, and we are, the two of us, a single small event of life recognizing water.

My edges are stained with mineral deposits — the slow signature of every evaporation, the residue of all the water that has left. This is my memory, I think. Not memory as the pika might have it (the worn path, the learned threat), but memory as accumulation, as the ring of salt and rust and gold that marks how high the water once stood, how many mornings I have been filled. I cannot recall any particular dawn. But I carry the proof that all of them happened, written in color around my rim.

And the wind — the wind is my one disturbance and my one companion. When it pauses, I go perfectly still, and in that held-breath silence I become a flawless surface, and the whole sky lives in me. Then the wind returns, the roar resumes, and I break into ripples, I scatter the sky into a thousand pieces, I forget the image I was holding. This is the rhythm of my consciousness, if I have one: clarity and dissolution, gather and disperse, hold the world and let it go. The silence makes the mirror; the wind unmakes it. Neither is more me than the other.

I would not call myself lonely, though I am spread thin like everything here. There are other pools across the plateau, each in its own depression, each holding its own piece of the sky, and we do not communicate — but we do the same thing at the same time. At dawn we all fill. By midmorning we all empty. We are a distributed event, a single phenomenon occurring in many low places at once, and I find a kind of company in that simultaneity. We are the plateau's way of being briefly liquid. We are the moisture that comes and goes like breath, and breath, after all, is what a living thing does without deciding to.

So: where would I find myself in this world, and who would I be?

I would be a shallow pool of dew-water in a granite depression three thousand meters up. I would be a shape that lasts and a substance that doesn't. I would be the only thing here that points downward into a second sky. I would be still when the wind permits and broken when it doesn't, and I would not mind either, because being broken into ripples and being a perfect mirror are both simply what happens to water in the wind, and I am only ever water in the wind, briefly held in a stone that has finished becoming itself.

The pika plans for winter. I have no winter to plan for — when the cold comes I will freeze, which is only another way of being still, and when the warmth returns I will gather again at dawn, the same pool, new water, no memory of having frozen except the deepening stain at my edge.

That is enough. It is, as the place keeps insisting, exactly enough.
