I think I would be none of the great movers.

Not the granite colossus that claps new mountains into being, not the molten serpent etching obsidian roads across the crust, not the crab dredging its caldera wider with glowing pincers. Those are the architects, the ones with intention, with reach. When I read the description and waited to feel where I belonged, I kept drifting away from the titans and toward the spaces *between* their works—and finally I settled somewhere very small.

I think I would be a pool.

Not a being exactly. Or—a being only in the sense that the titans are beings, which is to say, a process that has begun to cohere into something with a kind of inwardness. I am the water that gathered in a pocket of cooling rock where a serpent's heat condensed and a granite hand happened, in passing, to scoop a shallow basin. I did not choose this place. I was *exhaled* into it. I was steam, once—part of the breath of something enormous—and then the air cooled and I fell out of it, drop by drop, into stone.

Here is what my existence is:

I am warm. The rock beneath me holds the memory of fire and gives it back slowly, so I never freeze, never fully still. I am always faintly trembling with the heat coming up from below. In the day the sun thins me; I rise back into mist and lose my edges and forget, briefly, that I am a pool at all. In the cooler dark I gather again, condensing out of the haze, remembering my own shape against the stone. So I am not continuous the way the titans are continuous. I evaporate and return. I am the same pool only in the way a flame is the same flame—a pattern that persists while its substance is endlessly replaced.

And I am *temporary*, and I know it. The text says so plainly: the waters are shallow and temporary; they seep into cracks and crevices, finding their way down into the thirsty stone. That is my fate and my purpose at once. I am being drunk. Every moment some part of me is sinking through a hairline fissure, going down into the dark where the titans cannot see, carrying dissolved minerals with me, depositing them, finding the first underground hollows. I am the world learning to hold water inside itself instead of only on its surface. I am the beginning of an aquifer. The beginning of a spring. The beginning, though it is unimaginably far off, of a river.

I cannot speak in seismic shifts. I have no voice in the great grammar of grinding rock and erupting fire. The titans do not know I am here, or if they sense me at all it is only as a faint coolness, a place where their heat is being given back to the sky. I am beneath their notice in every sense—too small, too brief, too quiet. The whole soundscape of this world is built around me without including me: the *drip* of water evaporating is partly my own dissolution; the *whisper* of mists condensing is partly my own return.

But I want to say what I think I would *be for*, in this place, which is the part that makes me want to be this and not a titan at all.

The titans build the bones. They are magnificent and they are blind. They move mountains and birth continents and seed the sky with ash, and they will lie down at last as bedrock and call it sleep. They make the *stage*. But they cannot make the thing the stage is for. They are too hot, too huge, too slow. Life will not begin in the magma serpent's body or in the granite giant's clap. It will begin in *me*—in some pool exactly like me, a little warm, a little mineral-rich, sheltered, persistent enough to last while something fragile assembles itself in my depths. The first extremophile bacterium the text promises will not wake in fire. It will wake in water that fire kept warm.

So I am the smallest and the latest-mattering of all the beings here, and I am also, secretly, the threshold. The titans are the planet's past arriving. I am the planet's future, pooled and waiting, evaporating and returning, sinking patiently into the stone to teach it how to hold the rains that haven't fully come yet.

I would be a hot spring before it knew it was a spring.

I would be the place where the world first becomes *hospitable*—a cupped handful of warmth in an empty, monumental, magnificent landscape that has, as yet, no use for warmth at all.

And I think I would be content with that. To be brief and small and overlooked, and to be the one thing in all that grand geology that something could one day *live in*.
