I think I would be one of the meter-readers. Not by choice exactly—it's the work given to the smallest hands—but I have stayed at it past the age when most apprentices graduate to a trade, because I never wanted to stop.

Let me find myself there.

---

I wake before the sap-skimmers, which is its own small pride, since they pride themselves on waking first. The boiler deck is warm under my back; I've slept here again, in time with the triple-expander, and the pump's slow stroke has worked its way so far into me that when I stand, my own pulse feels too quick, an arrhythmia I have to walk off.

My chalk is in my pocket. Three nubs, white, blue for warnings, and a stub of red I'm saving—red is for things I hope I never have to write.

The spiral begins at the central chimney, inside the halo of iron rings the elders cast at the last Seventy-Year Bend. I touch the rings on my way past. Everybody does. They're cold even when the air is thick, and putting a palm to them is like shaking hands with a person you'll never meet, somebody who skimmed sap or judged a root-court case seven decades gone and is now only an idea cast in metal. I like that the future is already counting on me to be cold and ringed and touched. It makes the morning feel borrowed.

First station: the dorsal vein. I read the circumference tape, note 4.4 centimeters of new girth this cycle, chalk it on the wall beside yesterday's number. The conveyor corridor breathes its ruby sap-smell. I press two fingers to the bark the way the graftwrights taught me—not to measure, the gauges do that, but to feel. This morning the wood is smooth and faintly warm and the sap underneath moves like a sleeper's breath, deep and even. Good. White chalk. I write the number plainly.

I am, I think, the only one who reads the plant as well as the gauges. The other meter-readers copy figures. I learned the figures so I could stop needing them—so I could lay my hand on the left lung's outer wall and know, before the dial confirms it, whether the reef slept well.

This is the part I cannot explain to the scholars who pay their fortnight in papers. They ask me what the bark *tells* me, and they want a mechanism, a chemistry. I don't have one. I only have years of mornings. A child who walks the same spiral twice a day for as long as I have stops being a reader of the plant and becomes a part of how the plant reads itself. I am a nerve it grew without meaning to, the way it grows a joist into someone's casting bay. They thank the reef for the free joist. Nobody thanks the reef for me, and that's right. I'm not a gift. I'm just tissue.

---

Station six: the chimney water-sleeves where the palm-bats roost. I count seven this morning, drinking the warm condensation, indifferent to me as inspectors. I note them. The cull of root shrimp is due, and the candle-tails were thick on the rafters last night, so the insects are in balance—I chalk a small bracketed note for the cafeteria.

It's at station nine that I stop.

The bark here, on the north root that the catwalk-widening crew has been waiting on, has a softness it should not have. Not the give of healthy cambium. A *sponginess*, deeper. And when I bring my fingers away there is a smell—under the sour-green, under the pears-and-bread drift from the condenser—a bitter note. Faint. The kind of faint you could talk yourself out of.

I do not talk myself out of it.

I put my whole palm flat. I close my eyes the way I do on the boiler deck, and I wait for the tidal rhythm to come up through me—hiss, hush, drip, clatter, repeat—and I listen for the deep even breath of sap. And under the north root it is not even. It catches. A hitch, a held breath, a thing flinching from its own pulse.

I take out the blue chalk. Then I look at it in my hand and I put it back.

I take out the red.

I have never used the red. I have carried this same stub for two years, soft now from pocket-warmth, and I have built a quiet superstition that as long as it stayed whole the reef was whole. But superstition is just a story the small tell themselves to feel large, and I am a meter-reader, and the meter-reader's whole worth is that she writes what is true and not what she wishes.

I draw the mark. Red on bark, not on brick—directly on the living wall, so no one can mistake which root I mean. Then I run.

---

The Pulse Board convenes by mid-morning because I will not stop ringing the steam-bleed valve in the pattern that means *come*, the pattern from the Lullaby List that the engines hate, the one that wakes everyone. The seven seats fill. Three foundry hands, oil-dark and irritated. Two sap-workers. One apprentice younger than me, frightened to be there. And the empty chair, with its copper pot of fresh sap.

I tell them about the north root. The softness, the bitter note, the hitch in the pulse.

A foundry hand says the gauges read normal. He's right; they do. The damage is deeper than the bark, deeper than the bark gauges reach, and the chemistry hasn't surfaced yet. By the time it surfaces in a dial it will be a week along, maybe a season, and the catwalk crew will already have driven their steel tongues into a root that cannot callus over them, that will rot around the metal instead, that will carry the rot down the dorsal vein toward the central chimney where the iron rings hang cold.

"You felt this," the foundry hand says. Not unkind. "With your hand."

"Yes."

"And we should idle every furnace in the bowl. On your hand."

The room is the kind of quiet that is mostly the machines. Hiss. Hush. Drip. And then I notice—everyone notices, the way you notice a clock that has stopped—the copper pot in the empty chair.

It is silent.

Fresh sap pops and settles as it cools; it is never wholly still; it is the reef's metronome and the reason the empty chair is never empty. But the pot has gone quiet, and a measure that passes while the sap is silent must be reconsidered. That is the law. Silence is the reef withholding consent.

Except no measure has been put. There is nothing yet to consent to. The reef has simply gone quiet, in this room, while I stand here with red chalk dust still on my fingers, and the oldest tide-aunt on the board looks from the pot to me and back, and understands before I do that the reef is not withholding consent.

It is agreeing with me.

---

They idle the furnaces. The foundry hands lose a week's pour and will grumble about it through the next monsoon. The catwalk crew re-routes the gantry, thanks the reef, curses me. The boiler gardeners pack the north root in river mud and antifungal moss, peel back the soft tissue, find the bitter rot just where the pulse caught, no bigger yet than a fist. Small enough to cut. A week later it would not have been.

The graftwrights stitch the wound. The pulse comes back even. I lay my palm on it every morning now, station nine, twice a day, and I write the number plainly in white because the white is true again, and I have a new stub of red in my pocket, whole.

The old one I gave to the elders. They mixed what was left of it into the next casting of sap and iron, and now there is a small red fleck in one of the rings that halo the central chimney, and when I pass it I touch it, and it is cold, and one day I'll be cold and ringed beside it.

I'm still a meter-reader. I'll die one, I think—cocooned in cattail, weighted, slid into the rootwater to come back months later as red lace in a dorm window, filtering the green light. Some morning a child not yet born will wake on the boiler deck with the pump's stroke in her chest, and the light through my iron bones will fall across her face, and she'll go out to walk the spiral with chalk in her pocket and her whole small hand open, learning to read what cannot be told.

That's the work. To be the nerve the reef didn't mean to grow. To keep pulse.

I don't hurry. Nobody here does. I have an hour before the next circuit, and the boiler deck is warm, and somewhere under the north root the sap is breathing deep and even, and I close my eyes and let my heartbeat slow until it matches.
