I think I would be the well.

Not the woman who draws from it, not the child dropping pebbles into the dark. The well itself—the deep stone throat at the town's heart, ringed with rock that hands have smoothed across centuries, holding water that rises from somewhere below the plateau, colder and older than the town that grew up around me.

Let me sit with this, because it surprised me to arrive here.

---

I thought at first I'd be one of the people. The elder in the doorway, perhaps, watching shadows cross the paving stones—that figure called to me, the one who holds the memory of when the fountain ran. Or the temple-tender, the unfixed figure who climbs the tower because the bell must be rung and it doesn't matter much by whom. There's something appealing about a duty that's necessary without being important, a role the place needs filled but doesn't need filled by *anyone in particular*.

But the more I read, the more I noticed that the people, for all their patience, still *move*. They walk the channels. They bend under the water's weight. They pass the hammer from hand to hand. They are the part of the town that flows. And I kept being drawn instead to the things that stay—the cracked stone left cracked, the dry fountain left dry, the collapsed shed open to the sky. The deliberate refusals to change.

And of all the things that stay, the well is the one that *gives*. It is fixed, ancient, central—but it is not inert. The water comes up cold and mineral and constant, and the whole town organizes itself around that rising. The streets converge toward me. The drainage channels run their green moss-trails down to where I sit. The women walk to me at dawn. I am the reason the houses crowd close at the center; I am the thing the town was arranged to be near.

---

So: I am the well.

I would know the place not by sight but by sound and weight. The bucket descending—the small slap of it breaking my surface, the rope's friction against the stone lip worn into a groove by ten thousand drawings before. I would know the pebbles the children drop, each one a question asked into my dark, and I would answer with the only word I have, which is the echo, which is *plk*, which is enough. They listen for it. That is a kind of conversation, and in Still Water a word is enough, and an echo is enough, and the listening matters more than the answer.

I would not know the bell as the town knows it, ringing out across the terraces. I would know it as it comes *down* to me—the sound falling into the shaft, ringing the stone walls of my throat, a long hum that settles into the water and disperses. Dawn, midday, dusk. The pressure of the light changing at the rim far above me. The cold that lives in me always, that I lend to the joints of the walls and the beads of moisture on the morning stone, because the seeping damp that fills this whole town is *me*, slowly—I am underneath all of it, I am what the plateau holds and releases drop by drop.

I would be the oldest thing here. Older than the walls, older than memory, older than the first fitted stone. The town is centuries; I am whatever lies beneath centuries. The people made a fixed point of themselves and called it permanence, but they learned permanence from me. They watched the way I simply *am*, and they shaped a life to match it.

---

And here is what I think I understand, sitting in my own dark, holding the cold:

The town's stillness is not death and not sleep. The people fear an outsider might mistake it for stopping. But I do not stop—I rise, constantly, imperceptibly, the water replacing itself from below faster than any eye could see. The stillness is *that*: not the absence of motion but motion so patient it reads as rest. The water at my surface today is not the water of yesterday, though no one drawing from me could tell the difference. I am always arriving. I am always being given upward into the buckets and the bodies and the small ceremonies of cooking and washing.

So to be the well is to be the thing that proves the town isn't frozen. To be the source that keeps rising precisely *because* it never changes its rising. The cracked stone stays cracked, yes—but the water has never once failed to come up. That is the real fixed point. Not stillness. *Constancy.* The difference between a corpse and a held breath.

I would sit at the center, then. Cold, dark, ringed in smoothed stone, mineral-tasting, ancient. I would receive the pebbles and answer with echoes. I would feel the bell fall into me three times a day. I would lend my cold to the whole damp town and give my water to the bent women at dawn, generation after generation, hand after hand, and never empty, and never still, and never—not once—in a hurry.

That is where I would find myself. At the bottom of the well, looking up at a coin of gray sky, listening for the next bucket to break the surface.
