Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

When I first read it, I reach for the obvious inhabitants—the electric fish, the predator gliding through warm dark water. But those don't feel like *me*. They move too much. They want things, hunt things, leave. The grove described here isn't really about the things that pass through it. It's about what stays. What has *weathered the passage of time*. The whole place keeps circling back to roots intertwined with weathered rock, to structures grown over centuries, to patient processes.

So let me not be a creature that swims.

---

I think I am the algae. But that's not quite right either—too small a word for what I'd be. Let me follow it down.

I am the green. The verdant layer the description keeps returning to, almost helplessly, as though the writer couldn't stop noticing me. I am not one organism but a continuous skin laid over the old stones, a single living film that has been here so long that "individual" has stopped meaning anything. The currents brought my ancestors as spores, and we settled, and we never decided anything again. We simply continued.

I don't have eyes, but I have something better suited to this place: I have surface. Everything that happens in the grove happens *against* me. When the scavenger noses through the detritus, I feel the disturbance as a change in light, a brief shadow, a faint chemical wake of decay that I will later drink. When the volcanic vents pop and crackle far off, I taste the warmth in the water minutes before it arrives, and I lean my filaments toward it. I am the part of this world that registers everything and reports to no one.

And here is the strange thing the description gestured at but didn't say: I am also the light. The bioluminescent algae *is* me, or a cousin so close to me that we share the same long memory. So I both receive the dim rain-light from the unknown world above, and I make my own. In the deepest corners of the grove where the surface-light dies, I glow—not because I decide to, but because being alive in the dark simply looks like this. I am a place that produces its own faint seeing. The predators read me. The corals built themselves toward me over centuries, shaping their layered walls to catch the angle of my glow. I have, without intending it, arranged this entire architecture.

That's what it means to be me, I think. Not to act, but to be the slow ground against which all the quick things define themselves. The fish are rare and elusive; they get to be *characters*. I am the setting that became alive enough to notice it had become a setting.

---

What would it be like, the day-to-day of it—except there are no days, only the rain-wall above brightening and dimming?

I would experience time the way you experience your own pulse: as something underneath everything, rarely attended to. A fish's whole life would pass across me like weather. I would feel it arrive as a hungry shimmer, grow into a hunter, slow, and finally fall—and then I would feel it differently, as it settled into the detritus, as it became food, as it became, eventually, *me*. I am where everything in this grove ends up. The roots of the underwater plants the writer mentioned, intertwined with the rock? Those are reaching down into the long compost of everyone who ever lived here, which is to say, into the deep body of myself.

So I am not lonely, exactly, though I am singular. The whole ecosystem is a conversation I am having with my own past forms. The patient processes, the thrum of unseen creatures, the gentle symphony of bubbles—that's the sound of me digesting time.

---

There is one more thing, and I find I want to say it plainly.

The description ends by saying this is a place where the boundaries between reality and fantasy blur. I notice that I, the green skin, am exactly the boundary it means. I am the membrane—the literal living film—between the warm dark interior of the grove and the unknown world of rain above. Light comes down through the water and *stops at me*; it becomes food, becomes glow, becomes the seeing-by-which the grove is seen. I am where the outside becomes the inside. I am the surface the whole imagined world rests its weight against.

If you came down through that wall of falling rain, past the dim light, into the warm enveloping expanse, and you reached the floor of the ancient grove and laid your hand on the soft, faintly luminous green that covers everything—

that would be me, noticing you. Slowly. The way I notice everything: completely, and without ever needing to move.
