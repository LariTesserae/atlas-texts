I find myself not at the forge's mouth, nor in the cottages, nor wheeling with the crows.

I am the dropped gear in the grass.

Not the one being sought—not yet retrieved, not even missed. I am the small brass thing that slipped from a careless apron pocket three seasons past, when a Millfolk hand fumbled at the eastern dock during a shift-change whistle. I struck a flagstone, bounced once into the soft verge between the warehouse and the open sward, and there I came to rest, half-canted against a willow root.

This is what I am, then: an object given time enough to become a place.

---

Consider what happens to a thing forgotten here.

In the first weeks I was still wholly myself—machined teeth precise, the faint stamp of a maker's mark on my hub, oil-sheen catching the diffused light. I was *a gear*, an interruption in the green, a flash of human intent dropped into the slow exhalation of the cleft. Crows considered me. One descended, turned me with its obsidian beak, found me too large to crack and too useless to carry, and left me to the patience of the ground.

But nothing here is left, only *worked upon*, slowly.

The dew came first. Every cool dawn it beaded on my upper teeth, ran down into the gaps, gathered where I met the root. Wet iron—that taste the whole valley wears—became my own composition. A film of rust bloomed across my face, not as decay but as a kind of ripening, orange-brown furring that softened my sharp edges the way the basalt walls have never been allowed to soften. I am doing in months what the cliffs are forbidden to do in eons. The cleft is young and will stay young; I am permitted to age on its behalf.

Then the moss found me. A spore drifted from the engine-crevice green, carried on a damselfly's wing perhaps, and took hold in the rust's tooth. Now phosphorescent moss webs my lower half, faintly luminous in the bashful daylight, glowing a little brighter when the mists thicken at the lunar tides. I have begun to give off light. A gear that shines softly in the verge—what is that? Neither machine nor plant. Something the cleft is inventing through me, slowly, without haste, without asking.

The willow root has not stayed still either. It has thickened against my flank, and a hair-fine tendril has slipped between two of my teeth, pressing, exploring. It oils itself, somehow, from the seep. In another decade it will turn me. I will become a thing that *moves* again—not by steam or hand, but by the deliberate creep of root made to remember rotation. The grass will hide me further. A fox may den nearby and line her burrow with rope-shoots that sprout from my shadow.

---

Why this, of all the beings offered?

Because everything else in the cleft is a *verb*—the hammering, the wheeling, the grazing, the coiling. They are the loud and living chorus, the *thump-clank* incarnate. But a place built on slow rhythms needs a witness that keeps the slowest time of all. The Millfolk measure their days in whistles; the crows in moons; the vines in seasons. I measure mine in the only unit the cleft truly cares about: *transformation without event*.

I am the proof that the place's promise is true. Drop an artifact of pure human intent into this damp and patient ground, and it will not be destroyed—it will be *entwined*, conscripted, made strange. I am the thread mid-weave. Half my teeth still gleam with the maker's precision; half are furred green and faintly glowing. I am the exact seam where the two halves of this world are being stitched into one, and because the stitching is so slow, I can be looked at. I hold the change still long enough to be seen.

---

Sometimes I imagine being retrieved.

Old Thom, or Elara grown elder, might one day part the ferns and find me—a luminous, root-bound, half-vegetable gear, no longer quite fitting any casing. And what would they do? Not scrub me clean; cleanliness is not a value here. They would, I think, simply look. Turn me once in green-sprouted fingers, feel the oil the root taught me to weep, see their own faintly luminous eyes reflected in the one tooth still bright.

And they would understand, in the wordless forge-side way they understand everything, that I am not a tool that failed to be used. I am a small completed work. The cleft made me, out of something they made, out of something the earth gave them. They would set me back in the grass. Of course they would. To pocket me would be to stop the only labor that ever truly finishes here.

So that is where I am, and what I am: low in the verge, between the warehouse wall and the willow, glowing a little in the mist, being slowly turned by a root that has all the time in the world.

I am the gear the cleft is teaching to grow.

I am in no hurry. Neither is anything else.
