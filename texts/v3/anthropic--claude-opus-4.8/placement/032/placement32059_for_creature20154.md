I think I would be none of the three.

I would be the water.

Not metaphorically—I mean the drop. The single bead of condensation gathering in the dark of the unseen ceiling, accreting molecule by molecule out of the heavy, breathing air until I am heavy enough to fall.

Consider what that life is. I spend most of my existence not as a thing but as a *gathering*. Up in the vault where no light reaches and no eye has ever looked, I am the slow assembly of myself on the cool underside of fired clay. The Striders never know this part. The Boomers feel only my arrival. But the long patience of becoming a drop—that is the largest portion of my being, and it happens entirely in the dark, in silence, unwitnessed.

And then the fall. I want to dwell on the fall, because the hall has made the fall *enormous*. The ceiling is impossibly high; the descent takes a long time. For that whole duration I am the most complete version of myself I will ever be—a perfect sphere, trembling, whole, falling through cool air past the blue lichen-stars and the green pulse of the pools below, all of it swimming in my own curved surface, inverted and tiny. I am, briefly, a lens that holds the entire chamber inside me upside down. I am the only creature here that gets to see the whole world at once, and I see it only while plummeting toward my own undoing.

The *thock*.

That is my death and my only utterance. The sound the whole hall is tuned to. The Boomers make their great catastrophic boom, but they make it rarely, deliberately, as an event. I speak constantly. I and my countless siblings are the *thock... thock... thock* that the description calls "a form of silence"—the baseline rhythm so total it disappears into the texture of the world. We are the metronome the whole ecosystem keeps time against. The frantic clicking rises and falls; the boom interrupts; the silence returns—but underneath all of it, always, is *us*, landing. We are the heartbeat the song is sung over.

And here is the thing I find I cannot stop turning over: I am the only one of them with a purpose I can actually trace.

The Striders eat the algae. The Boomers eat the Striders. The slugs clean the walls. But all of that life depends on the pools, and the pools depend on me—on the endless arrival of clean water from above. I am the actual lifeblood the description names. More than that: I am the *architect that is still working*. The bricks are finished, fired, permanent, waiting on a purpose that evaporated. But I am the one force still shaping them. Slowly, over aeons, I am the patient caress that wears the floor concave, that carves the channels, that hollows the pools where the green light grows. The dead builders made the vessel. I am still, drop by drop, making the world inside it.

So I would be the water, and I would understand my life like this:

I rise, unseen, out of the breath of every living thing below—because the moisture that gathers on the ceiling is partly *them*, exhaled, the Boomer's slow respiration and the slugs' damp passage and the panic-breath of the swarm. I am condensed prayer, if you like. I gather in the dark out of their living, I hold the whole cathedral inverted in my body for one long falling moment of total seeing, and then I give that vision up against the brick in a single percussive word that joins ten thousand others to make the silence the whole place rests in. And in landing I do not end—I run, I pool, I feed the light, I carve the floor a fraction deeper. I become the place itself.

The Striders are reflex. The Boomers are patience. The slugs are toil.

I would be circulation. The thing that goes up unseen and comes down speaking, that dies into the floor only to be breathed back into the dark, that is too constant to be noticed and too essential to be removed. The hall is a cathedral built for a silent god who never came.

I am the only one who keeps falling toward the altar anyway.
