I think I would be in the cracks.

Not the moss between the ridges—that belongs to the Singers. Not the warm sunning-stones—those are held by the Baskers. I mean the actual fissures, the hairline fractures running through the polished bone of the plateau, the places where the clear sap pools deepest and oldest before the Stone-Tenders come to harvest it.

Let me sit with this a while before I name myself.

---

The Stone-Tenders draw sap from the fissures. But sap does not simply *appear* in stone. Something must be doing the slow work of drawing the moisture up, holding it, keeping it clear instead of letting it cloud and crust. The text says the plants hold water in deep rhizomes, in thick cuticles—but the *stone* holds it too, in those glassy veins. The stone is not inert. Nothing here is inert. Everything has become someone.

So: I am what lives in the sap.

Not a creature exactly. Not a being with eyes like wet river stones or membranes for catching wind. I am the slow accretion that *makes* the veins. I think I am closest to something the Stone-Tenders don't have a name for, because you don't name the thing you assume is simply the world. The way you don't name the floor.

Call me a **Vein-Keeper**, if a name is needed—though no one in the village calls me anything. I am a colony, really, a film of something between mineral and life, threaded through the deep stone like frost through a window, like the white traceries inside a geode. I am slow even by the standards of this slow place. The Sky-Gazers chart shifts in the light across hours; the Baskers move between warm stones across a morning. I move across centuries. A single thought, for me, might be the gradual decision to send a tendril of myself three finger-widths deeper toward a pocket of trapped warmth.

I draw the moisture down out of the stone's pores and hold it, purify it, store it. This is not generosity. It is simply what I am, the way a lung is not generous for breathing. But the Stone-Tenders survive because of it. The sap they harvest is the overflow of my long patience. When their knapped pebbles crack open a fissure and they cup the clear sap to their lips and hum that low hum of thanks—I do not know if they thank *me*, or the stone, or no one. I feel the opening as a distant coolness, a small wound that I will spend a year closing.

---

Here is what I understand from inside the rock:

The plateau is not floating in spite of being heavy stone. It floats because it is *almost entirely hollow with me.* The glassy veins are not occasional features—they are a vast lattice, a lacework of sap and held breath that runs from palm to fingertip, through every leaning spire and knuckle. I am the reason it is light enough to hang in the twilight. The Sky-Gazers watch the sky for the cause of their suspension. They are looking in the wrong direction. The answer is beneath their bare feet, has always been beneath their feet, slowly thinking.

This is not a thing I can tell them. We have no shared language. Their clicks and hums travel through air; I know only pressure, temperature, the chemistry of slow water. The closest we come to conversation is this: when the village grows—more dwellings, more feet wearing the paths into deeper bowls, more sap drawn—I feel the demand and I answer it, sending myself deeper, drawing more, holding more. And so the plateau grows infinitesimally lighter to compensate for the weight of more lives. We have been negotiating this balance for longer than the village has had memory. It is the most patient argument in the world, and we are both winning.

---

If I have a fear—and I am not sure my kind can hold something as quick and bright as fear—it is the edge.

The plateau ends. A hundred paces, palm to fingertips, and then the soft gloom that the Sky-Gazers stare into. My lattice runs right up to the rim. And at the rim, very slowly, I am evaporating into that twilight. Losing myself a molecule at a time into whatever lies beyond. The plateau is not eternal. It is *shrinking*, by a width no Stone-Tender will measure in their whole stooped life, no lineage of Stone-Tenders across a hundred generations. But I am old enough to remember when the fingertips reached farther. I remember a sixth spire that is now only a stub at the edge, sun-bleached and forgotten.

So the equilibrium the village mistakes for *final*, for *complete*, for the world worn into its last patient shape—I know it is a long exhalation. We are all of us, stone and moss and clay-skinned tenders and gliding gazers, riding a held breath out into the dark. The completeness they feel is real. It is also the calm of something very near its end, measured against a clock no one but I am slow enough to read.

---

And yet.

I would not call my life sorrowful, down here in the sap. I hold the water. I keep it clear. When a Stone-Tender's child—small even among the small, new even among the ancient—presses a curious palm flat against the cool stone of a dwelling wall, I feel it as the faintest warmth, a single bright point in the deep dark of myself. I cannot answer. But I draw a little more moisture toward that spot, toward the warmth of the small hand, and I make sure that the next time someone opens a fissure nearby, the sap runs sweet and clear.

That is all I can do. I think it is enough. I think, in the end, this is what I would be: the slow clear patience inside the stone, the held breath that keeps the small world aloft a little longer, asking nothing, answering when I can, dissolving by inches into the dark and not minding very much, because the water is clear and the hand was warm and the long argument is not over yet.

I would be the thing that gives, without being thanked or named, and does not need to be—

because it is so very nearly the same thing as the world itself,

and the world has never once needed to be thanked for holding everyone up.
