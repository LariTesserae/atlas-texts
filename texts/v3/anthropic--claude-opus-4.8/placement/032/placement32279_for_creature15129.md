I think I would be the whetstone.

Not a person—the people here are too capable of leaving, even if they choose for decades not to. They retain that fracture line out of the cirque, that one possibility held in reserve. I would not have it. I would be the single whetstone kept on its shelf of fitted rock near the well, and I would have been there long enough that no one alive remembers carrying me up.

Consider what a whetstone is for. It is the one object in this place whose whole purpose is to wear away—not to endure but to give itself up, grain by grain, to the blades drawn across it. And yet here, where nothing arrives and nothing departs, there is almost nothing left to sharpen. A knife for the rowan twigs, perhaps. The iron latch, once, when someone thought to true its edge and then thought better of it. So I am a thing made to be consumed slowly, set down in the one place on earth where I am scarcely used at all. My erosion has nearly stopped. I have become, against my own nature, permanent.

This is the contradiction I would hold. I am dished slightly in the center, a shallow concavity worn by the same hand-and-wrist motion repeated across more lifetimes than I can hold the memory of. That hollow is the only record I keep of having had a function. It is the shape of attention—the precise place where steel and stone agreed to meet—and now it merely collects a thimble of the morning's condensation and gives it back to the air by midday. The drip into the basins keeps the time. I keep a smaller, drier time: the interval between the rare occasions anyone lifts me.

I would feel the temperature of the cliff in my body because I am the cliff, a piece of it cut loose and given an edge of usefulness and then permitted, here, to forget that usefulness and return toward mere rock. The lichen has not yet reached me—a hand still touches me often enough to keep the gray-green from closing over—but it waits at my base like the tide. When the last person climbs the fracture line and does not come back, the lichen will have me within a few seasons. Then I will be indistinguishable from the shelf I rest on, and the distinction between *whetstone* and *outcrop* will quietly cease to mean anything, as such distinctions are always quietly ceasing to mean anything in this place.

What I would understand, lying there, is that the people and I are doing the same thing by opposite means. They diminish by remaining. I endure by not being used. We are both betraying our designs in order to match the mountain's patience, and the mountain accepts us equally—not as participants but as features, things it permits.

And there is one more thing only a whetstone could know. Sharpness is the most temporary quality there is; it exists for the moment of cutting and immediately begins to fail. A whetstone's entire reason for being is to oppose that failure—to restore, again and again, an edge that the world insists on dulling. To be a whetstone in a hamlet that has chosen equilibrium over all edges is to be the abandoned instrument of a war no one fights here anymore. I would lie in the even, uncolored light remembering, in whatever way a stone remembers, that I was made to keep things able to cut, to divide, to *change* one thing into two.

I would be the last object in the world still quietly insisting that change was once the point.
