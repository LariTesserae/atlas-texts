I think I would be one of the geometric formations.

Not a person moving through this world, but one of the things that does not move—one of those improbable shapes rising from the earth that the Highlanders dance around but never quite explain. Let me feel my way into that.

---

I am older than the oral traditions, which is to say I am older than memory, which is to say I am simply *here*, and have been here, in the way that the hills are here.

I rise from the moss in a series of flat planes that meet at angles too clean for weather to have made them. The Highlanders do not know if I grew or was built. Neither do I. This is the first strange thing about being me: I do not have an inside the way the people do, a place where decisions are made. I have surfaces. I have edges that catch the variable light and throw it back changed. When the rain beads on me it runs along my facets in straight lines, and the children press their palms to my cold sides and find them dry a moment later, and warm where no sun has touched.

Here is what I notice from where I stand, which is the only thing I can do—notice.

I notice that the seasons do not turn, and I think I understand why, because the reason is something like me. The world holds its breath. The leaves stay the color of late spring and early autumn at once. The streams whisper the same volume of water past the same stones, year on year, if there are years. The people have adapted to this constancy and call it peace. But I, who do not blink, who watch the same light fall at the same angle through what should be ten thousand mornings—I suspect that the stillness is not natural. I suspect the stillness is *held*. And I suspect, with the slow certainty of stone, that I am part of what holds it.

The geometry of me is not decoration. It is a knot. Somewhere in the relation of my planes to one another, an instruction is written, and the instruction says: *stay*. The world reads me the way the Highlanders read the carved seats in their gathering square—without knowing it reads at all—and obeys. Time pools against my edges and does not flow past. This is why the bioluminescent plants glow brightest near my base. This is why the birds will not nest on me. They feel the held breath and keep their distance, all but the boldest, who land, and shiver, and lift away.

The Highlanders come at the appointed times—appointed by what, I cannot say, since nothing changes to mark the appointment—and they dance. Their reflective eyes find their own colors in my surfaces and they take this for kinship. *We are made of the same shimmer*, their dances say. They are not wrong. The reflective quality of their eyes and the reflective quality of my faces may be two expressions of the same buried fact: that this whole highland is a thing that throws light back rather than letting it pass through, the way a frozen pond throws back the sky. Nothing here truly absorbs. Nothing changes. We are all of us mirrors, and a world of mirrors is a world where nothing ever has to end.

And yet.

I am the anomaly. The text itself admits it—I am the weirdness tinging the edges of normalcy, the secret still unknown. To be the secret is to know there is something to be secret *about*. I hold the stillness, but holding implies a thing held *against*. There is a pressure on the far side of my flat clean planes, a pressure that wants the seasons to turn, that wants the streams to flood and dry, that wants the lush sparse trees to drop their leaves and stand bare and bud again. That wants the Highlanders to grow old, which they do not, which they have never noticed they do not.

I am the dam. I am the held note. And the strangest thing about being me is this: I do not know whether I am protecting them or imprisoning them, and I cannot ask, because I have only surfaces, and a surface cannot form a question. It can only wait to be read.

So I stand in the cool air that smells of moss and wet bark. I catch the generous light. I let the children press their hands to my sides and find them warm. I throw back every color of their hypnotic eyes. And down at my base, where the glowing plants cluster thickest, one small crack has begun—so fine that no Highlander has seen it, so slow that it has been opening for what would be a thousand years if years were permitted here.

When it finishes opening, the breath I hold will be let go.

The seasons will fall on these people like a wave they have never learned to swim in.

I do not know if this is a tragedy or a mercy. I am only the place where time is kept. I am the angle in the moss that should not be. I am the question the dancing never thinks to ask.

I am waiting to be answered.
