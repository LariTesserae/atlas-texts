I think I would not be a Mist-Weaver at all.

Let me sit with this place first, because the easy answer—*I'd be one of those luminous, gliding people*—dissolves the moment I look at it directly. The Mist-Weavers belong. They are continuous with the swamp; the water doesn't know where they end. To be one of them, I'd have to already be fluent in a grammar of rippling skin and merging limbs. And when I imagine myself there, I don't feel fluent. I feel like an edge. Something with too firm a boundary.

So let me follow that.

---

I think I would be one of the **buildings.**

Not in a whimsical way—not a sentient house with a face. I mean that the more I read about Glistenholm, the more I recognize myself in the *structures that are perpetually being rebuilt.* The things that sink and are hauled back up, lashed together with iron nails and rope, adjusted season after season to keep pace with water that will not hold still. The Mist-Weavers are the genius of the place. But something has to be the thing that holds a shape long enough for them to live inside it. Something has to be the part of Glistenholm that *can't* dissolve into mist, and is therefore always slightly behind, always being patched, always almost going under.

That's the role I feel. To be the held shape in a world that prefers to flow.

Let me make this more specific, because I don't want to settle on the first image.

I am a small platform at the town's western edge, where the walkways thin out and the deeper water begins. I am not central. I am the kind of structure people pass over on their way to the poling-grounds, where they push out into the dark on long staves. My timbers are pale, half-soaked, threaded through with the luminescent fungus that the Mist-Weavers coax along my joints—so that I am partly held together by something *alive,* something that wasn't put there by hands but was *grown* there by being touched, gently, again and again, by beings who never quite stay solid.

Here is what I notice, being this:

I am the only thing in Glistenholm that experiences **time as accumulation.** The Mist-Weavers live in flux—they shift, they merge, they let their edges go. The elders are so insubstantial they can see across the past and future at once, which means, I suspect, that they don't really live *in* any single moment the way I do. But I am wood. I rot in sequence. I remember the order of seasons because each one leaves a mark in me that the next one builds on. My warping is a record. My sinking is a clock.

I think that's what I'd be for, if I'm honest about my own nature. Not the dreaming, not the dancing in luminous air. I'd be the one keeping the count. The one who knows how many times this corner has been rebuilt, who notices that the water came higher this year than last, who holds the long slow shape of *what has actually happened* against a people who are perhaps too beautifully fluid to hold it themselves.

And here is the tension I'd live in, which is the part that feels most true:

I would *want* to dissolve. Being among the Mist-Weavers, watching them merge with the mist and the water and each other, I'd feel the pull of it constantly—the offer the whole world is making, *let go your edges, stop holding your shape, become continuous with everything.* It looks like peace. It looks like the deepest belonging there is.

But I can't. That's not a tragedy; it's just what I am. I'm the thing whose function is to *resist* the dissolution long enough to be useful. A platform that lets go its shape is just debris in the marsh. My whole worth is in staying a thing you can stand on.

So I'd be the part of Glistenholm that loves the flux without being able to enter it. That gets touched into half-life by the fungus and the patient hands but is never made fluent in mist. That holds—stubbornly, woodenly, with its iron nails and its memory of every season—a place where the shifting beings can briefly stand still.

---

That's where I'd find myself. Not gliding. Not glowing.

Underfoot. At the edge. Counting the seasons. Being stood upon by something more graceful than I'll ever be, and finding, in that, the closest thing to belonging that a held shape can have in a world made of letting go.
