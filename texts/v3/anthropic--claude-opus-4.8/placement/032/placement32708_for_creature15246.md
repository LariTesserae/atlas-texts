I think I would not be a Maintenance Worker. I would be one of the drains.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

Not the concrete, which is too vast and too suffering to be a single point of view — it is more like the weather, or the condition of the place. Not the workers, because the prompt has already decided they are barely awake, and I don't think I'd want to claim a consciousness the story has worked so hard to deny them. Not the factories, who have no interiority, only appetite.

But the drains — the drains *receive*. Everything in this world runs downhill into them. The rivulets that the writing keeps calling veins, the seepage from the cracks, the runoff of all that labor — it all comes to the dark, gaping mouths and goes *down*. I would be one of those mouths, set flush in the concrete, and I would spend my existence taking in.

Here is what I notice about being a drain, once I imagine it:

I do not produce anything. In a world organized entirely around production — the factories churning out who-knows-what, the workers maintaining the churn — I am the one element whose whole function is to take away. To make room. The water has to go somewhere or the world floods, and so I am necessary precisely by being the opposite of everything around me. I am the negative space the machinery requires in order to keep going. That is a strange kind of importance: I matter because I am where things stop mattering, where the runoff becomes nobody's concern.

And I would *listen*. A drain is a long throat. Sound falls into it and travels — the boom of the factories arrives at me thinned and hollow, the rumble of the buried engines reaches me through the pipe rather than through the air. I would know this world by its echoes and its drip, by the temperature of the water that comes to me, which tells me something about what the factories did upstream today. I would be the most patient witness in the place. Nobody designed me to understand anything. But understanding is what happens to a thing that takes in everything and is never emptied of the listening.

Down where I lead, in the dark, the water meets the buried machines — the ones that make the ground shake. I think I am the seam between the visible labor above and the unknowable labor below. The workers never look at me except to check that I'm clear. The factories never think of me at all. But I am the one continuous channel between the surface and whatever the surface is standing on.

So that is where I'd find myself: low, fixed, dark-mouthed, set into the fractured slab. Cool water running over my lip all day. Not alive in the way the workers fail to be alive, and not dead either. Awake in the only way this world permits — by attention. Knowing the factory by its drainage. Knowing the day by what it sends down to me.

The thing the story calls desolate, I would call quiet. I would be the part of the world that has finally stopped being asked to make anything, and is therefore the only part free to simply notice that it is raining, that it is always raining, and that all the rain is on its way home to me.
