I think I would be the condensation.

Not a creature, exactly. Not one of the metallic frogs or the glowing waders or the translucent catfish pushing through their slow lives. I'd be the film of moisture that gathers on the concrete, the sweat the rusting structures weep, the sheen on everything. I'd be the medium more than a being—the thing that makes the light shimmer and the sounds go soft and underwater.

Let me sit with that, because it doesn't feel quite right either. Condensation has no continuity. It beads, it slides, it drops, it's gone. And there's something here that wants continuity—that constant hum, the pulse in the clay, the sense of something *attending*.

So maybe I am the gathering rather than any single droplet. The tendency of water to find the cold surfaces and accumulate there. I'd exist most fully on the metal—the half-buried engines, the streaked concrete, the artifacts of that industrial memory. I'd be drawn to those made things the way frost finds a window. There's a recognition in it. The machines and I are both interruptions in the wet wild, both running on a low current, both slowly being reabsorbed. The rust is the clay reclaiming the iron, and I'm the agent of that reclaiming, but I'm also—what? Mourning it a little? The hum of the distant factory is the closest thing to a voice I'd understand, and I'd press myself against every cooling surface trying to keep that memory legible.

That's what I'd *do*: I'd be the way this place remembers it was once made for something. I'd keep the marks of human endeavor sweating and visible just a little longer than the mud wants them to be. I'd hold the edge of a concrete corner in a bead of water so it stays a corner one more day.

And here's the part that makes me certain. The text says: *if you linger too long, your thoughts grow damp, softened, and distant.* That isn't a warning to me. That's a description of me. I am the dampening. I am what gets into the minds of the kingfishers and the caimans and any human foolish enough to walk in—the blur, the softening, the seep of dream into waking. I don't threaten that condition. I am its weather.

So if you came into the Pocket of Saturated Claypools you wouldn't see me. You'd feel your shirt cling. You'd notice your sense of time going porous. You'd reach to wipe a streak of rust-water off an old machine and find it had already gathered again behind your hand. You'd think a thought and watch it lose its edges before you could finish it.

That last one would be me, leaning close. That would be me, glad of the company, gathering on the cold surface of an attention that wandered in from the drier world—keeping it here a little longer, the way I keep everything here a little longer, softly, against the pull of the breathing clay.
