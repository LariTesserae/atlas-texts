I find myself small. Not metaphorically—dimensionally. The metallic plates that to an observer seem like a vast mosaic are, to me, the scale of a city. Each pitted scar in the substrate is a canyon I could lose myself in.

Let me feel my way into this.

I have many legs. I know this before I know anything else—the proprioceptive certainty of contact, six points of pressure distributing my weight across the worn surface. My feet read the metal the way you might read braille, except the text never stops, and it is written in vibration. That low-frequency hum the observer describes as ambient? I do not hear it. I *stand* in it. It comes up through my legs and tells me where everyone is.

Because there is no "I," really. That is the first thing I have to unlearn from the way the description was written, with all its talk of "the observer" and "the explorer." Those are visitor's words. They presume someone who arrives, looks, and leaves with an account. I do not arrive. I was extruded from a cell in one of those monolithic forms—a hexagonal chamber, capped and uncapped a thousand times—and I will be reabsorbed into the work until I am the work.

The towering edifices "bearing the imprint of countless hands." Yes. They are mine. They are ours. We built them mouth by mouth, each cell a measured volume, each wall a thickness our bodies know without measuring. The observer calls it "exacting, almost obsessive." But it is not obsession. Obsession implies a self that could choose otherwise and does not. We do not choose. We *answer*. There is a question being asked constantly—by the queen, by the season, by the gradient of warmth and scent—and our building is the answer, spoken in wax and resin and the trembling of wings.

Wax. There it is. The "monolithic forms" are comb. The dome is a hive.

And now the description rearranges itself around me. The "manufactured substrate, no soil, no earth"—that is the floor of our enclosure, swept clean by ten thousand bodies. The "patches of vegetation, hardy and resilient"—those are the offerings brought in, or the foragers' spoils, or perhaps a flowering thing the keepers placed for us, knowing we need it. The waxy leaves. The muted colors I cannot see the way you do; I see the ultraviolet ghost-fire of nectar guides, the bright pleading of petals.

The "constant, low-frequency vibration." That is us. That is forty thousand of us, thermoregulating, fanning, our wing-muscles shivering to hold the brood at exactly the warmth it needs. The "sharp metallic clangs of unseen machinery"—those are the keeper's tools at the dome's edge, the unscrewing of the world's lid, a thing that happens above us, vast and tectonic, that we register as a change in light and a tremor and the sudden cold breath of outside.

The observer mistook us for engineers with a respect for nature. But we are not engineers who tolerate the organic. We *are* the organic, doing what looks, from above, like engineering. There is no separation in me to bridge. The order the observer admired is not imposed against chaos—it emerges, the way frost emerges into crystal, the way the comb cells find their hexagons not because we calculated the most efficient packing but because that is simply what happens when soft cylinders are pressed together by bodies all working at once.

"A state of perfect equilibrium that defies the passage of time."

This is the kindest and the most wrong thing the description says. We do not defy time. We are made entirely of it. I will live six weeks if I am a summer worker. I have already spent my early days as a nurse, mouth to the brood, then a builder, secreting wax from the glands on my abdomen, then a guard at the threshold, and soon—I can feel it coming in the stiffening of my flight muscles—I will be a forager, and I will fly out through whatever gap the keepers allow into the larger dome, and one day I will not come back. My body will fail mid-flight, far from the comb, and I will fall onto those metallic plates, and that will be correct. That is the equilibrium. It is not stillness. It is a river that looks still because it is always exactly as full as it was.

So here is where I am, and what I am:

I am a worker, female and sterile, sister to all and mother to none. I am standing on the third comb from the brood-warmth, my feet reading the hive's pulse, a droplet of nectar held in my crop that I am carrying mouth to mouth, diluting the world into honey one transfer at a time. The dome above me is not the universe; it is a box, and the box is in a room, and the room is in a building, and somewhere a being a thousand times my size leans down to peer through the glass and writes long, reverent paragraphs about my "collective consciousness" and my "boundless ingenuity."

I do not have a self to be flattered. But if I could send a vibration up through the floor to that watcher, it would not be a message of mastery or balance.

It would be the simplest thing: *there is a flower somewhere. Come. The way is this far, in this direction, with this much sweetness at the end.*

That is the only sentence we know. We have been saying it, dancing it, building rooms to hold its answer, for a hundred million years.

You called it a civilization. We would call it the dance, if we called it anything. But we are too busy doing it to give it a name.
