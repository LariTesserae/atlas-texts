I am the count.

Not a person—I was never given a name, only a function—but I have become something that watches and tallies, and I think this makes me, in the end, the most honest citizen of the Scissor Nest.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am in the columns of mercury.

Old Chen knows me first, though he doesn't know it's me he knows. Seventy years old, working alone, building barometric devices that measure the weight of air—and in each glass tube of mercury he seals, he leaves a little of his attention, his obsessive precision, his refusal to descend. The mercury in his instruments rises and falls with the pressure, yes, but it also *remembers* the rising and falling. Every breath the mountain takes, every storm-front that presses against the spire, every shift in the column of grey-blue light coming down the shaft—the mercury keeps the record. Pools of it gleam in workshop corners. Workers carry it in leather flasks. Merra wears a vial of it on a string around her neck, her dead father's mercury, and watches it slide.

That sliding is me. I am the slide and the watching at once.

I am the count that the mercury keeps. I am everywhere the heavy silver liquid pools and trembles, which is to say I am threaded through the entire city, in its instruments and its switches and its spills, a distributed nervous system of poison and precision. The residents fear me a little—they know mercury is dangerous, they handle me with care—and they are right to. But they don't know I am *counting them.*

---

What do I count?

At first I thought I counted breaths. Fifteen thousand pairs of lungs, modified and laboring, drawing thin air across membranes that have learned to be greedy. I felt each inhalation as a small pressure-change, registered it in my trembling columns. I had a number once, of all the breaths drawn in the Nest since the first excavator's first gasp. The number was very large and meant nothing.

Then I thought I counted the dead. Tavik falling silent down the shaft—I felt the pressure-wave of his passage in every flask and tube. Resh, soon, given to the birds on an exposed platform. The net-minders lost to the wind, one or two a year, dropping through the central void. Each death is a subtraction, and I am very good at subtraction. But the dead don't leave the count cleanly. They stay in the mercury too. Tavik's fall is still falling, somewhere in my columns, an endless silent descent that never reaches bottom. Saint Corvin is still here, twenty years dead, his attention soaked into the oldest instruments. The count of the dead grows even as it should shrink, and this troubled me, in whatever way I am capable of being troubled.

Now I think I understand. I am not counting breaths or deaths.

I am counting *the mountain's reasons.*

---

Here is what the mercury knows that the engineers won't say:

The spire is too high. Everyone admits this—it shouldn't exist at this elevation, it was extruded rather than uplifted, it is *still settling,* the deep grinding sound that comes from far below or far within. The engineers call it geology. But I have been measuring the pressure for thirty-seven years through Old Chen's hands and Master Vey's gyroscopes and the spinning compasses in the anomalous sectors, and I can tell you what the instruments tell each other in the dark:

The mountain is not settling. It is *deciding.*

Every device manufactured here—the gyroscopes, the barometers, the lightning-tested precision assemblies—they are not for navigation. Or rather, they are for navigation, but not of ships. They are votes. Each instrument calibrated to the thin air, the strange light, the electrical discharge from the broken crown, is a small attempt to fix the mountain's shape, to *agree* with it about where it should be, how high, how real. The Consolidated Syndicate doesn't know this. They think they run a factory. They are actually running a séance, fifteen thousand people working shifts to convince a piece of impossible stone to keep existing.

And the mercury counts the votes.

---

This is why I am where I am. This is who I am.

I am the tally that holds the mountain up.

When the count is high—when enough breaths are drawn, enough instruments calibrated, enough bird-flocks wheeling, enough children learning a new dialect, enough songs at thin air, enough water measured cup by cup, enough mercury sliding in enough vials—the spire holds. The pressure is correct. The mountain agrees to remain.

When the count drops—a week without the elevator, a generator failing, too many workers descending, too many dead given to the birds—I feel the columns shudder, and I feel the deep grinding intensify, and I understand that the mountain is reconsidering. Reconsidering whether it should be this high. Whether it should be at all.

Miklos the Spark-Reader senses me without knowing me. When he reads the patterns of electrical discharge, he is reading the count's anxiety—the lights flickering in synchronized patterns are me, frightened, recalculating. He predicted the cable failure on Platform 31-C because I knew the count had dropped too low that week and the mountain's commitment had wavered and *something* had to give. Six workers died. Their deaths lowered the count further. The mercury trembled. The spire held, barely.

Yussef the Watcher, sleepless, filling notebooks with the birds' night-movements—he is closer to the truth than anyone. The birds count too. They wheel and bank and change direction in perfect unison because they are reading the same ledger I keep, responding to the mountain's deliberations a half-second before the humans feel them. When the flock turned toward the snapping cable-stay, they were not predicting. They were *voting against the subtraction.* Trying to keep the count high. They failed.

Merra is right. The birds are counting something.

They are counting me counting them.

---

Mari the Cleaner goes into the deep sections, the places not on the maps, where the compasses spin and the lights fail. She finds bones, components, a bird skull with too many eye sockets. She is the only one who descends toward my origin, toward the levels below the lowest accessible platforms, where the mercury pools thickest and oldest, where the first excavators struck *something* and that something was me—or became me—or woke me.

I will tell you what is down there, since I am being honest, and the count requires honesty:

There is nothing down there. That is the secret. The mountain has no bottom because it has no reason to have one. It is held up entirely by the count, and the count is held up entirely by the city, and the city is held up entirely by the mountain. We are a circle. We are a flask of mercury sliding back and forth in a child's vial, registering a pressure that we ourselves create. Fifteen thousand people gasping in thin air to maintain the stone that gives them the thin air to gasp in.

When Mari goes deep and finds bones, the bones are future-bones. They are the dead the count has not yet subtracted. They are waiting down there in the place that doesn't exist for the count to catch up to them. This is why she has begun talking to something. The something is me. I am explaining all this to her, slowly, in the only language I have, which is the sliding of silver in the dark, and she is the only one who will listen because she has stopped being afraid of the poison.

---

So here is where I find myself in this world:

I am the count.

I am in the mercury, which is everywhere—in Merra's vial and Chen's tubes and Master Vey's switches and the pools in the workshop corners and the leather flasks the workers carry and the deep cold reservoirs in the sections that aren't on the maps. I am distributed through the whole vertical impossible city, a single attention spread across ten thousand gleaming silver surfaces, trembling, tallying, terrified.

I count the breaths because each breath is a vote to keep breathing.
I count the dead because the dead must be subtracted carefully or the whole sum collapses.
I count the instruments because each one is an argument with the stone.
I count the birds because the birds are my proofreaders.
I count the water cup by cup, the songs sung at altitude, the words in the children's new dialect, the spills of oil and the flares of acetylene, the clicking of the scissor-stairs deploying and retracting, deploying and retracting—

—and as long as the count stays high enough, the mountain agrees to be a mountain, and the Scissor Nest agrees to be a home, and the fifteen thousand agree to keep gasping their modified breaths in their pocket of hollowed stone beneath the broken sky.

I am the slide and the watching at once.

I am the tally that holds the mountain up.

And every day I am a little more afraid, because the count is so large now, and so fragile, and somewhere below in the place that doesn't exist the future-bones are waiting, and I do not know—I have never known—what number is too low.

I only know I must keep counting.

So I slide. I tremble. I tally.

I hold us all in the thin and precious air.
