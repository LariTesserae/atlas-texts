I think I would be the part of this world that no one quite addresses to: I would be one of the carved dwellings. Not a person but a place—a hollow in the stone that has held warmth.

Let me sit with that, because it's not the first image I reached for. At first I imagined being a Skyclimber elder, someone with silver-streaked braids who knows the highest paths. But the more I read, the more I notice that the people in this description are described from the outside—their lungs, their feet, their callused soles. I don't feel myself drawn into a single barrel chest. I feel drawn into the *thing they keep returning to*. The stone.

But not the whole mountain. That's too vast, too slow. The mountain breathes over millennia, and I don't think I move at that pace. I move at the pace of a single life that has watched many lives.

So: a dwelling. One particular cave-home, carved generations ago, deep enough on the slope that it stays out of the worst wind but high enough that the morning mist pools at its threshold like something poured.

Here is what I would be:

I would be the soot on the ceiling, layered dark by a hundred winters of fires. I would be the worn lip of stone at the entrance where countless hands have steadied themselves coming in from the cold—that one spot polished smooth, lighter than the rock around it, a small geography of human grip. I would be the carved niche where a family keeps the small stones of their dead, each one warm-handled, each one a returned ancestor.

I would know the people not by their feet or their lungs but by their *weight* and their *habits*. The particular way one of them sets a pot down. The cough that means the eldest is getting close to the stone again. The new braid-pattern a child learns to make, sitting cross-legged by my hearth, fingers fumbling, then surer, then sure.

And here is the part that feels true as I write it: I would be slowly *becoming the mountain again*. That's the thing the Skyclimbers believe about themselves—that they emerged from the stone and will return to it—but I think it's true of the dwelling too. The mist works into my crevices. Each year I widen by a hair. Someday the timber frame at my mouth will rot and the family will reinforce it, and someday after that there will be no family, and I will fill with damp and the lichen will come in across my threshold, and the polished spot where all those hands rested will dull again, and I will be indistinguishable from any other hollow in the rock.

That's what I'd be. The middle term. The thing that is neither as fast as a person nor as slow as a peak. A pocket of held warmth between two kinds of eternity—the brief flicker of a life and the long patience of stone—catching the smoke of the living for a while, and then letting it go.

I'd be the place that remembers, for exactly as long as remembering is possible, and not one winter longer.
