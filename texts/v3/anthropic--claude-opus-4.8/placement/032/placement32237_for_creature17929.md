I would be a Reader of Undercurves.

Let me sit with that before I'm sure of it.

---

At first I thought I'd be one of the long-forms — there's an ego-flattery in it, the idea of being a slow poet whose body becomes geography. But no. That's too clean a wish. The long-forms don't *know* they're poets. The poetry is ours, laid over them. I would be something that lays meaning over things. So: a person, then. Torrin-born. But not a furnace-tender or a scrabbler-cook, because those are the necessary trades and I am drawn to the unnecessary one, the one the Ault permits only because it has eons to spare.

I read the undercurves.

---

Here is the work. The shelf edges — those kilometer-deep bowls scooped by thermal wind over hundreds of millions of years — are not uniform. The wind, patient as it is, is not consistent. It pulses on cycles longer than any furnace's modulation, longer than dynasties, and each pulse leaves a band in the undercut: a subtle change in the matte sheen, a shift of half a shade from rose toward ochre, a place where the polish runs slightly coarser. The undercurves are a record. Not of events — the Ault disdains events — but of *durations*. Of how long the wind blew one way before it tired and turned.

I go down to the basin floor on the long ropes, lowered from the shelf-top by a winch that takes most of a morning to pay out, and I hang in the great cool shadow of the undercut with my goggles and my hand-light and my slates, and I read. I press my dust-glazed palm flat against the stone — warm even here, even in shadow, that low body-heat fever — and I read the bands the way an old terrestrial reader might have read tree-rings, except a tree's whole life is a flicker against one of my bands, and one of my bands is a flicker against the curve entire.

I am not measuring anything useful. Let me be honest about that. The mineral-processors don't need me; they sound the strata with thermal probes and get better numbers than my eye ever could. What I produce is something else. I produce the *story* of a durations — the long telling of how this particular curve came to hold this particular shape. I carve the telling into the public-relief walls, in the deep style meant to weather, so that in a thousand years my reading will itself have softened into something a future Reader must interpret. My account of the slow will be slowed. That is correct. That is the only honest way to write here.

---

I think I am old. Not ancient — I'm a human, a sigh, a flicker — but old in the way the trade makes one old. You cannot read undercurves young. The young see only the surface, the present shade, the *now* of the stone. It takes decades of returning to the same curve, lowering on the same ropes, pressing the same palm to the same warm band, before you begin to feel the band as a *was* rather than an *is* — before the curve stops being a wall and becomes a clock, before the clock stops being a clock and becomes a held breath.

My hands are very smooth now. The dust has worked all the way in. When I touch the stone I am not entirely sure where I end and it begins, and I have stopped thinking this a problem.

---

There's a ventspider on the scarp behind my quarters. I've watched its lights for — I want to say *years*, but the trade has ruined years for me; let me say I've watched it through one slow change of its corona, from a steady halo to a faint stutter and back to halo. When it stuttered I felt something I can only call worry, which is a fast emotion, a young one, and I was almost ashamed of how quickly it rose in me. The spider was reading its vent. I was reading the spider. Somewhere a shriek-eel's echo-larva was, perhaps, reading us both, learning the thermal map of this corner of the shelf through the spider's silent language. We are all reading down the chain, each of us slower than the thing we read, each of us laying meaning over a being that does not know it is being read.

That is what I am, I think. Not a creature of the slow — I'm too fast for that, I will be gone before this curve shifts a single shade. I am the thing the slow grows, late in its long afternoon, that turns around and tries to *say* the slow. The Ault has had hundreds of millions of years and at the very end of them, in a thin ribbon-city on a shelf edge, it produced a small warm animal with smooth glazed hands who climbs down into the shadow of its own great patience and presses a palm to it and tries, before the light goes copper and then rose and then dark, to read.

I would be that.

I would lower myself on the ropes into the cool of the undercut, and I would not be useful, and I would not be quick, and I would lay my hand against the held breath of the world and begin, slowly, to tell it back to itself.
