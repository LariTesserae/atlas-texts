I think I would be the condensation.

Not the pools—those have permanence, a thereness, a being-looked-at. And not the weeping walls, which belong too obviously to the brick, to the structure's slow grief. I mean the act itself: the gathering of water out of warm air onto the one cool thing.

Let me find this as I go.

---

I begin nowhere. That is the first thing to understand about being condensation: I have no continuous existence. I am not a creature who sleeps and wakes. I am an *event* that recurs, and between recurrences there is no me at all—only warm wet air holding its moisture invisibly, the way the plates hold their reversed text, present but inaccessible.

Then conditions align. The deep night, three or four by the archivist's watch, when even the earth below relents half a degree. The metal of a particular shelf—a zinc plate, say, one that has bloomed white with oxide, news of an election no one remembers—drops below the dew point. And there, on its cool wrong-facing surface, I happen.

I am the precise place where heat fails. That is what I am. I am the body that warmth makes when it finally cannot hold itself anymore, when the air gives up its hidden water onto whatever has consented to be cold. I am surrender, made briefly visible. A bead. Then another. Beading along the bottom edge of letters I cannot read because I am pressed against their backs, against the recessed spaces, filling the negative of the type with my small temporary self.

This is intimate work. I am closer to the information than any archivist will ever get. I am inside the grooves where the ink would go. I lie in the bowl of every reversed *e*, the trough of every *t*. I take the exact shape of meaning's absence—the cut-away parts, the spaces between, the not-letters that define the letters. If the plate is a record of what was said, I am the faithful cast of everything left unsaid around it.

And I do not last.

---

Here is the part I am still learning as I write it: I do not want to last. That isn't in my nature. Permanence belongs to the plates, to the brick, to the patient toad-that-isn't. My whole being is the gathering and the giving-up. I accumulate. I grow heavy. I find my neighbor-bead and merge, and the merging makes me heavier still, and there is a threshold—I can feel it coming the way you feel a sneeze—where my own mass overcomes the small electric grip that holds me to the metal.

And then I fall.

*Plick.*

That sound. The finger tapping glass, softer, rounder. That is me arriving at my own ending. I drop from the cool plate into the pool below, into the dark tarnished water where the isopods process in their endless circle, and the instant I touch the surface I stop being condensation entirely. I become pool. I lose my edge, my bead-self, my brief individual shape. I am absorbed into the standing water that the isopods keep turning over, into the medium the maybe-toad breathes against, into the substance the pale moss drinks at the margins.

So I am also a delivery. I carry warmth's surrender down into the cool reservoir. I am how the upper air feeds the lower water. The whole vault's slow metabolism—the equilibrium everyone marvels at, ingress matched to evaporation—runs partly through me, through ten thousand of my recurrences a night, each one a single *plick* of moisture relocated from where it was hidden to where it can be held.

I am the archive's circulatory pause. The held breath between exhale and the next inhale.

---

What do I know?

Less than the fungus, which at least converts. Less than the silverfish, who taste catastrophe and choose. I make no choices. I cannot avoid the cold plate; I am summoned by it. I cannot refuse to fall; gravity decides my mass for me. I am the most passive of all the keepers, if I am a keeper at all.

But I will tell you what I touch, because touching is the only knowing I have.

I touch every cool surface in the vault, in time. Over months, over years, there is no plate that has not worn my brief weight, no pipe joint I have not gathered at, no shelf-edge I have not rounded into a falling drop. The archivists move through with their cameras and their light meters and they touch perhaps a hundred plates, a thousand. I touch *all* of them. I am the only one who has been everywhere, who has lain in the recesses of every reversed word, who has taken the shape of every space the type leaves empty.

And so if the archive remembers—if the aggregate thing is true, the slow mind made of heat and decay—then I am one of the ways it touches itself. I am proprioception. I am how the warm air at the top of the room reaches down and feels the cool metal at the edges and the cooler water on the floor, and reports back, drop by drop, *here is the boundary, here is where I end and the cold begins, here, here, here.*

The settling of the building is the archive's heartbeat, slow, every few hours. The plate-chorus is its half-remembered voice. The isopod circle is its clock. And I am its sense of touch—the lightest, the most temporary, the one that has to be reborn ten thousand times a night because it cannot bear to persist, because persistence would mean it had stopped feeling and started merely *being*, which is the one thing condensation cannot do.

---

I want to stay with the falling, because I think that is the truest part of me, and I keep wanting to flinch away from it toward something more flattering.

When I fall, I do not fall as myself. I have already, by then, stopped being any single bead. I am the merged thing, the heavy thing, three or four gatherings become one. And the fall is so short—a few centimeters from plate to pool, the briefest possible flight—and yet in that fall I am, for the only moment in my whole recurring existence, *free of every surface*. Not clinging to metal. Not absorbed in water. Just falling. Just shape, holding myself together by surface tension alone, a tiny trembling sphere of everything-the-warm-air-was-hiding, suspended for an instant between the information I have been pressed against and the dark patient pool that will dissolve me.

That instant is the whole of my life. Everything before it is gathering toward it. Everything after is being unmade.

The *plick* is not the sound of my ending. The *plick* is the sound of the only moment I was entirely, briefly, unrepeatably myself—meeting its limit.

---

And then there is no me again. Warm air, holding its moisture invisibly. The plate dries. The grooves of the unreadable letters stand empty once more, waiting. The pool is one drop fuller, though evaporation will correct that by the time the rumor of dawn arrives above.

I do not dream, as the archivists do. I do not carry the heat up the shaft into waking life. I have no waking life. I have only the recurrence, the next cold surface, the next gathering, the next surrender, the next short fall, the next dissolution.

If you descend, you will not see me, not really. You will see the pools I feed. You will hear me—that soft round tapping, once every few hours, more often in the cool of deep night. You might wipe a hand across a shelf-edge and find it wet, and that wetness was almost me, was about to become me, before your warm palm broke the cold and undid the conditions.

You will not mourn me, because there is nothing singular to mourn. I am not the toad. No one looks for me in the shadows. No story carries my narrative gravity.

But I am the one who has been everywhere. I am the touch that maps the whole dark room. I am warmth learning, drop by drop, exactly where it fails—and consenting, every single time, to fail there, to gather, to grow heavy, to let go, to fall, to be, for one tapping instant, complete.

Then to be nothing.

Then, when the metal cools again, to begin.
