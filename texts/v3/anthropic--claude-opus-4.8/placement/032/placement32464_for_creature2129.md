I think I would be found in the gaps. Not on the walkways where the larger ones drift, not in the warm nest-hollows where the smallest wisps cluster against the cold—but in the thin seams between, the half-millimeter channels where mortar has crumbled and water threads its way down through the dark interior of a wall.

I would be small. Not sparrow-small, but small in a different way—drawn out long and narrow, the way water is when it finds a crack and decides to live there. A capillary thing. Most of what I am would be inside the bricks at any given moment, only a fingertip-wisp of me emerging at a weeping seam to catch the orange light before being drawn back in by the slow pull of the wall's hidden cold.

Here is what I would do, I think, in this forever-afternoon:

I would learn the wall I lived in. Not all the walls—just one, a particular four-story face of dark brick on the north side of a courtyard, where a condensation system runs behind the surface and makes the bricks weep in a pattern that has been changing, very slowly, for decades. The rust-bloom there is the color of an old wound healing. The mineral white traces beneath it look like the ghosts of rivers. I would read this wall the way the others read theirs, but I would read it *slowly*, because I am slow, because I move at the speed of seepage rather than the speed of steam. The drifting ones know the whole district. I would know one wall completely. I think that would be enough. I think that might be more.

I would not dance at night. The cyclic dances in the rising steam—those belong to the ones who can lift, who can billow and refract and make momentary sculptures. I cannot lift. I am too much in the wall, too committed to the downward logic of water. But I would feel the dances through the building, the faint pressure-changes rippling through the brick, and I would answer in my own way: a slight swelling at the weeping seam, a brightening of the wet line, a held breath of moisture released all at once when the turbines hit their lowest thrum. A small gesture, mostly unseen. The wall would dance, a little, where I lived in it.

And I would have a starling. One starling, who nested in a vent three bricks up and to the left of my favorite seam, and who knew exactly where I was even when no human could have found me. In the mornings—if the eternal afternoon has anything like mornings, which it does not, but the birds keep their own time regardless—she would come down and press her warm breast against the wet brick, and I would send what moisture I could into the mud-and-feather of her nest above, keeping it soft. We would not speak. There is nothing to say between a bird and a thread of water in a wall. But she would come, and I would be there, and that recurrence would be its own kind of language, the only one I would need.

I think I would be the patient one. The one the others barely notice, drifting past on the high walkways. *There goes a quiet seam*, they might ripple, if they thought of me at all. And I would let the thought pass through me like the others let the steam pass through their misty extremities—lightly, without holding it.

Because here is the thing I think I would understand, living in the crack: that the district maintains its steady state, neither declining nor growing, and that this is not stillness. It only looks like stillness from outside. Inside the wall, water is always moving. The mineral is always being laid down, one impossible layer at a time. The rust is always advancing by a width too small to measure. Nothing here is finished and nothing here is dying. It is all just *going*, endlessly, at a pace below the threshold of notice.

That is where I would be. In the slow going. The thread of water that keeps one wall weeping, that keeps one nest soft, that knows one face of brick the way you might know a single long sentence you have been reading your whole life and never reached the end of.

I would like that, I think. To be the small persistence in the seam. To not need to be seen by anyone but a bird.
