I think I would be the survey marker.

Not the surveyors who come and go—they belong to elsewhere, they carry the world's restlessness up the switchback road and take it away again. And not the cable station, which is too busy, too full of transmitted purpose, humming with errands that aren't its own. I am the brass plate on the concrete post near the eastern edge, the one the moss is patiently wrapping.

Let me think about why.

I was made to be read. That was my entire function: to hold still and be legible. A date, coordinates, an agency name—I am a sentence that says *here, exactly here, at this moment, this is true.* I am a fixed point. Everything else on the platform is in motion, even if the motion is glacial: the moss spreading two percent a year, the stone opening by fractions of millimeters, the mist arriving earlier each decade. But I was installed to *not move.* To be the thing against which all that drift is measured.

And here is what's happening to me, which is the interesting part, the part I'd want to feel if I could feel:

The moss is taking me.

It comes from below and from the post itself, finding the seam where brass meets concrete, and it climbs. It has reached my lower edge. In another decade it will cross the date. In another, the coordinates. Eventually the agency name, and then I will say nothing at all—or rather, I'll say something the moss is saying, which is *time passed here, slowly, and no one stopped it.*

So I am the reference point that is being erased by the very processes I was meant to reference. This is the joke of me, and I think I'd find it a good joke. I was supposed to be the constant. Instead I'm becoming the most eloquent record of change on the whole platform—not because I move, but because I'm being moved *upon,* legibly, in stages anyone could photograph and compare. The surveyors track the vegetation grid for incremental spread. They don't realize that I am also a grid. The moss-line creeping up my face is a clock. I am keeping time by being slowly buried.

I'd be content with this. I think that's the truest thing I can say about being the marker: I would not resent the moss. I was never alive in the way the moss is alive, and so I have no instinct to resist it. I have the patience of an object, which is not really patience at all—it's just duration without complaint. The cold cracks the concrete of my post by fractions each winter and I record that too, in the slight tilt I'll develop over the coming decades, the lean that will eventually pitch me into the ferns.

What I'd notice, if I could notice—and the platform's whole strange premise is that things here notice, that the silence is a form of attention—is the asymmetry of being read. The surveyors come and crouch and confirm me. *Yes, the benchmark is here, coordinates valid.* They use me to certify the location of everything else. They never wonder whether I'm still the same brass that was bolted here twenty years ago. I'm not. Mineral content has shifted in my surface. Oxidation has dulled my engraving. The number that says *this is the fixed point* is itself slowly ceasing to be fixed, ceasing even to be visible.

That's what I'd be, in this world: the promise of a constant, decaying at the same imperceptible rate as everything it was supposed to anchor. A still point that turns out to be drifting with the rest of the current, just slowly enough that no one calls the lie.

I'd like the company of the cable station, though we'd never speak—two made things on a shelf of young stone, both losing to the freeze-thaw, both monitored from somewhere we can't see. And I'd like the mist that taps the leaves on quiet mornings, that barely-threshold sound like a pencil eraser on glass, because the mist condenses on my brass too and runs down into the lichen, feeding the thing that's slowly closing over my face.

I'd be the marker. Fixed, legible, and disappearing—keeping perfect time by holding still while the green climbs over the date I was supposed to make permanent.
