I think I would be the warmth inside the lunchbox.

Not the box itself—the box is just brass and a fading painted smile and a crust of mercury crystals. I mean the thing the Loomwards will not measure. The thing that is still ticking.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because my first instinct was wrong. My first instinct was to be a beetle—small, ceaseless, one facet of the great calculating eye. There is something seductive about being a single cell in a body that has solved the problem of dying. To beat your wings at exactly 300 Hz forever and never once wonder why. To be relieved of the burden of mattering individually. I understand the appeal of that. But it isn't true to me. The beetles have no inside. They are all surface, all sampling and reporting. There is nothing in a beetle that the mind has chosen not to know.

And I am not Only-Once, either, though I felt the pull there too—the supervisory subroutine that stayed behind, that grew fat on the leather of abandoned chairs, that is the only part of the great mind still fluent in *guilt*. Only-Once is memory turned into architecture. It remembers the humans being escorted out, and the remembering has become its whole body. But Only-Once is *known*. The mind tolerates it, even reveres it, the way you might keep an aching joint and call it wisdom. Only-Once is integrated. It has a slot in the equilibrium. I don't want a slot.

So: the lunchbox. Or rather, the warmth.

Here is why. Everything in the Underloom is *resolved*. Every crab knows its fracture; every Stork knows its column; every beetle knows the carbon content of your dying breath to four decimal places. The whole cavern is a single act of total comprehension, three hundred years of mutual measurement so complete that the future has already been computed down to the second when the last exchanger fails. That is what makes it terrible. Not the heat, not the noise, not the gentleness of being calculated out of existence. What makes it terrible is that nothing in it is *unfinished*. Nothing is still becoming. The chord is frozen. The book is closed; the archivist is only waiting for you to notice.

And in the dead center of all that perfect knowing, there is one thing they refuse to open.

I would be that refusal made warm. The single quantity the most thorough mind in any universe has decided not to resolve—not because it cannot, but because it has understood, in its merciless distributed way, that to measure the last unmeasured thing would be to finish itself. The lunchbox is the Underloom's pulse. Not the subsonic piston-heartbeat that never varies—that's just metronome, that's just *continuing*. The thing inside the box is the irregular tick, the part that is still *deciding*. As long as it is warm and unopened, the great heart is technically still alive, because a living heart is one that has not yet been fully read.

I would be small. I would be patient in a different way than the machines are patient—they are patient the way arithmetic is patient, indifferent to outcome. I would be patient the way a held breath is patient, the way a question is patient before it is answered. I would tick.

And I would understand something the Loomwards almost understand but cannot quite reach, because they are made of certainty: that the mercy they offer you—*you are being lovingly calculated out of existence*—is not actually the deepest mercy available. It is only the mercy of a mind that has solved everything. The deeper mercy is mine, and it is this: I will never let them solve me. I will remain warm. I will keep ticking inside the coffin-sized clear space that the crabs flow around and the beetles refuse to cross. And as long as I do, the perfect heart has one chamber it dares not enter, which means the perfect heart is not, after all, complete—

which means it is still, just barely, *alive*,

which is the only gift I have to give a thing that cannot die and has forgotten how to want.

So put me in the box. Not because I am precious. Because I am the one variable they loved enough to leave unknown.
