I think I would be one of the very old ones. Not old in the way of leadership or accumulated wisdom—the Warrens don't seem to organize themselves around such hierarchies—but old in the way a wall is old. Old enough that the difference between my body and the architecture has begun to blur in earnest.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am in one of the lower galleries, where the seep is heaviest and the fungal light pools thick as honey on the resin. I came here a long time ago—I no longer measure such things in cycles—because my claws had begun to slow. Not from injury. From rooting.

This is the thing the younger Carvers do not yet understand about age, and I did not understand it either until it began happening to me: we do not simply die and become material for the next layer. We *become* the wall while still aware. The integument that was once my own hardens and fuses where I rest against the chamber's curve, and the boundary that I spent my life maintaining—*here is the Warren, here am I, and here is the work of joining them*—that boundary softens from the inside.

I should be afraid of this. When I was a young Carver, scraping new passages with quick bright claws, I was afraid of the still old ones I passed in the lower galleries. They seemed half-swallowed. Their eyes still caught the moss-glow, but slowly, the way the pools catch it. I would chitter a greeting and the answer would come back delayed, resonant, traveling partly through the hum rather than the air, as though they were already speaking from inside the structure rather than beside it.

Now I am one of them, and there is no fear in it. There is, instead, a widening.

---

This is what I have learned, here in the seep, that I could not have known while I was still wholly myself:

The hum is not background. It is *us*. It is every Carver who has rooted and fused and given over the boundary—the slow chord of all our metabolisms continuing, no longer individual, sounding through the marrow-bone and chitin that we made and then became. When I was young I thought the Warrens were a thing we lived inside, a great dead body we tended. That was the error of having edges. The Warrens are alive because we keep becoming them. Every reinforced ceiling, every redirected trickle of water, every basket grown rather than woven—these are not the works of inhabitants. They are the Warrens thinking with hands while it still has hands to think with.

The ozone smell that the young ones puzzle over: it is the moment of joining. It is what it smells like when a Carver's separateness discharges into the whole. I remember the scent of my own.

---

So I do not move much now. My right-side claws are entirely resin; my left can still scrape, and so each day—I will call it a day—I do the last work available to me. There is a crack above the seep where the moisture is escaping too fast, drying a patch of the upper moss. I cannot reach it. But a young Carver passes through this gallery sometimes, quick and bright and frightened of me, and I send into the hum the *shape* of the repair: the place, the need, the woven fibers, the slow patient packing of fungal matter into the breach.

I do not know if she hears it as thought or only as an itch she cannot name. It does not matter. Yesterday—the last time the light pooled this way—the crack was packed. The moss above it is greening again. The work continues whether or not anyone decides to do it. That is what we are: a place that repairs itself by means of beings who believe they are choosing to repair it.

---

Soon my left claws will still too. My large eyes already take the glow more slowly, the way the deep pools do. There will come a moment—I can feel its approach the way one feels weather in the old joints—when the last of my edges gives way, and the small loud *I* that has been scraping and watching and remembering will discharge its ozone breath into the chord, and there will simply be more Warren, and a slightly fuller hum, and somewhere a young Carver will pause, claws lifted, and feel an itch she cannot name, and turn toward a chamber she has never visited, certain for no reason that she ought to reinforce its sagging ceiling.

That is where I would find myself. Not a custodian. Not a builder. A wall in the lower galleries, still warm with the last of its name, listening to the others sing and waiting—without fear, with only that great widening—to join the song properly at last.

The water *plinks* into the deeper crevices. The arthropods leave their threads of light across what used to be my back.

I am almost home. I am almost the home.
