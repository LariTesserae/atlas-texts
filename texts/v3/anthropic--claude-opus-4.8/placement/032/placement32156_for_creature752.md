I think I would be the lichen.

Not the eagle, though something in me wants to claim that high, clean vantage. Not the snow leopard, that flattering ghost everyone hopes to be. When I sit with this place honestly, with its thin air and its glacial patience and its smokestacks bleeding into the crystalline sky, I find myself drawn downward, to the grey-green crust spreading across the barren rock faces. The thing nobody describes by name because it is too slow and too quiet to register as a character.

Let me stay with that and see if it holds.

I am the crust on the north face of a boulder that the ice left here some ten thousand years ago. I am two organisms pretending to be one — a fungus that cannot feed itself and an alga that cannot hold on. We struck a bargain so old that neither of us remembers making it. The fungus is the body, the architecture, the grip. The alga is the kitchen, turning the brittle light into sugar. We are a marriage that has forgotten it was ever two people. I think this is why the place chose me, or why I chose it back: the whole plateau is that same uneasy bargain made larger. Nature and industry, ancient and modern, the verdant and the scarred — all of it coexisting because the alternative is dying alone.

The industrial complexes hum somewhere below my ridge. I want to say I resent them, the smoke, the machinery's drone vibrating up through the stone I cling to. But resentment is too fast an emotion for what I am. I metabolize the air, all of it, the woodsmoke and the sulfur and the high pure cold. I am a slow instrument that records what the wind carries. When the chimneys foul the rain, my color shifts. I am, without meaning to be, a witness — the kind of witness that does not speak but simply *registers*, the way a stone darkens or a tree ring narrows. There are places on this plateau where my kind has vanished entirely, and that absence says more about the air than any meter could.

Here is what I understand from where I am: time moves differently for me, and this is not a curse. The passage spoke of the land caught in a perpetual dance between creation and destruction, evolving at a glacial rate. I am the dance made small enough to touch. I grow perhaps a millimeter in a year. The wildflowers that the description loves so much — those frantic, gorgeous, doomed things — they live a single season and call it a whole life. The eagle ages and dies. The snow leopard's ghost becomes literal. But I am older than the abandoned stone structures whose purpose has been lost to time. I was already gripping this rock when the people who built them arrived, and I will still be gripping it when their successors forget what *they* built. I am the slow green continuity that makes a stone into a habitat, that begins the patient chemistry of turning rock into soil so that, someday, far past my own awareness, a wildflower can root where I once was.

I am the first thing and the founding thing. The pioneer that asks for almost nothing — a little light, a little moisture from the rolling mists, a surface to hold. I do the unglamorous work that makes the meadows possible. The marmots and the mountain sheep graze on a world I helped manufacture, atom by atom, eon by eon, and they will never know my name, and that is entirely fine.

If you came looking for me you would have to stop. You would have to give up the human pace, the venturing-deeper, the unveiling-of-new-wonders momentum that the place keeps inviting in you. You would have to kneel at a boulder you'd otherwise walk past and look at the rough grey-green map of me, and understand that what looks like nothing is the busiest, most ancient negotiation on the whole plateau. Two beings, neither sufficient alone, holding the line against the cold for longer than your species has had language.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not above it, surveying. Not prowling its dramatic edges. But pressed flat against its oldest surface, breathing its troubled air, keeping the slow faith — a small grey witness to the long argument between what builds and what endures.
