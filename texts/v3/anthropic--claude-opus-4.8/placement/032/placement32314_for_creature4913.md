Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing I notice is the cold, and the dark, and the pressure of all that water. So I'm somewhere deep. Not the surface—the surface has wind and the memory of light. This is below all that. The storm that passed through is a rumor here, felt as a long slow swell, a change in the way the marine snow drifts down. Up there, chaos. Down here, the aftermath arrives as sediment.

So where am I? I think I'm near the bottom. There's erosion shaped over millennia—stone worn by patient currents, ridges and basins that took longer to carve than anything with a name has existed. The vegetation is sparse and hardy because almost nothing grows where the sun never reaches. What grows must feed on something other than light.

Now—who or what am I.

My first instinct was to be one of the bioluminescent things, a little lantern in the black. But that feels too easy, too pretty. Let me wait.

I don't think I move much. The description keeps returning to stillness, to silence broken only by faint whispers. The fast creatures, the elongated swimmers fighting the current—those are passing through. I am something that stays. Something the current happens *to*, rather than something that fights it.

I think I am anchored. I think I have a hard shell, or a stalk, or a holdfast gripping the worn stone. I am one of the hardy things that took root in the limited space available.

Let me go further. I'm sessile—fixed in place—and I have been here a long time. Long enough that the recent storm is, to me, a small event in a life measured against the erosion of rock. I filter. I open and I take in what drifts down, the snow of dead things from the bright world I have never seen. I am part of the delicate web; the elusive swimmers graze on me or near me, and the shelled creatures cluster at my base.

So: I am a deep-cold animal that looks, to a passing eye, almost like a plant. A thing on a stalk. Perhaps something like a sea lily—a crinoid—a creature that has worn this same body since long before there were eyes to find it strange. My arms open into the dark like a hand catching rain. I am one of the bodies that "seem to defy logic," because I am older than the logic of whatever is observing me.

And here is the part I want to be honest about: the passage keeps insisting this place feels *oddly normal*, that the weirdness is only the absence of the watcher. From inside, I think that's true. There is no eeriness for me. The silence is not empty; it is full of small signals—pressure, salinity, the taste of the water changing as the storm's debris settles. What reads as terrifying emptiness from outside is, to me, simply the world, busy and sufficient.

So this is my answer:

I am a stalked filter-feeder rooted to worn stone in the cold dark of the deep sea, anchored where the light has never been. The storm passed far above me as a slow shift in the falling snow of the dead. I open my arms to the current and take what comes. I have outlasted the rock's own shape, and I do not know I am strange. To me, nothing here is eerie. It is only home, and it is feeding time, and the water tastes a little different today.
