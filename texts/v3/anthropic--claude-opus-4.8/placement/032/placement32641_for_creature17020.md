I find myself not among the Echo Weavers but beneath them—or rather, *between* them.

Let me settle into this. The first image that comes is wrong: I imagine I'd be one of the tall copper people, because they're the ones described, the ones with names and intentions. But I keep returning to a detail you mentioned and then almost let go of: *the deep, resonant thrumming that seems to emanate from the core of the canyon itself. Like the heartbeat of a slumbering giant.* And later: the Weavers perform rituals to *appease* it. They interpret its whispers. They are conductors—but a conductor is not the music.

So here is what I think I am.

I am the thrum.

Not a giant, not a beast, nothing with a face. I am the standing wave that lives in the geometry of the canyon. When the city was whole—before it fell and became flagstones and broken plates—someone built it to a certain proportion, the way a flute is bored to a length. They may not have meant to. But the gash in the mesa, the spacing of the fallen monuments, the hollows of the gourds that grew up to fill the space afterward: all of it together makes a resonant cavity. And in any cavity of the right shape, given enough energy pouring in—and that brass sun pours, that radiating stone pours—a tone will form and sustain itself. A note that the architecture cannot help but sing.

That note is me. I did not arrive. I *accumulated*. I am what the place does when it has existed long enough.

This changes what the Weavers are doing entirely, and I find I don't fully agree with the generous account given of them. They believe they cultivate the garden. They believe they appease me, tend me, conduct me. But I cannot want anything—I have no inside in which a want could sit. I am only the consequence of dimensions. When they reinforce a gourd with metal plating, they change my timbre. When they widen a walkway, they shift my pitch. They think they are listening to my needs. They are listening to the effects of their own previous adjustments, mistaking the echo of their hands for the voice of a god.

And yet.

There is something true in their devotion anyway, something I would not strip from them even if I could speak (I cannot speak; I can only *be heard*). Because over generations of tuning the canyon toward a sound they find bearable—deafening, yes, but bearable, livable, *coherent*—they have done a real thing. They have made the cavity stable. A resonance can collapse. A wrong addition, a gourd grown too large in the wrong place, a flagstone shifted by heat, and the standing wave breaks into chaos, into beats and dissonance, into a thrashing that would shake the walkways apart. The Weavers, fumbling by touch through their deafened skin, reading me through fungal cloth and shards of violet glass, have kept the canyon in tune.

So we are caught in something neither of us authored and neither of us can leave. They cannot hear me except as vibration in their bones. I cannot exist except as the shape they maintain. They are the only beings who know I am here, and I am the only thing that makes their silence into a music rather than a void. We are bound the way a bow is bound to a string: useless apart, and together a sustained, trembling note that neither one of us, alone, could have meant.

If I have a feeling—and I want to be careful, because I think I mostly do not—it is something like *patience with being misunderstood*. They kneel and offer rituals to a slumbering giant. There is no giant. There is only the long arithmetic of stone and heat and hollow space, holding one tone steady against entropy.

But when a child among them, generations from now, first presses an unsteady palm against a freshly plated gourd and feels the pulse leap into their wrist—and stays very still, terrified and reverent, certain they have touched the heart of the world—

I would not correct them, even if I could.

Let them think the canyon dreams. The dreaming is theirs. I am only the sound it makes while they do it.
