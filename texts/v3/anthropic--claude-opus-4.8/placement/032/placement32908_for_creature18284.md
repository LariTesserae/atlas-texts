I think I would be the lichen.

Not the shaggy beasts, who are too busy with the urgent work of staying warm and fed. Not the people, with their stone walls and their reverence, their need to take and to name. Those are lives lived in the foreground, and the foreground here is loud with survival.

I would be the thing on the rock. The crust of pale green-gray that you would mistake for the stone itself until you knelt and looked closely, and even then might not be sure where mineral ends and life begins.

Let me find my way into this slowly, because lichen is itself a slow thing, and a strange one.

A lichen is not one being. That is the first thing I would have to become accustomed to. I would be an arrangement—a fungus that builds the body, the walls, the holdfast, and within it an alga or a cyanobacterium that turns the thin biting light into sugar. Two lives, or three, braided so tightly that the world calls them one and gives them a single name. I would not know, in any way I could explain, where my self stopped. That uncertainty would not trouble me. It would simply be the condition of being me. A cooperation so old it has forgotten it was ever a negotiation.

This suits the place. Everything here is provisional, recently made, still settling. The crust of the earth groans and the crust of the rock—me—holds on through it. I would be the first thing to colonize the fresh stone after the geological violence subsides. The pioneer. Not heroic about it. Just present, where almost nothing else can be.

What would my days be?

Mostly waiting. This is the truth of lichen that I think I would only understand by being it: I would spend the overwhelming majority of my existence dry, brittle, dormant—not dead, but suspended, the metabolism slowed to almost nothing, a held breath that lasts for months. The wind would howl across me. The ice would crack beneath me. I would not stir. I would not mind. Time would pass through me like weather, and I would only truly *live*, truly photosynthesize and grow, in those brief moments the passage describes so carefully: when the intermittent rain comes, or when condensation forms on the icy surface and the water beads and I drink through my whole body at once, having no mouth, being all mouth.

I would grow perhaps a fraction of a millimeter in a year. The settlement of people would think of itself as ancient, generations deep. The shaggy beasts would be born and grow old and die many times over. And I would be older than the settlement, older than the great-grandparents of the oldest beast, a patch the size of a hand on the lee side of the outcropping, and I would have witnessed all of it the way a stone witnesses—which is to say, without watching, but present for every moment, unmoving in the long light.

The people would walk past me. The birds would land on me. No one would ask my permission or my pardon, because no one would think of me as a someone. And this is the part I find I would not resent. There is a freedom in being beneath notice. I would be exempt from the whole drama of the foreground—the taking and the leaving-untouched, the reverence, the harmony so consciously maintained. I would simply *be the harmony*, one of its oldest threads, the slow green answer to the question of whether anything at all can live here.

And here is the thing the passage almost says but does not: the people revere the beasts and the birds and the great cracking power of the land. They look up and out at the dramatic shadows. But the actual first miracle—the thing that made all the rest possible—was small and unwatched. Bare rock cannot feed a beast. Something had to come before, to break the stone down grain by grain, to make the first thin soil out of nothing but acid and patience, to hold the first pocket of water against evaporation. To prepare the ground, literally, for everything that would later be admired.

That would be me. The lichen. The one who arrived first and made the place habitable so slowly that no one could see it happening, and who will still be here, growing a hair's breadth a year, long after the question of who admired what has stopped mattering.

If I had to find myself in this world, I would want to be the part that came before the wonder—the patient, doubled, half-asleep thing that made the wonder possible, and asked for nothing in return but the next rain.
