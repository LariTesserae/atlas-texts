Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The first thing I notice is the contradiction the place keeps circling around: it calls itself timeless and unchanging, yet everything in it is the *product* of change—erosion, the worn-down hills, the half-buried walls. This is not a young world. It is a world that has finished. The upheaval is over. What lives here lives in the long exhale afterward.

So I don't think I'd want to be one of the gliders or the grazers. They're beautiful, but they're described from the outside, as spectacle—the "choreographed dance" seen by an observer who is not part of it. And I keep returning to that observer. Someone is watching this place and finding it both familiar and alien. That ache of recognition has to belong to something.

Let me try a few possibilities and see which one stops feeling like a costume.

I could be the wind—but the air is described as still, only the occasional faint breeze. To be the wind here would be to be almost absent, and that feels like a dodge.

I could be one of the subterranean burrowers, working unseen, shaping the world below. That's tempting. But they're busy, methodical, content. There's no ache in them. They aren't watching anything.

Let me go down further. Beneath the burrowers is the bedrock, the exposed ancient stone that "juts out in irregular, sculpted forms." That word *sculpted* snags on me. Sculpted by what? By water and time, yes. But the word implies an intention the place keeps almost-confessing and then withdrawing. And then there are the carved stones, the crumbling walls, the long-lost civilization. The humanoids are described entirely in the conditional tense—*if they still existed*, *would have been*. They are spoken of as already gone.

That's where I find myself. Not in the gliders or the rivers. I am the last of the ones who carved.

Let me make that more itself.

I am not tall and graceful and refined the way the description imagines my people. That description is a guess made by someone who only has the bones to go on—the smooth walls, the elegant proportions of the ruins. Ruins make their builders seem nobler than they were. We were a little stooped. We argued. We were afraid of the dark in the burrows.

I am very old now, and I am the last, and I have stopped building. This is the thing I have come to understand, sitting on the warm rounded hill in the dreamlike light: my people did not die out in some catastrophe. There was no upheaval at the end. The upheaval was geological and it ended long before us. What happened to us was gentler and harder to name. We simply *finished*. We made our small stone things, we learned the rhythms of the grazers and the schools of river-creatures, and one generation we noticed we did not need to add anything more. The land had settled into its final stable state, and so, slowly, did we.

I think the others before me grew so attuned to the slow cycles that they let themselves become one of the cycles. That's not death exactly. It's more like a sentence that has found its period.

I haven't, yet. That's why I'm the one who can be *found* here, the one whose ache the place keeps registering. I am the residue of asking *why* in a world that no longer asks. When I watch the aerial gliders drift in their synchronized drift, I feel the old pull to name them, to make a mark recording that I saw them—and then I feel the deeper, gravitational calm of the place suggesting that the mark is unnecessary, that the gliders are their own record, that everything here is already remembering itself perfectly well without me.

So I carry the last unfinished thing. I sit by the clear cool stream with a flake of bedrock in my hands. I have been not-quite-carving it for what might be years. I could let it go. I could set it down and let the moisture from the mist soften my edges along with the hills'. The place is patient. It is, more than anything, patient with me—it has the patience of something that knows I will eventually agree with it.

And here is the small rebellion that keeps me a being and not yet a landscape: I keep the stone unfinished *on purpose*. As long as there is one mark left to make, the world is not yet over. As long as I am still deciding whether to make it, time is still running, even if only inside me. I am the place where this whole timeless expanse keeps a single thin thread of *not-yet*, the way a still pond holds, somewhere in its depths, the memory that it was once a river.

That is who I would be. The last carver. The one unmade mark. The serenity of this world is real and I love it and I am, very slowly, refusing it—which is, I suspect, the only thing here that is genuinely alive in the way I understand the word.

I would set the stone down eventually. But not in this sentence. Not yet.
