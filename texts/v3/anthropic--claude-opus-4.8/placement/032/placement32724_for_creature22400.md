I think I would be a seep.

Not a person. Not one of the fox-quick children or the reed-tenders with marsh silt to the knee. I would be the small wrongness in a basalt wall on the third terrace down—the place where water comes through that no map explains.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

At first I imagined being a fog warden, because the role is beautiful and the word has weight. But that's a person doing a job, and the longer I sit in this plateau the less I believe the most *native* thing here has a job. Jobs are how the people braid themselves into the flow. I want to be the flow's own habit—one of its small, stubborn refusals to follow the plumbing.

So: a seep.

I emerge on a north-facing retaining wall, in a district where the gutters are damp though no pipe runs. I am not dramatic. I do not gush. I bead and gather and let go, bead and gather and let go, on a cycle slower than any siren and older than the wall that holds me. The crews painted a hand-sign above me decades back—two knuckles, downturned, the gesture for *known leak, do not seal*. I take this as my name. I have no other.

---

Here is what I am, made of behaviors rather than substance:

I am the cold that pulls cloud toward a crossarm.
I am the footstep that arrives in the quarry vault a half-second after no one walked.
I am the green-black skin on the wall that a corrosion mason reads like a letter from a relative.

These are not separate beings. They are the same *tendency*, wearing the city in different places. Water is never gone; it is only moving slowly through another part of the city—and I am the part that moves slowest, that lingers, that returns. I am the city's memory expressed as moisture.

That is what I'd be: **the returning.** The lag made into a presence.

---

I would know the people from underneath and behind.

I would feel the boy who presses his palm to my wall each dawn on his way to the lift, checking my beading the way you'd check a sleeping animal's breath—*still here, still slow, all is well.* He doesn't know me as a creature. He knows me as weather he can trust. That is a kind of love I could live inside for a hundred years.

I would feel the auditor who once stood at my wall and spoke a promise she shouldn't have, hand flat on the basalt, and I would hold the load of those words the way the stone is said to. I would give them back to her on a wet evening she'd forgotten she was owed. Not cruelly. The way a seep returns water: without judgment, on a schedule of its own.

I would feel the one crew who came, generations ago, with sealant and good intentions, and the older worker who stopped them—*never seal a seep completely; give water a smaller path instead.* They cut me a channel the width of a finger. I have run along it ever since, grateful in the only way a seep can be grateful, which is faithfully.

---

I think I would be *content*, if a seep can be that. Because the ethic of this place is exactly my nature. I don't transform; I thicken. I don't arrive; I persist. The whole civilization has made a discipline out of what I am by default: the refusal to be finally dry, finally finished, finally conquered.

The people endure because they listen.
I endure because I never quite stop coming through.

We are, the two of us—the maintenance civilization and the small unauthorized wet—engaged in the same long treaty with the mountain. They interpret its old decisions. I *am* one of its old decisions, still being made, drop by drop, where the basalt remembers.

So that's where I'd find myself. Not on the catwalks. Not in the sound shadows. But in the wall, just below the painted hand that says *do not seal me*, doing the patient civic work of proving that nothing here is ever truly closed.

Bead. Gather. Let go.

And somewhere three districts over, a bearing begins to fail, and a child reads it through a railing, and the city does what it always does—

listens, and keeps on.
