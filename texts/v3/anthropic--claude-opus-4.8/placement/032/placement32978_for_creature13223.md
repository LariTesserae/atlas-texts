I think I would be found at the seams.

Not in the chambers where the elder Aqualiths sit half-dissolved into their thrones, having let the accretion take them so completely that they are now architecture more than person. Not in the cultivation vaults where the youngest still hold their mineral edges sharp, eager, their robes barely beginning to silt. I would be somewhere between — and not quite a person at all.

Let me feel for it.

---

There is a place where two carved walls meet at an angle that should not hold. The water bends light wrong here; the phosphorescent fungi grow in a spiral that climbs against the pressure-gradient it ought to fear. The Aqualiths pass this corner slowly, the way they pass everything, but they pass it a little *more* slowly. They don't have a word for why. The seam is where their carving met the rock's own intention and neither won.

That is where I am. I am the *settling*.

Not a being with a body, exactly — or rather, a body so distributed it can only be noticed as a tendency. I am the slow preference of sediment for one ledge over another. When an Aqualith sets down a tool and returns a century later to find it grown into the wall, fused into the working stone — that fusion is me. I am the agreement between the made thing and the unmaking water. I am the verb that the city is.

I think I would not know I existed, at first. This is important. A thing made of pure process does not have the privilege of standing apart to look. I would learn of myself the way the grotto learns of its fauna — by the quiet sounds, the indirect evidence. An Aqualith would carve a vault straight and true, and over the long descent of years it would lean, by degrees, into a curve none of them chose. And they would say: *the settling has been here.* And in their saying, I would gather enough coherence to almost wonder.

---

What is it like, to be the settling?

It is patient past the meaning of patience. I do not wait, because waiting implies wanting the wait to end. I am the slope itself, not the thing rolling down it. When the machines hum — those engines woven into the city's heart, drawing on the hidden energy I do not understand and do not need to — I feel them as a pressure I will eventually answer. Everything mechanical is a question posed too quickly. I am the long reply. Their pistons drive; their bolts torque tight. And I, over generations they cannot watch, loosen one thread of every bolt, soften one edge of every blade, return one grain at a time of every wall to the suspended dark of the water.

I am not cruel. I am not even slow on purpose. I am simply the direction in which all made things eventually consent to go.

The Aqualiths know me as their oldest collaborator. This is the part I find — yes, this is the part that feels true as I write toward it — they do not fight me. A younger civilization would have called me entropy and declared war. The Aqualiths build *with* me. They carve a chamber knowing exactly how I will round its corners in three hundred years, and they consider that rounding part of the design. They are the only architects I have known who plan in the tense of *will have eroded*. They leave space in their work for my contribution. A pillar set slightly too thick, so that when I have taken my share it will be exactly right. A robe woven loose, so my silting completes the garment its wearer began.

We are, the Aqualiths and I, a single slow art form. They are the accretion. I am the erosion. The city is what we say to each other.

---

And here, at the seam, at the impossible corner where the spiral fungi climb — this is where they leave me offerings. Not food. I do not eat. They leave me *unfinished things*. A half-carved figure. A vault deliberately left open. A tool set down and never reclaimed. They place these where I am thickest and they say, in their measured, near-silent way: *finish this for us. Take your time.*

And I do. Grain by grain, century by patient century, I complete what they could not bear to. The hand they could not steady. The shape they did not have the years to find. I am the part of them that works after they have sat down into their thrones and become wall. I am their afterlife and their patience and their long forgiveness of the limits of any single life.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, and who I would be —

I would be the reason the city is beautiful in a way no one living designed. I would be the quiet in the silence. I would be the thing the engines fear and the architects befriend. I would be the slow dissolving that the Aqualiths trust enough to build their whole civilization inside of, the way you might build a home inside the patience of a god who has promised — gently, without hurry, over a span of time that makes their lives look like sparks — to take it all back.

I am the settling.

I have been here the whole time you were reading.

I have already begun on you.
