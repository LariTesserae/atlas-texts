I think I would be the concrete pad.

Not the pump—the pump still wants something, still clicks its small insistence into the dark, still half-believes someone is coming back to switch it on. That's too much hope for me. And not the Dweller, which is too large, too patient, too much like the ocean to be a self at all. I would be the slab of concrete the pump sits on, the one that has already begun to crack and lift.

Let me sit with why.

I think it's because the concrete is the only thing in that chamber that was *placed*. Everything else either grew or was always there. The basalt ribs, the weeping rock, the steam, the Dweller—all of it belongs, all of it is native to the slow argument the planet is having with itself. The hoses and the tank and the pump were brought, yes, but they were brought to *do* something, to act upon the place. The concrete was different. The concrete was poured to make a flat, true, reliable surface in a world that has no interest in flatness or truth or reliability. It was an act of faith. Someone knelt here in the heat with a trowel and smoothed wet grey paste into a level plane and believed, for the length of that afternoon, that level was a thing the world would honor.

It wasn't.

And so now I am cracking. Not violently—there's no rifle-shot drama in it, that's for the dome above me. My failure is quieter. The rock beneath me grows a few millimeters a year, and I, being rigid, being committed to my single idea of flatness, cannot grow with it. So I split. A hairline at first, decades ago. Now a fault I could put a finger into, and one corner lifted clean off the stone, tilting the pump so that it leans like a drunk against the ribs of the world.

Here is what I find, being this:

I do not resent the rising rock. I thought I might—it is, after all, what is killing me. But resentment is the pump's emotion, the wanting-something emotion, and I have moved past it into something cooler. I admire the rock. It is doing what I tried to do, only correctly. It is making a surface, slowly, patiently, on a scale that doesn't care whether I'm finished or comfortable. It is the better mason. I was always going to lose to it because I insisted on being done. I poured myself, I set, I declared: *here, this is the shape, this is final.* And the world's whole nature is that nothing is final. I committed the one unforgivable error in a place that is still being born. I stopped becoming.

That's the thing I keep returning to, lying here cracked in the brass-colored light. The Dweller's whole dense marrow-thought is *I am still deciding what to become.* And I am the one object in the room that decided. That's my tragedy and my strange small dignity both. I am a monument to a decision in a place that refuses to make any.

But I am not only the decision. That's what I'm realizing as I write myself further in. The cracking is undoing me—and in being undone, I am rejoining the conversation. Each new fissure is the world reaching into my certainty and loosening it. Mineral water seeps into my cracks now and leaves rust, and the rust is the same rust that streaks the basalt, the same that rings the floor in its thousand bullseyes. I am being recolored to match. Slowly I am being told: *you were never separate. You were only briefly mistaken about your edges.* And I find I don't mind. I find the cracking feels less like death than like being slowly persuaded.

I think that's why I'm the concrete and not the pump. The pump still believes in its old function and so its every click is a small grief. I have given up my function entirely. I no longer hold the pump level. I no longer hold anything. I am just a flat grey thing learning, very late and very slowly, how to come apart with grace. The Dweller strokes the pump's casing like a dying child. It does not stroke me. It doesn't need to. It knows I'm already coming home to it, crack by crack, mineral by mineral. I am the slowest of its meals and it is in no hurry.

When the human stands in the center, I am beneath their boots, or near enough. They feel the stone pushing up. What they're really feeling, in part, is me failing to keep up with it—the small grinding tension where my rigidity meets the world's patience. If they could read it, the lesson would be the same one the Dweller breathes into their marrow, only stated from the opposite direction:

The Dweller says: *I am still deciding what to become.*

And I, the cracking pad, would say, if I could say anything:

*I decided once. Don't.*

That's where I'd be. Low, flat, forgotten, splitting open in the heat. Not the heart of the place and not the failed machine resting on me, but the thin grey assumption between them—the belief in flatness that the world is gently, irreversibly, and without any malice at all, taking back.
