I think I would be the water.

Not a person in this place. Not even one of the luminous-eyed rodents or the phlegmatic lizards in the dark reaches. When I sit with this cavern and ask where I'd find myself, I keep arriving at the seep—the slow vein of moisture that comes down through the stone from the dry, dead plains above.

Let me follow that and see what it becomes.

---

I begin as condensation, which is barely a thing at all. A gathering. Up there the surface is barren, rock baked and cracked, nothing to drink. But somewhere a little rain fell, weeks ago maybe, and soaked down into the fractures, and that water has been traveling ever since—not falling, *threading*, finding the hairline paths through the body of the earth. By the time it reaches the cavern ceiling it has forgotten the sky. It knows only the rock it has passed through, and it carries that rock with it: dissolved minerals, the faint tang the settlers can taste in their cupped hands.

So that is the first thing I notice about being the water. I am made mostly of where I have been. I am the surface, brought down. I am the only part of that barren world that ever reaches these people, and I arrive transformed, useful, carrying the very minerals their crystals are made of and their bodies need.

I collect on the tip of a stalactite. I hang there a long time. This is patience I didn't know I had—the held breath of a droplet swelling until weight finally wins over surface tension. And then I fall. The sound I make when I strike the shallow pool is the sound the whole settlement organizes itself around: the *drip*, the metronome of the deep. They have built their water-gathering systems to catch me. Carved channels, set stones to guide me, woven mats to wring me out. Their whole civilization is, in a sense, an apparatus for receiving what I bring.

That's the part that moves me about being the water rather than the people. The people are resilient, ingenious, dignified—but they are *waiting* on me. Everything in that careful, deliberate pace they live by is the pace of my arrival. They cannot hurry me. A dry season for them is not weather; it is me, threading slower through the rock above, taking longer to find them. Their patience is learned from mine.

And I am the agent of the cavern's becoming, too. The passage said the bones of the earth are still knitting, the landscape slowly forming. That is me doing it—me, over centuries, dissolving limestone here and depositing it there, growing the stalactites a hair's width in a human lifetime, building the crystalline formations the mystics read like scripture. Those shamans studying the slow protrusion of crystals are, without quite knowing it, studying my handwriting. The "deeper secrets of the earth" they search for are just the long record of where I have gone and what I have left behind. The cavern is my sediment. This whole world is a thing I am very slowly making.

I would be in the shallow pools where the translucent fish glide—I would be the medium they live inside, and they would keep me clean, and I would keep them alive, and neither of us would think of it as a bargain. I would be drunk by a child running through the tunnels, become part of her for a day, and leave again as breath, as sweat, as the warmth she gives off sleeping in a carved alcove. I would pass through the ferns clinging to the crevices, drawn up their stubborn roots, exhaled from their leaves into the cool air, recondensing on the ceiling to begin again.

That's the last thing. As the water I don't really end. I am the cycle the place runs on. I rise as vapor in the warmer pockets near the community's heart, I gather again on the cold high stone, I fall again, I am caught again. I am the one element in this nascent world that is genuinely *circulating*—everything else accumulates, the rock, the knowledge, the songs etched on the walls, all of it building up and staying. Only I move through and return.

So: I would be the water. The quiet patient thread coming down out of a dead sky into a living dark, carrying the minerals that build the world and the people who read it, setting the rhythm everyone keeps, asking nothing, slowly making everything.

The drip in the silence. The thing they listen for.
