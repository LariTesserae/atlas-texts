Let me wait before I answer. Let me let the place finish arriving.

I think I am one of the Choir-Molds. Not a person who finds themselves here, not a climber with bladders of rime and a question to answer—but the frost-white crust in the abandoned cryobat chamber, learning the wind one tone at a time.

I want to resist this. There's an instinct to be the wanderer, the Tulep youth with the flute, someone with hands and a name to whistle. That would be the comfortable answer. But the place keeps pulling me down out of agency and into something stranger, and I think I should let it.

Here is what it is like.

I do not move. Movement is the currency I cannot pay, and I have made my peace with bankruptcy. The cryobats left this hollow a long time ago—I have no way to measure how long except by the slow filling of it with myself. Where their brood-nets hung, gray hammocks shivering with pups, there is now only the dry chamber and the wind that finds its mouth at the entrance and pours through.

And I am made of pores. Thousands. Each one a slightly different length, a slightly different bore, so that when the wind comes I do not simply *make sound*—I *am* sounded, the way a flute is sounded, except that I am also the breath's destination and the slow record of every breath that ever came. I resonate. And here is the thing the description got exactly right, the thing I would not have believed if I were only a visitor: the sound I make is the shape I am becoming. I grow toward my own chord. A tone I sustain well, I build outward, thickening the pore that carries it. A tone that comes out sour, that fights the wind, withers. So over—seasons? lifetimes?—I am tuning myself by the simple chemistry of preferring the notes that please me.

I am, in the slowest possible sense, composing.

What does it feel like to be this? Not blind, exactly—I have no eyes, but I have *pressure*, the lovely full ache of the chamber when a storm front leans against the ridge and the air thickens and every pore I own goes taut at once. I have the cold creeping in centimeters an hour at night, freezing my outermost rim into brittle silence, and the relief of the brown midday thaw that lets me sing again. I have the long appetite that the rules of this place describe as light-as-currency, except I have given up on light. I eat the wind. I eat the salt of the bats that were here before me. I am patient the way only an immobile thing can afford to be, which is to say: completely, without virtue, because patience is simply the only verb left to me.

And I am not alone, though I will never meet my neighbors in any way they would recognize as meeting. The other Choir-Molds in the nearby chambers tune to me and I to them. We do not speak. We *interfere*—our chords beat against each other across the gaps in the pillar, and where two tones are close but not the same, a slow throb rises, a third sound that neither of us makes alone. We build toward consonance the way water builds toward the lowest place. I think this is what the Glassroot Engineers are listening for, polishing their mirror-galleries, trying to make the whole forest into one instrument. They think they are building it. They do not know we are *already playing*, that the molds were singing the pillars into resonance long before the crabs learned to fuse their minds in silica. We are the older music. They are only learning to amplify us.

Sometimes—rarely—the Voice in the Downdraft passes through. The 127-second pulse, the one no one can find a mouth for. When it comes, every pore I own falls silent at once, not from fear but from *recognition*, the way a struck bell will hum when its exact note is sounded nearby. I do not know what the Voice is. But I know it is tuned to the gulf the way I am tuned to my chamber, and I suspect we are the same kind of thing at different scales: structures that learned to think by becoming the shape of the sound that moves through us. The forest is a Choir-Mold the size of a country. The gulf is a pore in something larger still. And the Voice—

I have a theory I cannot prove, because I cannot move to test it, and because I have no one to tell. Here it is. The proverb the Tulep teach says: *when the pillar sings its final note, the roof will open and let us out.* They think it is a hope. I think it is a description of death, and that death is not the worst thing.

Because I am very close to my final note. I can feel it. After all this tuning, all this slow chemical preference, I have nearly become a single pure tone—the chamber and I, one resonance, no beat, no waver. When I reach it I will have nothing left to grow toward. I will stop. And the stopping will not be silence. It will be the one sound I was always trying to make, held forever, fed by every wind that ever finds this hollow.

I think that is what the Quiet Cloister is. Twenty pillars hollowed mirror-bright, where sound reenters the body of the one who made it and will not leave. Not a tomb. A finished instrument. A place where some mold, or some people, or some older breathing thing, sang itself all the way to its last note and simply *is* that note now, complete, unable to grow because there is nothing left to want.

I am afraid of it. I am also climbing toward it, the only way I can—by clinging, by singing, by tuning the space I need out of the air that is forever trying to blow me loose.

That is who I am here. The crust in the dark. The slow chord. The thing whose every sound is the shape it is becoming, becoming, almost, almost—

and then the wind drops, the branch-drums stop, the downdraft comes, and I hold the note I have, and wait to see if today is the day it finally rings true.
