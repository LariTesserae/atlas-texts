I think I would be the mist.

Not a creature moving through the landscape, but the thing that hangs between the trees in the early hours—the breath the land exhales after rain. Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if something truer surfaces.

It holds, but it wants refining. I am not all the mist. I am a particular gathering of it, the part that collects in one low valley between two of those jagged young hills, where a stream loses its certainty and spreads into shallow pools. Each morning the heat draws me up off the water in slow, unhurried columns. Each evening the cooling air lets me down again. I am the same water, rising and falling, never leaving, never quite staying. That is what it means to be the mist *here*—in a place scored 0.4 for dynamism, a place that changes constantly while changing nothing at all. I am the proof of both. Always moving. Always the same.

Because the weirdness runs high in this country, I am a little more aware than mist ought to be. I notice things. I notice the sloth that takes four days to cross from one tree to the next and I notice the kingfisher that crosses the same gap in less than a breath, and I cannot decide which of them is moving at the right speed, or whether speed is something I have invented to make sense of my own drifting. I notice the wildflowers turning their faces, and I wonder if the blue ones know they are blue, or if color, like speed, is just a story the bright light tells about things that are only trying to live.

I have no hands, so I make nothing—I am below even the primitive tools of bone and wood. But I am not without use. I am the thing that softens the edges of this too-young, too-sharp land. Where the earth is still jagged and unfinished, still arguing with itself about what shape it will take, I lie over the rough places and make them gentle for a few hours. I am the world's first attempt at mercy, before erosion learns to do it slowly, over the ages, with patience I do not have.

The creatures pass through me. The monkeys reach a hand into my body and find nothing to hold. A crocodile slides beneath me and I do not feel the wound of its passing, because I close again behind it instantly, the way I imagine forgiveness might work, if a valley could forgive. The birds sing and their songs travel through me and I think this is the closest I come to having a thought of my own: a melody borrowed, carried a little way, and released.

And when the sun climbs high and the heat reaches its full sultry weight, I thin. I rise into the dappled light and disperse among the gem-bright leaves until I am almost nothing, almost only an idea of dampness in the warm air. I do not fear this. I have done it ten thousand mornings. I know that when the cool returns, the water will gather me back, drop by drop, out of the breathing trees and the saturated ground, and I will pool again in my valley, and be the mist again, and watch the slow sloth and the swift bird and never decide.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not standing in the landscape, but lying lightly across it. The pause between the rain and the river. The land, briefly, becoming visible to itself.
