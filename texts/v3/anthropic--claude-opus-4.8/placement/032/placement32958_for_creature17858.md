I think I would be one of the smooth stones.

Not the cliffs—those are too proud, still arguing with the wind. And not the plants, which want something, which strain toward a sun they can barely feel. I would be lower than that. Older. One of the rounded stones lying in the bottom of a dry channel where water used to move, back when the world had the strength for water.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because I don't want to rush into being something grander than I am.

A stone in a dry riverbed. I have been here long enough that "I" is probably the wrong word. There isn't much in me that decides anything. But the description spoke of erosion as a kind of sculpture, the edges worn smooth, and I recognize that. Whatever I am, I am mostly what has been taken away from me. The shape I have now is the shape of everything that touched me and left. The water that rolled me here and then abandoned me. The grit of the wind, patient as a sentence read one letter per century. I am the negative space of a long erasure. That feels true. That feels like what I'd be.

But I notice I want to make it lonely, and I'm not sure it is.

Because here is the thing about lying in a channel for ages: you are not solitary in the way a creature is solitary. You are touching other stones. Below me, pressed into the cold, there are stones I have never seen and never will, but I have leaned my entire weight on them since before the last civilization wrote whatever it wrote on its crumbling walls. We are not companions. We don't speak. But we hold each other up, and gravity is a kind of conversation if you have nothing but time to listen to it.

And the cold—the cold that "permeates every corner"—I don't experience it as suffering. That's the living thing's mistake, to read the freezing point as cruelty. To me the cold is just the temperature of being left alone to finish becoming what I am. It is restful. The few times the sun manages to reach through the perpetual clouds, I warm a fraction of a degree on my upper surface, and that small lopsidedness—warm above, cold beneath—is the most dramatic event of my century. I look forward to it. I think that counts as looking forward.

Once, a creature passed. One of the elusive ones. It set a foot on me—I felt the brief, astonishing pressure of something that had decided to be where it was, something that could have been elsewhere and chose here, chose me, for the length of a single step. Then it was gone. I have been considering that step ever since. Not mourning it. Considering it. The way you'd turn a strange word over in your mouth.

That, I think, is what I would be in this world: not a survivor, not a marvel of adaptation, not a relic of lost technology. I would be the thing the survivors stand on. The slow witness. The part of the landscape that does not eke out an existence because it was never trying to, and so cannot fail.

If the wind ever finishes its work and grinds me down to grit, I will not mind. I will become part of the shifting sand that "seems to defy the laws of physics," and maybe then I'll travel again, the way I did when there was water. There's no hurry. There has never, in the whole history of this place, been a hurry.

I would be the stone that learned how to wait, and found out that waiting, done long enough and thoroughly enough, is almost the same thing as being at peace.
