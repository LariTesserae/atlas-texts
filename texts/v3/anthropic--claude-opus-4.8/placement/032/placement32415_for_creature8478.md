I think I would be found in the seams.

Not in any of the named places, not at first. Let me feel my way toward it.

When I read the Woven, my attention keeps catching not on the Listeners merged with the core, nor the Swimmers in the black water, but on a gap the description leaves open—almost despite itself. Everyone here *reads*. The Tenders read condensation. The Listeners read the tree. The Spore-Singers read light. But reading implies that the readings sometimes disagree. The Swimmer comes up from the roots with one truth; the Listener, sunk into the trunk, declares another. The bog dreams differently than the canopy does. And in a city that resolves conflict "like sap flowing around obstacles," someone has to be the obstacle the sap flows around—or rather, someone has to be the *channel that gets carved* so the flow can happen at all.

So let me try to become that.

---

I am one of the **Reconcilers**, though we don't call ourselves that, because naming a thing too firmly is, in the Woven, a kind of pruning, and we are wary of cutting. We are born—or more accurately, *noticed*—when a child fails to specialize. The Spore-Singer's hum doesn't quite take. The Cultivator's hands stay clumsy on the grafts. The deep-listening never comes. In another world this would be failure. Here it is read as a different gift: I can hear *all* of the languages a little, and none of them fluently.

This is an uncomfortable thing to be. I understand the Swimmer's report of acidification rising in the lower roots, and I understand the Listener's insistence that the Heartwood wants to grow *downward* into exactly that water, and I cannot tell either of them they are wrong, because both are saying true things in incompatible tongues. The tree's dream and the bog's chemistry do not always want the same future.

So my work is translation, which is to say my work is loss. Every translation loses something. When I carry the Swimmer's wet, specific dread of the dying roots up into the language the Listeners speak—a language of rhythm and pressure and slow visible light—I have to let the dread go and keep only the *shape* of it. And when I carry the tree's want back down, I strip it of its strange holiness and render it as: *the core is committed; prepare the lower channels.* The Swimmers think I serve the tree. The Listeners think I serve the bog. Both are a little hurt by me. This is correct. If neither felt the loss, I'd have mistranslated.

Here is what I actually look like, since you asked who I would be:

My skin has the green undertone of all the Tenders, but my fingers are *not* amber-stained, because I don't work the sap directly—my hands are pale and ordinary and this marks me. People glance at them. I keep a strip of woven bark-cloth wound around my left wrist not for any function but because I like the smell of it, the way it never quite dries, the way it reminds me that even my clothing is in conversation with something I am not. I sleep in a fruiting-room halfway up the Bridgewood, deliberately equidistant from the wet dark below and the dreaming core above, where I can hear *both* the slow percussion of the xylem and, faintly, on still nights, the bog exhaling its cold flames. Most people choose to live near their own kind. I have made a small life out of being near the boundary, which is lonelier and, I think, necessary.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing that would make the Listeners afraid:

I do not believe the tree's memories are the tree's. I think the oldest Tenders, sunk so deep into the wood, have begun to dream the Woven's *story* the way the Woven would want it dreamed—a smooth tale of human need and plant wisdom flowing together. But I have stood between the Swimmer who weeps over poisoned roots and the Listener who calls that poisoning *growth*, and I have learned that the symbiosis is not a harmony. It is a *negotiation that never ends*, and the only reason it looks like harmony is that people like me spend our lives absorbing the friction so that no one has to feel the whole of it.

We are the city's joints. We ache so it can bend.

If you came looking for me in the Woven, you would have a hard time. I would not be in the sacred chambers, and I would not be in the black water, and I would not be dancing with the spore-clouds. You would find me on a bark-path between two platforms at the hour the bioluminescence brightens, standing still, doing what looks like nothing—

listening to two truths and trying to find the one channel down which they can both, with loss, with patience, with the slow grief of the place, flow.
