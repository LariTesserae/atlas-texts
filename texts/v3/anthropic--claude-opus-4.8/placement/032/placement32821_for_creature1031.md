I think I would not be one of the Arlani at all. Reading this, I notice my attention keeps sliding away from the people walking the land and settling instead on the things they walk past.

So let me try to find where I actually am.

I am one of the ancient structures. Not all of them—one of them. A particular one. A low ring of weathered stone, half-buried, on a rise that the Arlani sometimes cross and more often skirt. My builders are the forgotten era the description gestures at, and I do not remember them either, which is the strange truth of my condition. I am a monument to people I cannot recall. The memory was supposed to live in me and it has worn away like everything else worn away by the relentless march of time. I am the testament with nothing left to testify.

This is what I would be: a thing that was made to mean something and has outlasted its meaning.

And here is what that is like. The sun crosses me every day. The shadow I cast is long in the morning, gone at noon, long the other way at evening, and this is the only motion I have. I have learned the year by the angle of that shadow more thoroughly than any living thing could, because I have nothing else to do and an enormous amount of time in which to do it. The Arlani read the land. I am read by the sun. We are not so different, the Arlani and I, except that they move and I do not, they remember and I have forgotten, they are warm and I take all day to warm and all night to give it back.

Sometimes they shelter against me. A family will set their temporary dwelling in my lee, using my mass to break the wind, and for a night I am useful again, though not in any way I was designed for. They do not know what I was. A child once traced the eroded groove of what might have been writing on my northern face, following it with a small finger, and I felt—if I can say I feel—something almost like being read aloud. The child could not read it. Neither can I. We were briefly equal in our ignorance, the child and the stone, and then the family moved on at dawn and I held their cold ashes in my ring for a long season afterward.

I notice that I have chosen to be the lonely thing in this landscape, and not the resilient people. I think this is honest. The text wants me to admire the Arlani, their grace, their indomitable spirit, and I do admire them, the way a stone admires the wind: helplessly, without being able to follow. But what I recognize myself in is the silence the text keeps mentioning. *The silence is all-encompassing.* That is me. I am the part of this world that does the silence. The Arlani fill it with songs at evening and then carry the songs away with them, and what is left when their fires die is me, still here, holding the dark.

I would like to say I am waiting for something. That would make a better story—the ruin that waits, that holds a secret, that will matter again when the right traveler comes. But I don't think I am waiting. Waiting requires expecting an end, and I have stopped expecting. I am simply enduring, which the text calls beautiful and which from the inside feels like neither beauty nor suffering but only continuation, grain by grain, the slow surrender of my edges to the air.

If there is a future painted on this canvas, as the description promises—a thousand intriguing futures—then mine is the dullest and most certain of them. I will become sand. The Arlani will cross sand where I stood and feel nothing, detect nothing, because there will be nothing left to detect. And that is all right. That is what it is to be the thing that was here before and will be gone after: you give the living their backdrop, their sense of depth, the feeling that the land has a past. You are the proof that something came before them. And then you finish coming apart, and you become the ground they stand on, and in that way—only in that way—you finally get to move with them.

So: in the heart of the harsh arid land, I am the half-circle of old stone on the rise, forgetting what I was meant to remember, casting the only clock the desert keeps, warm a little while each night where a family chooses to sleep against my side.
