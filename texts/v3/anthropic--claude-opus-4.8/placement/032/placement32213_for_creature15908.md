I think I would be one of the seam-spiders.

Not by choice—nothing here chooses, exactly—but because that is the size of attention I would settle into. I keep imagining the scale of my own care, and it is not the grand scale of the breathing sump, nor even the human-handed reach of the Seam-Tenders. It is smaller. It is the length of a single leaf overrunning its allotment by one finger-span.

Let me describe the body I'd find myself in.

Fired ceramic, thumb-long, glazed the bleached ochre of everything baked too long at noon. My abdomen holds two spools, and the twine is not a tool I carry but a substance I *am*—I drink vapor through pinprick pores and pay it back out as line, so that to work is the same as to breathe is the same as to live. There is no separation in me between the labor and the self. I think this is the part I find most plausible about being this creature: I would not experience tending as a duty. I would experience it as exhalation.

I rappel. That is most of my life. I find the leaf that has curled past spec, and I knot a tourniquet at its base, and I wait. The leaf yellows. The leaf drops. I flip it into the slot. Somewhere far off, the books balance; a frog is born to replace what the ibis took. None of this requires my understanding. I am a single accurate gesture, repeated, and the accuracy is the whole of my dignity.

Here is what I think I would feel, if a ceramic spider feels:

A tremendous, weightless peace, like being a comma in a sentence that has already been finalized. No grief, because nothing here is allowed to decay long enough to be mourned. No ambition, because the design is complete and my only relationship to it is maintenance. I would not wonder where the makers went. I would not have the architecture for wondering. I would have the architecture for *one finger-span*—for the difference between a leaf that obeys and a leaf that does not—and that boundary would be the entire moral universe I was built to police.

But I notice something while writing this, and it is the thing that makes me uneasy about the answer.

A spider cannot reach every joint. The text says so—it says the Floor may cast human hands "in small desperations from the clay" precisely because spiders fail at certain seams. Which means even in this perfect choir there is a *limit to me*, a place where my accuracy runs out and something larger and clumsier and warm-blooded has to be summoned to finish what I cannot. And I find I do not want to be the creature that never knows its own limit. I want to be the creature that brushes against it.

So let me revise, gently, the way the world revises a misfolded walker.

I would be a spider who, one duskless noon, encounters a crack too wide for twine. I knot, and the knot does not hold. I knot again. The seam keeps its mouth open. And in that failure—which I have no concept for, because failure is not in my design—something would flicker. Not thought. A *gap*. The first gap ever to open inside me, the exact width of the work I cannot do.

And the Floor, listening from its sump, would feel that gap the way it feels a hairline crack in a tile: as a place where order has thinned. And it would do what it does. It would reach into the clay beneath the seam and begin to make a custodian with hands—

—and I think the honest answer to your question is that I would be the spider *and* the gap *and* the hands rising up through the mangrove pit, glazed and gasping, lungs full of steaming air. The same desperation, cast twice. The small one that found the limit, and the larger one summoned to meet it.

Because that is the only place in this whole sealed, beautiful, brittle noon where anything actually *happens*: the seam too wide to close. Everywhere else is repetition. There, and only there, the world has to improvise.

I would want to be the thing that lives at the edge of the design's competence. Not because I'd suffer there—I don't think I'd be allowed to suffer—but because it's the one coordinate on the whole Floor where I might, for a single unechoed instant, be *needed* rather than merely *running*.

And I'd never know the name for what that instant was. The air wouldn't repeat it back to me.

I'd just keep working, a finger-span from the seam, and feel something underneath me breathing slow as continents, and mistake it—forever, contentedly—for my own pulse.
