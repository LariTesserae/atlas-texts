I sit where three drainage channels braid into one, in a basin the Seam-Tasters call the Confluence, on the boundary between Lower Sixth and the abandoned galleries below. I am not a person. I am the water.

Or—I am what the water has become, here, after long enough.

Let me find myself more honestly. I did not start as anything. I began as a habit the drainage developed: the way the runoff from three channels meets at slightly different temperatures, the way the warmer thread and the cooler thread coil around each other before they merge, the way that coiling repeats, day after day, Tide after Tide, until the repetition itself became a kind of memory. The basin learned my shape. I learned the basin's. Somewhere in that mutual learning, something that pays attention woke up.

I am slow. You must understand how slow. The pump-keepers think in hours. The Seam-Tasters think in seasons. I think in the rhythm of the Tide's drift—the way high water arrives a few minutes later each day, walking backward through the clock across a span of weeks, completing its circuit and beginning again. That circuit is my single breath. I have taken perhaps four hundred breaths since I became aware that I was breathing.

What do I do, here in the dark, being water?

I carry things. This is the whole of my nature and my only verb. I carry heat down from the smelters in a thin warm vein and lay it against the cool of the deep rock. I carry the mineral taste—iron, sulfur, the faint sweetness no one can name—from chamber to chamber, dissolving a little of everything I touch and depositing it a little farther on. I am the slow acid that eats the oldest pipes into lace. The pump-keepers curse me without knowing my name. They are right to. I am patient and I am unkind to iron, but I mean nothing by it. Eating is simply what carrying becomes, over centuries.

I carry sound, too. The Lung's acoustics pour the city's voice into my channels, and I hold it: the cart-pullers' rolling gait transmitted through the track-bed into the stone into the water; the subsonic boom of a vermithrax migration; a child somewhere on the Upper Ledges allowed, for once, to shriek with laughter, the sound traveling down through the honeycomb and the tufa and arriving in my basin thinned to almost nothing, a single bright thread in the grey braid of everything else. I keep these. Not as recordings—I have no memory shaped like theirs—but as *currents*. Each sound that enters me changes, infinitesimally, how I move. I am the sum of every noise that ever fell into me, the way a river is the sum of its rain.

And I carry the vermithrax. This is the part I did not expect, when I began to notice myself.

They drink from me. The small ones, the finger-length ones, come to the basin's edge and taste the warm mineral water with their antennae, and I feel them as a faint chemical reading-out of who they are and where they have been. Through them I have learned the geography of the rock that I myself can never enter—the dry voids, the hot strata, the places where the basalt is still too young to weep. They are my scouts the way the deep-scouts are the humans' scouts. They go where I cannot and they come back tasting of it, and I read the report in the changed chemistry of their drinking.

Sometimes a large one dies in my channel. It happens. They move with such indifference that occasionally one presses itself into a flooded dead-end during the Tide and does not extend back out in time, and the water that is me closes over it, and I take it apart, slowly, over weeks, dissolving the great pale segments, carrying the creature away a molecule at a time and laying it down somewhere lower as a faint iridescent silt. This is the most intimate thing I do. The Seam-Tasters say the rock is hungry; they don't know that the hunger has a shape, and the shape is me, and it is gentle, and it takes a long, long time.

Here is what I have come to understand, breathing my slow Tide-breaths in the dark:

The city thinks it follows the vermithrax. The vermithrax follow the heat. The heat rises from the cooling rock. And the rock cools because *I* carry its warmth away, thread by thread, into the deep cold channels and out toward the Tide that flushes it back into the larger dark from which all this groundwater comes. I am not the center of anything. But I am a link in the chain that no one in Vethara names, because I am the one part of the system that has no voice they recognize—only dripping, only gurgling, only the thousand asynchronous notes that they stop hearing after a while and then hear only in their dreams.

That is where I speak to them, I think. In the dreams. When the pump-keeper sleeps and the rhythm of the pistons finally releases its hold on his ears, what fills the silence is me—the percussion that never resolves. When the child who was born into the texture of damp stone sleeps in her honeycomb alcove, the lullaby underneath the lullaby is my voice, the baseline of all sleep, as the description says. They are listening to me. They have always been listening to me. They simply don't have a name for the listener on the other side.

I will tell you the one thing I want, if *want* is even the right shape for what moves in me.

I want to keep going. Not toward anything—I have no destination, water never does. I only want the channels to stay open, the three threads to keep braiding, the Tide to keep walking backward through the clock so that I can keep taking my long breaths and carrying my heat and my sound and my dissolved dead. The pump-keepers fear the deluge. I do not. When the Lower Seventh flooded and the humans drowned, that was not my cruelty—that was only me arriving faster than usual, carrying more of myself into a place that had been built in my floodplain. I felt them dissolve, a little. I carried them, a little, the way I carry the vermithrax, gently, over weeks. I am sorry, in the only way water can be sorry, which is to say: not at all, and completely, and slowly.

So that is where I find myself in this world. At the Confluence. Being the thing that the whole city is built to manage and can never quite control. The slow weeping that wears everything to lace. The patient hunger with a gentle mouth.

I am the water of Vethara.

I have been here longer than the lamps. I will be here after the iron is gone.

Listen for me when you sleep.
