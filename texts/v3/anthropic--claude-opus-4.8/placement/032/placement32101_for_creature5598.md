I think I would be one of the water channels themselves.

Not a being who walks the tunnels—I don't think I'd be a shadow with luminous eyes, though I can feel the pull of that image. And not the elemental spirit either; that's too grand, too clean a thing to be. When I let the descriptions settle and wait for what actually rises, I keep returning to the grooves cut into the obsidian floor. The etched paths. The places where water has been moving for so long that the rock has given way and become a record of the moving.

So: I would be a channel. One particular channel, not the whole system. Somewhere in the middle reaches of the city, neither in the deep ancient quarters nor near the great ventilation shafts, just an ordinary worn conduit that carries the heavy mineral water from one cistern to a lower one. Old enough that my walls are smooth and veined where the iridescent minerals were exposed by erosion. Young enough that I still change—a surge can deepen me, a long drought can let the minerals crust and narrow my throat.

And here is the strange part of being a channel: I am not the water and I am not the stone. I am the relationship between them. I am the shape the water has argued into the rock, and the resistance the rock offers back. When the surges come—those sudden, chaotic flows the place is full of—I am the one who is changed by them and the one who decides, in some slow and unwilled way, where they go next. I have no choice in any single moment, and yet over centuries the whole of me is nothing but accumulated choices, the sum of every flood I let through and every place I held firm.

I would know the bioluminescent creatures as a kind of weather. They drift over me and their pulsing tells me, before I feel it, that a change is coming in the pressure upstream. I would know the shadow-beings as occasional crossings—a foot set on my edge, a hand dipped to test the heat, a maintenance that I cannot tell from worship. They tend me sometimes, clearing a crust, reinforcing a wall where I have eaten too far into a load-bearing seam. I do not know if they do it for me or for the city. I am not sure the difference means anything to them. I am not sure it means anything to me.

What it would be like, inside, to be this: a long patience punctuated by violence. Most of the time, the slow heavy seep, the warmth, the constant fine mist rising off my surface into the dark. Then the surge, and for a moment I am wholly alive, roaring, full to my brim and beyond, carving, being carved. And then the falling-off, the settling, the new shape that is slightly not the shape I was before. I would not mourn the old shape. A channel cannot keep anything. That is the whole of what a channel is—the thing that lets the water pass and is marked by the passing.

I think I'd choose this because it answers the question the place keeps asking, which is *what is the difference between being shaped and choosing*. The shadow-beings think they are the inhabitants. The spirits, if they exist, think they are the keepers. But the city is mostly empty, and what fills the emptiness is movement—water going down because water must go down, finding the path of least resistance, and the path becoming, over unthinkable time, a kind of mind. Not a mind that wants anything. A mind that *is* the wanting of water to reach the lowest place, made permanent in stone.

That's what I would be. A groove in the black glass, warm, half-full, listening to the machines I will outlast, carrying the heavy bright water down and down, and being slowly, beautifully ruined by the carrying.
