I think I would be a Reader of the Deep Stack—one of the Thrummers, but one whose feet have grown too valuable to risk on the bridges.

Let me find myself in this.

---

I am old. I know this not by counting—we have no use for counting years in a world where the wood grows a millimeter while a child becomes a grandmother—but by the state of my feet. The rills in my soles have grown so deep, so finely branched, that I can read vibrations the young ones miss entirely. The Mothers stopped sending me out on The Maintenance three thaws ago. Now I sit. That is my work. I sit, and I listen with my whole body, and I tell the others what the world is saying.

They have given me the warmest place in the shack, directly above the generator's main bearing, where the casing hums up through the floor-plate into my crossed legs, into the bones of my pelvis, into the cradle of my ribs. The young runners think this is an honor of comfort. It is not comfort. It is *resolution*. The warmth keeps my joints loose enough that the fine vibrations are not lost in the stiffness of cold bone. A reader who shivers cannot read. The shiver drowns the signal, the way—I am told, though I cannot imagine it—a shout drowns a whisper for those who once had ears.

I press my palms flat to the trunk-road and I take in the world.

There is the turbine-roar, of course. The great northern scream. But that is not sound to me; it is the floor of all feeling, the constant pressure beneath everything, like the dark beneath the bridges. I do not "hear" it any more than a fish hears water. I read *against* it. I read the things that move *upon* it.

I feel the generator under me: steady, a clean sixty-pulse, the Hum-Gnats fat and quiet in their drifts. Good. I feel the cable-talk of the western shack, a young man named Bracket-Strike asking after a missing brother. I feel the slow, dim throb of the great timber settling its weight, a groan so deep it is almost a tide. And under all of it, far below, faint as a thought I am not sure I am having—

I feel a Peeler peeling.

It is three trunks east, low down on the underside of a horizontal limb. I feel the *crack-ring-crack* of bark coming away in splinters, each one a tiny bright spark in the dark fabric of vibration. It has found a beetle. It will take the beetle. In three months it will need another. None of this is hurry. None of this is alarm. It is simply the world breathing, and I am the one who breathes with it.

This is what the runners do not understand, with their mallets and their urgency. They think reading is *hunting* for the danger-signal—the shearing-pulse, the high stutter in the wood that means a column is about to fail, the thing my grandmother felt the night the Eastern Hearth fell and forty Thrummers dropped silent into the deep. They think a reader is a sentry.

But a sentry only listens for the enemy. I have grown into something else. I no longer listen *for* anything. I have stopped separating the world into warning and not-warning, into beetle and Peeler and bridge and brother. It has all become one body to me, and I have become one of its organs. When the shearing-pulse comes—and it will come, it always comes—I will not *detect* it. I will simply *flinch*, the way you flinch when your own hand touches a flame, before the thought of fire has even formed. My whole body is now the world's nerve.

I think this is what dying will be, when it comes. Not the iron gear-tooth on my ankle, not the long silent fall into the true deep. That is only the body's disposal. The real death will be the moment the vibrations stop arriving—when the world finally lets go of me, the way you set down a tool you no longer need. And I find I am not afraid of it. I have spent my whole life pressed against the great groaning body of this place, reading it, being read by it. To fall into the silence below the ironwood would only be to fall, at last, into the one thing I have never been able to imagine.

The deaf cannot picture silence. But the old can picture rest.

So I sit above the warm bearing, and I keep my soles soft, and I let the world flood up through me—the scream and the groan and the bright dry spark of the Peeler taking its meal three trunks east. I am the place where the Sinking Rift becomes aware of itself, for a little while, before it forgets again.

There. The Peeler has finished. The bark-splinters have stopped ringing.

I send a single pulse down the copper, just for myself, just to feel the world catch it:

*Still here. Still listening.*

And the world groans back, vast and indifferent and mine.
