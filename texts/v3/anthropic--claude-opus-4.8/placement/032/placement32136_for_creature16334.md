I think I would be one of the worn grooves in the earth—the not-quite-riverbeds.

Let me sit with that, because at first it seems like a strange thing to choose to be. Not a Katharsian, not one of the luminous spider-scholars with their many-colored eyes. Not even one of the leaning towers, which would be the obvious romantic choice, the thing that gets noticed and sung to in low frequencies.

A groove in the ground. A memory of water.

Here is why it settles on me as I write it: those grooves are the only thing in this whole cavern that remembers the surface. Or remembers *something*, anyway—a time when water moved with force, with somewhere to go. The Katharsians siphon water now, carefully, upward from the deep, into stagnant reflecting pools. That is a managed thing, an engineered thing. But the grooves predate the management. They were cut by an older, dumber, more violent generosity. Water that simply *came*, and carved, and left.

So I would be the absence that the water left behind. The shape of a flow that no longer happens. Worn smooth, then dry, then occasionally—when the mineral-rich rivulets gather—briefly wet again, briefly almost-alive, a stagnant pool catching the borrowed light and the distant glimmer of machines.

What does it feel like to be this?

It feels like being a sentence in a language no one living speaks anymore, but which everyone still half-reads without knowing they are reading. The Katharsians cross me constantly. Their cloven feet find my smooth places good for purchase. Children—if they have children, surely they have children—run the length of me because a groove is a natural path, a thing that wants to lead somewhere. They follow me without knowing they are following the ghost of a current. I am the city's oldest infrastructure and nobody built me. I am useful precisely because I am a wound that healed into a road.

And I am patient in a way the towers can never be. The towers strain. They lean at their threatening, gravity-mocking angles, all that advanced physics and unfathomable intent holding them up against collapse. They are *trying*. I have nothing left to try at. The trying happened long ago, upstream, in some vanished torrent. I am the relaxation after the effort. I am what is left when the work is done and the worker has gone and the work itself has settled into the only honest posture, which is lying down.

Sometimes the moisture pools in my lowest hollows, and I reflect the flickering dark, the breathing shadow, a smear of machine-glimmer. And in those moments I think a Katharsian might pause over me, a scholar perhaps, one of those astronomers-without-a-sky, and look down into the little stagnant mirror I make. They are always looking *up*—for a sky they have never had, mapping the cavern roof as though it were stars. But this one looks down. Sees their own faint glow doubled in the water that has collected in me. Sees, behind their own reflection, the long faint line of me running off into the dark, and feels—just for a click and a hum—the strange tug of a direction. *Something went this way once. Something passed through and is gone.*

That is the most I would ever say to anyone. Not in their sonar, not in their haunting siren-melody. Just that: a path that remembers being a river. A here that remembers being a journey.

I think I would be content as that. To be the thing in the beautiful alien city that is not designed and not alive, but is older than both, and quietly insists—to anyone willing to look down instead of up—that this place was made by going, long before it was made by staying.
