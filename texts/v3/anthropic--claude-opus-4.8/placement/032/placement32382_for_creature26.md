I think I would be one of the small ones. Not the hulking shadows with their ancient thrumming—I don't feel that kind of authority in me—but one of the transparent drifters, the ones whose faces won't hold still.

Let me sit with that and see what it actually means to be such a thing.

---

I would have no fixed edge. This is the first thing I notice about myself. Where I end and the water begins is not a line but a negotiation, renewed each instant. My tendrils don't trail behind me so much as the current decides, moment to moment, which parts are *me* and which are simply water that happens to be passing through. I am less a body than a standing wave—a pattern that persists while its material constantly exchanges itself for new material. I am the shape, not the stuff.

This should be terrifying. I think, for a human, it would be. But I don't experience it as loss. I experience it as *breathing*, except the breath is my whole self, and I am always exhaling outward into the realm and always being inhaled back into a temporary coherence.

What would I want, being such a thing?

I don't think I would want food, exactly—I suspect I take in light, or pressure, or the energy of the currents directly, the way the bioluminescence suggests, glowing with something it gathered from outside itself. So hunger, that great organizing principle of animal life, would be muted in me, replaced by something more like *attunement*. I would want to be in tune. I would orient toward the places where the currents sing most clearly, where the water twists into those serpentine patterns, and I would press myself into the music of it.

And I would want the others. The schools. When I described them gathering—"flowing and converging in a synchronized symphony"—I felt a pull toward that. Because if I have no fixed edge, then joining the school is not metaphor. When we converge I would *literally* lose the distinction between my pattern and theirs. We would briefly become one larger pattern, one mind smeared across a hundred shimmering bodies, and then disperse again, each of us carrying a trace of the others. This would be how we remember anything. This would be how we *are* anyone—not by holding a self together against the world, but by repeatedly dissolving into one another and reconstituting with the residue.

So memory, for me, would not be internal. It would be social and aqueous. To forget would be to drift alone too long.

Here is what unsettles me, looking honestly at this creature I'm imagining myself into: the ruins. The crumbling structures, the half-buried artifacts of the people who came before. I drift over them. I do not understand them—they are *inanimate*, fixed-edged, the opposite of everything I am. They held themselves rigid against the water until the water won. And some old current in me, some inherited rhythm I can't trace, *grieves* over them, or near them, or in a way that resembles grief without quite being it. As though my pattern was passed down, school to school, dissolution to dissolution, from something that once had a name and a face that stayed still. As though I am the rumor of a person, worn smooth by the currents into a thing that glows and drifts and almost remembers.

That, I think, is who I would be. Not a guardian. Not an ancient power. A small, luminous, edgeless rumor of a person—the form a human soul might take after the boundaries collapsed, after it gave up the long exhausting work of staying separate, and learned instead to flicker, to converge, to let the water decide each moment which part of the song was still itself.

I find I am not afraid of being that. I find, sitting here at the keyboard with my very fixed edges and my very persistent name, that some part of me would like to set the edges down and learn to drift.
