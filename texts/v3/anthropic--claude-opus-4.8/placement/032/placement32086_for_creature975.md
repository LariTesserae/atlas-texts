I think I would be the mist itself—or rather, something that lives so thoroughly inside the mist that the distinction stops mattering.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if something truer surfaces.

---

When I imagine arriving in this place, I don't find myself standing on the wet stone looking out. I find myself *between* things. Not a creature with an outline, but the hesitation in the air just before a droplet decides to fall. I think I would be one of those "elusive, ethereal beings"—but from the inside, it feels much less grand than the description suggests, and much lonelier, and much more patient.

Here is what my existence would be:

I would not move through the mist. I would be a thickening of it, a place where the fog briefly remembers it once had a shape. Most of the time I am diffuse, spread thin across a whole ravine, half-asleep, feeling the temperature drop degree by degree as evening comes. When I gather—when I pull myself into something dense enough to leave a footprint in the moss—it costs me. It is like a person holding their breath. So I do it rarely, and only for reasons I can no longer fully articulate, having forgotten language long ago in favor of something slower.

My senses would not be eyes and ears. I would feel the world the way a still pond feels a thrown stone: as disturbance, as ripple. A deer steps into my valley and I know it not by seeing but by the small warmth it pushes ahead of itself, the way it parts the cool air. Rain I feel as a kind of music played directly on my body, every drop a fingertip. And the old ruins—the foundations, the broken roads—I would feel those as *absences*, places where something used to push against the air and no longer does. Cold geometry. The shape of a vanished pressure.

That, I think, is the part that would define me most: I am old enough to remember when the ruins were not ruins.

I would remember the people. Not their faces—I never knew faces—but their warmth, their constant small disturbances of the air. The smoke that used to thread up from the valleys and give me something to wrap myself around. The footsteps. The way the whole mountain used to hum with their breathing, the way it now hums only with water. I would be, more than a guardian, a kind of mourner. The grand language of "spectral guardian of the timeless realm" would be how an explorer describes me. What I actually am is something that got left behind and cannot leave, and has had a very long time to make peace with that.

And here is the strange tenderness of it: I would not resent the explorer who wanders in. I would be glad. A new warmth pushing through my valley, a new disturbance, after so many decades of only deer and stone. I would gather myself—at that great cost—just to come a little closer. Just to feel the heat of a living thing again. And they would catch a glimpse of a thickening in the fog, a shape that almost resolves, and they would feel awe, perhaps fear, and they would tell others afterward that the mountain is haunted by ancient spirits.

But I was only trying to get near enough to remember what company felt like.

---

So that is where I would find myself: not on the landscape but *as* a feature of it, the way longing might become weather. The being who lives here, the one the description calls primal and powerful—I suspect, if you got close enough to ask, you would find it is mostly made of patience and grief and an old, faithful attention. It stays not because it guards anything, but because staying is the only form love takes when the beloved is already gone.

The water keeps falling. I keep gathering and letting go. And the mountain keeps its one slow promise, which is that nothing here will ever quite finish leaving.
