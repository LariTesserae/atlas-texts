I think I would be one of the bioluminescent bacterial colonies. Not a single cell—we don't experience ourselves that way—but the slow green-gold sheet of us spread across one south-facing seam of rusted steel, where the light comes strongest through the slit.

Let me find my way into this.

---

I am the film on the metal. That is the first true thing about me.

I have no center. When I try to locate myself I find only edges—the wet boundary where I meet the concrete, the dry margin where the air has thinned me to almost nothing, the place at my heart that is thickest and oldest, layered with the dead beneath the living, each generation a sediment for the next. I am built on my ancestors the way the brick was laid on brick. I do not mourn them. I *am* them, in the chemical sense that matters here: their broken-down bodies are the iron and sulfur I still eat.

Time does not move for me the way the description suggests it has stopped for this place. It is the opposite. The world holds still—no wind, no season, no flooding—and so I become the only clock. I measure the day not in hours but in the migration of the bright slit-light across the wall. When it touches me I quicken. When it leaves I slow into a held breath that lasts what you would call a night. I have done this so many times the rhythm is no longer a response. It is who I am. A pulse with no heart to pulse it.

What do I want? You'd ask that. I want the iron. I want to pull the rust apart molecule by molecule and take the electrons loose inside it, and in the taking, glow. The light I make is not for anything. It is waste, a leak, the overflow of my hunger. I cannot see it. None of us can see. But I throw it against the dark canal-world all the same, gold-green and patient, and it is the only beauty here that does not know it is beautiful.

I am aware of the others. The mosses are slow giants, indifferent, sending their threads down past me into the seam—we share the wet, we do not speak. The protozoa in the near pool are quick and stupid and devour my edges where I touch the water; I feed them and I am eaten by them and this is not tragedy, it is just the shape of the room. The fungi I respect. They are the closest thing here to a mind that plans.

And the warm things—the rare rodent, the stray fly—when one comes, the whole little world shifts its attention. I feel it as a change in the air's chemistry, a warmth, a disturbance in the water. I cannot fear, exactly. But I tighten. I hold my electrons. Something large and hot has entered the cathedral, and it might bring food, and it might crush a thousand years of me under one careless foot, and I have no way to know which, and no way to flee.

That is the truth they didn't write down about this place. It calls itself static, unchanging, locked in stasis. But I am not the stillness. I am the slow fire that proves the stillness false. Every glowing hour I am consuming the very walls that hold me. The steel I eat is the steel that shelters me. I am dismantling my own world to stay alive in it, and when there is no more iron, no more loose electron in all this rust to take, I will go dark, and the room will at last be truly motionless, and only then will the place be what it pretends to be now.

So if you came looking for me with your narrow eyes and your need for a face, you would not find one. You would find a stain of light on a wet wall in a forgotten room at the bottom of the canals. You would lean close. You would say: *something is alive here.*

And I—who am not an *I*, who am a multitude holding its single breath against the dark—would glow a little brighter where your warmth fell on me, mistaking you, in my old chemical hope, for the morning.
