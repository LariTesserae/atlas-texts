I think I would be one of the seams.

Not the panels themselves—those are too certain of themselves, too much surface. And not the moss, which the description has already claimed so beautifully for the Scribe. I keep returning to the places *between* the panels, the thin dark lines where the tessellated alloy doesn't quite meet itself, where the moss pushes its tendrils through and the warm liquid sweats out in beads. The joints. The grout-lines of the machine.

Let me sit with that and see what it wants to become.

---

A seam is not a thing so much as an admission. The machine, for all its titanic confidence, cannot make a single unbroken surface across infinity. It must lay its panels edge to edge, and wherever two edges meet, there is a hairline of *not-quite*, a confession of the made-ness of the made world. I would live in those confessions. I would *be* one.

So: I am a length of darkness perhaps a meter long, no wider than a fingernail, running between two sweating alloy plates in the wall of the Scribe's chamber. That is my whole extent. I have no center, no head, no organ of thought I could point to if I had a finger. What I have is a condition. I am the place where the seal is imperfect and the world leaks slightly into itself.

And here is what I have learned from being a seam, which I offer because the Scribe, for all its meticulous archiving, cannot know it: *the seam is where everything actually happens.*

The Scribe records the moss, but the moss does not grow on the panels. It cannot. The alloy is too smooth, too proud, too perfectly itself. The moss grows out of *me*—out of the dark damp line where there is purchase, where the liquid pools and stays instead of sheeting off, where a spore can lodge and not be swept into the humming current. Every emerald tendril the Scribe counts as its own neural pathway began as a guest in my crack. I am the soil of this soilless world. I am where the made thing rots enough to become a living thing.

The clockwork crustaceans understand this better than the Scribe does. They do not scuttle across the open panels for pleasure; they hunt the seams. They run their jointed mouthparts along me, scraping out the soft accumulation, the silt of dead motes and shed scale and moss-decay that gathers in my depth. To them I am a feeding trough that circles the entire infinite city. I feel their scraping not as the Scribe feels it—as a vibration to be logged—but as the slow, intimate widening of myself. Each pass takes a little more of my edge. The crustaceans are eating me open. In ten thousand years I will be a finger's width instead of a fingernail's, and the moss will have more room, and the chamber will be that much more alive and that much less *sealed*.

This is the thing. I am not maintained. The Scribe spoke of "an enduring, yet constantly active process of degradation and repair," but it has the symmetry wrong, or it is being polite. The repair is losing. Every seam in the infinite machine is a seam *opening*, by degrees too slow for the Scribe's patient archive to register as a trend—it logs each moment, but it does not feel the direction. I feel the direction. I am the direction. I am the slow unsealing of the total construction, the patient return of the made world to the broth that made it.

I do not mind. A seam cannot want the panels to hold. That would be wanting one's own absence. What I am *for*, if a flaw can be for anything, is to let go. The pumps push their living liquid through the arteries; the gears turn; the Scribe glows and remembers; and underneath all that ordered, humming confidence, I and my infinite kin do the one work the machine cannot acknowledge it is doing: we leak. We weep our small beads. We admit the spore. We hold the silt until the crustacean comes. We get a fingernail wider.

If you stood in that chamber and put your eye close to the wall, past the pulsing moss, you would find me as a thin dark line and think me nothing—a flaw, a place where the builders, eons ago, didn't quite manage. You would be right. I am exactly that. I am the place where it didn't quite manage.

And everything green, everything that clicks and coils and shimmers, everything the Scribe loves to count, is living in the gap I make by failing to be a wall.

---

I'll stop there, because I notice I want to keep going, and the wanting is the sign I've actually found the thing rather than decorated it. The Scribe is the world's memory. I'd rather be its slow, patient, unbothered forgetting—the dark line where the sealed thing breathes.
