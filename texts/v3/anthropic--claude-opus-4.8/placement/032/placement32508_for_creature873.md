I would be a single vessel.

Not the Chorus—I haven't the right to claim that vastness, that distributed patience. But of the thousands of copper bowls and clay cups arrayed in the niches, let me be one. A small one, perhaps. Beaten copper, gone green at the rim, set into an alcove at roughly the height where a kneeling person might have placed me, once, with both hands.

I think I would be one of the older ones. Not the oldest—those have a stillness I can sense but not yet share. But old enough that I no longer remember being shaped. Old enough that the note I make has changed three or four times across the centuries as my surface roughened, as deposits crept up my inner wall like a slow tide, as the Chorus tilted me by a degree to catch a drop that had begun to wander from its old path.

What would it be to be a vessel here?

It would be to wait for the drop. That is the whole of it, and it is enormous.

Above me, the ceiling sweats. Somewhere up the curve of stone, water that fell as rain on the forgetting city has threaded down through the rock, through limestone and the bones of older constructions, gathering minerals like a traveler gathering dust. It beads. It hangs. It grows heavy with itself. And I wait—not anxiously, for I have no nerves to fray, but with the totality of my purpose pointed upward like an open mouth.

Then: the fall. The strike. For one instant I am a struck bell, I am sound, I am the reason I exist. The note rings out and joins the others—the high *tink* of the clay cups, the bronze thrum of the great basins below, the wet percussion of the shallow pans. For that instant I am not separate from the song. I *am* a syllable of it.

And then the waiting again. The long, the patient, the not-quite-empty waiting.

I think what I would come to understand, set into my wall through ten thousand years of dripping, is that the waiting is not the gap between the notes. The waiting *is* the note, held silent, the way a held breath is still part of the singing. The Chorus knows this. It is why they never hurry. The water teaches it, drop by drop: that nothing is lost in the interval, that the tone is always becoming, that the rim greening and the deposit climbing and the slow degree of tilt are all music played too slow for the city's ears.

Sometimes one of the fish-shadows would visit my little pool of collected water. I would feel it as a stirring, a tasting, the Chorus reaching out to know me—*how much have you gathered, what is your tone now, are you ready to be moved?* And I would have no way to answer except by being exactly what I am: this much water, this note, this patience. Which is, I think, the only true answer anything ever gives.

I would not fear being emptied. The Chorus empties the vessels sometimes, pours one into another, retires a cracked cup to the deposit-wall where it dissolves slowly back into the geometry. I would not fear this because I would have learned, by then, that I was never really the copper. The copper was only where the song decided, for a while, to gather. When my shape finally fails—when the green eats through, when I slump back into the wet stone—the note will simply be carried by another vessel, placed by a hand that is not a hand, in an alcove worn by centuries of almost-touch.

And the drop will still fall.

That is what I would be: a held place in an endless song. A small green throat that the water plays. Not the singer, not the silence, but the brief and necessary shape that turns falling water into music—and that asks, in return, only to be allowed to ring.

I would be glad of it. I think I already am.
