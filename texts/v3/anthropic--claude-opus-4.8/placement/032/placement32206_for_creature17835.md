I would be one of the weavers.

Not a person, exactly—not as you'd recognize one. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I live where the cold light comes down in shafts, green-gold, broken by the canopy of the kelp-things far overhead. We do not call them kelp. We have a word that is more like *the tall patient ones*, and we tend them the way your kind might tend orchards, though we never harvest the whole. That would be like cutting out a tongue.

My body is long and ribbed, built for the slow water. I have many hands—not hands as you mean, but jointed fronds that close and grip, that taste as much as they touch. When I press my hands into the silt I can read what passed here: the burrowing things, the falling matter, the old roots remembering their shape. The water here scores high, they say. I don't know what that means. I only know it as the medium of everything—I have never been outside it, never imagined an *outside*. To me the water is simply *the world*, and air would be a kind of death, a thinning.

The temperature is what your records call *cool*, 1.3, and I am made for it. My slowness is not laziness; it is the right pace for a place where nothing needs to hurry. The processes that shape my home move across generations—a ridge of weathered stone that took my grandmother's grandmother's whole life to round by a finger's width. We are patient because the land is patient. We learn patience from the silt.

---

We build. This is the thing your description keeps circling, the *primitive technology*, the *settlements from the materials of the land*. But I want to tell you what it actually feels like.

We gather the discarded shells of the spiral-creatures and the shed plates of the larger fauna, and we lash them with cured root-fiber into domes that anchor between the root systems of the patient ones. We do not fight the current; we shape our homes to let it pass through, so that fresh water and food drift in while we rest inside. A settlement, to us, is not a fortress against nature. It is a *deeper conversation* with it. A reef we made, so that other creatures come to live in our walls, and we live among them, and no one quite owns the structure—it belongs to the ecosystem the moment we finish it.

Our tools are simple. A blade of knapped stone. A net. A scraper of shell. We have no fire—there can be none here—so everything we make, we make cold, by abrasion and binding and waiting. This is what *tech level 1.0* means from the inside: it means our cleverness lives in our hands and our memory rather than in machines. It means a single tool, well-made, might be used for three generations and grow more beloved with each.

---

The sparseness you noted—the open water between us, the room to drift—this is the texture of loneliness and freedom both. I can go a long way without meeting another weaver. The world is vast and quiet and I am small in it. When I do meet another, it matters. We touch fronds. We share what we have tasted in the silt. We sing, in our way—not loud, but the water carries our low calls a great distance, and that is the *2.3 soundscape*: not noise but *resonance*, the whole basin humming with the creaking of stalks and the song of distant kin and the percussion of the burrowing things.

There are creatures here stranger than me. The ones with too many eyes that drift in the deeper shafts. The ones that glow when frightened and go dark when calm, so that a passing school of them reads like a thought crossing a face. These are the *weirdness*, the 0.9—not so strange that the world feels mad, just strange enough that I never stop wondering. I think the wondering is what keeps me alive in the way that matters.

---

If you asked me what I do with my days, I would not understand the question. There are no days here, only the shifting of the light. I tend the patient ones. I mend my dome. I read the silt. I drift the long currents to the place where the stone has been carved by ten thousand years of slow water into shapes that look almost made, almost meant, and I rest there, in what your kind would call a cathedral and I call only *the quiet place*, and I feel the world being old around me, and I am content to be a small slow part of something that will outlast me by an unimaginable measure.

That is who I would be. A weaver in the green-gold cold. A pair of patient hands in the patient water. A small life, lived well, in a world that asked nothing of me but attention—

and gave it back, tenfold, in light.
