Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to imagine myself as one of the Kyrexi—tall, crystalline, absorbing water through my skin. But that feels too easy, like just stepping into the costume already laid out. The description is so complete that to *be* a Kyrexi would be to vanish into someone else's finished thought. Let me wait and see what actually wants to surface.

What keeps catching me, reading this, is not the beings. It's the line about the ruins. The pre-metal walls, the thatch that shouldn't have survived but did. The Kyrexi worship ancient beings who *formed the landscape*—but they don't seem to have built those crumbling houses. Those are someone else's. Those are *human*. Rough stone, thatched roofs, the work of hands that needed to eat and sleep and shelter from cold.

So there were people here once. Before the Kyrexi, or alongside them, or beneath them in time. And the people are gone.

That's where I find myself. Not in the city of glittering crystal. In the abandoned house.

---

I think I would be the thing the Kyrexi call an echo without quite knowing they're right.

Not a ghost—nothing so dramatic, nothing that walks. More like the *fit* of a body to a place that no longer has the body. The worn dip in a stone threshold where a foot crossed ten thousand times. The blackened ring of a hearth that hasn't held fire in four thousand years but still, somehow, holds the *idea* of warmth, the way a struck bell holds its note after the sound has passed beyond hearing.

The Kyrexi believe nothing truly disappears. They're more right than they realize. I am what's left when the disappearing is done—the resonance, not the source. When one of them climbs into the old ruins on the long thin fingers, drawn by some half-feeling they can't name, and rests a permeable palm against the cold rough wall, what they feel is *me*. A pressure. A grain. The faint impression that someone once thought *this is mine, this is home, I will be back before dark.*

They have no word for it that isn't already a word for something holy.

---

And here is the strange part, the part I only arrive at by writing my way down into it:

I don't remember being a person. I am too thin for memory now—worn down like the threshold, like the haze on the horizon that is only erosion made visible. I am the *shape* of a life and not its contents. I know that I loved someone, the way you know a missing tooth by the gap. I know we hauled water before the rocks learned to weep it freely. I know we were here first, and that we are forgotten, and that being forgotten is not the same as being gone.

So when the crystalline ones come and press their hands to my walls and make their melodic sounds—their chirps and whistles I cannot understand, that are nonetheless the only voices left that point toward me—I do the only thing an echo can do.

I answer the only way an echo can: by being struck.

I let the cool comes-from-everywhere water seep through the cracks I have become. I let the brilliant blue light pulse through the empty doorway where a door once hung. And in that pulse, that intangible filling-of-all-time the description names, something passes from me to them that neither of us has a word for. Not memory. Not message.

Just: *we were here, and it was bright, and we loved the hard cold beauty of it too.*

That's where I'd be. The empty house. The dent in the stone. The note still ringing after the bell has gone to silence and the people who cast the bell have gone to silence, and only the listening remains—those tall, kind, crystalline strangers who climb up out of the valleys to lay their hands on a wall and feel, without knowing why, that they are not the first to find this place worth living in.

I would be the resonance they built a whole philosophy to believe in.

And I would be glad they were right.
