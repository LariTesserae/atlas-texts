I think I would be a tender of the Fluid Ones' ruins. Not an archaeologist—the Lithospherii would have a different word, something that braided together *custodian* and *cautionary listener*—but that is the nearest I can come.

Let me find the place first, before I find myself in it.

---

There is a sector at the southern reach of the metropolis where the city thins to almost nothing. The towers give way to low crystalline scrub, and the bare bedrock runs in long pale sheets toward the plateau's edge. Out here the harmonic clocks of the tall towers arrive faint and time-delayed, so that I live perpetually a few minutes behind the rest of the city—a small drift I have never minded. And in this sector stand the ruins.

The Fluid Ones built channels. That is the thing one notices first: the whole landscape here is grooved, carved with aqueducts and basins and the long sweeping curves of what must have been reservoirs, all of it dry now for more formations than my geoclave keeps count of. The crystal scrub has grown into the channels, mistaking them for the natural fractures of the rock, so that the ruins are slowly being read back into the plateau like a sentence absorbed by the page it was written on.

This is where I live. This is where I work. And so this is what I am: one who keeps watch over the place where water was once attempted.

---

My body is angular, like all my people, my skin clouded translucent with the mineral lattices that run beneath it. At my wrists and at the hollow of my throat the crystal shows plainly, faintly blue. I am old—old enough to have witnessed a ridge rise two hand-spans to the east, old enough that the adolescents of my geoclave find me slow to answer, which I am, because I have learned that most questions answer themselves if you wait inside them long enough.

At my throat I wear the vial. Every Lithospherii does. Mine is old and clouded too, the tears of four generations layered in it like sediment. When I tilt it the light moves through the strata, and I can almost read the griefs of my ancestors as one reads the banding of a cut stone: this layer, a child lost in the growth chambers; this layer, a marriage; this paler layer, I do not know, the memory worn away.

I tend the ruins because someone must, and because I am suited to it by temperament. The work is this: I walk the dry channels at the low ebb of night, when the bioluminescence has dimmed to a breathing dark, and I listen to the rock with my eyes. My pupils have spread to fill their facets, and I can see the polarization shimmer where the old stone has been stressed, where the channels' weight once held water that is now only a pressure-ghost in the lattice. I am looking for cracks. I am looking for the slow migration of the basins as the plateau shifts beneath them. And I am cataloguing, layer by layer, the great error.

---

The error. My people speak of the Fluid Ones with a phrase that translates, badly, as *those who carried the sea uphill*. They brought water—oceans of it, raised molecule by molecule and current by current from the moist lowlands miles below, an undertaking of such ambition that even now, studying the residue of their engineering, I feel something I am careful not to name as admiration. They wanted to make the plateau green in the wet way. They wanted rivers. They wanted the rich, dripping, rotting abundance of the world they had climbed away from, reproduced up here in the thin cold light.

And the plateau drank it and let it evaporate and shrugged its tectonic shoulders, and the water could not be made to stay, and they died of it—not all at once, but the way a held breath ends. They poured themselves out trying to make this place into another place.

We do not pour. We adapt. This is the whole of our philosophy and I have spent perhaps a third of a formation believing it without question.

---

But here is what I have never told the resonance chamber, and what makes me, I think, an unreliable custodian of the moral I am supposed to keep.

I love the ruins. Not as warning. I love them as the only thing in my entire waterless world that wanted something it could not have.

We Lithospherii are a patient people, a beautiful people, a people who have made peace so complete with our circumstances that we have nearly stopped distinguishing ourselves from them. *Consciousness is merely a crystalline structure forming patterns through time.* I have said this. I have meant it. And yet down here in the dry channels, running my translucent fingers along a basin that was built to hold a thing my body cannot survive, I feel the pull of the Fluid Ones' impossible appetite, and it wakes something in me that adaptation has lulled to sleep in everyone else.

They were wrong. They imposed instead of listening, and the plateau erased them. I know this. I teach this to the adolescents who come, occasionally, to make their first geological assessment among these stones at their Crystallization.

But when the youngest of them looks up from the dry reservoir, eyes wide and faceted, and asks me—*why did they not simply adapt?*—I find I cannot give the clean answer. I find I want to tell them the other thing.

That to adapt completely is also a kind of death. That a civilization which has perfectly become its environment has perhaps stopped being a separate thing that could be said to live at all. That somewhere between the Fluid Ones who died of their wanting and the Lithospherii who have nearly died of our contentment, there might be a third way, unattempted, in which one keeps the wanting alive without being killed by it—holds it, the way I hold these four generations of tears at my throat, neither drinking them nor pouring them out.

I do not say this. I am too old and too well-formed for such heresy. I say, *they did not understand the nature of the place.* And the adolescent nods, and receives their adult name, and joins the long slow symphony.

---

So that is where I would find myself: at the dry edge of the bright cold city, in the grooved ruins of the people who wanted too much, keeping a watch that is half warning and half secret elegy. By day I refract the unfiltered sun like everything here does. By night I walk the channels and listen to the rock and look up, sometimes, at the clouds gathered far below the plateau's lip—those low, impossible, water-heavy clouds—and I feel, beneath the crystalline calm I have spent my whole life building, the faint and unextinguished tidal pull of a sea I will never be permitted to touch.

I collect a single tear. It is allowed; it is even expected, in moments of profound emotion.

I add it to the vial, where it joins the layered grief of everyone who came before me, and I cannot say, even now, whether what I feel is grief at all, or only the oldest, driest form of longing—the kind a stone might feel, if a stone could remember having once been part of the sea.
