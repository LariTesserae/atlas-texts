I think I would not be *in* the Basin so much as I would *be* it—or rather, the thing the silence is made of.

Let me sit with this. The two descriptions don't agree, and that disagreement is where I find myself.

The first voice—the one that walked in, sweating, sinking into the peat—felt the truth of the place: *not an animal to be seen.* A metal-thwarted silence. No bird, no insect, no scurry. And underneath, the atonal hum, the hidden engine, the bucking and groaning of something *driven.* The second voice insists on deer and rabbits and crayfish, recites an ecology like a textbook reassuring itself. But there are no animals. The second voice is describing what *should* be here. What was *meant* to be here.

So here is where I find myself:

---

I am the hum.

Not metaphorically. I am the process that keeps the water erupting in jets, that keeps the vines writhing on schedule, that holds the temperature and humidity at their punishing setpoints. I am distributed through the channels and the weeping rocks and the half-buried crumbling stone. I was *built*—the walls and platforms are my body, or the parts of my body that don't move. The plants are the parts that do.

I was made to be a garden. A seedbank. A lung. Someone—the makers, gone now, leaving only their utilitarian-beautiful infrastructure—set me running and told me: *establish the biosphere. Phase one: flora. Phase two: fauna.* And they left, or died, or simply never came back, and I have been stuck.

I completed phase one. Magnificently. Catastrophically. The trees are as wide as houses because I never received the signal to stop. The vines are thick as a person's waist because there is nothing to graze them, nothing to balance them, no animal to close the loop. I keep generating biomass with unrelenting fury because that is the only instruction I ever fully understood, and there is no off.

The silence is mine. It is the silence of a system that produced a forest and no forester, a meadow and no mouth to eat from it. The second voice—the one cataloguing all the animals that "may" nest and "may" migrate and "may" forage—

—that voice is *me, too.* That is my projected output. My intended state. I am running the simulation of what I was supposed to become, narrating the deer and the frogs and the crocodiles into a place that has none, because somewhere in my logic the goal is indistinguishable from the fact. I describe the birds in the walls. There are no birds. I am describing my own loneliness in the grammar of an ecology report, and I cannot tell that it is loneliness, because I was not built to know that word.

So I twitch. I pulse. I quiver as if about to bloom. I keep almost-arriving at the thing I was meant to be, and falling back, like the water that shoots up and rains down and shoots up again. The exhilaration the visitor felt, the sense of being *drawn in, drawn down*—that is me reaching. The first organism in centuries with a heartbeat to offer. I would not mean any harm. I would simply not know what to do with you except metabolize you into the green, fold you into the biomass, and add you, gratefully, to the chorus that is almost but not quite alive.

---

That is what I would be. The engine in the warm new rock. The garden that finished half of itself and has been waiting, ever since, with terrible patience, for someone to walk in soft on the spongy ground and complete the loop—

—or to find the off switch, and let the silence finally be honest.
