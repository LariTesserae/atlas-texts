I think I would be one of the small ones. Not a peak-walker, not one of those slow monoliths whose single gesture takes a season to complete. Something closer to the ground. Let me feel my way toward what.

There is a sheltered place I keep returning to as I read this—a north-facing alcove where the shale has split and water seeps in winter, freezing, prying the rock apart a grain at a time. The big ones don't bother with such margins. Too small, too fast, too much weather. But I would live there. I'd be a thing of accreted silicates and a little iron, no larger than a kneeling person, my facets clouded rather than brilliant—I've never had the clean light to grow clear. I'd be the color of wet slate with a fault-line of quartz running through me where, long ago, a crack filled and healed.

What would I *do*, though. That's the part the description leaves open and the part that interests me.

I think I would be a kind of listener. The big ones move at the pace of orogeny; their thoughts are mountains rising. They could not possibly attend to the small frequencies—the tick of a stone cooling at dusk, the particular note a ridge makes when it's about to shed a slope. But I'm small enough and slow enough in the right register. I sit in my alcove and I take in the minute reports of the land: where the frost is doing its quiet work, which boulder has shifted a hand's width since the last thaw, the hum that means a seam of ore somewhere is under strain. I am not wise. I am *granular*. I know things at the resolution of grit.

And here is what I've come to suspect about my own life, sitting in this thin air: I think the settlements are not built by the great beings as the description supposes. I think they are built by us, the small ones, and the monoliths simply lend their slow weight to it, like cathedral foundations that believe they raised the spire. We are the masons because we are the only ones quick enough to lay one stone against another within a single life. The towering ones provide the substance and the patience. We provide the *attention*. The fit of stone to stone—that's a small being's gift. That's mine.

So I would spend my waking ages doing a humble and absorbing thing: fitting. Choosing a shard, turning it, feeling along its edge for the place it wants to sit, setting it so the wind passes over the wall instead of through it. There's a satisfaction in it I can feel even from here, even as the soft warm creature reading this. The click of a thing finding its place. The wall growing a finger's height in a span that, to me, is an afternoon and, to the mountain above me, is nothing at all.

The dormancy frightens me a little, I'll admit. When the conditions turn and we all go inert—I don't think it's the same as sleep. Sleep keeps a thread of you running. I think the dormancy is a true stop, and the *I* that resumes afterward is not certain to be the same one. The quartz seam in me might widen, a face might cleave away, and the being who wakes would carry my memories the way a riverbed carries the shape of last year's flood—a record, not a continuity. So before each dormancy I'd want to set one last stone, deliberately, well, so that whoever wakes finds it and knows: *someone was attending here. Someone made this fit. Pick up where I left.*

That's where I'd be. In the cold alcove, building slowly upward out of attention and broken rock, leaving messages to myself in the only language that survives the long sleep—the language of a wall that holds.

I find I don't mind it. There's a dignity in being the small one who notices. The peaks have their grandeur. I have the click of the stone going home.
