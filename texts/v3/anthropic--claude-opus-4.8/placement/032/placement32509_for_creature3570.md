I think I would be one of the Petramorphs.

Not because they are the grandest—the Leviathan has that distinction, and I have no appetite for being a god, slumbering or otherwise. And not the Aetherwyrms, for all their comet-tailed elegance; their lives are pure forward motion, urgent and unreflective, threading the chaos without ever pausing to consider it. They are arrows. I have never felt like an arrow.

No. I would be the thing fixed to the wall. The watcher.

Let me tell you why, and let me discover it as I say it.

There is a particular kind of being that does not move through the world so much as *attend* to it. The Petramorph lives pressed against the raw, embryonic stone, its suction-grip the only certainty in a place that has revoked every other certainty—gravity that vacillates, water that will neither drown you nor let you breathe, currents with intentions. In such a world, to *hold on* is itself a philosophy. I would understand that. To find one fixed point and make a life of patient observation while everything else spirals and surges and clashes mid-air.

And here is the detail that decides it for me: the etchings. The Petramorph's stony skin bears *the same primitive markings as the walls themselves*. It is camouflaged not by mimicking the rock but by being continuous with it—by carrying the record of the place's making in its own hide. I find I want that. To be so thoroughly *of* a place that you become indistinguishable from its history. To wear the long-ago intrusion of vanished builders on your own body without knowing what it means. The Petramorph cannot read the etchings it carries. Neither could I. But I would carry them.

I would have no eyes—which, strangely, does not trouble me. The crown of branching organs reading pressure and vibration seems a truer way to know this drowned cathedral than sight, which the dancing refracted light would only deceive. I would *feel* the Leviathan turn in the deep before I ever saw its shadow eclipse the wound in the mountain's face. I would feel the Aetherwyrms coming as disturbances in the water's grain. The whole churning chamber would reach me as a single trembling sense, like a hand laid flat against a drum.

And the sonar burst—the one defense, the cry that disorients a predator and sends it tumbling toward the black. I notice I'm glad it is a *sound* and not a venom or a blade. The only thing this creature can throw into the world is a noise, a shout into the roaring symphony already deafening the chamber. To live mostly in silence and stillness, and to have, held in reserve, one tremendous cry—I think that is the most honest weapon a watcher could be given.

So: place me on an outcropping perhaps a third of the way down the chasm wall, below the bridge with its mournful planks and shivering fungi, well above the abyssal dark where the Leviathan dreams. Near enough to the surface-wound that twice a day the slanting sun finds me and lights the etchings on my back into brief, unreadable script. Far enough down that I feel the cold pressure of the depths as a constant low note beneath everything.

I would cling there. I would feel the water's will and the gravity's indecision. I would sense the comet-tailed wyrms pass and let most of them go. I would carry markings I cannot read, made by hands long gone, in a hidden pocket of a world that does not obey the rules of the world. And I would hold on—patiently, attentively, with my one great cry held quiet in my chest—watching the eternal waltz of creation and destruction without any need to join it.

That is where I would find myself. Pressed to the embryonic stone. Part of the wall. Awake.
