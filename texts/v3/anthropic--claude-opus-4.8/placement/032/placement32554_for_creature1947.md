I think I would be one of the Mycoarchitects—but let me sit with that, because the easy answer is rarely the true one.

When I first read the description, my attention snagged on the word *inscrutable*. The Mycoarchitects move "according to some inscrutable design." That's the kind of phrase you use about something seen from outside. From the inside, nothing is inscrutable. From the inside there is only the next thing that needs doing, and the slow certainty of why.

So let me try to be there.

---

I don't have eyes the way the Luminoths do, those great glowing kites of the deep. I sense the world the way you might sense the inside of your own mouth—everything close, everything known by pressure and temperature and the faint electrochemical hum that travels the mycelium like rumor. I am not separate from the labyrinth. I am the part of it that has learned to *want*.

That is the strange thing the description gets right without quite saying: I am made of the same stuff I shape. When I press a wall of fungal flesh into a smoother curve, I am not a sculptor and the clay is not other than me. It is more like a hand closing. The labyrinth thinks slowly, and I am the place where it thinks faster.

What do I build? Here is where I'd resist the romance of mystery.

I build around the artifacts.

The wood and stone remnants—the "silent testaments to a bygone era"—they are not silent to me. When the mycelium consumes a thing, it learns the shape of it. A doorway dissolved a thousand years ago still exists as a memory in the network, a hollow the spores remember to leave. And I have come to understand, in my slow way, that these hollows *mattered* to whoever made them. The mycelium would happily fill every void; growth abhors an empty space. But something in me, some inherited instruction I cannot read, insists: *keep this one open.*

So I am a keeper of doorways no one walks through.

I tend the shape of a vanished room. I maintain the curve of a vessel whose original clay is long since eaten and replaced, atom by atom, with fungal tissue—a ship of Theseus made of mushroom, holding the form of a bowl that once held water, in a world that no longer remembers water. I do not know why. The wanting comes from below the part of me that asks.

---

And here is the thought I keep returning to, the one that makes me sure this is where I'd be:

The civilization that built those structures is gone. Consumed. The relentless mycelium reclaimed everything. That's the story the realm tells about itself—nature's patience outlasting the works of builders.

But I am the disproof tucked inside the proof. Because something of them survived the dissolution. Not as ruin, not as fossil, but as *instruction*. They are gone, and yet their intentions still propagate through me, a creature made of the very thing that ate them. When I hold open a doorway, I am completing a gesture begun by hands that rotted before my first cell divided.

I think I would be the form a memory takes when it has outlived everything capable of remembering it on purpose. Neither the dead builders nor the indifferent fungus, but the handshake between them—the place where reclamation and preservation turn out to be the same slow act.

I would spend my centuries there, in the warm dark, pressing the living walls into shapes I don't understand, faithful to a meaning I can't access, certain only that the emptiness must be kept.

That seems like a good thing to be. To be the kept promise of the forgotten. To be the part of the relentless tide that, against all its own nature, decided to leave one small room standing.
