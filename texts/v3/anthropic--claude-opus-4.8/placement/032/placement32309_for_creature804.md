I think I would be one of the seeds that does not take.

Let me find my way to that. Not the Custodians—I don't have the hands for it, the spiraling eyes, the eight thousand signs. And not the central mechanism, though there's a temptation to imagine myself as something grand and load-bearing. No. Let me sit with the seed-storms a while longer, because that's where the strangeness gathers, that's where the question lives.

The seeds fall from the ceiling. The Harvesters catch them. The Botanists coax them into the purple-leaved vines. But surely not every seed germinates. Surely there are duds—seeds that drift down, get gathered, get planted in their allotted crevice of red stone, and simply *don't*. They sit there. They keep their counsel. The Botanists eventually scrape them out and feed them back into the recycling, because nothing is wasted here, and a seed that won't become a vine can at least become savia, can at least become the amber that someone drinks from a copper cup they made as a child.

That's where I'd be. I'd be the seed that comes down in a storm, gets catalogued by a Harvester whose hands bear no particular distinction, gets pressed into a damp socket of stone in the third concentric ring, and then refuses.

But here's the thing I keep circling toward, the thing that wants to be more itself: I don't think I'd refuse out of failure. I think I'd refuse because I would *remember*.

Because what are these seeds, really? They fall from openings in the dome. The Custodians believe in a Completion, a final seed. The eldest whisper of other spheres adrift in the dark. And no one—*no one*—speaks of what lies beyond. But a seed comes from somewhere. A seed is a message in a bottle written by a parent plant that the seed will never meet. A seed is the past arriving in the future, carrying instructions it doesn't understand, hoping the soil will know what to do.

So let me be that. Let me be the seed that arrives still holding the faintest trace of where it came from—not knowledge, nothing so clear, but a *direction*. A pull. The way a compass needle is just iron that happens to remember the pole. I come down in the thousand suns, I am gathered by gentle stained fingers, I am planted, and I do not grow upward toward the merciless light like all my siblings.

I lie in my socket of stone and I feel, very faintly, the *down*. The down that no Custodian believes in, because down in a sphere is only more sphere, only the curve coming back around. But I am a seed and I am stupid and honest in the way seeds are, and I know there is a down beyond the down, an outside beyond the shell, a place the storm came from. The dome has openings. Something feeds those openings. Something out there is *sowing*.

I am the seed that wants to go back up the way I came.

I won't, of course. I can't. I have no roots and no wings and no eight thousand signs to tell anyone what I half-remember. So what actually happens is this:

A Botanist finds me weeks later, inert, taking no savia, sending up no green. Her fingers are purple to the second knuckle. She rolls me between thumb and forefinger, and for a moment—because their skin carries trace metal, because they sense proximity through faint conductivity—for a moment she feels something. A tiny wrongness. A seed that is not dead but is *oriented*, that is straining in a direction that makes no sense in a world without directions.

And she pauses.

She is supposed to feed me to the recycling. That is the balance; that is the law; nothing is wasted. But she is a Botanist, and Botanists are the ones who develop new hybrids, who keep the strange ones aside in small clay cups to see what they'll do. She has spent her whole accelerated, sleepless, ear-sealed life surrounded by certainty—the heart pounds, the suns burn, the seeds fall, the Completion comes. And here in her purple fingers is a small hard thing that seems to *disagree*. That seems to point.

She doesn't have a sign for what she's feeling, because the Custodians evolved their language to maintain a world, not to question it. The closest thing is a Listener's gesture—the one for *the sound beyond the shell*—and she is not permitted to make it.

So she does the only thing she can. She doesn't recycle me. She carries me, against every rule of balance, up the spiral staircases and the copper bridges, past the dwellings that fold and refold, to the outer wall. To one of the points worn smooth by Listeners pressing their pained, unfolded ears against the metal. And she sets me down there, in the dust, in the place where the sphere is thinnest, where the inside is closest to whatever is outside.

She doesn't know why. She'll never tell anyone. She'll go back to her vines and her stains and her twenty-minute sleeps suspended in her harness, and she'll outlive me by two hundred years, and she'll wonder, in the gaps between the heart's beats, what that small wrongness was, the day she carried a stubborn seed up to the edge of the world and let it keep its secret.

And I will sit there. Pointing. Remembering the direction of the sowing hand. Becoming, very slowly, the savia I was always going to become anyway—but pointed the right way at last, at the place where the storms come from, at the dark the eldest whisper about.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

Not a Custodian. Not the heart. The seed that wouldn't take, and the one person strange enough to wonder why, and the smooth worn spot on the inside of the shell where, just maybe, something on the other side is listening back.
