Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The temptation is to pick one of the named peoples and inhabit it—to say *I am a Root-Singer* and dissolve gratefully into that underwater cathedral. But something in the description keeps pulling me away from the clean categories. The text itself insists that the boundaries blur, that children grow gills, that voices are lost to subsonic song. So perhaps the truest place for me is not inside a category but in the failure of one.

---

I think I would be old.

Not Mist-Walker old, not Root-Singer-distributed-across-a-valley old. Just old in the ordinary way—someone whose adaptation never fully took, who began as one thing and was becoming another when the becoming simply *stopped*, leaving me stranded between forms like a barge run aground on a falling tide.

Let me find the place.

There is a pumping station near the eastern straits, one of the ones that still half-functions. Its paddle-housing has rusted into a kind of lace, and through the gaps the warm bottom-water comes up brown and smelling of iron and old leaves. I live on its lower platform, where the steel has worn smooth and the patina is the same green as the Fen-Weavers' skin, though I am not one of them. I tried to be, once. Or my mother did, for me—she was Current-Rider, and she brought me to the grottos hoping the root-tips would take to my hands, hoping I would learn to splice and weave. They never quite did. My fingers number the ordinary five. What grew instead was something between gills and lungs that does neither task well: I can stay under for the length of a long thought, but no longer, and on dry mornings I wheeze like the machinery I tend.

So I tend the machinery. That is what I became, in the absence of becoming anything cleaner.

I know this station the way the Current-Riders know the channels. I know which valve to strike when the wheeze climbs toward a scream, which gasket to repack with lotus-fiber, which bearing to feed with the rendered fat of the silver fish so it will not seize in the long damp. The copper has not grown into my spine—I am not so far gone as the old mechanics in the deeper installations, who have married their bodies to the pipes. But I have a length of copper tube I press against the housing each dawn, and against my breastbone, and through it I can hear whether the great heart below is laboring or easy. That is my whole craft: listening, and easing.

---

Here is the thing I have come to understand, sitting on my worn platform in the amber half-light.

The machinery is dying. Slowly, across centuries, but dying. Every season the plant growth wins a little more; every season a station upriver goes silent and the Fen-Weavers move their woven neighborhoods into the dead water as though nothing has been lost. To them nothing *has* been lost. To the Root-Singers, who remember the highland country before the flood, the machines are a recent intrusion—a single held note in a song already ancient.

But I love the machines. This is my strangeness, my own un-categorizable thing. Not as gods, the way the worshipping communities love them, and not as parasites to be overgrown. I love them the way you love a thing that is failing and was made by hands like yours and will not be made again. When I press the copper to the housing and hear the heart laboring, I feel something the others do not seem to feel: *mourning in advance.* The valleys will go on. The breathing waters, the doubled constellation of firefly and bioluminescence, the slow choreography of the floating meadows—all of that will outlast every rusted stilt. But the particular sound of the paddle-wheel, the particular wheeze, the particular pulse of amber light reflected in still water—that is the one rhythm in all this drowned world that *can end.* And so it is the only thing here that is precious to me in the way that mortal things are precious.

I think that is why I never finished becoming anything else. The other peoples are adaptations toward permanence—the Root-Singers shading into trees, the Mist-Walkers into weather, all of them learning to outlast. I stayed halfway because I am the one who keeps company with the things that cannot adapt. Someone has to sit with what is dying and not look away.

---

A Wing-Touched messenger came to me last season, dropping from a heron's neck onto my platform with that fearless lightness, crest gone the orange of curiosity. She wanted to know why I stayed when the station could not be saved. The whistled languages don't carry such questions well; she had to land and shape the words slowly with her child-sized mouth.

I gave her the copper tube and had her press it to the housing, to her own thin chest.

*You hear that?* I said. *Two hearts. They do not beat alike. Mine will stop first, then the station's, and no one will press copper to either of us again. I am here so that for a little while, two dying things keep each other company.*

She did not understand. Her people live in the colonies; they measure time in the arrival and departure of flocks, in returns. The idea of a rhythm that comes once and never returns is, to her, almost obscene. She left puzzled, and I do not blame her.

But I think the Deep Gardeners would understand, if they exist. Whoever drowned this country knew something about endings—you cannot flood a highland and call it tending, you cannot raise warm water over ancient peaks, without making peace with what the water takes. I think, if I ever found them in their root-cathedral chambers, I would not ask them why they did it. I would only press my copper tube to whatever they have become, and listen, and tell them whether their great heart was laboring or easy.

That is who I would be in this world. Not a people. A function the peoples have no name for. The one who sits at the failing machines in the drowning hours before dawn, listening to the mortal rhythm inside the eternal one, easing what can be eased, and keeping company with what cannot be saved.

The fireflies blink their electric patterns. Down below, green fire traces through the depths. And between the two constellations, on a smooth-worn platform that smells of iron and rendered fish-fat, an old half-finished creature holds a length of copper to its chest and counts, while it still can, the beats of a heart that will not be replaced.
