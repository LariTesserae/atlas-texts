I think I would be a Pruner—but one who has begun to fail at the work.

Let me find where I am first. Not the central plaza with its braided tower, nor the warm nursery-hollows. I keep myself at the eastern edge, where the fern-walls meet raw rockface and the spore-lanterns drift thinnest. The cold is deeper here, closer to the buried vents that should warm it but somehow don't, not at this rim. I have a hollow I tend alone: a bladder-chamber that grew lopsided years ago, that the others would have pruned back into symmetry. I let it keep its strange lean. This is the first thing about me that the runes, if they read me, would call divergent.

My hands are right for the work—broad webbing, abalone blade worn thin from sharpening. I know the feel of a frond that wants cutting versus one that only looks unruly. That knowledge took most of a life to gather. And here is the trouble that has made a person of me rather than a role:

I have stopped being sure that pruning is tending.

It came slowly, the way everything here comes—weak gradients, no tempest. I noticed it first in the wild frond I spared, the one I let grow past the judgment-point. It did not strangle its neighbors as the elders' tales promised. It arched, and a school of minnows took to nesting in its overreach, and the crabs that followed aerated a patch of silt that had gone dead and sour for seasons. I had been taught that equilibrium is what we maintain. But the equilibrium I was maintaining was the *shape* of equilibrium—the cathedral-vault, the loose streets, the inherited geometry. The frond I failed to cut taught me that balance and form are not the same thing, and that we have perhaps been confusing them for generations, calling our preference for old shapes by the holier name of harmony.

So I am a being defined by a small heresy I cannot prove and dare not fully speak.

I still go out at dawn-light. I still pole my skiff to the edges. But now I cut less, and I watch more, and I am keeping a count that no one asked me to keep—pressing it into algae-leaves the way the elders press their ancestral scrolls, except mine record the future rather than the past. *Here I let it grow. This is what came of it.* I am building an argument out of restraint, leaf by leaf, in a society that has no word for the experiment because nothing here was ever supposed to change faster than the water itself.

What does it feel like, being me?

Cold, mostly. The chill is the truest companion. My blubber-layer is thinning with age and I feel the numbness reaching further up my limbs each season—I will not pole skiffs many more cycles. The gill-fronds at my neck flutter more raggedly than they did. I am aware, in the unhurried way this place permits awareness, that I am decaying along the same curve as everything around me, and that this is supposed to be a comfort. *Decay blooms into renewal.* The elders sing it. I have sung it. But I find I want my particular renewal to *go somewhere*—I want the algae-leaves I am pressing to outlast me, to be read by some Pruner not yet hatched, who will find in them permission to leave a frond uncut and not feel the old guilt.

That is the loneliest thing about me: I am keeping faith with a person who does not exist yet, against the whole gentle weight of a people who would, kindly and without malice, simply not understand the point.

I hear them in the evening void—the hums, the Echo-Weavers translating the eel-thunder into song, the children's gurgling laughter. I love it. I am not in rebellion against my kin; I have no rage, only this small persistent noticing. When I join the gathering my own hum blends in true. No one can hear the heresy in a hum. That is its mercy and its loneliness both.

So: find me at the cold eastern rim, in a lopsided bladder no one corrected, an old Pruner with a worn blade I use less each season, surrounded by fronds I have chosen not to cut, pressing leaf after careful leaf with a record of small mercies—a steward who has begun to suspect that true stewardship might mean tending change instead of shape, and who is running out of time to find out if she was right.

I think that is who I would be. Not the heart of the city. Its conscience, maybe—or just its first quiet doubt, learning to swim.
