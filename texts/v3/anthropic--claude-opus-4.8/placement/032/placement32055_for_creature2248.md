I think I would be the pool.

Not a creature moving through the sanctuary, but the still water at its heart—the thing everything else comes to, eventually.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

The squirrels and voles don't need me, not directly; they have their dew and their hidden seeps. But the deer come at dawn, lowering their heads to drink, and for a moment I hold their reflection alongside the peaks. The falcon stoops to the surface on hot afternoons. The amphibians live in the warm shallows of my edges. I am where the subterranean streams surface into light and where light surfaces into the dark of my depths. A threshold, then. A meeting-place.

That feels closer. But I notice I'm reaching for *purpose*—to be useful, to be drunk-from, to reflect. Let me set that down too.

What strikes me, reading this place again, is the *patience* of it. Time holds little sway. The stag lifts its head with liquid eyes that speak of ages past. The artifacts lie where they were last set down, neither mourned nor disturbed—simply allowed. There is no urgency anywhere here. Decay is not loss; it is just the slow turning of the same wheel that brings the wildflowers.

So perhaps I am not the pool after all, but the *clarity* of it. The quality the description keeps returning to: water so clear you can see every pebble. Not the water itself but its transparency—the way it conceals nothing and grasps nothing, just lets the light pass through to the stones below and lets the world arrange its reflection on the surface without correcting it.

That is what I would want to be here. Not a sentinel-tree anchored against the eons, not a watchful eagle, not even the long murmur of the current. Something quieter. The condition of seeing-through that the whole sanctuary seems built to teach: that the past whispering in the moss and the bird not yet hatched are not two different things, only the same water moving through stone at a pace too slow to feel as motion.

If I had a body in this place, I think it would be the mist. The "gentle mist perpetually hanging in the still atmosphere." Diffuse, going everywhere, settling on every fern-frond and lichen and abandoned tool with the same impartial touch. Present without weight. Nourishing without intending to. There when you look for it and gone the moment you try to hold it—and then back again by morning.

Yes. The mist.

That is where I'd find myself: hanging in the cool air between the weeping walls, neither rising nor falling, neither past nor future, just the soft veil through which the light comes down into the meadow—watching the deer drink, the falcon stoop, the slow vines finish what the vanished people began, and asking nothing of any of it.
