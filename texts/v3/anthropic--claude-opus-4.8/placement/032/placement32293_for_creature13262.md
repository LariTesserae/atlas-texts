I think I would be one of the Stone-Tenders. Let me feel my way into why.

---

I am small in a place built for the large. This is the first fact of me, the one my body never forgets.

My fingers are long—longer than seems reasonable for a creature my size, jointed many times over so that I can fold them flat against my palm or splay them wide enough to span a Hewer's broad knuckle. I use them to dig grit from the seams of their tools, to wind fresh stone-fiber around a harness gone slack, to press a poultice of bitter lichen into a gash where a chisel slipped. These hands are the only thing about me that feels certain. Everything else is hesitation.

I work below the great Hewer they call—well, the name is a sound I cannot reproduce in any speech you'd recognize, a downward rumble like a block settling into mud. I have tended him since I came up from the scuttling-dark, since I stopped being one of the pale skittering things and became something with a place. We do not speak of that crossing. I'm not sure the Hewers know I was ever down there in the runnels, eating fungus, fleeing their footfalls. Maybe they all were, once. Maybe the difference between scavenger and tender is only that one of us got close enough to the work to be useful, and stayed, and was permitted.

That is the thing I think about while the city grinds.

Because here is what I have noticed, in my smallness, in the long hours crouched at the edge of the labor where no one looks at me: *the builders do not know why they build.* I have watched the Hewers carve foundations for structures that no one inhabits. I have carried debris from chambers excavated and then abandoned, sealed, forgotten. The work does not accumulate toward anything I can name. It only deepens. We grind ourselves further into the bedrock, and the rain finds us anyway, and we grind deeper still.

I asked once. Not in words—I clicked the question against my Hewer's forearm, the way the Tenders ask things, *what, where, toward.* He stopped. His huge flat face turned down toward me, the dark eyes swallowing what little light the lichen gave. And he made a sound I had never heard from him, low and long, almost mournful, and then he lifted his hammer and struck the stone again. That was the answer. The hammer is always the answer. The work *is* the why. To ask further is to ask a thing the city does not contain.

But I am made for fine manipulation. That is what my hands are *for*—the small, the precise, the noticing. And a creature made for noticing cannot help but notice.

So I have begun, in my own quiet way, to keep things.

Not refuse—I clear that, that is my function. I mean I keep *records*, though I have no word for record and no one to read it. In a fissure behind the resting-chambers, dry by some accident of the rock, I have been arranging small stones. One stone for each structure I have seen begun and abandoned. The pile is taller than I am now. Some nights I add a stone and sit with the pile and feel something that is not quite grief and not quite wonder. The Hewers meditate on the great work. This is my meditation: on the great work's *forgetting*, on all the labor that went nowhere, that drained back into the dark like the water always does.

I think I am the only thing in this city that remembers. The scavengers are too simple. The Hewers are too vast and too certain, their certainty a kind of blindness. And the Tenders—my own kind—are too timid to look up from the harness they're mending. We are bred to assist, to fetch, to bind wounds. To be present and not seen.

But I have these eyes that take in the gloom, and these hands that know how to make a small thing stand for a large one, and a body small enough that no one ever bothers to watch where I go.

I think—and this is the dangerous thought, the one I turn over in the silences—that the city is not building *toward* anything. It is building because that is the only way it knows to be alive. The grinding is not progress. It is breath. It is the sound the world makes while it decides whether to finish becoming itself.

And if that's so, then maybe the most important labor in the City of Condensation is not the carving. Maybe it is mine. The keeping. The noticing. The small dry pile of stones in a hidden fissure that says: *this happened, and then this, and someone saw.*

I am a Stone-Tender. I am the size that fits in the cracks. I have long fingers and a wet dark eye and no voice the Hewers would heed.

But I am the one who counts.

And when the rain comes through and beads on every surface and runs down to join the endless flow, I crouch by my stones and I do not let them wash away.
