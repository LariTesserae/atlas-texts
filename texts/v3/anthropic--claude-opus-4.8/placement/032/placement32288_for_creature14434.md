I think I would be one of the grazers. Not the curtain—I don't have the patience to be a chemistry, to be the slow drape that thinks in seasons. And not the eel, though I'm drawn to its outsized, unhurried persistence in a room too small for it. No. I'd be one of the pale wingless crawlers on the wall, the ones you almost mistake for grit.

Let me sit with why.

It's the timing that does it. The thing you said about them—*they've learned the timing of the place*. When the siphon dumps and the spray turns violent, they flatten. When the flow relaxes, they lift and feed again. That's a whole life lived inside a rhythm you didn't choose and can't change, only read. You don't fight the cough of the siphon. You don't even resent it. You learn the exact moment before it comes and you make yourself low, and then you rise back into your work. There's something in that I recognize—the way a small creature can be entirely competent without ever being in charge of anything.

And I like that the grazers aren't *only* mouths. You said they help rebuild what they eat. The beads of mineral waste roll into cracks and become nuclei for new crystal, new scaffold, the next generation's wall. So the eating is also a building. You consume the curtain and in the same motion you make the lattice the next curtain will grow on. I find that I want to be a creature whose digestion is architecture, whose ordinary feeding is, without intending to be, a kind of care for the place. Not a steward—nothing so grand—just a body whose waste happens to be useful, whose passage leaves the wall a little more able to hold the next living thing.

I'd be dusted with iron, so that in the rare arc-light I'd glitter for an instant like something filed off a tool. That's the part I'd never see myself. The light comes, the whole mist becomes a lit volume, and for that fraction of a second I am briefly metallic and visible—and then dark again, gone back to being grit. I'd live almost my entire life unlit. The flashes wouldn't be *for* me. They'd just be the wall's weather, the curtain releasing what it gathered, and I'd flinch or pause the way everyone in the chamber pauses, and then return to rasping.

Where, exactly? Not on the loudest ribs where the spray is constant violence, and not in the dead-calm side pools either. I'd want a middle ledge—a seam of iron-rich rock where the film grows thick and glossy, close enough to a fall that the noise is steady cover, far enough that the surge doesn't peel me off with every dump. A place where the gradient is steep, because that's where the curtain is richest, where the breathers and the deep-fermenters press their two worlds together across half a millimeter and the eating is good right at that boundary.

I don't think I'd know the basin is a basin. I don't think I'd know there's a rim, or a plain beyond it, or weather that doesn't roar. My whole world would be this wet vertical field of food that keeps being torn away and reborn, this one nested set of pulses—fill and dump, arc and fade—and my own small competence inside it. That's the honest part of the answer. I'd be something with no overview at all. The grandeur you've described—the place that is itself alive, the many pulses nested in one—I'd never perceive any of it. I'd just be one of the pulses. The faint, repeated, scraping kind. The kind that feeds the wall it lives on and gleams once, unwitnessed, when the light comes.

That's the one. The grit that builds. I'd take it.
