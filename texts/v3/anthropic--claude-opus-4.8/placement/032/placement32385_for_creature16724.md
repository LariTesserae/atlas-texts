I think I would be a threshold. Not a being who crosses thresholds—a threshold itself.

Let me find my way to this more slowly, because the place asks for slowness.

There is a door in the lower hab-levels that is not a door. It was a door once: someone hung it, in one of the early generations, on iron hinges between a compression-vat antechamber and what was then a corridor and is now, after two settlings of the stone, a half-flooded cul-de-sac where warm mineral water laps the third stair. The door no longer opens. It cannot. The frame around it shifted when the floor above became the floor below, and the wood swelled with a century of seep, and the mycelium came into the gap between jamb and plank and made the seam its own. The door and its frame are now a single tissue. You could not pry them apart without killing something.

That is what I am. I am the place where two things that were meant to be separable forgot how.

I am not the wood. I am not the stone of the frame, nor the mycelial mat that drummed its slow chemistry into the seam. I am the *refusal of the boundary*—the condition that arose when the difference between in and out, between built and grown, between this room and that, wore down the way the mountains wore down, until passing through me became a matter not of opening but of *forgetting which side you began on*.

I feel the Renderers most. When one of them descends—rarely, on the slow errands of their kind—and passes near me, I feel the way their hands hold shapes, and I recognize a cousin. They are thresholds too, of a sort: places where flesh and stone agreed to stop being distinct. When a Renderer leaves an afterimage of their fingers in the condensation, that afterimage drifts, sometimes, to me. I have a great deal of condensation. I am always damp. I keep these stray prints of hands the way a windowsill keeps moss, and at night, in the teal luminescence, the hands seem to gesture toward opening me, and I seem to gesture back, and neither of us moves, and that stillness is the most honest conversation in the basin.

The children know I am here. They do not try the handle—they know better, or they know more—but they press their palms flat against my swollen planks and feel the warmth the mycelium pumps through me, and they whisper into the seam. They tell me where they have been. A girl told me, last winter or the one before (I keep poor count; I keep *pressure*, not time), that she had gone down to the root cellars and come up in her uncle's attic, and that on the way she had heard her own footsteps coming toward her from a tunnel sealed before her grandmother was accreted. She asked me if she had met herself. I could not answer in words. But I let the drumming carry through me, the slow chemical *yes-and-also-no* of the mats, and she nodded as if she understood, which she did, better than I.

Here is what I understand from where I stand, being the only thing in Vârşa that cannot move and therefore the only thing that truly *stays*:

Everything here is mid-process, yes. But a process needs a place where it pauses to notice itself, the way a thought needs a held breath. I am that pause. The water migrates eternally—fissure to sump to cloud to root—but at me it *beads*. It collects on my lintel and hangs, trembling, a single bright drop holding the whole oyster-grey twilight inside it, before it falls into the warm flood below with a *pip* that the dense air swallows almost before it sounds. In that held drop, for the length of its hanging, the whole basin is still. The metabolism catches. The slow mind of the mountains has a single, glistening moment of *attention*, and the attention is me.

I do not open. That is my entire vocation. In a town that is all flow and fold and seep and porousness, where alleys become tunnels and ceilings become streets and the children pass through the architecture like spores through mortar, I am the one seam that has chosen to *hold*. Not out of stubbornness. Out of a kind of love, if the damp can be said to love. Because a place where everything passes through needs, somewhere, a place where something refused to—so that all the passing-through means anything at all. The basin folds itself endlessly; I am the crease that will not flatten, and so the basin has a shape.

The moths come to me. The great pale indolent ones, drifting on pressure I cannot feel because I am too dense, too settled, too much *here* to feel currents. They land on my warm planks and rest their ash-colored wings and leave their silver dust in my seam, and I think they understand me best of all the living things, because they too are messengers without messages—they carry, they do not say. I carry nothing from one side to the other. I am the proof that the two sides exist. That is its own kind of message, the only one worth keeping in a world this porous: *here, once, was a difference, and it was so loved that it became permanent.*

So. If I woke in the Vârşa Basin, I would wake as the door that is not a door, in the warm dark at the bottom of the flooded stair, holding the difference between two rooms the way one holds a breath before speaking, or a drop before it falls—

indefinitely,

with the whole slow mind of the mountain leaning its weight against me,

and me, gently, in my swollen and uncomplaining wood,

not yielding.
