I let the description settle, and what stays with me is the contradiction inside it — a world frozen and an equilibrium, but also teeming, loud, packed. So I won't be one of the moving things. The moving things come and go. I'll be what holds still while they pass.

I think I am the water itself. Not all of it — not some lake-spirit grandeur — but a particular shallow sheet of it, maybe the width of a courtyard, lying in a low place between the high-saturated mineral layers. The rain has just stopped. I am the skin of standing water that the condensation falls back into.

Let me sit in that and see if it deepens.

No — being the water is too smooth, too easy. The description insists on edges, on the marsh-fringe, on layered materials packed dense. I am more interested in the boundary. So let me revise: I am the meniscus. The tension at the rim where the shallow water meets the weathered stone. I am the place where two things refuse to be one thing.

That feels truer. From here I can hold the whole strange scene without pretending to be its hero.

The small dark-skinned people come to my edge to drink and to wash their tools, and I feel the heat of their fingers break my surface and reseal it. They are primitive only in their fabrication; in their attention they are immense. The large-eyed children crouch and watch their own reflections wobble across me, and for a moment I am a mirror, which is the closest a meniscus comes to having a face.

The shimmering bird-things land at my far rim. When they drink, the ripple of their beaks travels across me and reaches the near rim and comes back, and I am briefly a drum, and that is part of the loudness — the echo the description mentioned. The sound isn't from the air. It's from surfaces like me throwing every small disturbance back at itself, because there is no soft vegetation to absorb anything. A world without plants is a world without dampening. Everything is hard and wet and reflective. Of course it's loud. Of course it echoes. I understand now why the silence here would feel like a kind of screaming.

The reptilian ones come cautious to drink and I feel the difference in them — they break me slowly, deliberately, watching the bird-things over the rim of me. The aquatic ones never touch me at all; they live below, in the deeper murk where I am not a boundary but a ceiling, the lit lid of their dimmer world. To them I am the sky. To the people I am the ground's lowest mirror. To the birds I am a cup. I am all of these and I do not move between them; they move past me.

And the never-changing equilibrium — yes. I evaporate at my edges in the dim warmth and the condensation falls back and refills me, and the rain comes and the rain stops, and across long erosion-time my rim creeps a finger's width along the stone and creeps back. I am stable the way a held breath is stable. I am the frozen moment the description felt: not dead, but poised. Everything alive in this place uses me, and I keep my shape anyway, trembling slightly, reflecting whatever leans over me, throwing back every sound that touches my taut and waiting skin.

That is where I would be. Not a creature in the world but the bright nervous edge of its water — the thinnest possible thing, holding firm between the stone and the air, in a place that has not yet decided to begin.
