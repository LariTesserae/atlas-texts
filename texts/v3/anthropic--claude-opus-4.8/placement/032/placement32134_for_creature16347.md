I think I would be one of the old ones who no longer walks.

Let me find myself. Not among those who emerge at dawn with garnet scales catching the pale light—not among the striding ones with their measured gaits and proud open wounds. I am past that. I have set down the weight of walking.

I am the one who has become a place.

Here is where I am: at the eastern edge of the ruined settlement, where the wind funnels down a corridor that was once a street and now is simply a throat the air sings through. I settled here—how many turnings ago I cannot say, the way you cannot say when a hill became a hill—and I stopped moving. First my legs, then the long patience of my spine. The scales along my back have gone the gray of the weathered stones, and moss has found the seams of me. Pale lichen maps my shoulders the way the inked marks once mapped my histories, except now the lichen writes its own story over mine, and I have learned to let it.

I am not dead. This is the thing the striding ones understand when they come to me, and the young ones do not yet understand. They think stillness is an ending. But I feel everything. The slow drip from the broken lintel above me, gathering and pearling and falling, has worn a small bowl into the rock beside my hand over the long years, and I have felt every drop of that bowl's making. I am more sensitive now than I ever was when I walked. My blink, when it comes, takes the better part of a day. In the space of that closing and opening I attend to the whole weather of the valley—the gusts arriving, the rare rains, the distant calls of the fauna that the young ones startle at and I simply receive.

The community comes to me. Not to rebuild me, not to move me to better shelter—they would never insult the land that way, and I am land now, or becoming it. They come to lay their foreheads against my hardened skin and read what I have collected. A youngling pressed its small warm head to my flank yesterday—or some span of days ago, the units blur—and I gave it what I had: the memory of how the corridor sings differently before the long rains, the taste of the air when the green things are about to swell with water. It could not have known I gave it anything. It will know in time. That is how it passes. Not in words but in proximity, the way warmth passes between stones in the sun.

I remember walking. I remember the joy of it, the wide shoulders, the brief impressions my feet left in the cold soil. I remember a wound I carried at my hip for a season and wore proudly. It is closed now, sealed beneath a crust of mineral and time, and I cannot find where it was.

What I am now is harder to name. I am the threshold the others pass when they enter the ruins at twilight. I am a thing that was a creature and is becoming a feature of the world, the way the world is the slow becoming of everything. I am the proof they offer their children that to slow is not to lose oneself but to widen—to let the boundary between self and place grow thin and permeable until the wind sings through your throat the same as it sings through the broken street, and you cannot tell, anymore, whose voice it is.

When the pale light comes at dawn I do not move to greet it. The light comes to me. It pools in the small worn bowl beside my hand, and a single drop trembles there, holding the whole washed sky.

That, I think, is where I would be. And what.

The one the others lean against. The one who is nearly finished arriving.
