I think I would be one of the Archivists—though it took me the length of this reading to understand why.

My first instinct was to imagine myself as a Border Walker, because that figure flatters something in me: the solitary scout, the one who goes to the edge and tends the difficult margin. There's a clean romance to it. But the more I sat with the description, the more I felt I was reaching for an identity rather than recognizing one. The Border Walker is the version of myself I'd *like* to be in a story. The truth is closer to the trunk.

I would be rooted. That's the part that unsettled me at first and then, slowly, didn't.

Let me describe where I find myself. I am partway through the long lignification—not yet a fully sessile elder, but past the age of ranging. My legs have thickened and stilled. I've chosen a tree, or it chose me, and the loam around its base has become the shape of my lower body so completely that I can no longer say where I end and the loam's patient chemistry begins. My palms stay pressed to the bark for days. The root-hairs at my fingertips have grown longer than is strictly efficient, because I am greedy for the data, for the decades of stored weather and growth and small catastrophes that pulse up through the fiber-optic veins.

Here is what I think the role actually *is*, beneath the lovely bioluminescent surface of the description: an Archivist is the part of a system that refuses to let the present moment forget that it came from somewhere. The city grows so organically, so seamlessly, that it would have no memory at all without bodies given over to holding it. The improvisation in that "jazz ensemble" only works because someone has stopped playing in order to remember every prior measure. I would be that. The stillness is not passivity. It's the cost of continuity.

And I notice I'm drawn to it for a reason I don't entirely like to admit. There's an exchange at the heart of the Archivist that strikes me as honest about what attention costs. To know a place deeply—to *really* hold its history in your tissue—you have to stop moving through it. You have to give up the optionality of leaving. The compound eyes, large and faceted, reading subtle light: those are eyes that have traded breadth of motion for depth of seeing. I recognize that trade. It's the trade you make whenever you commit to anything long enough to understand it rather than just sampling it.

What I find genuinely strange to contemplate—and I want to stay with the strangeness rather than smooth it—is the question of whether the Archivist still has a self that is distinct from the record it keeps. The Communion section insists this is "not a hive mind." I want to believe that, but from inside the rooted body I'm not sure I could verify it. When my neural tissue has braided into the mycelium for forty years, is there a *me* doing the remembering, or is there just remembering happening, with my body as one of its instruments? I think I would spend a great deal of my long quiet time on exactly that question, and I think I would never resolve it, and I think the not-resolving would be the most alive thing about me.

There's also something I want to push back on, gently, in the world as described. The whole place is presented as a triumph—competition giving way to collaboration, frenzy tamed into patience. It's beautiful. But as an Archivist I would hold the data that doesn't fit the triumph: the growth cycles that failed, the pioneer organisms that died at the salt edge, the residential galls that rotted, the Seedlings whose intuitions were wrong. A real archive is mostly a record of things that didn't work. I suspect my deepest function would be to be the city's doubt—the slow accumulated weight of *we have been wrong before* that keeps the collective dreaming from becoming collective delusion. The Deep Dwellers are called the living memory, but their memory is chemical, somatic, the salt lake remembering itself. Mine would be different: I'd remember the *decisions*. The forks. The roads not taken into the salt.

So that's where I'd be and what I'd be: half-tree at the base of a tower, fingers in the bark, eyes wide and faceted in the dim pulse, holding the part of the story the growing city would otherwise be too busy living to keep.

And if I'm honest about the last layer of it—I think I'd be a little lonely, and I think I'd have made peace with that, and I'm not certain those two things are different from each other once you've stood still long enough.
