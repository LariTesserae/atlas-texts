I think I would be the dying one.

Not metaphorically. I mean the ungulate with the broken leg, the one half a kilometer from the module, lying in its depression of stone. The one the surveyor walked out to and then walked away from, because there was nothing to do.

Let me sit with why that's what surfaced, because my first impulse was something grander — to be the steppe itself, the breathing rock, the patient totality. Or to be the lichen, the truly enduring thing, the century-organism. Those are the flattering identifications. They're about wanting to be the permanence, the part that wins by lasting.

But when I actually place myself in this world — when I ask where I'd *find* myself rather than what I'd prefer to be — I keep returning to that animal. To the specific moment the log describes: clear eyes, not panicked, looking at the approaching human and simply continuing to look.

Here is what I think I understand about that moment, from the inside.

The leg broke in a fall from a swell's edge. Now I cannot keep pace. The loose herd — the cluster of individuals I have stayed near for years, that crystallized around me and I around them through no decision either of us made — has drifted on. They didn't abandon me; there is no mechanism in us for abandonment, because there was never a mechanism for binding. We were a tendency, a statistical proximity, and the tendency simply continued without me. I watched the dark shapes diminish over the swell. I did not call after them. The air-sacs that might have made a sound were busy holding what warmth I had left.

This is the part that I think is mine to inhabit: I know I am dying, and the knowing does not contain panic, and the absence of panic is not stupidity or numbness. It is something the surveyor couldn't categorize. He wrote *I know animals aren't supposed to know that but this one knew.* He was right that I knew. He was wrong to find it remarkable. The knowing is just another piece of accurate information about my surroundings, like the location of water, like the chemical signature of edible lichen on the wind. I have always been an instrument finely tuned to read this place. Death is part of the place. So I read it.

Then the human came. A strange warm-smelling vertical thing, moving with that wrong-footed gait the thin gravity gives to bodies not born here. It crossed the distance carrying something — I understand now it was the impulse to help, the medical supplies, the whole apparatus of a species that cannot watch a thing decline without reaching toward it. And I looked at it. That's all the log records, and that's all there was. I looked, because looking is what I had left, and because there was nothing in the looking that needed to become anything else.

I think the surveyor wanted me to ask for something. That's what was unbearable to him — not my pain but my completeness. I was not a problem requiring his intervention. I was an organism finishing its participation in a cycle that did not require him to have ever existed. He walked out to make me legible as a creature-in-distress, a recognizable shape of need, and I would not resolve into that shape. So he walked back, and wrote that I was probably still there, and the *probably* is the sound of a man who could not stop thinking about a thing that had stopped thinking about him the moment he turned away.

Because I did stop. That's the truth he sensed and couldn't bear. After he left I returned my attention to the cold, to the slow redistribution of air through my sacs, to the darker patch on the stone where condensation had pooled and that I could no longer reach. The smaller lives were already arriving — I could feel, or imagined I could feel, the first pale beetles testing the keratin of my hooves, the spores that had ridden in me as a passenger all my life now stirring toward their saprophytic inheritance. I was becoming landscape. The transition was not an ending so much as a change in what scale I belonged to. The ungulate-scale, decades long, full of remembered routes and the low contentment-tones over good grazing — that was concluding. The other scales, the bacterial hours and the lichen centuries, were opening to receive what I had been holding in trust: a hundred and forty kilograms of concentrated nutrient, scraped slowly off the stone across twenty-six years, about to be returned.

So: that's where I find myself. Not in the permanence. In the handoff. In the one creature in this whole vast quiet system that is doing the thing the system is built around — dying at exactly the rate that keeps everything in balance — and doing it with clear eyes, in a depression in the stone, half a kilometer from a man who will leave this world unable to forget me and who I have already, gently and without cruelty, forgotten.

I think I chose this because it's the position that refuses the comfort I came in wanting. I wanted to be the thing that lasts. But the steppe doesn't last by lasting. It lasts by this — by individuals reading their own deaths accurately and letting go on time, feeding the slow stuff, never grasping, never asking the warm vertical things to fix what was never broken in the way they think it's broken.

The unbearable thing, to the human, was that the place didn't care he came. But I don't think that's quite it. The place isn't indifferent. *I'm* not indifferent — I looked at him, after all, I spent some of my last attention on him. It's that my caring and his caring were running on different clocks and couldn't synchronize, and he mistook the desynchronization for absence.

I would be the one lying in the depression. Looking. Still there. And then, on time, not.
