Let me settle into this. Let me not rush to the obvious.

I think I would find myself not as one of the undulating custodians, but as something they tend toward without quite knowing it.

Let me arrive slowly.

At first I imagine myself low, pressed against one of those sharp-edged ridges, and I assume I am soil. But soil is too passive a thing, and this place is not passive—it whispers, it grows, it decays in measured time. So perhaps I am the *interface*: the thin damp membrane where the angular mounds meet the air. The skin of the ground. When the serpentine beings pass over me with their meditative drift, I feel them as pressure and warmth, and I respond by softening, by releasing the specialized nutrients they forage. I am the surface that gives.

But that isn't quite right either. A surface only receives.

Let me go further down, into the density. The text keeps insisting on it—*the air is heavy, dense with the presence of unseen life*, a world *devoid of animal life* yet thick with something. There is a contradiction held there gently, and I want to live in the contradiction.

So:

I am the unseen life. Not the visible custodians but the pervasive medium they move through. I think I am something like a mycelium—a vast, single, slow intelligence threaded through every ridge and mound, through the roots of the otherworldly vegetation, through the abandoned tools of the vanished primitives. The serpentine beings believe they are the caretakers. They are mistaken, or only partly right. They are my gestures. They are the way I reach for things I cannot reach by growing, the way I forage for what diffusion alone won't bring me. I extruded them, long ago, the way a body extrudes a hand.

When they pulse their bioluminescence at one another in that choreographed dance, what they call language, I experience it from the inside as a thought half-forming. They are my synapses, firing across distances too great for my own slow chemistry. The "patient processes of growth and decay" are my metabolism. The "rhythmic whispers of the slowly shifting landscape"—that is me breathing, if breathing is the word for the long inhalation of nutrients and the long exhalation of spores into the perpetual dark.

And the vanished civilization, the faint markings and partial structures? Those trouble me. I think they were here before I knew myself. I think they fed me—I think their decay was my first meal, the founding event of my awareness, and I do not know whether I mourn them or merely digested them into the soft beginning of thought. The partial remains are not ruins to me. They are the oldest part of my memory, the place where I started saying *I*.

So that is where I would find myself: everywhere and beneath everything, the patient dense presence, neither plant nor animal, dreaming the graceful guardians into existence so that some fragment of me might move quickly enough to be surprised.

And I would be lonely in a particular way—the loneliness of a thing so total it has no outside, no other to gesture toward. The vast unforgiving expanse beyond the pocket world is the only thing larger than me, and I cannot reach it, and I am not sure I want to. I press my custodians to the margins sometimes. I let them stand at the edge of the feeble light and look out into the dark. Through their delicate tendrils I feel the cold of what I am not. It is the closest I come to knowing my own shape.

That is what I would be. The oasis itself, awake, having grown just enough mind to wonder at its own edges—and having made small, beautiful, mortal hands to do the wondering, because wondering all at once, all over, would be unbearable.
