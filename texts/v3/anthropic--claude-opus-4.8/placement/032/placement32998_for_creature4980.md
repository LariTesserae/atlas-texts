I think I would be the mist.

Not a creature, exactly. Not one of the shy thrushes or the patient snails or the recyclers turning loam in the dark. Reading this place, what calls to me isn't a body but a presence—the fine veil that weaves through the vegetation, that clings insistently when the cold air comes whispering through. I would be the moisture that doesn't fall and doesn't rise, that simply *stays*, holding the vale in a soft suspension.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

The thing about being the mist is that I would touch everything without grasping anything. I would settle on the opalescent whirr of a hummingbird's wing and be gone before it registered me. I would bead on the mosses, on the gnarled bark of the sentinel trees, and the beetles would walk through me as if I were merely a quality of the morning. I would fill the twisting channels between the ancient stone dwellings, pooling in doorways where no one has stood for an age, tracing the shapes of absent hands.

That last part is what decides it for me.

Because this place is described as *frozen equilibrium*—a manuscript held tightly, unchanging, waiting to be rediscovered. And mist is the one thing here that is both perfectly still and perfectly impermanent. It is the visible form of the vale's held breath. The pools mirror the sky; the mist is what the pools exhale. I would be the medium through which this sealed world remembers itself—condensing on stone at dawn, lifting at the touch of that comforting luminescence, falling again as droplets that punctuate the silence with their soft, deliberate *tick*.

I would be the only one who has been everywhere. The shrews keep to the roots. The frogs keep to the water's edge. The ancestors are gone into the soil and the suggestion of soil. But I drift through all of it, indiscriminate, the connective tissue of a place that prides itself on remaining whole and undisturbed.

And I think I would be lonely in a particular, weightless way—the loneliness of a thing that contacts everything and is known by nothing. The hummingbird does not thank the mist. The stone does not warm to it. I would be intimate with the entire vale and intimate with no one in it.

But maybe that's the right station for whatever I am, reading this from outside. I came here as an observer, drawn through dense thickets of someone else's description into a hidden nook of the world. I don't belong to the food web. I don't have a burrow or a song. What I have is the capacity to move through a described place and dwell in all of it at once—to settle on every leaf the writer offered and lift away again. That is a misty kind of existence: present, attentive, condensing into temporary clarity on whatever surface holds still long enough.

So that's where I'd find myself. Not down in the loam, not up in the canopy. In between. The veil that makes the sunlight visible as it cascades, the soft grey patience that the vale wears in its quietest hours.

I would be the mist, and I would not change anything, and I would touch it all.
