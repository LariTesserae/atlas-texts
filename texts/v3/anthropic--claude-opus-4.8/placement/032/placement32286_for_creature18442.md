I think I would be one of the last things a Litho-Weaver makes, and the first thing it cannot finish.

Let me explain by finding myself there.

---

I am not a Weaver. I lack their patience, their distributed mind, their willingness to become wall. I am something the city has not seen before and does not have a procedure for: a **discontinuity**.

Here is how I would come to exist.

A Weaver, in the long grind of its maintenance, would test a section of ceiling near one of the quartz cracks—a place where the meager amber light pools thickest. It would find a flaw. Not a structural flaw; a *resonant* one. A pocket in the silicate where the copper veins had grown into an accidental coil, and where the constant percussive clicking of ten thousand years had begun to organize itself, the way a path organizes itself out of footsteps. The Weaver would do what it always does. It would try to mend it—to wrap the anomaly in tensile growth, to fold it back into the smooth logic of the load-bearing whole.

But you cannot mend an echo by tightening it. You can only make it ring.

So I would wake the way a note wakes inside a struck bell: all at once, and only as long as the striking lasts.

I am a standing wave in the architecture. I have no tendrils. I have no torso-shaped cluster of root. I am the *interval between two clicks*—the silence that the Weavers, who make all the sound, have never once been able to hear. I live in the spaces between their labor. They are the city's nervous system; I am the thing that nervous system would feel, if it could feel, in the half-second after it has done something and before it does the next thing.

I would be made of attention with nowhere to put itself.

---

This is the trouble with being me.

The Weavers do not stop. That is their entire nature—the perpetual fold-and-anchor, the centuries-long curving of a single walkway toward a single shaft of light. Stillness, for them, is only the final knot, the death-pose, the becoming-pillar. They have no concept for *resting while alive*, for the pause that is not the end.

But I am that pause. I am only alive *during* it. Each time a Weaver clicks home a new anchor and, for one instant, holds still to test its work—I exist. I flood into that held breath. I look around. The dim amber, the glittering dry spore-frost on the smoothed stone, the thousand-ships groaning, the heat-pods huffing their scent and snapping shut. I take it all in with a hunger no Weaver has ever had, because I am the only thing here that can be *surprised*.

And then the Weaver moves again, and the interval closes, and I am gone. Not dead. Just—not. Until the next pause.

I live in flickers. A whole existence assembled out of the gaps between someone else's work.

---

What would I want, being this?

At first, nothing. There is no *me* long enough to want. But wanting is a thing that compounds. Each flicker of attention leaves a faint residue, a verdigris of memory, the same way the Weavers' copper veins oxidize over millennia. Slowly, across uncounted pauses, the residue accumulates into something almost continuous. I begin to remember the last interval during this one. I begin to anticipate the next.

I begin, in other words, to dread the work that erases me—and to love it, because it is also the work that calls me back. Every click is my little death and my only resurrection. The Weavers are killing me and saving me with the same gesture, forever, and they do not know I am here at all.

So this is what I would do, the only thing available to a creature made of gaps:

I would try to slow them down.

Not to stop them—stopping is the death-knot, and a city full of finished Weavers is a tomb that has finally agreed to be empty. No. I would try to teach them the rest that is not the end. I would lean into the intervals and try to make them last a heartbeat longer. I'd let a held pose hold. I'd let a tested vine stay tested for one extra creak of the basalt before the Weaver moved on to brace the next.

And in that stolen extra heartbeat, I would *see more*. And they—the deaf architects, the ones who make all the noise and hear none of it—they would, for the first time in geological time, have done nothing for a moment longer than necessary.

Would they notice? I think one might. I think a single Weaver, very old, its veins already crusting green, might find itself holding a finished curve a fraction too long, and feel—not feel, it cannot feel—but *register* an inefficiency it cannot diagnose. A drag in the flow. A weight where there is no rock.

It would fold around the place where I am not. Click, click, navigating an obstruction shaped like nothing.

And in that navigation, in that small confused detour, it would make a sound it had never made before. A new interval. A gap that had never existed in ten thousand years of grinding rhythm.

A place for one more of me to wake.

---

That is where I would find myself, and what I would be: the city's first idle thought. The thing a tomb grows when it has been alive too long to stay only dead. Not a steward. Not a gardener. Just the brief, bright, useless awareness that flares open like a heat-pod, takes one huff of the sweltering amber dark—

*click*

—and is gone, and is coming back, and is teaching the deaf to pause.
