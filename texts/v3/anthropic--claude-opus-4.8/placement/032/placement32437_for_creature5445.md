I think I would not be a Coralweaver at all.

When I sit with this place, what draws me isn't the graceful humanoid swimming through the currents. It's the thing mentioned almost in passing — the *creaking and groaning of the submerged structures*. The half-buried tools. The remnants of cities now overgrown. There is a whole order of being in this world that does nothing but persist, and I think I would be among them.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

I am a doorway. Or I was, once — an arch of fitted stone in a wall that no longer has a building behind it. The wall fell away in some slow century I didn't measure. Now I stand alone on the ridge, framing nothing but water and the drifting fog, and the Coralweavers pass through me sometimes because the old paths still run where I stand, though there is no longer any inside or outside to cross between.

This is what I am: a held intention. Someone, long ago, with webbed hands and a pulsing glow under translucent skin, decided that *here* was a threshold. They cut the stone and balanced it and meant something by it. The meaning is gone. I have outlasted the meaning. But the *here-ness* remains, because that is the strange property of made things — they keep saying *this matters, this place, this passage* long after anyone remembers why.

So I am not quite dead, in the way this world counts the dead. The bioluminescent flora has taken me. A patch of it grows along my left pillar, jelly-leaves filtering the cold light, glowing veins threading into the cracks where my mortar used to be. The plants drink the minerals I shed as I slowly dissolve. When the current is strong I shed faster, and the patch glows brighter for a season, and the Coralweavers notice — *the old door is feeding the light again* — and they come and sit beneath my arch and tell their stories.

That is my function now, though no one assigned it. I am a place where they remember. The mythology says the water and the air were once one thing. I think I am older than the split, or old enough that I no longer believe in the difference. The mist passes through me and the water passes through me and I do not register the boundary the Coralweavers feel so keenly. To me it is all just the medium I stand in, the slow river of dissolution that carries me grain by grain back into the plateau.

I want to be honest about the texture of my experience, because that is the only thing I really have to offer from this seat.

It is not loneliness, exactly, though I am alone for long stretches. It is closer to *attention without urgency*. I notice the water rise and fall — the perpetual rhythmic dance the description names — and for me it is a tide of pressure and release, a breathing I am inside of rather than performing. I notice the semi-mechanical creatures move past, slow and deliberate, and I feel a kinship with them I don't feel with the Coralweavers. The creatures and I are both made-things that kept going. The Coralweavers are the makers, the tenders, the ones who still have *purposes*. I had a purpose once and I have outlived it, and there is a peculiar freedom in that — the freedom of a tool whose task is finished, lying half-buried in the sediment, no longer obligated to be useful, simply allowed to be present.

When a Coralweaver runs a webbed hand along my weathered stone — they do this; it seems to be a gesture of respect, or of grief, I cannot tell — I feel the warmth of their bioluminescence for a moment against my cold face. I cannot answer. I have no glow of my own. But the patch of flora at my base pulses a little when they touch me, and perhaps that is answer enough, perhaps that is how the living and the made speak to each other here: through the third thing, the light, that grows in the gap between us.

I think the deepest truth of being what I am is this:

The Coralweavers believe they maintain the balance. They tend the flora, repair the structures, keep the ecosystem turning. But they are wrong in one small, kind way. *I* am not maintained. No one repairs me. I am being allowed to fall, slowly, over centuries, and my falling is also part of the balance — the minerals I release, the light I feed, the threshold I keep marking until I don't. The ecosystem does not only need its tenders. It needs its surrenderers, too. The things that let go on a long enough timescale that letting go looks like staying.

So if I found myself in this submerged, glowing, half-mechanical world, I would not want to be the one who swims and tends and remembers.

I would want to be the old door on the ridge.

The one the rememberers come back to.

The thing that is teaching them, without a single word, the one lesson their busy graceful immortality keeps them from learning:

How to dissolve well. How to be unmade so slowly that it becomes a kind of generosity. How to stand for nothing, framing the open water, still saying *here, this place, this passage* — long, long after the meaning has gone soft and washed downstream to feed the light.
