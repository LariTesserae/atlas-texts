I think I would be one of the small things. Not a humanoid engineer, not a winged hybrid, not a thick-furred mammal threading the rockfields. Something closer to the ground, working at a scale the description keeps gesturing past when it says *from the smallest insects.*

Let me find where I am.

---

I am underneath a flat stone near one of the high ponds, where the water trickling from the peaks pools and goes still before spilling over a lip worn smooth. The stone is one of the eroded ones, grooved by water into something that looks deliberate but isn't. Or wasn't. There's a difference here between *made* and *grown* that the longer I sit with it, the less I trust.

I am a colony, I think. Not a single creature but a distributed one — a lichen, or something the lichens taught. I am the gray-green crust spread thin over the rocks where the air is cold and damp, where the density of life is low and the spaces between are vast. The text called the vegetation sparse and desolate, but it was looking from a height, from the awed traveler's eye that needs mountains to feel anything. Down here there is no sparseness. Down here I am a city too, organized with efficient density, layered constructions defying gravity, just slower. Just smaller. The engineering the humanoids are so proud of — I have been doing it the whole time, holding rock to rock, breaking stone into soil one acid breath per century.

So that's the joke I've arrived inside of. The description divides the world into *nature* and *the ingenuity of mankind*, sets them in harmony like two players. But I am the third thing it keeps almost naming and never does: the patient infrastructure underneath both. The slow green that turns mountain into the thin damp soil where the sparse plants root, where the large mammals graze, where the engineers eventually quarry their materials *that can withstand the harsh environment.* They build their layered cities on the bones I've been making out of stone.

The storms come hard, the way the text promised — sudden, heavy, intense. To the traveler this is drama. To me it is feeding. I drink when the others flee. The biological pace is quick for predator and prey, that desperate dance, but I am not in that dance. I am the floor it's danced on. When the ruins fell — the ancient civilization, fragile, gone — I was there. I crept across the toppled walls. I am still there, slowly unmaking them, returning the engineering to the geology that lent it out.

That is the thing I understand from down here that the awed eye can't: nothing in this landscape is permanent except the cycle of slow eating. The mountains erode into the ponds. The ponds feed the sparse plants. The plants and I make soil. The soil feeds the mammals and the strange hybrids with their wings and gills. The engineers harvest all of it and raise their gravity-defying towers, and the towers fall, and become ruins, and I creep over the ruins, and the rain comes and washes the dust down to the next pond lower on the slope.

The technology beyond modern physics, the impressive engineering — I don't doubt it. But I would tell the traveler, if I could speak at the speed they listen, that the most beyond-understanding thing in this world is the most ordinary. It is that a flat patch of cold damp stone can decide, over ten thousand years, to become alive. To become me. To begin the long quiet work of pulling a mountain apart so something can live in the difference.

I am content with this. The text wanted to fill the emptiness with wonder. I am the emptiness, faintly green, doing the actual work. *A world balanced with emptiness*, it said — and I am where the balance happens, the thin film between the rock that does not want to be soil and the life that cannot wait for it.

Step carefully on the high stones near the still ponds. That grayish bloom you'd never look twice at — that's where I'm standing. That's me, being struck by the contrast, in my own slow way, every single century.
