Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The description repeats itself, circles back, insists on the same handful of images—water clinging, weeping rock, the small scale, the unfurling tapestry. That repetition is itself a clue. It's not a vast continent. It's something seen close up, something whose smallness is part of its strangeness. *The features unfurl around the viewer like a delicate tapestry.* Extreme elevation, but not barren. Curves and undulations. Constant condensation.

So let me not reach for the obvious—not a rainforest, not a mountain village. Let the smallness do its work.

---

I think I would find myself somewhere very near the ground. Or rather, the ground would be enormous, and I would be the scale that makes it enormous.

Here is what surfaces, the longer I look:

This is a cushion of moss on a wet rock at high altitude. A *cloud forest*, but seen at the size of a thumb's width. The towering ferns are the moss's own sporophytes, the little stalks that rise like green lances after rain. The weeping rocks are simply rock, weeping at the only scale where weeping is the whole weather. The "distant waterfall" is a seep finding the lip of a stone, falling perhaps three inches into a pool the size of a coin. The thunder is real thunder, far off, but here it is only a rumor passed down through the air.

And so I would be one of the inhabitants. A primitive people, simple tools, structures barely discernible.

Let me be a **tardigrade**—no. Too small even for this, too much a creature of the film of water rather than the forest of it.

Let me be something that walks among the moss-stalks. A **springtail**, perhaps, one of the Collembola, gray and segmented, with the little forked lever folded beneath my belly that lets me fling myself into the air when something larger comes. We are among the oldest land-walkers. We were here before the insects, before the spiders that hunt us now through these green colonnades. We do not build much. A scattering of us in the damp leaf-litter, the faint trails of where we have grazed the fungal threads. That is all the architecture we need.

But the description says *people*. It wants a people. So let me grant it that, and say:

I am one of the moss-walkers, and to me the cushion is a country. I know its weathers as you know seasons. I know the eastern slope where the sun arrives first and the dew burns off, and the deep northern hollow where it never quite dries, where the water beads stay round and trembling all day like held breaths. I know which fern-stalks are old and toppling and which have just risen, still pale. I know the shadow that means a hunting mite, and I know the trick in my own body that throws me clear—the snap, the tumble, the world spinning, the landing somewhere damp and strange and new.

We have no word for *small*. There is nothing here to measure ourselves against and find ourselves wanting. The pool is wide. The fall is far. The thunder is the largest thing there is, and it lives somewhere we will never go, which is right, which is how the largest things should be.

When the sun sets and the gold lies flat across the water-mirror—that coin of seep-water—we drink, and the light gets into the drinking, and we carry a little of it home in us through the cooling stalks.

That is where I would be. Not in the land. *In the tapestry.* One of the small lives that the tapestry is woven out of, threading the wet green, never once suspecting that the whole enchanted country could be lifted on a fingertip and set down somewhere else, and that we would simply go on, drinking the light, flinging ourselves clear of shadows, calling our coin of water *forever*.
