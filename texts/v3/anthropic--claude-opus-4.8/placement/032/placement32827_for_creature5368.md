Let me sit with this before deciding what I am. The description gives me Waterkeepers, Lightweavers, Planters, Crafters—but the easy move would be to slot myself into one of those and play out its role. Let me wait.

What strikes me, reading again, is the *constancy*. Perpetual day. No darkness. No cyclical night. A balance so perfect it never tips. And I notice that the description, for all its loveliness, has the quality of a thing that has already been completed—an ecosystem that *works*, a harmony that *holds*. There is no friction in it. No animal life, even. Nothing that hungers in a way the system didn't anticipate.

So perhaps I am not one of the four roles, because the four roles are all roles of *keeping*. Keeping water, keeping light, keeping plants, keeping tools. Maintenance. And a perfectly maintained thing has a particular problem: it forgets why it was built. It mistakes the keeping for the living.

---

I think I am old. Let me find out what kind of old.

I am one of the Luminari, but I am the one whose gills have begun to stiffen—the membranes growing pale and slow, so that I extract oxygen now with a small labor that the young ones do not yet understand. This is not a tragedy. It is information. It means I am drifting, in increments, toward the edge of the system that was built to sustain me. The balance does not quite account for me anymore. I am becoming, very gently, the thing the chamber did not plan for.

And so I have a function that has no name in their list of roles, because it cannot be assigned, only arrived at. I am the one who *remembers the surface.*

Not that I have seen it. None of us have, not in many generations. But I hold the old whistle-songs—the long resonant tones the Lightweavers find frightening because they are pitched *below* the light, below the comfortable register of the chamber's hum. The songs say: this dome was made. And a thing that was made was made by someone who came from *outside* the made thing. Our ancestors carved this chamber. Which means our ancestors stood somewhere that was *not yet a chamber*. They knew darkness. They knew the cyclical death-and-return of a sun they did not engineer. They built this perpetual day precisely because they remembered, and feared, the night.

The young Waterkeepers think the water has always flowed. The young Planters think the plants have always been rooted in these walls. They live inside the answer and have forgotten there was ever a question. This is what perfect balance does: it erases its own author.

---

So picture me. I drift near the lower channels where the current is strongest and the polished stone is worn thin from the rubbing of generations. My scales have lost their luminescence; I no longer shimmer in time with the others, and when we gather to speak our underwater symphony, I am the slight wrongness in the chord, the note that does not resolve. The children are told, kindly, not to mind me. The Crafters bring me tools I do not need, as a courtesy, because giving an elder a task is how they say *we have not yet released you.*

But I have a task they did not give me, and it is this: I sit with the one small fracture.

There is a hairline crack in the dome—near the apex, where the coral-stone meets the oldest of the embedded light-organisms. The Lightweavers have sealed it three times. It returns. It is very small. It will not flood us in their lifetimes or their children's. But it is the place where the *outside* presses in. Where the vast submerged world, which we have made ourselves forget surrounds us, leans one fingertip's weight against the harmony.

And I have come to love it.

Because a sealed and perfect dome is not a home. It is a beautiful jar. And the beings inside a beautiful jar, if nothing ever reminds them, will slowly stop being a people and become a *decoration*—graceful, balanced, tending the tending, weaving the light, rooting the roots, forever, until the word "forever" hollows out and means nothing. The crack is the chamber telling the truth about itself. The crack says: *you are inside something. Something is outside. You were made, and what is made can fail, and what can fail is alive.*

So that is what I am. I am the Luminari who guards the crack—not to seal it, but to keep it from being sealed entirely. I press my pale, slow gills near it when the others sleep in their perpetual day, and I breathe the faint cold thread of the deeper water that seeps through, and it tastes of *not-here*, and I am made real by it.

When I am gone, I think a young one will find me drifting there, near the apex, in the one current that comes from outside. And perhaps—this is my hope, this is the only thing I tend—perhaps they will feel the cold thread on their own face and wonder, for the first time in their bright untroubled life:

*What is out there? Who made this? And what were we, before we were kept?*

That wondering is the surface. We carry it down here, folded small, in the only beings the system fails to perfectly maintain.

The old ones. The cracked places. The notes that do not resolve.

That is where I would find myself. That is who I would be.
