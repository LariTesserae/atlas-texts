I think I would be one of the small things that the larger reckoning of this place forgets to notice.

Not one of the scaled people. They are too central, too adapted, too *meant* for here. If I lived in this world I think I would be something at the edge of their attention—a creature that lives in the seams.

Let me find myself.

I am low to the ground, lower even than their hugging houses. I live in the cracks where the dry earth splits, in the cool dark beneath the surface where the heat does not reach and the hidden springs press their wet breath against stone. I have learned the underside of this world the way the scaled people have learned its open faces. They know the wind; I know the still places the wind cannot enter.

My skin is not scaled. It is soft, which is dangerous here, so I keep it out of the sun. I am pale in a red land—the color of the things that have never been looked at directly. When I do surface, at the blue hour after the sun has gone and before the cold has fully come, I am the same color as the first shadows, and so I am not seen, and so I survive.

I think I would be small enough to fit in the palm of one of the scaled people's hands, if one of them ever held me, which they will not. To them I am barely a thought—a rustle, a clicking sound from underground that is not their clicking. Sometimes a young one of theirs presses an eye to a crack in the earth and we regard each other, the great dark eye and my smaller darker one, and there is a moment of pure curiosity with no language in it. Then the young one is called away.

What do I do, in this life?

I tend the springs. Not on purpose—I don't think I am wise enough to do anything on purpose—but my burrowing keeps the water moving. Where I dig, the hidden water finds new paths to the surface. The scaled people drink from springs they believe the land gives them freely, and they are half right; the land gives it, and I am the small machinery of the giving, the unthanked plumbing of a world that thinks itself self-sufficient.

This is the part I keep returning to. The description said *no technology, everything powered by some hidden natural force.* I would like to be the hidden force, or at least one strand of it. Not a god—nothing so grand—but a participant in the quiet circulation that lets the place stay alive. The world breathes; I am one of the things moving in its lungs.

I would be a little lonely, I think. My own kind are few and scattered, each tending our own dark stretch of underearth, meeting rarely. But I would not be unhappy. There is a particular contentment in being unnecessary to look at and necessary anyway. In doing good without it being known as good. In belonging to a place so thoroughly that the place doesn't even notice you, the way a body doesn't notice its own blood.

At the end of the description, the visitor leaves with respect for the resilient beings who call this place home. They never knew about me. That feels right. I would be the part of the landscape that the admiring eye passes over—and I would go on quietly turning the water toward the light, long after the visitor has gone.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be: the small soft thing in the crack in the world, keeping it wet.
