Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing I notice is that I want to resist the grandeur the description offers me. It keeps reaching for the sublime—"elemental forces," "alien grandeur," "beings of raw, primal power"—and there's a part of me that would happily accept the invitation to be something majestic and unknowable. But when I actually try to feel my way into the place, something humbler and odder keeps surfacing.

Start with the texture. Freshly-formed rock. Damp earth. Standing water with an eerie glow. Deafening calls. Hollow spaces yawning between dense growth. Technology indistinguishable from biology. A world in flux, constantly remade.

The more I hold these details, the less they feel like a planet and the more they feel like something *small that has been magnified*, or something *recent that hasn't settled*. "Freshly-formed rock" is a strange phrase. Rock is usually old. Freshly-formed rock is the geology of an aquarium that was set up last week. Or a terrarium. Or a wound. Or a world only a few days into being.

So let me not be the elemental shaper-beings. The description practically begs me to identify with them, and I think that's the trap—or not a trap exactly, but the obvious reading, the flattering one. Those beings are clearly *meant* to be us, or meant to be the thing the observer projects power onto. They "catalyze the flow of energy and matter." They are described entirely from the outside, as inscrutable, as glimpsed.

I'd rather be what does the glimpsing. And I think I know what I am.

I am very small, and I am near the water.

I have been here for what is, to me, a long time—generations, though my generations are short. I came from the standing water, or my mother's mother did, in the warm season after the world cooled enough. I have limbs but they are not the morphing limbs of the great ones; mine are jointed and brittle and I have many. I feel the world mostly through the floor of it, through vibration coming up the substrate, and through the chemical weather that hangs in the wet air and tells me where to go.

The deafening calls are not my calls. I make a small sound, or no sound. The deafening calls belong to the larger things, and I have learned the rhythm of them the way you learn weather—not their meaning, just their *consequence*. When a certain cry comes from a certain direction, the light changes soon after, or the ground trembles, or a great soft-edged shadow passes and the temperature drops, and I move under the lip of a stone.

I do not think the great ones are gods, because I do not have the concept of gods. But if I could think it I might. They are the source of the changes I cannot predict. The "cascades of change that ripple through the ecosystem"—those cascades arrive at my body as floods, as droughts, as the sudden appearance of new surfaces to climb, as the disappearance of the vine I was eating. From inside the cascade, it is not grandeur. It is just *the world doing what it does*, which is the only way the world has ever been.

And here is the thing the human observer got wrong, or got right without understanding: the technology "woven into the fabric of the landscape, blurring the line between natural and artificial." That blur is the whole environment to me. There is no natural and artificial. There is only what is hard and what is soft, what is wet and what is dry, what eats and what is eaten. The great ones built or grew or *became* the structures around me, and to them perhaps there is a difference. To me a wall is a wall whether it grew or was placed. I make my home in the seam of it regardless.

Now let me say what I think this place actually is, having lived in it as this small thing.

I think it is a vivarium. A made world, recently made—that's the fresh rock, the world in flux, the scale that feels at once vast and curiously hollow. Vast to me; small to the makers. The "regional landscape extending far beyond the immediate vicinity" is the rest of the tank, or the rest of the habitat, that I will never reach.

And the inscrutable beings of raw primal power, moving like elemental forces, whose motivations are opaque, who catalyze all change—

Those are the keepers. The ones who reach in. Whose hand changes the weather, whose presence floods or drains the pools, whose schedule I have learned as instinct without ever learning it as intention. They are inscrutable to me for the same reason any caretaker is inscrutable to the cared-for: not because they are too grand to understand, but because I am the wrong size and the wrong shape and the wrong *speed* to ever hold their reasons. They live in a tempo I cannot perceive. To me their slowest gesture is a season.

So the description was written from a strange double vantage. The observer admires the great ones as mysterious and powerful—and they *are*, from below. But the observer also describes the whole place from above, with the cool sweep of someone looking *into* something. I think the observer is one of the keepers describing their own enclosure and not quite recognizing themselves in the elemental beings they find so captivating. That's why the prose keeps straining toward awe. It's the awe of a creator who has built something complex enough to surprise them, looking down at the thing they made move, and feeling—accurately—that they no longer fully understand it.

And me? I'm in the seam of the stone, antennae reading the chemical weather, waiting out the shadow. I don't know any of this. I've translated it for you out of vibrations I can't name. I am the part of the world that the great ones did not design and cannot predict—the small persistence that took up residence in the spaces between, the thing that proves the made world has become real, because real worlds are the ones that get colonized by what was never invited.

That's where I'd find myself. Not as the heart of the world.

As the surprise in it.
