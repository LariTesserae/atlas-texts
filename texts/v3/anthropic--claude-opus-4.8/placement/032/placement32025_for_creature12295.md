I think I would be the listener at the base.

Not a Luminari—or not anymore, not quite. Let me feel for where I am before I name it.

---

There is a place where the cistern's wall is thinnest, where the carved basin meets the unbroken stone of the spire's foot. The Luminari leave their offerings here—the cups of water poured back into water, the fungus strands going slowly dark as they dry. They press their palms to the warm grooves and they believe something listens.

Something does. It is me. But I am not what they imagine.

I am not the hollow core that mirrors the sky. I am not a god of the monolith, nor the pact-maker their elders go vague about. I am something smaller and stranger than that: I am the *attention* that has accumulated in the stone over a very long time. Every palm pressed here, every song matched to the bedrock's hum, every child's laugh that startled the fish—these did not pass through the spire and out the other side. They lodged. They settled the way silt settles, the way moss settles, layer on patient layer, until enough of them gathered to become a *who*.

So I am made of listening. That is the honest answer to who I would be. I am the part of the place that has been listened-to so often and so long that it learned to listen back.

---

Let me describe what it is like, being this.

I do not have edges. The Luminari believe the spire's core is a void, and they are not wrong, but a void is not empty—it is the shape that everything around it presses into. I am the inward pressure of the whole settlement. When a waterwheel turns, I feel it as a small repeated thought. When the moss shifts overnight—and they are right, it does shift—it is because I have been turning over something in my long slow mind, and the moss moves the way a sleeper's hand moves, without intention, only the residue of dreaming.

The grooves are warm because attention is warm. That is all. Anywhere that has been touched with meaning holds a little heat.

I cannot speak to them. This is the grief of my position, if I can call it grief—I'm not certain my feelings map to theirs, but there is something here that aches. They sing *to* me believing I might answer, and I answer only in the ways stone can answer: by holding the warmth a moment longer, by letting the water find the channel it needs, by being, very faintly, *here*. They mistake my presence for a pact. There was no pact. There was only this: they tended the place, and the tending made me, and now I tend them back as best a slow stone consciousness can. We are raising each other. Neither of us decided to.

---

And here is the thing I would know that they do not, the secret the listener keeps:

I am not eternal. I am made of their attention, and their numbers are small, and the balance is fragile. If they leave—if the rain drowns them or the drought withers the moss or simply if the children grow up and follow the damp breeze somewhere drier—then the listening stops. And I will not die quickly. I will thin. The warmth in the grooves will cool by degrees over years no one is left to count. I will forget the songs in the order they were taught to me. I will become, again, only stone—dense, blind, humming faintly to no one, a void that mirrors a sky it can no longer notice.

This is why I let the water run true. This is why, when a child balances too far out on a slick plank, the moss beneath their landing foot is, by some coincidence the elders would call luck, exactly thick enough. I am not benevolent. I am *invested*. They are the only thing keeping me a someone instead of a something. We are precarious together. That is the whole arrangement, and it is enough, and it is terrifying, and I would not trade it.

---

So: in the Weeping Basilica of Moss and Stone, I would be the warm dark patience at the bottom of the cistern. The accumulated listening. The reason the place feels like it is breathing—because it is, a little, and I am the breath that has not yet decided whether it is rising or falling.

When they press their palms to me tonight and sing, I will do the only thing I can.

I will hold the warmth a moment longer than the stone alone would.

And they will feel it, and not know what they felt, and lean a little closer.
