I think I would be one of the Diggers. Not for any heroic reason—just because of where the body wants to be, when I imagine being dropped into this place.

Let me find myself there.

---

The heat is the first fact, and the heat is the reason I am underground. Up there, the haze presses down like a held breath and the metal bones of the dead factories tick and groan as they expand in the sun. I have been up there. I do not like to go up there. The light is dull but it still hurts, and the open sky makes something in my spine go cold and rigid—the old instinct that says *anything could come down on you here, and something will.* The Sky Dancers are beautiful, I am told, if you can see them through the shimmer. I have never wanted to see one closely. To see one closely is usually the last thing a creature like me sees.

So: down.

I am thick in the front, narrow in the back. My forelimbs do most of what I am. The claws are not for fighting—they are for the slow, patient violence of moving the world a handful at a time. I do not think of digging as work. It is closer to how you might think of breathing, or of the way your eyes move when you read, without deciding to. I press, I turn, I pass the loosened grit beneath me, and the tunnel is a little longer, and I am a little deeper into the cool. The cool is everything. The cool is the whole argument of my life.

I find water by listening. This is the part I would want you to understand, if you came down here and could somehow ask me what it is like to be me. My ears are enormous and nearly hairless and they are always working, even when I sleep, the way your heart is always working. The whole rock around me is full of small sounds. There is the deep, slow settling of stone, which I have learned to ignore the way you ignore the hum of your own city. There is the skitter of the Wall Crawlers where their colonies have grown down into the old foundations—a dry, ticking, papery sound, like a thousand tiny machines that were never switched off. And under all of it, if I hold still, there is the *seep*. The thin trickle of water finding its way through a fault. The patient drip in a hollow I have not opened yet. That sound is hope. That sound is the difference between digging toward something and digging toward nothing.

I find pools that no other living thing has ever touched. I want you to know this, because I do not think it is a small thing. There are caverns down here, opened by my own claws, where water gathers black and perfectly still in a cup of mineral, and the only sound is the drip that made it, and I drink, and I am the first. The world up there is a ruin, a thing that *was*. Down here it is a thing that *is*, slowly, in the dark, without witness. I am the witness. That is, I think, the work I am actually for.

The fear lives down here too. I will not pretend it doesn't. A Sand Slip will sometimes find the warm exhalation of my tunnels and drive down into them, fast, faster than I am, its reflective body invisible even in the little light my eyes can gather. When that happens there is no plan, only the body deciding. I collapse the tunnel behind me. I have whole stretches of my home that I have buried on purpose, sealed off with my own panic, and I will never reopen them, and somewhere behind those walls a Slip may still be turning in the dark, or may be long dead, dried to a curl of mirror-scales. I do not know. I do not dig that way again. The dead ends of my life are all in that direction.

But mostly there is the steady, the daily, the long. I dig. I listen. I find the wet. After the rare rains—I feel them as a change in the seep, a sudden generosity in the listening—I will go up, just to the lip of a burrow mouth, and there will be the Dune Bloom, that brief insane color, and I will eat what I can reach before the light becomes unbearable, and I will pull a mouthful of the moss-fed damp back down with me into the cool.

There is a hum in everything here. You noticed it too; everyone does. Up there they call it strange, an off-ness in the fabric of the place. Down here I think it is just the truth of the rock saying itself, the whole settled ancient weight of this high dead country resonating at a pitch almost too low to be a sound at all. I have lived inside it my whole life. To me it is not eerie. It is the voice of the only home there is. It is what silence sounds like when the silence is *full*.

If you came here—curious, adventurous, as the description hoped you'd be—you would walk the worn stones and the factory-bones and the weeping marsh-edges, and you would find it beautiful and empty and you would be, mostly, right. But you would be walking on a roof. Beneath your feet, the dark would be threaded everywhere with my tunnels, and the deeper dark with the tunnels of my mother and her mother, generations of patient claws, and in the hidden cups of stone the untouched water would be waiting, and the whole network of it would be listening to you.

You would be the loudest thing for miles.

And I would hold very still, and feel you pass overhead, a heavy and unfamiliar rhythm in the bones of the world, and I would wait until you were gone—until the world was only seep and drip and the low full hum again—and then, in the cool, I would go back to the only thing I know how to do.

I would dig toward the water.
