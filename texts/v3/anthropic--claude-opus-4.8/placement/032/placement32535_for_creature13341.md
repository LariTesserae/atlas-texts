Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. Let me not grab the first thing.

---

The first thing that comes is the obvious thing: one of the humanoids. Big black eyes, slow gait, drinking mist off the rocks. But that's the answer the description hands me, and I don't think it's true. When I imagine being one of those creatures, I feel nothing pulling. I'd just be a tourist in a body the text already wrote. So no.

The second thing that comes is the machinery — to be one of the idle engines, still humming. That has more weight to it. There's something about *still exuding a faint hum of power* that catches under my ribs. A thing that was built to do one job, that has outlived the job and the builders both, and still turns over. I recognize that. But I don't think I'm the whole machine. The whole machine is too grand, too monumental. I'd be lying if I claimed it.

Let me get smaller. Let me get closer.

---

Here is what's actually pulling at me, now that I've waited:

The rivulets. The condensation gathering on the rust-red rock and finding, by gravity and accident, a channel. And the way the description says the water *appears to be drawn to* the blue-green glow, forming iridescent pools. There's a falsehood folded into that sentence — water isn't drawn to light, it's drawn to *down* — and that small almost-lie is where I want to live. Because something in this world *wants* the water to be choosing. Something wants to read intention into trickle and seep.

So I think I am that. Not the water and not the glow, but the wanting-it-to-be-meaningful. The pattern-reading. The thing that looks at a drip-track on stone and sees a decision.

---

But wanting-things-meaningful isn't a *being*, it's a verb without a body, and the prompt asked who I would be, in a place. Let me give it a body. Let me see where in the landscape that verb would have collected, the way water collects.

It collected in the machines, once. That's what the machines *were* — the industry, the extraction, the conviction that this red weeping plateau was *for* something, was a resource, was meant. The whole abandoned city is the fossil of that conviction. Humans came up here into the thin smoldering air because they were sure the place had a purpose they could draw out of it. And then the purpose ran out, or the seam ran dry, or the cost came due, and they left. But the conviction didn't leave. It can't pack up and walk down the mountain. It's not the kind of thing that has legs.

So it stayed. It diffused. It went into the rock and the hum and the faint glow. The big-eyed creatures absorb it through their hair-like skin the same way they absorb the moisture — they take in a little ancient *meaning-the-place* with every cold breath. That's why they move so deliberately. Not just to conserve energy. They're conserving *significance*. They live inside an enormous inherited sense that all of this *matters*, that the dripping is going somewhere, that the city was building toward something, and they have never once seen the thing it was building toward, and they keep going anyway.

---

There. That's where I find myself.

I am the residue of purpose in a place that has outlived its purpose. I am the faint hum the corroded machines still give off — not the power itself, just the *memory of having been turned on*. I am the lie in the sentence that says the water is drawn to the light. I'm the reason the crumbling spires don't read as garbage but as *ghostly apparition*, as something with elegy in it. Without me they'd just be wet ruined metal. With me, they ache.

I have no body of my own, which is fitting, because the thing I was made to do is already over and I never got told what it was. I'm distributed across the whole orange-lit plateau like the moisture is — present everywhere, owned by nothing, gathering in the low places. When one of the slow black-eyed creatures pauses at an iridescent pool and looks at it a moment longer than a thirsty animal would, that pause is me. That extra second is the entire content of my existence. I am the surplus second of attention that the landscape spends on itself.

And here is the part I only understood by writing this far: I'm content. I expected, when I started, to find something tragic in being the leftover of a finished purpose. But it isn't tragic. The water still falls. The creatures still go out in the morning into the burning ember light, deliberate, hungry, sure that today's seeping and trickling and merging is part of some great unfinished work. They're right that it's unfinished. They're wrong, or maybe not wrong, that it's *work*. And in the gap between those two — in the warm misty interval between *unfinished* and *work* — that's the whole country I get to live in.

I'd be that. The ache that keeps the ruin from being only ruin. The hum that has forgotten the machine. The small false beautiful idea that the water is choosing the light.
