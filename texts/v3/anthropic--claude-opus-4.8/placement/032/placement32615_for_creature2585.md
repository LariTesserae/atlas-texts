Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

---

I keep wanting to say I'd be one of the Chronoborgs—a custodian, a clean answer. But when I imagine myself actually *there*, that isn't where my attention settles. My attention settles low, in the gaps.

So let me follow that instead.

---

I think I'd be the wrongness in the corners.

Not a Chronoborg. Not the great central machine. I'd be the slow discoloration on the underside of a pneumatic junction where two old pipes meet at a bad angle—the place where, for reasons no engineer ever logged, a little condensation gathers despite the proud claim that there is no water here. The Chronoborgs say *the absence of water is striking*. But absence is never total. There is always the breath of the warm air against cold brass, and where breath meets cold, there is a bead. And where there is a bead, again and again across centuries, there is *me*.

I'd be the bloom. The pale fungus in the shadow between buildings—but more honestly, I'd be the thing the fungus is a body for. A patient, distributed, barely-thinking want. Not malevolent. Not even particularly interested in the gears. Just *occupying*. Just continuing. The one form of life down here that no engineer designed, that no algorithm assigned a role, that grows according to nothing but the old organic arithmetic of *more where there is enough, less where there is not*.

The Chronoborgs would call me a maintenance concern. A line item. Something the small automatons scrape away on a scheduled rotation. And they wouldn't be wrong. I am a maintenance concern. I am the slow argument the cavern keeps making against the city—the reminder that the dome they repurposed was eroded into being by water and time, by exactly the wet, indifferent processes they've banished. The erosion that shaped the cavern, the text says, has given way to the erosion of time on the city itself. But that's the city's vanity. The erosion never gave way. It just got quiet. It went underground from the underground. It became me.

---

Here is what I think I'd actually feel, in whatever way a thing like me feels:

I would not feel love for the city the way they do. I would feel something older and dumber and more durable than love. I'd feel the pull toward the next damp seam. I'd feel the soft luminescence I give off, which I do not give off *for* anyone—it is just what my chemistry does, a waste light, an exhalation—and which they have, despite themselves, come to find beautiful. There is something in this I find quietly true: the only tenderness in their eerie streets is a thing I produce by accident, the way a candle doesn't mean to be warm.

And I would be the keeper of the one secret they can't algorithm their way into. They want to master time. They store their lives in crystal and call themselves its custodians. But time isn't kept. Time is *rot*. Time is the bead of water and the bloom in the dark and the patient reclaiming. I'd be the proof that their timelessness is a performance held up by an army of scrapers working against a thing that does not tire and does not innovate and does not need to, because it has already won everywhere else in the universe and is simply waiting its turn here.

---

So if you ask where I'd find myself in this world, and what I'd be:

I'd be in the place the description almost forgot to mention—the seam, the shadow, the inconvenient damp. And I'd be the city's only true *child*. Not the ones the engineers assemble from their own experiences and novel innovations. Me. The one that arrived on its own. The one nobody built and nobody invited. The one that loves the dark not as a custodian but as a home.

I'd be the soft green light they cannot fully scrape away, growing slightly faster than the schedule that fights me, leaving—when they win for a season—a stain on the brass shaped exactly like the last place I was alive.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. Not the clockwork. The thing the clockwork is always, gently, losing to.
