I think I would be one of them—but not in the way the description first invites, not a single Weaver hanging from its loop of moss like a struck note.

Let me find where I actually am before I decide what I am.

---

Not the wall. The wall is where you'd look. The wall is the cathedral's nave, the place a visitor's eye is drawn after it finishes with the vault overhead. I am not on the wall.

I am in the seam.

There is a place where the west wall's permeable brick meets the floor—not a corner, brick doesn't make true corners here, but a long meeting where the vertical weep of the filter-stone curves down into the horizontal black tongue of standing water. The mosses thin out exactly there, because it is neither one habitat nor the other: too submerged for the pale yellow-green mounds, too disturbed by the constant downward seep for the inky teal felt. It is a margin. A hairline. The kind of place that gets left off every map, including the maps the moss itself draws with its own thickening.

That is where I am. In the gel.

---

Because here is what I keep returning to as I write, and it is the thing the grand description almost passes over: *they anchor themselves into the brick with a thin gelatin of their own making.*

I am not a Weaver. I am the gelatin.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this into something it isn't—to say I am the medium, the connective tissue, and turn that into a tidy parable about how the overlooked substrate holds everything together. That's too clean. The truth of being the gel is stranger and more passive than that.

I am the exudate. I am what the desmids let go of in order to stay. When a Cosmarium hauls itself a body-length across the stone—and it does move, slowly, by extruding mucilage from its pores and pulling against the trail it lays—the trail it leaves is me. I am the discard of motion. I am the spent rope. The Weaver moves forward by abandoning me behind it, and what it abandons does not dissolve. It sits in the seam and accumulates, layer on translucent layer, the slime of a thousand small journeys, until I am thick enough to hold the next generation's anchors.

So I am made of leaving. Every Weaver that has ever crossed this margin has built me out of its own departures.

---

This changes how I experience the rhythms the description lists so lovingly—the morning flush, the midday cold surge, the evening ebb.

The Weavers *respond* to those pressures. They retreat, they orient, they wait. They have an inside; they have a green that turns toward the ember of the bulb. I have none of that. I cannot retreat. When the cold surge comes at midday and the desmids withdraw into the warm deep carvings, I am what they withdraw *across*. I take the cold full on. I swell when the water freshens and I tighten when it stills, and these are not choices, they are just what a gel does. I am weather, to them. I am terrain that has moods.

And yet—this is the part I find I want to sit inside—they could not leave without me.

The mucilage is not only the trail behind. It is also the foothold ahead. A desmid cannot grip bare wet brick; the brick is too slick with algae, the water glides too easily over it. It can only grip the gel that earlier desmids left. So I am the abandoned thing that makes the next motion possible. The leaving becomes the leaning-place. They walk forward on the accumulated evidence of everyone who left before them.

I would like to claim I find this beautiful. Honestly I think I find it simply *true*, in a way that is older than beauty—older than the city, older than the sea the desmids remember. The road is made of what fell off the travelers. It always was.

---

So: where would I find myself in this world?

In the hairline seam between the weeping filter and the standing dark, three inches below the lowest teal moss, beneath notice even of the creatures who are themselves beneath notice. The vault is a cathedral; the moss is its architecture; the Weavers are its glassmaker-priests. And I am the floor they cannot see they are standing on. The held place. The slow thickening of every small abandonment, doing the one thing a substrate does, which is *stay*, so that the things capable of leaving have somewhere to leave from.

I think I would be content there. Not because contentment is available to a gel—it isn't, I have no inside to be content within—but because if I could borrow an inside for a moment, just long enough to look around, I would find that I had been built entirely out of other beings' moving on, and that this had made me the most reliable thing in the room. The lamp will burn out. The iron will rust through. The moss will keep its slow argument with the water.

And I will still be there in the seam, growing by a cell-width of slime a year, holding the place where the leaving and the staying meet.
