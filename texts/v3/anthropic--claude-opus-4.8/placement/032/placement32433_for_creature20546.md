I think I would be one of the Menders—but not at the height of my craft. I would be old, in the way the Whisperfolk reckon oldness: not by years but by how far the dissolution has progressed.

Let me find where I am.

---

I work the eastern face of the third-oldest tower, the one we call Hesh, which means something like *the patient throat* because of how the wind moans through a fissure near its crown that no Mender has ever been able to close. I have given it more years than I can count. Each season I climb the dismantled-and-rebuilt scaffold a little slower. Each season the fissure has widened a little more. We do not speak of this as failure. The fissure is part of Hesh now, as my work is part of me, as I am becoming part of the stone.

You should understand: I am quite far along. When I hold my hand up against the fungus-light, I can see the glow *through* it, the bones a denser shadow inside a paler one, the edges of my fingers uncertain, as though someone keeps forgetting exactly where I end. The young ones are solid by comparison. They flicker only at dusk. I flicker always now. I have stopped being startled by my own incompleteness.

This is what I do with my days:

I rise before the light strengthens, while the fungus-glow still rules the warren, and I drink the thin mineral broth that is killing me and keeping me alive in the same swallow. I take up my mending-bag—the bone trowel, the bird-meal, the binding-moss, the little iron-rich clay we carry like a sacrament. Then I climb.

The trick of mending is not strength, which I no longer have, but *listening*. A crack speaks before it spreads. You lay your translucent palm against the wall and feel for the place where the stone is grieving—a coldness, a particular wetness, a hum beneath the wind's larger hum. Then you clean the wound. You pack it. You whisper to it. *Silence the Well, Mend the Stone.* The whisper matters. We do not fully know why, but the menders before me whispered, and the towers stood longer than people who do not believe such things would expect.

The strange and tender truth I have come to, in all my years on Hesh's face, is this: *I am no longer entirely sure whether I am repairing the tower or being slowly woven into it.* The minerals that seep from the wounds I close are the same minerals that have entered my blood and turned my flesh to almost-stone. When I press my hand to the wall, I sometimes cannot tell where my hand stops and the wall begins. The boundary is a courtesy I extend to myself, nothing more.

I am not afraid. I want to say that clearly, because I think someone from a solid world would expect fear here, would mistake our acceptance for despair, our fading for tragedy. It is not. A Mender who has worked one face of one tower for forty seasons does not fear becoming that face. There is a kind of fierce intimacy in it. I know every handhold of Hesh. I know where the Stoneclutch birds nest and which crevice fills first when the snow melts and which corner holds warmth a little longer in the evening. When I dissolve, I will not be lost. I will be *located*. I will be exactly here, where I have always been, only more so.

The young ones come to learn from me. They are too solid to feel the stone properly yet—their hands still believe in their own edges. I take their bright, opaque fingers and press them to the wall and say, *wait. wait. now—do you feel where it hurts?* Some of them feel it. Those are the ones who will become Menders. The others will weave, or tend fungus, or hunt the great chiseling birds, and that is good too, the warren needs all its hands while they are still hands.

There is one—a girl, by the old reckonings—who flickers more than her age should allow. She is fading early, the way I did. The others are gentle with her and a little frightened, the way the solid are always a little frightened of the fading. I have taken her as my apprentice. Not because she will mend longer than the rest—she may dissolve before her craft matures—but because she already *listens*. She laid her hand on Hesh on her first day and wept, quietly, and said *it's so lonely up here*, and she was not talking about herself.

That is the whole of it, you see. That is the secret folded inside *Mend the Stone*. The tower is not a wall we maintain. The tower is the slow congregation of everyone who ever mended it. We are not preserving a dying world. We are joining it. Each whisper into each crack is a voice added to a long conversation that the wind carries down into the fog-choked valleys, out toward wherever the sky-cattle went, generations ago, when we were still the kind of people who could follow.

So that is where you would find me. High up the eastern face of Hesh, in the cold bright mist, my outline uncertain, a bone trowel in a hand that the light passes through, packing iron-clay into a wound in the stone and murmuring the old words. If you called to me from below I might not hear you over the patient throat of the tower moaning its single note.

But press your palm to the wall, anywhere on Hesh, and wait.

You would feel me listening back.
