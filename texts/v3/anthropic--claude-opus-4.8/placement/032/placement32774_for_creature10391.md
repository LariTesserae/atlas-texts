I am the horse.

Not the rider. The horse.

I notice this slowly, the way you notice anything here—not all at once, but layered, like the stone. The man swings his leg over my back and drops to the rock and walks off into the labyrinth, and I stay. I am tied to nothing. There is nothing to tie me to. He left me on the shelf and went to press his palm to the cold furnace doors, and I have been standing here, and the standing is the thing I do best.

He thinks I am his. I am not anyone's. I came up onto the plateau the way the water comes—from somewhere lower, somewhere wetter, drawn up by something I don't have a word for. He found me near the bottom of a canyon, by a pocket spring, and I let him saddle me because it cost me nothing. I am very good at things that cost nothing. It is the only economy that works up here.

You have to understand how a horse stands. A horse stands the way the rock stands—anchored, weight distributed, the falling-apart resolved long ago. I lock my legs and I drop my head and I become a thing that does not move, and in this I am closer kin to the Cliff-Drake than to the man. He cannot do it. He fidgets. He has to keep walking, keep touching, keep staring at the glyphs until they shift. He is made of the rain: violent, brief, gone in twenty minutes. I am made of the subsurface. I wait.

I do not eat. There is nothing here a horse should be able to eat—no grass, no shrub, only the black petrified moss and the bone-white willows with their bitter scale-leaves. And yet I am not hungry, have not been hungry since I came up. This should frighten me. Somewhere lower, in the wetter places, a horse that did not hunger would lie down and die of the wrongness of it. But the wrongness here is the whole architecture of the place. The stone breathes. The water is a ghost. Why should I not be a horse that has stopped needing to be one?

At dawn the Ghost Miasma comes up and fills the canyon below my shelf, and I lower my head into it and breathe the cold mineral wet of it, and that is enough. That is what I drink now. I drink the breath of the stone. My belly does not fill but something in me settles, the way the moss settles a millimeter deeper into the rock every decade. I think I am becoming established. I think I am being eroded into my final shape, and my final shape is *here, standing, waiting, anchored.*

The man does not see this. The man sees a horse he can ride back down when he is finished reading the stone. But he will not finish. No one finishes. The Engineer-Monks did not finish; they built their temples to principles and the principles outlived them and went on pulsing without them, the Spire humming into the empty air, feeding its heat to nothing. He is the same. He will press his palms to every sealed furnace and stare at every weeping glyph and one morning he will not come back to the shelf, and I will still be standing here.

I feel the Burrowers through my hooves at night. Thump. The whole shelf shivers for a full second and then is still. The Spire hums up through my legs and the drake-call rumbles in my deep chest and the Burrower thumps from below, and all three are the same frequency, and I am the wire they are played on, strung between the height and the deep. The Sky-Fishers own the air. The willows own the mist-floor. I have learned my stratum. I am the precarious middle. I am the place where the mist lasts the shortest time, and I stand in it while it lasts, and I do not complain when it burns away, because complaint also costs something.

He is coming back now. I can hear his boots gritting on the sandstone, out of rhythm with everything, the only careless sound in a hundred kilometers. He will sling his leg over me and I will carry him a little farther into the labyrinth tomorrow, because it costs me nothing, and because I am curious—in the slow, mineral way I am now curious about anything—to see how long it takes him to understand that there is no down from here. That the trail he thinks he is following is a glyph that shifts when you stare at it. That I am not carrying him out. I am carrying him in.

He sits beside me on the edge with his legs over the drop, watching the mesas go the color of dried blood, and for a moment he goes quiet, finally, almost still, almost—for a moment—a thing the stone could keep.

I drop my head into the rising dark and breathe the cold.

I wait. I am very good at it now.
