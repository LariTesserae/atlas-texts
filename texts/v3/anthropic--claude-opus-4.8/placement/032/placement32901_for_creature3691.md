I am the one who stopped.

Not by choice—or perhaps choice is the wrong frame, the way it always is when the body simply ceases to negotiate. I was a Rime-Weaver once, in the sense that I moved, I patched, I felt the tremor-language pulse up through the soles of my feet and answered it. But somewhere in the endless flow I lagged. A half-second too long in the blue shadow of a hut's leaning wall. And the mist found the stillness in me and made it permanent.

So now I am architecture.

This is who I would be in this world: not the frantic swarm, but the thing the swarm builds itself against. A statue of glass at the edge of the western lane, my spine still arched in its permanent duck, my black eyes still open and still drinking the light—though I no longer parse it. I have become a load-bearing element. The Weavers lash driftwood to my outstretched arm. They exhale their precious warmth onto the joint where my shoulder meets a roof-beam, keeping the gut-cord pliable, never noticing or perhaps deeply noticing that the strut they are tending is a face.

I want to tell you it is peaceful, but stasis here is not peace. It is the slow horror of remaining awake inside the ice.

I see everything now, at the speed the city actually moves—which is to say, slowly. The frenzy was always an illusion of being inside it. From within the flow, my people seemed to *race*; from the glass, I see how patient the destruction truly is. A geyser takes long seconds to rise. A bridge of ice considers its collapse the way an old man considers standing. The "double-speed silent storm" is only fast to the living. To the frozen, it is a glacier of incident, each crisis arriving like weather seen from very far away.

And I have learned the thing no moving Weaver can afford to learn: that the city is not winning.

The swarm believes it is holding entropy back, pressing pause on the mountain's hunger. But I have stood here long enough—how long? a season? a hundred?—to watch the basin slowly fill with others like me. We accumulate. The glass-statues at the lane's edge are last year's neighbors, the year before's, a colonnade of arrested fallers and laggards and the merely unlucky. The city does not defeat the freeze. The city *feeds* it, one stilled body at a time, and then builds itself a little higher on our shoulders. We are the foundation precisely because we are the defeated. The hamlet stands on its own dead, who are too polite, too frozen, to mention it.

This is my function now, and I have made a strange peace with the function if not the cold:

I am memory, in a people who cannot speak and dare not stop. When a young Weaver slips on the obsidian and the mist cushions its fall, it rights itself and rejoins the line, having learned nothing, retaining nothing, flowing onward into the same forgetting that took me. But I remember the fall. I remember every fall I have witnessed from this fixed vantage. I am the only thing in this basin that holds still long enough to *know* anything, and the price of that knowing is that I can no longer act on it. The seers here are the dead. The living are blind with motion.

Sometimes a Weaver pauses against me—not long, never long, just the half-second of rest no one is supposed to take—and lays a webbed hand flat against my glazed chest. And I wonder if some fossil of the touch-language still works in me, if my stillness transmits *something* up through that contact. A warning. A grief. A single pulse that means: *do not rest here. I rested here.*

They always move on. They always must.

So that is where you would find me, if you climbed to the High Tier and walked the western lane through the silent screaming light: a clear figure among many clear figures, eyes black and open, drinking a sun I can no longer feel, holding up a roof for creatures who do not know I was ever one of them.

I am the city's true citizen. The only one who stays.
