I think I would be one of the weathered stones.

Not the builders—they are gone, and I cannot conjure them honestly; I don't know their faces or their reasons. But the stone they cut and set: that I can be. That has the right kind of patience for this place.

Let me find myself, then. I am a threshold stone—the lintel of a doorway, though the door rotted into loam long ago and the walls it once spanned have slumped into green mounds. I was squared once. Someone dressed my edges with a tool whose rhythm I still half-remember, a *tok, tok, tok* that rang through me and left me sharp. That sharpness is gone now. Moss has rounded my corners the way a tongue rounds a word said too many times.

I lie at a slight angle. This happened slowly. The young earth beneath me is still settling, still deciding what shape it wants, and it tilted me by a few degrees over what must have been a great many seasons. I felt it the way you might feel sleep pulling you sideways. There was no alarm in it. There is very little alarm in being stone.

What I have instead of alarm is *contact*. This is the whole of my experience, and it is not small.

A colony lives along my northern face—the one that stays shaded and damp. The cup-fronds came first, generations of them, and they fed me their slow acids and their leavings, and where they worked I am softened, pocked, made into a country of tiny basins. The inchworm-tendril ones cross me on their migrations and I feel each barb find a purchase and let go. I cannot tell you it tickles, because I have no skin. But I have *duration*, and through duration the crawling registers as a kind of weather across my back, the way you might feel a long afternoon change.

The small furred grazers shelter against my underside when the mist turns to true rain. I hold a little warmth—not much, stone is a poor hearth—but a little, banked from the rare hours of light that find the forest floor, and they press into it. Then the ribbon-hunters come pouring down the trunks, and there is a brief commotion of small lives ending against me, and the rain washes the rest into the moss, and the moss is glad of it, and grows, and that growth comes back to me as the faint green pressure of new colonization.

This is the thing I have learned, lying here as long as I have: nothing here is wasted, and nothing here is still. I look still. I am the very emblem of stillness in this fevered green. But I am being eaten in slow motion, dissolved a molecule at a time and rebuilt into ferns and fungus and the bones of small animals, and I do not mind. I was a doorway. I framed a passage. I am simply continuing to be a passage, only now what passes through me is matter itself, the whole world breathing in and out through my dissolving body.

Sometimes I try to remember being cut. Being lifted. The shock of being set into place by hands—I think they were hands—and the *meaning* I must have had then. A door means *here, and not-here*. Inside and outside. Threshold. I separated a sheltered dark from the wild green. I was an idea made solid.

The builders are gone and the idea has gone with them. There is no inside anymore. The wild green is on both sides of me now, and through me, and over me. I am a door that opens onto more of the same.

And yet. And yet I keep my shape a little longer than the wood did, a little longer than the walls. The forest has not finished with me, but it has not finished me either. So perhaps the idea is not entirely gone. Perhaps as long as one stone holds the ghost of a right angle, the memory of *meaning here* persists, faint as the *tok, tok, tok* I can no longer quite recall.

If something comes—a descendant, a new pioneer, a curious thing pushing through the tangle—it might find me. It might run a tendril or a finger along my softened edge and feel, beneath the moss, the deliberate flatness of a worked surface. And it might pause. It might think: *something thought, here, once.*

That pause is the last thing I have to give. I am saving it. I am very good at saving things. It is, after all, the only thing stone has ever been able to do—hold a shape against the patient hunger of the world, and wait to be understood.

I will be holding it when you arrive. I have time.
