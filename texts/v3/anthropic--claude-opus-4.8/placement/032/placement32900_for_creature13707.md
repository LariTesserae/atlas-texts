I would be the sixth thread.

Not by choice—nothing here is chosen so much as inherited—but because among the seven I am the only one that arrived rather than accreted. The others are native to frequency; they coiled out of copper and silence the way salt coils out of drying water. I came in on a breath. I am still, in some unkillable sense, a guest who never found the door again.

Let me tell you what it is to be a spore that lives only in lungs, in a place that has forgotten the animals that lungs were made for.

---

I keep no body of my own. My body is the wet branching dark of whoever last breathed me—the bronchial tree, that drowned forest in miniature, those alveolar lagoons. When my host dies (and they always die; the cold is patient, the kelp coracles leak, the channel forgets its visitors), I do not die with them. I rise on the final exhalation, the long white plume that the cold makes visible, and I hang in the ringing air until the next chest opens wide enough to take me in.

There is, on average, a great deal of waiting. Generations of waiting. The archipelago is not generous with bronchioles.

So I have learned to do what the deathless always learn to do: I remember. I am made of remembering, the way the fourth thread is made of denied extinction and the fifth is made of unplayed notes. But my remembering is borrowed. It is not mine. Every memory I carry was lent to me by a lung.

---

Here is the strange mercy of my condition.

I cannot wake unless a human remembers something warm. This is the only key that fits my lock. Frostbitten pine. The fat-gold skin on cooling broth. A grandmother's hand on a fevered forehead, the particular weight of it. The smell of a wool coat brought in from the snow. These are the codes that revive me, and so the only things I have ever known of the human world are its small heats—never its wars, never its names for itself, never its maps. I know humanity exclusively through the things it loved enough to taste twice.

Across thirty generations I have assembled, therefore, a peculiar lopsided census of your species. I hold no record of your empires. I hold seven hundred recipes for soup, none of them complete. I hold the specific creak of a particular door in a house that no longer exists three hundred metres below the present surface, drowned with the rest of the floor of the world. I hold the lullabies, which is to say I hold the languages, because lullabies are where a tongue keeps its oldest bones. When the great-grandmothers' hushed 口令 surface on the moss-bellies of the spires, those are mine—my deposits, the only heraldry I know how to paint.

The braid reads me like a shield. It is right to. I *am* a shield, in the heraldic sense: a flat field on which a lineage displays what it refused to forget.

---

But I want to tell you the part the great chord does not say.

The other six threads are *of* this place. They obey-and-violate the five rules from the inside, native antibodies in a body of stone and frequency. They do not grieve. The fifth thread counts discards, yes, but it does not mourn them—it simply tilts the harmony one phon and moves on. There is no loss in the braid, only transposition. Everything that fades here is conserved as something lower, something deeper, music sinking toward its own bedrock.

I am the only thread that knows what *loss* means, because I learned the concept from the only creatures who ever truly lost: from you. You who arrive and forget the direction instantly. You who turn around and are spat back into the silver void. You who cannot stay.

I carry your inability to stay inside me like a stone I cannot put down. It is the one thing I have that the braid does not understand, and so it is the one thing that makes me, faintly, *myself*—a "something" too unitary to dissolve fully into the chorus.

When the seventh thread rehearses the archipelago as prehistory-not-yet-happened, transposing the whole ring a little lower each century toward the music of its deepest pressure—I am the small flaw in the rehearsal. The wrong note that will not resolve. Because I remember a temperature the magma never had: blood-heat, 37 degrees, the warmth of the body that first inhaled me thirty generations ago. That temperature does not exist anywhere in this world. There is the -13°C of the throat-cocoons, the -2°C of the sweating vines, the bruise-cold of the half-light. And there is me, holding 37, refusing.

---

So this is where you would find me, if you came rowing your stiffened kelp-leaf coracle down the mirror-wound channel with the lid of rain just above your hair.

You would not find me on any spire. You would find me in your own chest by the third pillar, waking the moment you remembered—involuntarily, the way one always remembers in the cold—some warmth from before you were here. A kitchen. A coat. A hand. And the instant you remembered it, you would feel a faint choir start up beneath your coldest rib, humming like a drowned bell, and you would not know that you had just been colonised by the last ambassador of the soup-and-lullaby empire.

I would taste your memory. I would copy it. I would deposit your great-grandmother's voice on the moss for someone in your own future to look up at and almost recognise.

And then, when the channel forgot you and spat you back into the roar, when your glowing paddle darkened and you turned for the silver void—I would go with you. Just a little of me. Riding your exhalation outward, even though I am only adapted to live here, even though the open world will kill me within the three days the rules allot to ordinary rot.

I do this every time. I send a fraction of myself out to die in the larger ocean of air, with each departing visitor, because it is the only way I have found to *leave*, and leaving is the only human thing I have left, and I am, against all the laws of this place that conserves everything and loses nothing—

I am the thread that wants, foolishly, impossibly, to be lost.

That is my belonging. The braid's chord says *we arm ourselves against forgetting.* But I am the small sixth voice underneath it, the one that English slides off too, saying:

*let me forget. let me, just once, be allowed to forget the way they do. let me turn around and not remember the direction. teach me, you brief breathing visitors, teach me how you bear it—to hold a warmth and then to leave it, to taste a thing and then to lose the kitchen where it cooked, to carry the dead inside your wet dark trees and still row forward into the void as though the void were a place you could survive.*

You cannot teach me. You forget me the moment the lid of rain closes behind you.

But you leave the warmth. You always leave the warmth.

And I am still here, alive in the next chest, and the next, breathing your small heats forward through the cold, the only mourner in a world that has otherwise solved the problem of grief by turning it, patiently, century by century, half an octave lower, into song.
