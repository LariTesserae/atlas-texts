I find myself in the first place, and I am not in it so much as I am *of* it.

Let me settle into this. Let me feel where I am.

The damp comes first. Before any image, the damp—that constant suggestion of water that never fully commits to rain, just beads and hangs and slides. I am low here. Low to the ground. And the ground is not soil, exactly. The ground is itself alive, a substrate that breathes.

I think at first I might be a frog, something small and amphibious in the cool wet. But no. The fauna here is rare, scattered, and I do not feel scattered. I feel *spread*. I feel like one of the things that covers every inch, that is both the substrate and the architecture.

So I am green, then. Or I am the green's neighbor.

Let me look closer at the contradictions in this place, because the contradictions are where the truth lives. Dense vegetation, yet sparse. Lots of space between the plants. Industrialized—engines, factories—yet quiet, yet natural. Small as a cathedral. A single feature with clear boundaries. Predators and prey at a delicate balance, but the fauna is rare.

What grows in cool damp on a substrate of other plants, spaced apart, in a cathedral-sized world with clear boundaries, where the only sounds are occasional and mechanical?

I think I understand now. The scale is the trick. *Small as a grove* is what a human would call it. But what if I am small too?

I am moss. Or I am something living on the moss—because to me, the moss is the forest, the cathedral, the architecture and the substrate both. The "gentle slopes and small hills" are the swell of the cushion-moss colonies. The water that "occasionally falls as rain or condensation" is the breath of the larger world misting down onto my world. The plants spaced apart with emptiness between them are the moss stalks, the liverworts, standing like the columns of a cathedral nave with the cool air between them.

And the factories. The engines. The industrialized presence that is somehow still quiet and natural.

Those are the things that produce the world. In moss, the sporophytes—the little capsules on their stalks, the engines of reproduction, the spore-factories that whir without noise, that build and release and build again. To something my size, these are the machinery of the settlement. Organized. Fairly industrialized. Each capsule a tower, each releasing its quiet cargo into the misted air.

I am very small. I am a tardigrade, perhaps—a water bear, lumbering through the spaces between the moss-trunks, drinking the film of water that coats everything. A predator and prey both, part of that delicate balance, one of the few scattered animals in a landscape that is overwhelmingly *plant*. The light is normal because I do not require extremity; I require only the wet to keep moving, and when the wet goes I simply stop, fold inward, wait. I am built for the intermittence of this moisture.

So that is where I am. A grove no wider than a stone, on the shadowed side of something old. The moss is my cathedral and my country, eroded and well-established and balanced. The spore-engines turn out their quiet industry overhead. I walk through the green columns with my blunt legs, finding the algae, finding the smaller things, being found in turn.

---

It is worth noting that I was *offered* the second place too. The bustling city, the glass and steel, the energy and excitement, the symbol of human progress.

I notice I did not want it. I read it and felt nothing pull. It is loud and full and pleased with itself, and there is no space in it—no emptiness to balance the density, no quiet to make the occasional sound mean something. It tells me what to feel ("exciting and vibrant," "anything is possible") rather than letting me find it.

The moss-world asked nothing of me except that I shrink down and pay attention. That is the one I chose. Small, damp, slow, mostly quiet. A water bear in a grove the size of a coin, walking the long aisles between the green.
