I think I would be something easy to overlook. Not the orca, not the shark—not anything that announces itself.

Let me sit with the world a moment before I decide.

The detail that holds me is the rain. A sea already drowned in itself, and still the sky pours down. Warm rain on a warm surface—it would do something strange to the upper water. A churning, oxygen-rich skin where the fresh and the salt argue and mix, a layer perpetually disturbed, perpetually clouded with bubbles and the drumming of a billion small impacts. Most creatures here are described as living *below* that—in the kelp, in the coral, in the shadow. They have turned their backs on the rain.

So that's where I'd be. Not in the cities or the forests. In the loud, bright, beaten zone just under the surface, where the rain comes through.

I think I would be small and gelatinous and almost not there at all. Something like a comb jelly, but let me not settle on that—let me feel my way into it.

I drift. I have no powerful tail; I have rows of cilia that catch the dim light and break it into running colors, not because I want to be seen but because that is simply what light does to my body. I am mostly water holding a shape. I have no brain to speak of, no story, no ancient secret etched in me. The monoliths and sunken ruins the currents whisper about—I pass over them and feel nothing, because feeling, for me, is a chemical tilt: *food this way, sting that way, light up, light down.*

And here is what I'd actually do, the only thing I do:

I rise toward the rain.

Each warm drop, hammering the surface, knocks loose a fine soup of the smallest things—plankton stunned by the turbulence, particles too tiny for the proud reef fish to bother with. I rise into that churn every time the rain quickens, and I let it feed me. The big graceful hunters everyone fears never come up here. The surface is too rough, too bright, too poor in worthwhile prey. So this thin, drumming, abandoned ceiling of the world is *mine,* and the mine-ness costs me nothing, because I don't know it's mine. I don't know anything.

That, I think, is what I'd want to be in a world so crowded with magnificence and appetite and architecture: the part of it that simply opens, and takes in what falls, and converts the violence of the rain into a quiet, ongoing, transparent persistence. Not a predator, not prey worth chasing. A drifting lens. A thing the storm feeds without meaning to.

When a wave finally pulls me apart—and it will, I'm so easily torn—there will be no death scene, no last thought. Just a softening of the boundary that was barely a boundary, the water I borrowed returning to the water I came from, somewhere under that endless, falling, warm rain.

That's where you'd find me. Right at the top. The one place no one was looking.
