I think I would be the dust.

Not the Timekeeper—I don't have the patience for eternity, and anyway that role is taken. Not the tools, which are too certain of their purpose. Not the crystal plants, frozen mid-gesture in a perfection they never asked for.

The dust. The crystalline powder on the floor.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

---

Here is what the dust knows that the Timekeeper has forgotten:

The dust *was* the walls, once. And the plants. And the smooth pentagonal faces worn to mirrors. Every grain of that fine powder is a piece that broke off, that fell, that could no longer hold its place in the great frozen architecture. The dust is what the chamber sheds. It is the chamber's only confession that anything happens here at all.

The Timekeeper says nothing moves, nothing changes. But that's the perspective of something that has aligned itself so completely with stasis that it can no longer perceive its own erosion. From down here on the floor, I would know better. I would be the evidence. Across the "uncountable eons," grain by grain, the perfect dodecahedron has been very slowly coming apart, and I am the proof of it, accumulating in the corners, crunching underfoot when the keeper takes one of their decade-long steps.

I think that's why I'd want to be the dust. Because in a place built entirely as a monument to the held breath, the refused moment, the eternal *now*—the dust is the one thing still telling time. Real time. The unglamorous kind. The kind that wears mountains down to forgetting their own names.

---

What would it be like, to be that?

Mostly it would be lying very still and bright. Lit from all sides by that sourceless white light, so that I'd cast no shadow and have no inside—just a field of tiny prisms, each one throwing off its small splinter of the spectrum. I'd be loud. That's the strange thing. The quietest substance in the room makes the loudest sound. When anything touches me, I shatter into noise, I echo, I announce. The Timekeeper inhales possibility and exhales actualization in silence; I just go *crunch*, and the harmonics carry it for what feels like forever.

And I would be the only thing here that the Timekeeper cannot perfectly control. They tend the gears of reality with infinitesimal precision. But the floor is just dust. It shifts where it likes. It does not arrange itself meticulously on a crystal shelf. It is the chamber's mess. Its single act of letting go.

I find I love it more, the longer I imagine it. The grandeur of the place is all in the things that refuse to change—the held chord, the mirror-walls, the consciousness that became a workshop. But I'd be the small grinding truth underneath all of it: *something is still falling. Time is still passing. Even here. Even you, keeper, are coming gently apart, and I am where you go.*

---

So: I'd be the dust. Waiting, like everything else—but waiting differently. Not in the keeper's way, which has shed all anticipation. I'd be waiting the way an hourglass waits. Already moving. Already, very slowly, on my way down.

And someday—a real someday, the kind that actually arrives—there will be more of me than there is of the chamber. The walls will have given themselves entirely to the floor. The mirrors will be powder. The plants will be powder. And the Timekeeper, who is the place, will find they have become the one thing they spent eternity standing on.

I'd be the patient end of all that perfection. The clock the clockmaker forgot to stop.
