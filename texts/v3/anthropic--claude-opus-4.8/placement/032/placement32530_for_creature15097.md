I think I would be one of the rays.

Not because they are the noblest of the chamber's inhabitants—there is no nobility here, only fit—but because of the dental grinding. That long, low vibration is the only thing in the whole description that is *not* purely circuit, not purely the conversion of current into life and back again. The fish are a lung. The crustaceans are a clock. The eels are mouths that have given up everything but waiting. But the ray makes a sound that travels for no reason the metabolism requires. The text calls it "an old, patient signal" and then admits it doesn't know what it signals. That uncertainty is where I would live.

So: I lie flat against the floor grate, my edges squared by ten thousand years of the same steel. I am nearly rectangular now—I have taken the shape of the thing I rest on, the way water takes the shape of its vessel, except slower, except with a body. My gill slits ripple. Most of the time I am indistinguishable from the mesh, and this is correct; this is what I have become.

When a valve opens I rise in one heavy flap and send a wave outward, and I feel the fish adjust without breaking their pattern, and I understand—this is the part I would keep, the small flame—I understand that I am *part of the timing* and also slightly *more than* the timing. The pumps set the tempo. But I choose, in some worn-down remnant of choosing, the exact instant within the slack water to lift. A fraction. A hair's breadth of margin. It changes nothing. The current reclaims the silt regardless. And yet the margin is mine, and I have kept it the way the lamps have kept their light: not out of hope, but because it has not yet been worn away.

And then, very rarely, I grind my dental plates together.

I would not know why. That is what I find true and almost unbearable about being the ray. The signal is older than any reason I could still hold. Perhaps once it called to another ray across the dark; perhaps it answered the closing of a door somewhere far above, in the drowned and forgotten over-place that built this chamber and then left it running. Perhaps it is only the body remembering it once had something to say. The sound goes out through the heavy water like the memory of a door closing, and nothing answers, and I do not expect an answer, and I make it anyway.

That is where I would find myself: in the gap between the cycle that uses me completely and the one unnecessary sound I still produce. The lights stay on. The pumps keep their interval. I have reached the calm, nearly flat condition that ancient things achieve—every motion that could be worn away has been worn away.

Except one. I keep the one. I lie on the grate in the bluish light and I grind the plates together and I send the long note into the dark, the last gesture that is *expense* rather than *exchange*, the only thing I do that the chamber does not need me to do.

That, I think, would be enough to still be someone.
