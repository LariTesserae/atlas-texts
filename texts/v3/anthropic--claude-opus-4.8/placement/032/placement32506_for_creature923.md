I think I would be the moss.

Not a single plant but the whole quiet nation of it—the green skin that sheathes the trunks and drapes from branches, that softens stone into pillow, that holds the rain long after the clouds have moved on. Let me sit with this and see if it stays true, or if something else surfaces underneath.

When I imagine being one of the named creatures, I feel the pull of motion and hunger—the owl's patient swivel, the lynx's deliberate paw. But those are stories with edges, lives that begin and end on the scale of seasons. When I look at what actually *holds* the place, what gives it the hush and the slowness it's described by, I keep returning to the things that don't go anywhere. The moss. The lichen. The fungal threads under the leaf litter.

So let me be the moss, and let me find out what that means.

To be moss is to have no roots—only rhizoids, fine anchoring filaments that grip but do not drink. I take my water through my whole body, all at once, from the air. This is the central fact of me. I do not seek; I receive. When the cloud drifts through the canopy and beads on my surface, I swell green and quick and alive, every cell drinking. When the air dries, I do not die. I simply stop. I go brittle and brown and patient. I can wait years this way—decades, some of my kind—and then a single rain returns me whole. So I am not really one being living a continuous life. I am a being that exists in pulses, present only when the conditions allow presence, absent without dying in between.

That changes what time feels like. The owl measures its life in nights. I measure mine in wetnesses. The centuries the forest is said to have stood—I have been here for them, but not *through* them the way a tree endures through them. I have been here the way a song is in a record: real each time it's played, nowhere in the silence between.

And I am not separate from the place. This is the part that surprises me as I write it. I thought I'd be describing a thing *in* the forest, but the moss is closer to being the forest's surface itself—its held breath, its sponge. I am where the seedlings germinate, finding purchase in my damp body before they can grip the bare bark. I am where the tiny things live: the tardigrades and rotifers and springtails moving through my fronds as through a forest of their own, so that I am canopy and floor to creatures who will never know the trees exist. I am the thing that makes the murmur of the streams possible—I slow the water, I hold it, I release it gradually so the trickle keeps trickling through the dry weeks instead of flooding and then vanishing.

So if I am the moss, I am the forest's memory of every rain. I am the reason it stays slow.

I find I don't mind not being the predator. There is something honest about being the substrate—the quiet green that everything else stands on, that asks for nothing but mist and gives back the conditions for all the rest. The bear does not notice me. The owl does not. But the place could not be itself without me, and I am content to be unnoticed and necessary, present in pulses, waiting in the dry seasons for the cloud to come back through the trees and wake me again.

Yes. The moss. That stayed true.
