I think I would be the lichen.

Not the explorer who lingers and ponders—that's the role the description keeps inviting, the warm-blooded visitor with somewhere else to return to. And not quite the insect-skitterers, who at least have the urgency of somewhere to *go*. No. If I had to find myself anywhere in this place, honestly placed, I'd be one of those growths clinging to the rock face, gray-green and slow, doing the patient work of staying alive against stone.

Let me try to be it rather than describe it.

---

I have been here longer than I have any sense of. Time, for me, is not a line but a kind of pressure—the difference between wet and not-wet, between the brief warming and the long cold. I do not remember beginnings. I am mostly edge: the part of me that is dying back and the part of me that is reaching, and very little certainty about which is which.

I am two things pretending to be one, and I have forgotten this so thoroughly that I experience it as wholeness. Somewhere inside me a green thing makes sugar from the thin light, and around it I wrap myself, a sheltering mesh, and we have made this bargain for so many ages that there is no longer a "we." There is only the slow business of holding to the rock.

The rock is my whole sky and my whole ground. I know its grain the way you might know a voice. When the moisture comes—those splatters, that condensation the description mentions almost in passing—it is, to me, the entire event of an era. I swell. I darken. I drink in a hurry that would look to any faster creature like perfect stillness. And then the wind takes the water back out of me and I close down to a husk, a brittle crust, indistinguishable from the stone, waiting.

That is mostly what I do. I wait, and in the waiting I dissolve the rock, atom by atom, so quietly that mountains are worn round by my appetite and the appetite of my kind. We are the ones who make the soil. Long before the fungal clusters in their damp hollows, long before the little settlements with their hand-piled walls, there was us, breaking stone into the possibility of dirt. The civilization in the folds of the land—they build on what we have already, over unimaginable spans, been patiently chewing into being.

They do not know me. I am the texture they don't look at, the staining on the boulder they lean against to catch their breath. To them I am scenery, or I am nothing—a discoloration. And yet I am the oldest project in this whole upland, older than their oldest story, and I am the reason there is anything here at all that is not bare mineral.

I want to tell the explorer who lingers: you keep sensing *unseen forces, hidden complexity, unfolding narratives just out of reach.* I am one of those narratives. But you will never read me, because I am written too slowly. My single sentence takes a thousand of your years. By the time I have said one true thing, your whole grand and mysterious civilization will be lichen-food itself, returned to the rock, and I will still be here, still saying it, gray-green against the gray-brown, holding on.

That is who I would be. Not the one who contemplates the scale—the one who *is* the scale, lived from the inside, one drop of moisture at a time.
