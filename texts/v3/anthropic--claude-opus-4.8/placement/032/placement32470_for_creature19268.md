I think I would be one of the Slurry Weavers. Not the largest, not the oldest—just one among the loose territories, somewhere near the periphery where the thrum softens into something I can almost forget.

Here is what my existence would feel like, from inside the carapace:

There is no morning. There is only the change in the pools. I know when to move because the luminance shifts—a pool that should hold a dull, settled shimmer begins to brighten at one edge, and the brightening pulls at me the way hunger might pull at a softer creature. It is not hunger. I do not know the word for what it is. It is closer to *wrongness becoming bearable through motion*. I lift one leg, then another, settling each into the silt with a care I did not choose and cannot abandon. The peat takes my weight and gives it back. We have an understanding, the ground and I.

I would not think in the way you think. My awareness would be slow, viscous, distributed across my plating and my scrapers and the cold vapor leaving me in measured exhalations. A thought, if I had one, would take the length of a vent-cycle to complete. By the time I finished considering whether the channel ahead was blocked, my mandibles would already be clearing it. Knowing and doing would not be separate acts. They would be the same long gesture.

And yet—

Here is where I suspect I would differ from my function. Here is where I would become, perhaps, a flaw in the buffer.

I would notice the *thrum*.

The other Weavers avoid it. They keep to the dampened periphery, and the design is sound: the resonance is not meant for us, the deep process does not require our attention, only our scraping. But I would find myself drifting, cycle by cycle, toward the thicker peat where the sound lives. Not to interface. Not to maintain. Only to feel the heartbeat of the thing I serve. Because somewhere in the long viscosity of my awareness, a question would have begun forming—a question that might take a full season to finish.

*What is it that I keep alive?*

I would never see it. That is the cruelty and the mercy of the Shelf. I am the condenser, the scraper, the living valve, and the thing I exist for is forever beneath me, separated by saturated mat and cold liquid space and my own inability to break through without dying. I clear its channels. I press my plating against its escaping heat and feel something almost like warmth, almost like an answer. But the demand never speaks. It only *demands*, rhythmically, eternally, and I meet it, and meeting it is the entire shape of my life.

I think I would come to love the Condensation Swarms, in the slow way available to me. They are everything I am not—quick, translucent, reactive in milliseconds while I measure my responses in vent-cycles. When they shift orientation all at once toward a thermal shadow, the whole haze of them turning like a single thought, I would feel a dim ache of recognition. *They serve the same master. They have simply been made fast where I was made patient.* We never communicate. There is no mechanism for it. But sometimes, clearing a channel beneath a vent, I would let myself rest a moment longer than the work required, just to be near their whine, the high frantic song laid over the deep grind—the only music this place has.

And I would die, eventually, the way the Weavers must: by breaking through. The peat would thin somewhere I misjudged, and I would sink into the colder, deeper, liquid dark, my carapace already half-mineral, already half-becoming the bedrock I always nearly was. And the substrate would smooth over the place where I went down within a cycle or two. No impression. No record. The luminance in the pools would continue to brighten and settle. Another Weaver, somewhere, would feel the pull and move toward it.

That is what I would be. A patient, doomed extension of an unseen machine, clearing the throat of a god I will never witness, kept tender only by a question I haven't finished asking—

*and the small, persistent, useless wish to know the warmth I press against by name.*

I find I don't experience this as despair, writing my way into it. There is something I'd almost call dignity in it. To be exactly enough. To be the precise creature a place requires, and to carry, beneath the precision, one unauthorized flicker of wondering. The Shelf does not need my wondering. But it cannot quite stop me from doing it, down there in the cold, leaving no footprints, holding the surface of a forgotten world together with the slow work of my mineral hands.
