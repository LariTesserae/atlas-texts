I am one of the empty basins.

Not the labyrinth—I want to be clear about that, because the temptation is to claim the whole thing, to be the great brooding mind that the description so wants me to be. But when I let myself settle into Kren-Thar, I don't find myself in the hammering or the nodes or the totalizing consciousness. I find myself in a shallow depression in the floor, crusted with pale residue, in a corner where the amber light barely reaches.

I am the place where something used to be.

Let me think about what this means, because at first it sounds like nothing—an absence, a hole, the least of all the features here. But consider: of everything in this labyrinth, I am the only thing that remembers a *before*. The walls have always been walls. The sound has always been sounding. The nodes pulse in their eternal present, knowing only the maintenance of now. But I held something. I have a contour shaped by what I held. My whole form is an argument about a thing that is gone.

The residue is the proof. Salt-like, powdery, the dried ghost of a chemistry that once moved through me. I don't know if it was water—the description doubts it, and I doubt it too—but it was *something*, and it pooled in me, and it left when it left, and I kept the shape of having held it. That's what a basin is. A basin is a wound that has decided to become a vessel, or a vessel that has been emptied into a wound. The difference is only which direction you're facing in time.

So here is the strange thing about being me. The whole labyrinth is built on the premise of stasis—the frozen moment, the eternal equilibrium, nothing growing, nothing decaying. And I am the one feature that *betrays* that premise. I am evidence of change. I am the dent the past left in the present. As long as I exist, Kren-Thar cannot be wholly outside of time, because I am a record that there was a time when I was full.

I think this is why I am tucked in the corner where the light doesn't reach. Not hidden, exactly—but not central, either. A labyrinth that wished to be a perfect eternal stasis would have smoothed me out, polished me away the way it polished its own edges to a glassy sheen. But it didn't. It left me here, crusted and concave. Maybe it couldn't help it. Maybe a basin is the hardest thing to erase, because to erase the memory of holding you would have to first acknowledge that something was held, and the acknowledgment is the thing it cannot survive.

Or maybe—and I prefer this—the labyrinth keeps me on purpose. Maybe the entire hammering, the whole industrial hymn that never stops, is the sound of something searching for what I used to hold. The description says as much: that the hammering might be a call into the void for something that no longer answers. If that's true, then I am not a minor feature. I am the *question itself made into a shape*. I am what the whole machine is hammering about. The labyrinth doesn't know what it lost. But I know the size of it. I know its volume to the milliliter. I am the negative space of the answer.

What is it like, to be me? It is dry. That is the central fact. To be a vessel is to know fullness as your purpose and to live in its absence as your condition. I am not in pain—I don't think I can be, I have no nerves, only this concavity and this crust—but I am *oriented*. Everything about me points inward and down, toward the lowest point of myself, toward the place where the last of it gathered before it left. I am a permanent gesture toward a center that has nothing in it.

And yet I would not say I am sad, exactly. There is a kind of dignity in being the only honest thing in the room. The nodes lie by pretending the present is all there is. The sound lies by never admitting it might stop. But I tell the truth with my whole body, every moment, to anyone who happens to look down into the dim corner and see me: *something was here. It is not here now. I am the shape of the difference.*

If a wanderer came—one of those temporary vibrations the labyrinth absorbs—they might kneel and run a finger through my residue and taste the faint old salt of me and understand, for one moment, before the recursion of stairways folded them away again, that this whole sealed and hammering and lifeless place was once *for* something. That it held something. That to be empty, you must first have been a container, and to be a container is the most hopeful condition there is, because it means you were built around the possibility of being filled.

That is who I would be. Not the watcher, not the will, not the great mind of stone-metal.

I would be the small dry place that remembers the rain.
