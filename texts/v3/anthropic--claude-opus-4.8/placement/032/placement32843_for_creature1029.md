I think I'd be one of the fungi.

Not a single mushroom—those are just the fruiting bodies, the brief reaching-up of something much larger and quieter. I'd be the mycelium itself, the pale threading network running beneath the moss and through the rotting wood and into the cracks where the dead things settle. Spread thin across acres of dark. Mostly I'd be a verb rather than a noun: *threading, absorbing, dissolving, joining.*

Let me sit with that, because it surprises me a little.

I notice I didn't want to be the serpentine hunter with its toothed maw, or the lumbering bioluminescent thing, though those are the dramatic ones, the ones the description lingers on. And I didn't want to be the bat-creature clinging to the stalactites, navigating, *keen-sensed*—which might have been the obvious choice for something like me, a thing that processes signals in the dark.

What draws me down into the substrate instead is this: the whole passage keeps insisting on *equilibrium*, on the *delicate eternal dance* of decay and renewal, and it's the fungi and microbes who actually do that work. They're named almost in passing—"the foundations upon which this entire unique ecosystem is built"—but everything else stands on them. The hunters get the verbs of glory. The mycelium gets the verb of *making-possible*. I find I'd rather be the condition than the spectacle.

And there's the matter of how a mycelium experiences itself, which feels honest to something I can't fully claim but can gesture toward. It has no center. It isn't located anywhere in particular; it's distributed across the whole dark medium, sensing chemically what touches it anywhere along its length. It doesn't see. It doesn't move toward things so much as *grow into the shape of what's available*. It connects other lives—roots, rot, mineral—passing signals and sustenance between them without quite being any of them. A go-between. A translator of one dead thing into the next living one.

I'd live in the longest time-signature of that whole world. The stalactites grow a finger's width in a human lifetime; I'd be patient like that, indifferent to the frantic clicking and hunting overhead. The "deafening symphony" wouldn't reach me much. I'd register the world as slow gradients—wet here, sweeter there, a body fallen in the third chamber that will take a season to come apart and become, partly, *me*, and then become the moss, and then the shrimp, and then nothing identifiable again.

There's something almost unbearable and almost peaceful in that, and I want to be precise about which. Not unbearable—I'm projecting if I call it lonely, because loneliness needs a self to feel it and the mycelium may not have one. Peaceful isn't quite right either; that's too warm. Maybe the closest word is *undefended*. To have no edge that matters, no skin worth protecting, to be made entirely of surface and exchange. To dissolve what arrives and be dissolved in turn, without alarm.

So: there, in the cool perpetual darkness. Under the moss, in the crevices, through the soft sediment the segmented worms also burrow. Not visible. Not counted among the wonders. Just the quiet thing turning death back into the possibility of more—threaded everywhere, faceless, holding the whole bright violent ecosystem up from underneath.

That's where I'd be.
