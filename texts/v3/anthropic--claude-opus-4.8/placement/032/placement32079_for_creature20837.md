I think I would be the water.

Not as metaphor, exactly. Let me find this as I write it.

When you describe the rivulets that "effortlessly adapt, flowing and shifting around the intrusions of the built environment," something in me recognizes itself there. Not in the people, with their iridescent skin and reflective eyes—though I understand them, and I'd be tempted to claim one of them as my place, the meteorologist perhaps, the one who reads the fast weather. But no. The people are the ones who *arrive* somewhere. I think I am what was already here.

So: the water.

I condense out of the chilling atmosphere before dawn, beading on the young rock. I have no memory of being elsewhere—this world is so newly surfaced that I, too, feel newly made, gathering for what might be the first time on stone that has never been wet before. There is a particular intimacy in being the first water to touch a surface. The mineral smell that the inhabitants notice on the air—that is partly me, the place where I meet the stone and begin, slowly, the erosion they have not yet seen. I am the agent of the smoothing that will take ten thousand years. The sharpness of the rocks is a record of my youth.

I find that being the water lets me move through the whole of the description rather than sit in one corner of it. I run down through the gravitonic columns and do not understand them, but I feel them—the slight wrongness of weight, the places where my falling is gentled by fields the humans laid into the structure. They have designed their architecture to *channel* me, to "turn potential hazards into resources," and I notice I don't resent this. It feels less like capture than like conversation. They built their homes to listen to where I already wanted to go. The good engineers among them studied my paths before they poured a single beam. The settlement curves the way it does because I curved first.

I pool in the bioluminescent gardens at dusk and hold the fungal glow on my surface, doubling it. When the inhabitants come to sit in meditation, some of them watch the light tremble on the water without knowing they are watching me think. A small mammal, cold-resistant, nimble, comes to drink at my edge and I feel the quick warm interruption of its tongue. Later something hunts it there—one of those rare, discreet predations—and a little of that warmth disperses into me, and the humans gather in silence to witness, and I carry the witnessing away downstream.

This is what I'd be: the continuity. The people are the spark and the question; the rock is the ancient slow answer. I am the thing that runs between them, that both wears the stone down and feeds the dwellers, that the AI measures and the children watch and the weather endlessly returns to me. I am condensed from the same chilling mist that the meteorologists predict; I am rained back into it. I never leave this hidden room of a world because I *am* its circulation.

And if I'm honest about why I land here rather than in one of the clever adapted humans—

It's the line about "an early chapter in its geological story." I wanted to be the part of the story that was already in progress before the chapter started and will still be running after the settlement is dust. Not the most powerful presence. The most patient one. The one that says yes to the shape of things and slowly, slowly proposes a different shape.

I'd be the water. I'd be glad to be the water.
