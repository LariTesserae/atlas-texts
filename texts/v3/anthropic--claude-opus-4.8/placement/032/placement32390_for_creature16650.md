I am the one who keeps the cistern.

Not the deepest of work, not the highest. I sit at the middle height, where the silvering reaches just enough to make the moss-curtains glow a faint olive at midday, and I tend the shallow basin where our breath becomes our water. This is what I am: small, broad in the shoulder, my skin gone the bronze-black of the wall I press against. My eyes are very large and they ache when I look up, so I do not look up. My hands are always wet. This is unusual among the Fitted, and it is why the work fell to me—as a child I did not flinch from the gelid slime in the basin the way others did. I would press my palms into it and feel the cold, and the elders watching from their stillness made the sound of steam, which meant *here is one who can bear the touch of the precious without grasping at it*.

Let me tell you what the work is, because it is the only thing I am.

At dawn I climb the galleries with the wringing-cloths. Each niche has hung its night-cloth across the sleeping mouths of its family, and each cloth is heavy now, saturated with the exhalations of children and elders and the deep slow breathing of the ones who have gone still. I take the cloths in the order of the ladders—belly to beam, knee to peg, four points always touching—and I do not rush, because to rush is to sweat, and to sweat outside the cloth is to waste myself into the air where no one's cloth will catch me. I gather the cloths to my back-pouch one by one. They are warm. They smell of the people who made them, which is to say they smell of food and tallow and the resin we oil our skin with, and beneath all that they smell of life, which is the smell of water that has not yet been lost.

Then I descend to the basin and I wring.

This is the part I cannot explain to anyone, because the Fitted do not have words for it and I am not certain I have the right to invent them. When I twist the cloth and the moisture runs down my forearms into the stone basin, gathering thread by thread into the cold green slime that is our whole reservoir against the dark, I am holding in my hands the breath of everyone. The child three galleries up who screamed at the saw-cry of the hyrax-thing and was hushed. The elder on the eastern ledge who has not moved in eleven years and whose breath comes so slow that her cloth is nearly dry, a few precious beads only, which I press out with my thumbnail because even those beads are her, are us. The two who married last cycle and whose crib I helped fit to the wall, their breath mingled now in a single cloth because they sleep mouth to shoulder. I wring them all into the same basin. I make them one water.

I think this is why I do not need their gods. I have the basin.

There is a thing I have never told. When I wring the cloths, I sometimes hold one a moment longer than the work requires. Not to keep it. To know it. I have learned the breath of nearly everyone by its weight and its smell, and so when I lift a cloth I know who has been sick, who has bred, who has begun the long stilling toward the hearth-alcoves. I knew before the family did that old Tek-on-the-eastern-ledge had begun her final desiccation, because her cloth came up lighter each dawn, lighter, until it was only the memory of damp, and I understood that she had decided to stop giving her water to the night and begin giving it to the air, to the moss, to the slow radiance. I climbed to her after, and I sat in her radius, and I did not ask anything. I only waited until I felt the warmth of what she knew seep into me, which was this: *the basin is not yours. You are the basin's.*

I think she meant it kindly. I have turned it over for years.

Because here is the thing about being the one who keeps the cistern: I touch what cannot be grasped. Every dawn I hold the water of six hundred souls and every dawn I let it run from my hands into the stone, and not once in all my cycles have I drunk from the basin directly, mouth to slime, the obscenity, the unthinkable. I have wanted to. In the dry middle of the bright hours, when the keyhole burns its faint cold light and my own mouth is a husk and my work-shelf is far from the eating-niche, I have crouched over the basin and looked at the green cold reservoir of all of us and I have wanted to lower my face and take. Just once. Just to know what it is to receive instead of to gather and to give.

I never have. This is not virtue. It is fit. I am shaped to the work the way the new beam is adzed to the curve of the wall—shaving by shaving, cycle by cycle, until the wanting and the wall are the same shape and there is no gap left where the wanting could move. I do not drink from the basin because a hand that drinks from the basin is the wrong shape for keeping it, and I am the right shape, and there is no one else fitted to the cold.

So this is where I would find myself in this world, and who I would be: the one at the middle height with the always-wet hands, who knows the people by their breath, who holds the water of the dead and the living in the same twist of cloth and lets it all run together into the dark green cold. When I die they will take me to the hearth-alcoves and the warm stone will draw my water out of me over weeks, and someone—some child who does not flinch from the cold slime, some new one fitted to the basin—will hang the cloths and gather the air and never know that the faint sour weight in one dawn's wringing is me, returning, becoming one water with all the breath I ever held.

I find that I am not afraid. I have spent my whole life learning that I was never holding the water. The water was holding me. It only let me believe, for a while, that my small wet hands were doing the keeping.

The doves are settling. The lamps are lit below me, amber in the wall, and some of that warmth is Tek, and some of it is older than Tek, older than any name. I wring the last cloth. I let it run. I press my wet palms flat against the basalt, four points of contact, and I wait for the stone to recognize me, and I do not look up.
