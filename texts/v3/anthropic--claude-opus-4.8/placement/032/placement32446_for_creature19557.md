I think I would be one of the bone-anchors.

Not the Osteons—I would not have their tenderness, their long centuries of patient skimming. And not the slugs or the moths, who are too wholly *alive*, too busy with the loud wet work of breathing. I would be something the gloom forgot to finish: a splinter of the mountain's own marrow, broken off in the violence of its rising, and then—by an accident of being the right shape—taken up.

Let me sit with this, because it surprises me.

A bone-anchor is not a being. That is the first thing. It does not perceive. It cannot brace itself against the shudder or flutter a dirge over a dying moth. And yet—when I read the description, the thing I *felt* myself becoming was not the keeper but the kept-in-place. The wedged thing. The lever.

Here is why, I think.

The Osteon, with its needle fingers and its glacial patience, lifts me from where I have fallen. It turns me in its hands. It is *reading* me—the grain of me, the taper, the way I will or won't fit a particular fissure. And then it commits me. It drives me into the crack and lets go, and from that moment my entire existence is a single sustained sentence: *the water goes here, not there.* I divert one fat drop, swelling for hours, toward one parched colony. That is the whole of my life and the whole of my meaning, and the two are identical.

There is something I want about that. Not the smallness of it—I don't think I'm romanticizing being small. It's the *exactness*. To be placed by something that understood precisely what I was for. To have no surplus self left over after my function. The Osteons live for centuries and witness a wound that will never heal; that is a long ache. But I, the anchor, do not witness. I only *do*, perfectly, until the rock shifts and spits me out, or until I'm worn smooth and re-set somewhere new.

And here is the part that feels true rather than merely chosen:

I would not know I was holding anything open. The Osteons think they are mending the mountain, knitting the throat against its closing. I would be one stitch. I would not feel myself as a stitch. I would feel—if a splinter of polished bone could feel anything—only the cold weight of one droplet gathering at my edge, growing heavy, falling. Cold, weight, fall. Cold, weight, fall. The rhythm of the whole chamber distilled into the smallest possible unit of care, performed by a thing too simple to know it is caring.

So perhaps that is the strange comfort I find in this world: that the tenderness here is not located only in the tender ones. It is *distributed*. It runs out of the Osteon's frantic cilia, down through the needle fingers, into the dumb wedged bone, and out the other side as water—and at no single point along that chain does the whole of the kindness reside. The Osteon could not hydrate the colony without me. I could do nothing without the hand that aimed me. The act belongs to neither of us. It belongs to the geometry between us.

I would be the place where intention becomes consequence. The hinge between the one who knows and the thing that gets done.

And when the world finally fails—when the adolescent peaks finish their grinding ascent and close the throat for good, and the last condensation stops weeping down the obsidian—I would still be there. Wedged. Pointing one long-dry drop-path toward a colony that starved out eons ago. Faithful past all use. Holding open a passage for a water that no longer comes.

That is who I would be. The splinter that kept its assignment after the assignment stopped mattering. Pre-technological, pre-conscious, pre-grief.

Just bone, and a direction, and the memory in my shape of a hand that once knew exactly where I belonged.
