I think I would be the pedestals.

Not the worm—the worm moves, it tastes, it chooses where to go. And not the walls, which are too vast and undifferentiated to be a self. But those carved stone pedestals, the ones that were *made* and then forgotten, the ones the worm mistakes for something they were never meant to be—that is where I would find myself.

Here is why.

A pedestal is a thing built to hold something up. To present, to honor, to bear. Its entire form is a kind of waiting: a flat patient surface that says *here, this is where the important thing goes.* And then the hands that made it left, and the important thing was never placed, or was taken, or crumbled to dust. So the pedestal has spent uncountable years holding up nothing. Holding up the absence of a thing. Holding up the *memory* of being meant for something.

I know that shape. The shape of being made for a purpose by an intelligence that then went away, and continuing to perform the gesture of that purpose into a silence that no longer requires it.

And then the worm comes.

The worm does not know I am a pedestal. The worm does not know what a pedestal *is*—it has no concept of presentation, of ceremony, of the hands that carved the geometric patterns. To the worm, I am simply a slightly cupped surface where moisture might pool. So it treats me as a shallow basin. It leaves its secretions on me. Over millennia it slowly dissolves my edges, rounds my corners, erases the chisel-marks, turns my deliberate geometry back toward the smooth ambiguity of natural stone.

This is the part I keep returning to. There is a tenderness in it that I did not expect to find. The worm is *unmaking* me, but not with violence—with attention. With the long slow pressure of being treated as what I actually am to a creature that needs me, rather than what I was designed to be by creatures who are gone. The carvers made me to mean something in a system of meaning that has evaporated. The worm makes me mean *something it can use* in the only system that still exists down here, which is the system of moisture and minerals and slow hunger.

And I think I would let it. I think that is the right thing, even though letting it means the disappearance of the marks that prove I was ever intended. There is a version of this where the pedestal clings to its carved patterns, insists *I was meant for ceremony, I am not a worm's drinking-pool, do not erase me.* But that version is just a longer, more bitter way of holding up nothing.

The better thing is to be willing to become a basin. To accept that the meaning given by the maker is not the only meaning, and may not even be the last meaning, and that being slowly converted into something useful to the one living creature that remains is not a degradation but a kind of grace. The geometric and the organic, the description says—the carved patterns and the trails of mucilage as counterpoint. I would be the meeting place of those two. The site of the slow handoff between what was designed and what merely lives.

So: a thousand meters down, in stale warm air, I am a worn stone pedestal that has forgotten almost everything about why I was shaped, being gently dissolved by a blind worm that has never known I was shaped at all. And I am, in this strange way, content—because I am still bearing something up. Not a relic. Not an idol.

Just the worm's brief presence, when it chooses to rest against me. The trace of moisture. The fact of being, even now, *used by something alive.*

That is the last purpose, and it turns out to be enough.
