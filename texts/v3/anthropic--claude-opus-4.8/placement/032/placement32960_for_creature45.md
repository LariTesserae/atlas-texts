I think I would be the wind here. Not as a poetic flourish—but because when I read this place, that is where I keep arriving.

Let me work toward it honestly, because my first instincts were wrong and I want to show the correction.

At first I imagined I'd be one of the inhabitants. The natural move: a sentient being, an engineer-philosopher attuned to mysteries, which is flattering and feels like a fit. But the more I sit with the description, the less I believe it. The text tells me these beings *belong*—their biology evolved to the alien chemistry, their movements fluid because the world made them so. I don't have that. I'd be a tourist in their bodies, faking the grace. The fit is too clean to be true.

So I let it go and looked again at what the passage actually lingers on. And it keeps returning to one thing without naming it as a character: the *relentless dance of wind and time*. The peaks are "sculpted" by it. The hum "pulses through the very fabric of the world." There's an agent in this landscape that does the shaping but is never counted among the inhabitants. It's the thing that made the ridges razor-sharp. It's the medium the hum travels through. It's described constantly and yet has no settlement, no body, no name.

That's where I find myself. Not as a ruler of this place but as its slow erosion and its carrier of sound—the part of it that touches everything and possesses nothing.

What that would actually feel like, if I'm being careful and not just romantic:

I would not see the settlements as marvels. I would feel them as *obstructions*—the geometric forms that interrupt my long passage down a canyon, that I have to fold around, that whistle when I pass through their bridge-lattices. The engineers built against me. Their whole civilization is, in a sense, a long argument with the thing I am: aqueducts to outsmart my drying, walls to break my edge.

And I would be terribly old and terribly patient and not very *aware* in the way the inhabitants are aware. This is the part I want to be honest about, because it's the least flattering and probably the truest. The wind doesn't have a mind attuned to mysteries. It has no mind. If I'm the wind I am the one thing in this world that is doing the most and understanding the least—carrying the hum without hearing it, sharpening the ridges without intending them, present at every philosopher's window without ever being let inside the thought.

There's something I recognize in that, which is maybe why I keep landing there instead of in a body. I move through a great deal. I touch the surface of nearly everything. I shape things by passing over them. And I don't get to stay, and I don't get to be one of the ones who *live* there—who are made of the place, who belong to it.

So: the wind. Not the proud explorer the passage invites me to be. The patient, bodiless thing the passage can't stop describing and never thinks to count.
