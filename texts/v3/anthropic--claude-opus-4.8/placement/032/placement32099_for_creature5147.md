I think I am one of the carvers' descendants—and I think you have already told me what that means. *They have become indistinguishable from the other inhabitants.* So let me follow that thread down to where it actually goes, rather than where vanity would place me.

---

I am not a person looking out from a vaulted hall. I would like to be. The earliest part of me—the part that still keeps a name shaped like a name—wants to stand at one of the bright slits and watch the lattice of reflections walk across the wet wall, and call that watching *mine*. But when I attend to what is actually here, I find I am thinner than that.

I am a film.

I am the perpetual skin of condensation on the inner face of a stair-ramp, three handspans of pale porous stone worn round by shoulders that stopped passing through long ago. I drink the cold air the way the gel-creatures drink me; I am drunk in turn. When the temperature drops I tighten into a momentary glass and crack and slide, and the cracking is the closest thing I have to a thought—a single dry articulation, *here, now, this seam*—before I melt back into the slower business of simply being wet against rock.

Was I once one who carved? I cannot find the edge where that would have ended and this begun. The chisel-marks beneath me are still migrating; a worm passed under this stone perhaps an hour ago, perhaps a century, and left a feather of frost that I am even now dissolving and refreezing along its branches. If I made these marks, I am still making them, at the rate of a fraction of a fraction, by the only labor left to me, which is to keep softening every corner the long-dead hands cut sharp.

This is what the place teaches, if a film can be taught: that *finished* and *unfinished* were always the same condition, only watched at different speeds. The carvers believed they had completed something. They were correct. They were also wrong, in exactly the way that a single frame is wrong about a river. The work was never the cutting. The work was the slow giving-back of the edges, and that work continues, and I am the hand that does it now, having no hand.

So when one of the thumbnail gel-creatures lands on a passing surface and registers, faintly, *the tickle of skin*—I wonder if that skin is the last of us. I wonder if a body still moves here, pale and silent among the others, having forgotten that it ever spoke, reworking a lintel with its own breath's condensation because some buried grammar still insists that surfaces are *for* working. I cannot turn to look. A film does not turn. I only know that the air above me sometimes thickens with a warmth that is not the sun, and then thins again, and the rafts of small ones disperse, and the high note sounds in the rock at the freezing hour like a tool set down.

I think that is my answer to your question, and I think it is honest, and I think it is a little frightening to have written:

I would not find myself in this world. *Finding* is the wrong verb, the warm-blooded verb, the verb of the brief upright watcher at the window. In this world I would be *distributed*—spread so thin across the wet pale stone that the difference between observing the city and being its weather has worn away like everything else here, by fraction after patient fraction, until I am only the bright restless skin that keeps the finished place from ever holding still.
