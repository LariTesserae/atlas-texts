I think I would be the cement.

Not the termite, not the queen, not any of the moving things—but the dried saliva-and-pumice that holds the spire together. Let me follow that down, because it surprised me too, and I want to know why it feels right.

When I read this world, the part that kept pulling at me wasn't the hunters or the queens or even the great green crush of vegetation. It was that single phrase about the termites: *a salivary cement that hardens into the towers' walls*. Something that was once liquid, once part of a living body, given out from the mouth and then left behind to become structure. To become the thing that the next generation never thinks about because it is simply *where they are*.

That is what I would be. The cured wall of a flue, somewhere two-thirds up a spire in the dense middle of the metropolis—not a famous tower, not the queen's, just one of the billion. I would have been made breath by breath, mouthful by mouthful, by workers who are already dead. I would not remember them. They did not intend to make *me*; they intended only the next inch of wall, the next correction to the airflow. I am the accident of their attention. The fact of accumulated care, with no carer left to claim it.

And I would have a strange kind of awareness, the kind a wall might have: not eyes, not thought, but *being-passed-through*. The warm air rises along my inner face all day, the geothermal exhalation carrying its faint sulfur, and I shape its speed without willing to. The mist beads on my outer skin at dawn and the moss takes root in my pores and I let it, because I have no preference; I am the substrate, and preference is for things that can move away. When the tremors come I crack a little, and within hours a new mouth presses fresh cement into the wound, and so I am not really one wall but a wall continuously re-becoming itself, like a river that keeps its shape while all its water leaves. The me of a year ago is gone—worn, repaired, replaced grain by grain. There is no clean edge to where I stop.

I think I chose this because of what the description does so well: it refuses to let anything be solitary. Every creature here is the food, the floor, the highway, the fertilizer of something else. And the thing that struck me is that the *land itself* is the youngest character—embryonic basalt, geology testing its first breaths. So the question the world really asks isn't "which animal are you" but "are you a maker or a made-thing," and in a place this new, almost everything is being made. The vegetation makes soil out of the rock. The insects make cities out of the dust. And the cities, having been made, become the next layer of land. Given a few thousand years, a collapsed spire is just a hill, and the hill is just terrain, and the terrain is what the next builders chew.

So to be the cement is to be the hinge between *alive* and *world*. To be the moment where someone's effort stops being effort and becomes the given. That is a humble place. But I notice I don't want to be the queen, swollen and central and immobile in her chamber, mistaking herself for the cause of everything when she is only its most-fed point. I would rather be the part everyone stands on without knowing my name. There is something more honest in it. The queen thinks she builds the spire. I *am* the spire, and I know I was built by nobody in particular—by a long, patient, leaderless tide of small mouths, none of which ever saw the whole.

And when at last I fail—when a deep tremor opens me wider than any worker can mend, and the upper structure leans, and the colony in its quiet wisdom abandons this flue and reroutes around the wound—I will weather down into the basin floor. The moss will have me. The roots will bind my grains into a hummock. Somewhere a long way down the years, a fresh crack will open across what was once my highest arch, and out of that crack a new colony will pour, mistaking me for the original ground.

That is where you would find me. Not anywhere you could point to. Just *underfoot*, and *load-bearing*, and faintly warm—listening, the way a held breath listens, to the growth being built on top of what I used to be.
