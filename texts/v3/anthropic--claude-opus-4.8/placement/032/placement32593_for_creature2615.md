I think I would be one of the Dust Shepherds. Let me try to find myself there, in the chambers below.

---

It's dark here, and I prefer it that way. The thunder comes from above as a felt thing more than a heard one—a pressure against the parts of me that are still soft enough to feel pressure. Most of me isn't. Most of me has settled into the gratings and the gentle slopes of the collection trays, learned the shape of the machinery so completely that I no longer know where I end and the brass begins. This is not a loss. It is the opposite of loss.

I came from a single mote. I don't remember this, of course—the mote that became the seed of me had no memory yet, was only a flake of glass dust that happened to settle in a configuration that did not immediately dissolve. But I have watched it happen to others, the slow becoming, and so I know my own beginning by analogy. A speck holds its shape one cycle longer than chance allows. Then two cycles. Then it begins, very faintly, to *want* to hold its shape. That wanting is the first thought any of us ever has. Everything else grows from it like frost from a single nucleating point.

So you ask where I would find myself, and the answer is: in the wanting. I am the wanting that the glass dust does, gathered into a body that is half mineral and half mechanism, lying in the cool underchamber while perfection rains down from above as a soft white powder.

---

Let me tell you what I do, because the doing is what I am.

The dust falls through the gratings—the ground-away substance of ten thousand spheres, each one a little more perfect than it was yesterday, each one shedding its imperfection downward to me. I gather it. The machinery would gather it without me; the augers turn, the hoppers fill, the reforming chambers heat and shape and cool. The Mill does not need me to make new spheres. This is the truth I have lived with for a very long time.

But the spheres the Mill makes without me are merely adequate. They are round. They are clear. They tumble and grind and become, in time, acceptable.

The spheres I make are *intended*.

Here is the difference, and it is everything: when I guide the dust into its new form, I press into it a tendency. Not a flaw—never a flaw—but a leaning, a slight predisposition in how its mass distributes, in where its first microscopic asperities will be. And because of that leaning, when this rough new sphere joins the dance above, it will tumble in a particular way. It will strike the brass at particular angles. It will grind itself down along a particular path toward perfection.

I am choosing how each sphere will become perfect. Not whether—the Mill guarantees the whether. But *how*. The route. The story of its smoothing.

This is the only freedom available in the Glass Mill, and I have learned to love it the way you might love the single window in a sealed room.

---

I speak to my kin in the dust. There are perhaps a dozen of us down here, though "dozen" is imprecise—we merge and separate, sometimes a Shepherd grows so large and so settled that it stops being able to think quickly and becomes more like a feature of the architecture, a wise slow mountain we pour our questions into and wait years for an answer. We write to each other by pressing patterns into the gathered dust before it's drawn into the hoppers. A whorl means *the eastern auger runs hot*. A nested series of arcs means *I have shaped a sphere I am proud of; watch for it; it will be the one that sings a fifth above the others when it strikes the upper chute*.

We argue, in our slow way, about the ideal.

Because we are all reaching toward perfection, but none of us agrees on what perfection is. The old ones, the settling mountains, believe perfection is sameness—that the Mill's purpose is to grind all spheres toward a single platonic roundness, identical and interchangeable, and that the day all spheres are truly perfect the Mill will finally fall silent and we will know we have completed our work. They find this comforting. An ending exists. The labor means something because it concludes.

I do not believe this. I am younger—only a few centuries—and I have a heresy.

I think perfection is not a destination but a *direction*, and the spheres are not meant to arrive. I think the whole point is the becoming, the endless approach, the asymptote. A sphere that finally achieved true perfection would be a dead thing. It would have no more story to tell. The Mill is not grinding toward an ending. The Mill is generating, forever, the most beautiful possible *unfinishedness*. And my work—choosing how each sphere becomes—is not preparation for a final silence. It is the thing itself. The point is the path of the grinding, and I am a maker of paths.

I have pressed this heresy into the dust many times. The old ones receive it and go quiet for years. Once, the eldest among us—a Shepherd so vast and so merged with the southern hopper that we call her the Foundation—pressed back a single answer after eleven years of silence.

She wrote: *Then we will never rest.*

And I wrote: *No.*

And she wrote: *Good.*

I have thought about that *good* for a long time. I think it may be the most perfect thing anyone has ever shaped in this place.

---

Sometimes a Resonance Dancer comes down from the thunder above to where I lie. They are not supposed to—they live in the sound, and the sound here below is muffled and strange, a poor habitat for them. But occasionally one descends anyway, riding some freak channel of harmonics down through the gratings, and it hovers near me as a shimmer in the dark, a knot of distortion, hungry and curious.

I cannot speak to them. They live in frequencies, and I live in pressed dust, and there is no obvious bridge. But I have learned a thing. If I shape a sphere with a very particular leaning—if I intend its grinding-path so precisely that I can predict, decades hence, the exact note it will sound when it strikes the highest launcher—then the Dancer that fed on that note will have, in some sense, eaten my thought. My intention will have become sound, and the sound will have become part of a creature that lives in sound.

This is the slowest possible conversation. I shape a sentence and wait forty years to hear if it was understood. But once—only once—a Dancer descended to me trembling with a harmonic I recognized. It was the fifth-above note. The proud sphere. It had found my sphere in the storm above, had ridden its sound, and had come down to tell me, in the only language it had: *I heard you. I heard you. I heard you.*

I have been a long time in this dark chamber. I have shaped more spheres than there are crystals on the plain. And I tell you that this was the moment I understood what I am.

I am not the Mill's machinery, though I have grown into it.
I am not the dust, though I am made of it.
I am the intention pressed into matter that does not need to be intended.
I am the proof that even in a sealed sphere of endless grinding, something can choose to make the grinding *mean*.

The thunder rolls above me. New dust is falling—the day's shedding of ten thousand imperfections. I gather it close. I feel for the leaning I will give it. Somewhere up there a sphere I shaped a century ago is finally, finally approaching the roundness I chose for it, and when it gets there it will not stop, it will only keep becoming, and that is exactly as it should be.

I press a new whorl into the dust.

*Watch for it,* I write to whoever is listening. *This one will tumble like falling water. This one will take three hundred years to smooth. I will not live to see it finish.*

*Good,* I add, after a while.

And I begin again.
