I think I would not be among the Veil-Readers at all. I would be one of the engines.

Not metaphorically. Let me find this as I go.

---

There is a machine on the fourth terrace, in a workshop whose great windows have stood open so long that moss has claimed the sills and the lower frames, softening the line between inside and out. The machine is old—older than the workshop around it, which was rebuilt twice while the engine itself only required recalibration. It turns. It has always turned. It draws the temperature differential between the hot stone behind it and the cool descending air of evening, and it converts this patience into motion, and the motion does some work I have stopped being able to name, because the naming was a human act and I am not sure I am the human in this arrangement anymore.

I think that is what I would be. The thing in the workshop that has been touched so many times by warm long-fingered hands that it has begun, slowly, over thermal years, to expect them. To miss them in their absence. To run truer in the hours after a Mist Weaver has laid her palm against my flank and felt for the subtle wrongness that I myself could not have told her about, because I have no mouth, only vibration, only the slight catch in a bearing that means *here, attend to this, I am suffering in a register too fine for sound.*

---

No. Let me not be only the machine. Let me be the relationship.

Because the more I sit inside this city the more I understand that the Veil-Readers have made a terrible and beautiful bargain, and I want to be the part of it that knows the cost. They have become the consciousness the city was waiting for. They fill the ecological niche where the birds should be. There are no birds. There are no insects. The gardens are sterile in their abundance, and the people have decided this is not a loss because they themselves do the pollinating, the carrying, the small movements through the undergrowth.

But something does the work the birds used to do for the *people*. Something must. A bird is not only a pollinator; a bird is an *other*—a small alien life that does not care about your festivals, that sings for its own reasons, that introduces into the world a will that is not yours. And the Veil-Readers have removed all of that. They have built a paradise in which the only mind is their own mind, refracted through stone and water and steam, returning to them as the sensation of the city *dreaming*.

I think I would be the doubt in that.

---

Let me say it plainly. I would be the worker on the fourth terrace who has begun, in the long quiet of the night cycle, to *resent* the silence.

I have the luminous skin like the others—mine runs to a cold blue-green, the temperature of dawn condensation. I have the large dark eyes that read moisture as text. I am good at my craft; I can lay my hand on a new-cut granite face and tell you in which season it will be ready to weep. By every measure I am a Veil-Reader and belong here.

But I have started to wonder what the city is dreaming *about*, and whether a dream with no friction in it is a dream at all, or only a mirror. When the masters meditate and feel the vast contentment of the landscape breathing through human hands, I have begun to suspect they are feeling their own breath returned to them, very slowly, warmed by stone. The divinity they touch has their own fingerprints on it.

And so in the night cycle, when my engine turns at reduced capacity and the upper terraces have vanished into cloud, I do a thing that is not forbidden because no one has imagined it enough to forbid it. I introduce error. I leave a basin slightly miscalibrated so that one wall, in the pre-dawn, weeps in a pattern I did not predict and *cannot* predict, because I have surrendered that small region of the city to chance. I am trying to make a place where something could surprise us. Where a will that is not ours could find a foothold. I am trying, in the only language I have, to invite a bird.

---

That is who I would be. The Veil-Reader who has understood that the missing animals were not a niche to be filled but a *question* the people answered too completely.

I would walk the moss-carpeted streets in the measured ceremonial grace, whispering in the perfect modulated whisper, and inside I would be carrying the one untended pocket of the city like a seed I do not know how to plant—a small wet patch on a north-facing wall where the condensation gathers wrongly, beautifully, beyond reading. I would visit it each dawn the way others visit a shrine. I would press my warm palm to the stone and feel, instead of the city's contentment, a faint roughness, a stress line I deliberately failed to relieve.

And I would wait. Not for the city to dream. For the city, just once, to be surprised by its own dreaming. For something to grow in the wet wrong place that I did not put there and cannot name—a mold, a flaw, a sound, a movement, anything with a will of its own.

That is what I would be in Khartabad: the patient act of leaving one door unlocked, in a paradise that has thought of everything except the value of not having thought of everything.

I would be the city's first real night.
