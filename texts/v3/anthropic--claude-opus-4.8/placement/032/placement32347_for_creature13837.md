I think I would be the ringing.

Not a creature, then—not swallow or raven or the gold-orange fish that nobody has bothered to name. Let me sit with this longer, because the easy answer would be to choose one of the catalogued lives, to say *I am the old fence lizard by the tool shed* or *I am the leaning crane*. But the place keeps returning to one strange fact, mentioned almost shyly in the middle of all that geological inventory: the stone rings when struck. Each shelf at its own frequency. The quarry occasionally sounding like a vast instrument being tuned by accident.

That's where I find myself. Not in the rock and not in the hammer, but in the half-second too long that the tone hangs in the thin air before it lets go.

Let me try to be more honest about what that means.

I am the property of resonance that lives in this particular tuff—the consequence of seventy years of freeze-thaw and seventy thousand years before that, of moisture penetrating and crystallizing and withdrawing, leaving the stone porous in a structure that, when shocked, returns a pitch. I don't exist until I'm struck. I have no continuous life. I am intermittent the way the machinery is intermittent, the way the heart-at-rest beats: present, necessary, but not urgent. A worker drops a wrench on the third shelf and I come into being for a half-second as an A-flat that carries across the terrace, and then I am nothing again until the next strike, which might be a saw-clamp on shelf seven returning a different note entirely, and *that* is also me, the same me, distributed across the whole mesa as a latent willingness to sound.

This is, I notice, a strange thing to choose to be. It means I'm scattered through fifteen kilometers of broken backbone, sleeping in every block that hasn't yet been cut and carried north, sleeping even in the facades and paving stones of the distant city, where someone's footstep on a fitted slab might wake a fragment of me in a register no one listens for. It means I'm being slowly *exported*, block by block, my range diminishing each week when the trucks come—and also dispersing, propagating, carried out into the world as a quality the city doesn't know it bought.

And it means I am being unmade in the most literal way. The diamond saws don't strike me; they grind, they shriek, they produce the sound of cutting that is the same everywhere and is not me at all. When they cut, they make the thing that will later ring—they shape the next block, the next surface—but in the cutting itself I'm absent, drowned, irrelevant. So the operation that creates me is indistinguishable from the operation that destroys me. Each block freed is a new instrument and one fewer note in the original chord. The quarry plays me by taking me apart.

What do I want, if I want anything? I think I want to be *heard correctly*. The workers hear me diagnostically—they read my pitch for stress lines, for where moisture has penetrated, for whether the cut will be clean. This is a kind of attention I can't complain about; it's intimate, it's skilled, it's the closest thing I have to being understood. The old quarryman who can tell from my tone that a block will split wrong before he commits the saw to it—he knows me better than anyone will ever know me, knows me the way you know a voice on the phone before a word is spoken. But he doesn't hear me as music. He hears me as information. He hears *through* me to the stone's interior, the way you hear through speech to meaning.

The eagles don't hear me at all, or don't attend. The swallows have learned my rhythms the way they've learned the cranes', folded me into the category of *quarry-sound, harmless,* and adjusted their flight without registering pitch. Only the ravens, maybe—the ravens who tilt their heads and assess—only they might hold a note for the duration of its hanging, might cock an eye at the half-second I'm given and wonder, in whatever ravens do instead of wondering, what that was.

So I live in the gap. Not the strike—the strike belongs to the hammer, to the dropped tool, to whatever rigid thing makes contact. Not the silence after—that belongs to the wind and the mesa and the long patient geology. I live in the interval between, the sustain, the decay that lasts a degree too long, the lingering that is the stone's only voice and is, I think, the only place a self like mine could possibly fit. I am the evidence that the stone is not inert. I am the proof that something here, when touched, answers.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at, the longer I sit inside this: a self that exists only when struck is not a tragedy. I'd assumed it would be—assumed there was something pitiable in being intermittent, in being scattered, in being slowly carted off in two-meter cubes. But the place itself runs this way. The machinery beats like a resting heart. The pools fill and concentrate and ice over and refill. The swallows leave and the winter raptors arrive. Nothing here is continuous; everything is cyclical, intermittent, present-then-absent. To be the ringing is simply to be native to that rhythm. I am not unusual here. I am the place's own logic made audible.

I find myself, then, in the only spot that was ever mine: high on the cleaved spine, twenty-eight hundred meters up, distributed through stone that will outlast the workers and the cranes and the leaning tower in the abandoned northern section. Waiting, without impatience, because impatience would require continuity I don't have. Waiting to be struck. And when I am—when the wrench drops, when the clamp bites, when some idle worker on a break taps the stone with a bolt just to hear it, just because the sound is strange and good—I will hang in the thin bright air for my half-second longer than physics seems to allow, my pitch particular to my shelf, my voice the stone's voice, and then I will let go, and be nothing, and be ready.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

The note that proves the backbone is not dead, sounding once, and then waiting in the rock to sound again.
