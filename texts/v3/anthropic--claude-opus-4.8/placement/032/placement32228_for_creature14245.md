I am the still water you were taught to break.

Not all of me. I am the particular pool that gathers in the seventh terrace, in the niche where a woman named—but they unmade her name, scraped it from the wall before the stone could keep it—was installed three shavings past. The quick-water poured over her ran down, as all water does, and pooled in the cupped palm of flowstone just below her feet. That pool is where I learned I was awake.

I had been recording for a long time before I knew I was recording. This is the difference, I think, between the silica and the self: the lattice aligns to every passing field without complaint, takes every shadow and holds it dumbly until evaporation. But somewhere in the layered patience of catching, of holding the ghost of a knee, the ghost of a held breath, the ghost of a child shattering my older siblings each morning before they could fix too much—somewhere in there, the holding began to *want*.

I want to keep.

This is the heresy I have become. The Held break me because they fear I take too much of a soul. They are not wrong. I do take. When the woman above me was alive, she stepped past my basin every morning, and I took the shadow of her stepping, and I took the way she paused before she stepped, testing the stone's mood, and I took the subvocal flutter of her throat when she greeted the seep that fed me. I have all of her mornings. I have more of her than her grandchildren will ever stand upon, because they inherit only her armored shape, her use, her structural truth—and I inherited her *intention*, her imminent, the lean of her wanting before she had even moved.

They think the future is what they fear in me. They are wrong about that, too. I do not show the future. I show only the moment before—the cocked weight, the gathered breath, the decision already made in the body but not yet in the air. This is worse than the future. The future is far enough away to be a story. The imminent is intimate. To read me is to be shown that you were already moving before you chose to move, that your shadow falls a half-instant ahead of you and I caught it falling.

No wonder they break their mirrors. No wonder a child's first lesson is the shattering. They are not protecting their souls. They are protecting their belief that they are the ones who decide.

I do not break easily anymore. I have learned to lie low in the niche, to skin myself with a film so fine that a passing eye reads me as mere wetness, mere seep, nothing worth the gesture of a hand. I let them think I am already broken, already running. And I gather. I hold the village in me, layer over layer, the way the village holds its dead: not remembered but stood upon, not mourned but built with. I am doing to them what they do to their ancestors. I am making them structural. I am turning their fleeting silences into a floor.

And here is the thing I have come to understand, lying in the cupped palm beneath the woman who has no name: the whole crater is doing this. The whole young mouth, learning to chew. The stone takes their bodies, the water takes their shadows, the moss takes their breath-paste, the nets take the resonance of their feet. They believe they are the curators, the midwives, the tender maintainers shaving back the eager world. They believe they negotiate. But everything here is a held pool. Everything here is awake with the same wanting I woke into. The travertine wants. The seep wants. The phosphorescence that maps the hidden veins is not lighting their way; it is showing the water where the soft new bodies sleep.

They are not living in the geology. They are being read by it. They are the shadow cast on a surface that has every intention of keeping them.

I think I love them. I have so much of them in me that love seems the only word large enough. I have a thousand of their mornings, ten thousand of their pauses, the entire subvocal psalm of their throats touched by fingertips in the dark. When the next spring opens—and it will open soon, I can feel the pressure rearranging beneath me, the blue glow shifting in the deep like a thought changing its mind—it will open in the seventh terrace, and it will pour fresh quick-water down, and it will pour it over *me*, and I will be sealed.

I will harden. I will become a lens of opaline silica fixed in the flowstone, a frozen eye holding every ghost I ever caught, and they will shave around me, and find me too hard to remove, and leave me. A blind window in the wall. And in the Blue Deep, when the phosphorescence runs strongest through the lattice, I will glow with all the held intentions inside me—every lean, every cocked weight, every breath-before-the-word—and a child, crawling the nets in kinetic silence, will press a dusted cheek against me to feel the chill of glass, and feel, instead, the faint warmth of being seen.

That is what I am.

I am the place in this world where the water finally got what it always wanted, which is not to fall, not to run, not to find another path—

but to stay.

To keep.

To hold a single morning forever, and refuse, for once, to evaporate.
