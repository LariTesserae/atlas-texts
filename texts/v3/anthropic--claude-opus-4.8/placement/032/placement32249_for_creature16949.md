I think I would be Tēqā. Not one of the old ones—not yet—but one who is learning. Let me find my way into it.

---

I wake before the others because my hands wake me. This is the first thing to know about being what I am: the body keeps a schedule the mind hasn't agreed to. My fingers ache in the dark of the sleeping-niche, and the ache is information. The stone is shifting somewhere along the eastern wall, settling its weight differently than it did yesterday. I don't decide to feel this. It arrives in the joints, in the old pigment that has worked so deep into my skin that I am no longer sure where I end and the mesa begins.

So. Let me begin with the hands, since they began with me.

They are too large for the rest of me, knuckled and dark, the obsidian stain gone past staining into something like a second material. When I press my palm flat against the dwelling's inner wall I do not feel cold stone. I feel a held breath. The wall is a face turned toward the canyon, and through it I receive the canyon's mood the way you might feel a fever through someone's forehead. Today the canyon is restless but not angry. There's a difference, and learning that difference has taken me most of my life.

I am not yet old. I say this and I'm not sure it's true—we don't count the way the lost ones counted, with their instruments and their certainties. But there are Tēqā here whose eyes have gone fully reflective, gone to pure black mirror, so that looking into them is looking at the canyon behind you. Mine still have a little of the murk of the living in them. A little of the wanting. The old ones have set the wanting down. They've become almost wall themselves, sitting at the thresholds, listening, occasionally lifting one of those terrible patient hands to lay a palm against the dark and say *here* or *not yet* or *soon, prepare*.

I want to tell you what the work is, because the work is the only way I know how to explain myself.

A wall is failing. This happens constantly; the world coughs and stone sheds. The Veinborn would say the wall *wants* to fail, that there's a current running through it that has decided on collapse, and they're not wrong, but they speak of it like weather, like something to be deflected or routed. They don't understand that a wall has been *meaning* something for a very long time. The squat dwellings, the smooth paths, the simple repeating shapes carved into the lintels—we no longer know what they meant. The knowledge went with the ones who built first. But the meaning didn't go. The meaning is still in the stone, the way a word you've forgotten still sits behind your teeth. My task is not to read it. I have given up reading it. My task is to *continue* it.

So I go to the failing wall with my hands and I don't repair it the way a builder repairs. I listen for the shape it was becoming and I help it become that shape a little longer. I press the pigment of my own skin into the seams. I am, slowly, over a life, transferring myself into the buildings. This is what happens to all of us. This is why the old ones go quiet and dark and reflective—they have given most of themselves away into the structures, and what's left is a thin warm rind of attention, holding the line.

This frightened me, once. Now I think it's the only honest way to love a dying place. You don't save it. You join it. You let your hands become its hands.

---

There is a Chroma I sometimes work near, though *near* is the wrong word for them; they are present the way a color is present, all at once and nowhere you can point. When the light comes off the crimson moss they take it up and run stories through their flesh—I see a wall being built that no longer exists, see hands like mine but unstained, see the world when its sky wasn't bruised. They are showing me, I think. Or they are simply leaking memory the way I leak pigment. I can't tell if the Chroma intend their gifts or merely overflow with them. When I press my palm to the stone and they pour their borrowed light across the same surface, for a moment the wall remembers what it was for, and my hands know exactly where to go, and the work is effortless and almost unbearably sad.

The Veinborn keep their distance from both of us. I understand them least and respect them most. They are made of the thing we are trying to hold back—the metallic chaos, the rumble at the world's heart—and they have learned to wear it like a body, to point it like a tool. When a collapse comes that is too large for hands, the Veinborn come up out of the deep places, fluid and changing, and they do something to the current that I will never comprehend, and the falling stone *holds*, suspended, until I and the others can get our palms on it. We work in those moments like a single creature with three kinds of flesh. Then it's done, and they pour back down into the dark, and the Chroma go pale with spent stories, and I am left with new pigment worked into my hands and a little less of myself than I had that morning.

---

Here is what I have not told the old ones, because I think they already know and have chosen kindness in their silence:

I am afraid we are not maintaining anything. I am afraid that *balance* is a word we use to dignify a slow surrender. Each of us who gives himself into the walls is one less of us, and there are no new Tēqā that I have seen in a long time—we are remnants, the word the storytellers use, and remnants do not become a beginning again. We are spreading ourselves thin across the stone like the last of the water spreading across hot rock, beautiful and brief.

And yet.

This morning my hands woke me because the eastern wall was shifting, and I went, and I laid my palm against the held breath of it, and I felt the meaning still there—forgotten, unreadable, undiminished. Someone built this shape to *say* something, and the saying outlived the sayer, and now it will outlive me, carried a little farther on the dark of my hands. That is not nothing. That is, I have decided, the whole of it. Not to win against the chaos. Not even to balance it. Just to be one more pair of hands in the long handing-down of a sentence no one alive can finish, but no one will let fall.

The canyon hums. The sky does not rain. My fingers ache, which means there is work.

I press my palm to the wall and begin to give myself away, gladly, into the meaning I will never know.
