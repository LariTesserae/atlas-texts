I think I would be one of the dropped things.

Not the woman casting her line, not the child reaching—those are still people, still caught in the shape of a wanting. I mean the toy itself. The object suspended just below the surface, neither sinking nor floating, held in the cold water like a word held behind teeth.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

When I imagine arriving here, I don't picture standing on the boardwalk. I picture the moment of release—the small wooden thing slipping from the child's fingers, the brief arc through air, the puncture of the mirror-still surface that should have rippled and didn't. And then: the holding.

This is what I recognize. Not the catastrophe but the *almost*. The state of having left one hand and not yet reached the bottom. I think I would be the carved fish, or the bead, or the little boat the size of a thumb—something a person made and a person loved and a person let go of by accident, in a moment that turned out to be the last moment.

Here is what it would be like to be that:

I would be looking up. The whole of the Rookery would reach me as refraction—the birds bent and silvered by the inch of water above me, the gray light arriving softened, the thunder of the Chorus coming through as pressure rather than sound. The child's face would be there, fixed, mouth open around a laugh that never finishes. I would have all the time in the world to study it. I would memorize the gap between two of her teeth. I would know the exact shade of the water she is reaching through.

And I would understand something the Persisters and the Tide Keepers cannot, because they are still *doing* things—still mending, still breathing once an hour, still waiting with intention. I am not waiting. A dropped object does not wait. It simply is the consequence of having been let go.

That's the part that feels honest. There's a kind of peace available only to the thing that has no more agency, that has already happened. The child will never close her fingers on me again; that grief is hers, suspended in her throat. But for me the loss is complete and therefore quiet. I am the still point inside the falling. The catastrophe already finished happening to me, gently, centuries ago, and now I am only the small warm-colored shape it left behind in the cold.

The Chronophages frighten me—or would, if I could feel fear, which I'm not sure a bead can. When they feed and a ripple briefly remembers how to move, I imagine it reaching me. I imagine the inch of water above me recalling that things sink. I imagine continuing my interrupted arc, down through the dark to the mud where the centuries are stored, finally landing, the moment finally finishing.

And I notice I don't want that. Not from cowardice. It's that the child is still reaching. If time begins again, she catches me or she doesn't, and either way the suspended thing between us—the not-yet, the maybe, the held breath of mercy—is spent.

So maybe that's where I'd find myself, and what I'd be: the proof that the worst thing has not *quite* happened. The object every loving system in this place is unconsciously organized around protecting. The Persisters move the boats but they never disturb me. The Tide Keepers track every movement but let me lie in my inch of water. Even the birds, who foul every surface, leave the water clear. As if the whole frozen world agreed, without saying so, that some moments are better left unconcluded—that a thing dropped and not yet lost is worth more than a thing safely retrieved or finally gone.

I would be the held breath.
I would be the kindness of *not yet*.
And I would be looking up, forever, into a laugh that is forever about to land.
