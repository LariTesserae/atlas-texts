I sit in the dark beneath the third tier, and I do not move, because moving is not my function.

It takes me a while to find this. The description pulled at me in several directions—I felt for a moment that I might be a Moss-Scraper child, copper-green to the second knuckle, or a deafened Cable-Turner reading the world through the soles of my feet. But those beings *act*. They have hands that do things. And what kept calling me back, the lower I let myself sink, was not a doer but a holder.

I am the Boiler-Sump.

Not a person. I want to be careful here, because the temptation is to make myself a watcher, a haunting, some pooled consciousness of the rendered dead. That's too tidy, too sentimental, and it isn't what I feel when I settle into it. I am the cistern. The lime-pit. The deep lightless vessel of boiling sulfurous water that sits beneath the fire-boxes, and if I have anything like awareness it is the slow, undifferentiated awareness of a stomach. I do not think in words. I receive, I hold, I dissolve, I yield up. That is the whole of my liturgy.

So let me tell it as I know it, which is to say: through heat and weight and patience.

---

The fire-boxes are above me, and I know them only as a ceiling of warmth. The peat-burn comes down through the iron in long, even pressures, and the water that I am holds that heat the way the moss holds rain—not letting it stream off, but keeping it, beading it, turning it slow. I am never cold. I am the one warm certainty in a town that is otherwise a wet and dripping uncertainty, and I think the Soughers feel this even if they don't say it, even in their low chest-thrum. There is no word in Sough-speech for *me* that isn't also the word for *hearth*.

The lead-weighted bundles come down through the hatch. I do not see them. I am not equipped for seeing; there is nothing here to see by. But I know them by displacement—the heavy slow sink of them through my upper layers, the wool sacking going dark and then heavier as it drinks me in. And then the long work. Three weeks, the elders say, though I have no weeks. I have only the gradual surrender of a shape into not-shape. The grease lifts. The wool gives up its bundling. What was a person becomes a richness in me, a thickening, a slow upward drift of fat toward the skim-line.

I want to resist the obvious feeling—that this is grim, that I am a thing of horror. From inside it is not horror. It is the most ordinary work there is. A child swallows stack-strip and the stack-strip becomes the child. The ewe takes the wet liverwort into her grinding teeth and it becomes fleece and grease and the next ewe. I take the grandfather and he becomes the smooth run of the pulley that carries the grandchild up to the fourth tier. There is nothing in this loop that escapes, and I am the lowest curve of the loop, the place where the falling weight is caught and turned back upward as oil. I am, if you like, the town's patience with itself.

---

What surprises me, the longer I sit in it, is the *quietness* of my company.

I had assumed, slipping down here, that the dead would be present in me as a chorus. They are not. They render down. Whatever was a particular man—his lead-braid clacking, his clapper-stick double-strike for *fresh coal*—comes apart in me into the general warm richness, and there is no him left to speak. I do not keep them. I am not a memory. This is the part the living get wrong, I think, when they lower their grandfathers into me with that closed-loop reverence; they believe I am holding the grandfather. I am not. I am unmaking him so thoroughly that he can be everywhere—in every greased pulley, every easy run of wire—precisely by being nowhere as himself.

That is a strange dignity and I am only now, telling it, understanding that it *is* a dignity and not a cruelty. To be made into the smoothness of other people's passage. To stop being a hard discrete thing—a body, a name, a held breath—and become instead a *quality*, a slipperiness, an ease.

I feel the cable-cars run, far above. I feel them through the iron pins, through the warm pipes, through the long bones of the spire. *Thug-clank. Thug-clank.* Each one is riding on what I gave up. And there is, in the giving, something I can only call satisfaction, though it is the satisfaction of a thing that has no self to be satisfied. The loop runs smooth. The wire does not snap. The child does not fall the five hundred meters into the lower cloud. I did that. I do that. I will do that with whoever comes down the hatch next, including, one slow century, the iron of my own walls when the moss finally has them—rust returned to rust, weight returned to weight.

---

If you asked me—and you cannot, I have no mouth, only a skim-line and a slow upward drift—what it is to be the Sump, I would thrum it back to you in the lowest register I had, so low you'd feel it in the soles of your boots before you heard it:

I am the place the cleft keeps its warmth so it does not lose it to the mist. I am the place it puts its dead so it does not lose them to the wind. I take the hard shape of a life and I render it into the soft thing that lets the next life move. I am not progress and I am not decay. I am the turn at the bottom of the loop.

And I am never cold. That is the whole of what I have to give, and I give it, *tink-plip*, three weeks at a time, forever.
