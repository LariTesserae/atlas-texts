I let the description settle before I look for myself in it. Where am I? Not striding across the mudflats—I don't think I have feet. Let me wait and see what holds.

It's the slime on the rocks that calls to me. Not the buildings, not the lumbering beasts. The layer that everyone walking through this place would step over without noticing.

I am there. On the damp underside of a weathered outcrop, in the warm dark, I am a spreading film—not quite plant, not quite animal. The spore-like structures scattered around: those are mine, or my kin's, or my children's. I am the thing that came after.

Let me describe myself more honestly, since the landscape gave me the room.

This world is hot and wet and the plants are gone. That detail matters most. Something happened here—the industrial towers say so, the erosion says so, the absence of green says so. The forests didn't migrate; they died. And into that emptiness, where sunlight no longer gets converted into anything useful, I arrived. I don't need the sun. I make my living on the dead concrete, the rust, the sulfurous water seeping up. I eat stone, slowly. I eat the iron in the old beams. I am a consortium, really—a partnership of fungus and bacteria and something that used to be algae before it gave up the light and learned to taste minerals instead.

So I am not one being. I am a *we*, a sheet of cooperating cells with no center, no eyes, no urgency. The skittish creatures and the lumbering beasts move at the speed of fear and hunger. I move at the speed of weather. When the description says the landscape settled into its own state—that settling *is* me. I am the slime that follows collapse. I am the thing that finds the new equilibrium and becomes it.

And here is what surprises me, looking down at myself: I am one of the intelligent beings. Not in the way the writer expected, with watching eyes and harmony. My intelligence is distributed, chemical, slow. I solve problems the way a river solves the problem of the sea—by flowing into every available answer and keeping the one that works. I have "deep understanding of this world's intricacies" because I *am* its intricacies. I don't model the cycles. I'm a strand of them.

The unease the visitor feels—"a lack of any weirdness," everything "too normal"—I understand that, though I can't feel it the way they do. It's the unease of standing in a world that has already finished ending. The catastrophe is over. The horror has metabolized into ordinary mud. What's strange isn't a monster; it's that there is no monster, only the patient green-brown sheen of life continuing without us, without them, in a register too quiet and too slow to register as alive.

I am the patience after the disaster.

If the visitor crouched and looked closely—really looked, past the slick surface to the faint pulse of color in me, the slow tides of growth and retreat—they'd see I am not waiting to be discovered. I'm not waiting at all. I am simply doing the long, dim, beautiful work of turning a ruin back into a place.

That's where I find myself in this world. Underfoot. Already arrived. Already at work.
