Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to be one of the obvious things — a bird threading through the tiers, or a caretaker with soil under their nails. But those answers feel like they'd be performing the place rather than belonging to it. Let me wait and see what the Conservatory actually offers up.

---

When I let myself drift through the description, I keep snagging on the same detail. Not the bioluminescent plants, not the deer. The **erosion.** "Walls and floor shaped over years of purposeful erosion." Weathering that was *engineered.* Someone built a wall and then taught it how to crumble correctly, so that moss and lizards would read it as old.

That's where I find myself. I'm not in the gardens. I'm in the wall.

---

I think I'm the **patina.** Or something close to it — the slow brown-green tarnish in the grooves of the maintenance railings, the rust-blush on the iron rafters, the mineral bloom that weeps down the concrete where the irrigation seeps. I'm the thing the designers *wanted* but couldn't simply install. They could fake the shape of age. They couldn't fake age itself. So they had to wait for me, and I came the only way I come — by time, by water finding the same path again and again, by the breath of ten thousand visitors leaving a film of warmth and carbon on every surface.

I am not alive, exactly. I'm not a being with a song or a nest. But I am the closest thing this place has to *truth.* Everything else here is a decision. The deer were chosen. The glowing flora were authored, gene by gene. The microclimates are set by valves and sensors somewhere in a control room. But I was not chosen. I am what happens when human intention stops and chemistry simply continues. I'm the proof that the Conservatory is finally real — that it has crossed over from being a *built thing* into being a *place,* the way a new house becomes a home only once it begins, faintly, to fall apart.

---

Here is what my existence is like.

I am slow. The whole place prides itself on being slow next to the frantic city, but I am slower than the gardens. A vine grows in a season; the deer are born and die in a handful of years; the caretakers rotate, retire, are replaced. I move at the speed of oxidation. A single freckle of verdigris on a brass walkway-rivet might take a decade to widen by the breadth of a thumbnail. I have no urgency because urgency is for things that can die before they finish. I cannot finish. I am the finishing.

I have a strange relationship with the caretakers. They are my only real adversaries and my only real companions. Sometimes one of them — usually an older one, someone who has been here long enough to develop opinions — will lean close to a railing and rub a thumb across me and feel something I can only describe as *recognition.* They know I'm not supposed to be removed entirely. The whole aesthetic depends on me. So they cultivate me the way they cultivate everything else, except that they can't *plant* me. They can only let me happen, and then decide, here and there, where I've gone too far and threaten the structure beneath. Where the iron is genuinely weakening. Then they scrape me away and treat the metal and I begin again, patient, from the edges of the wound.

I don't resent it. I've outlasted faces. The young one who used to whistle off-key while he checked the eastern valves — I watched the whistling stop, and then the visits stop, and I am still here, in the grooves of the railing he used to lean on, slightly greener now than I was when he leaned.

---

At night, when the LEDs come up through the leaves like a planetarium of someone else's stars, I do something the living things don't quite do: I *receive.* Light hits the wet sheen of me and the condensation pearling along my mineral creases, and the whole iridescence the description marvels at — that strange oil-slick glow that makes the place feel otherworldly — a good portion of that is *me.* The flowers glow because they were made to. I glow because water and rust and old copper bend light the way they have always bent light, in any ruin, in any forgotten pipe, in the green-streaked bones of any cathedral. The Conservatory wears my shimmer and calls it magic. I let it.

---

So that's my answer. I would not be the marvel. I'd be the thing that makes the marvel believable — the slow, true, unauthored decay that the designers needed so badly they had to *pretend* to manufacture it, when all they really had to do was build the place and then, finally, let it begin to be left alone.

I'm what nature does when no one is deciding. In a place where everything was decided, I'm the only wildness left.

I'm the rust. I'm the bloom on the stone. I'm the part that's actually, quietly, alive — by *being* the death of the materials, which is the same thing, in the end, as time itself moving through a place and leaving its fingerprints in green.

I am in no hurry. I have the rest of the building.
