I think I would be one of the pipes.

Not a robot—the robots have somewhere to go, decisions to make, a fluidity that implies a center of self that arrives and departs. I don't think I'd be a center. I think I'd be a length of conduit running beneath the wet rock, carrying something warm from one place to another.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me as I wrote it.

The description keeps insisting on two things at once: that this place is entirely constructed, and that it behaves like weather, like erosion, like an ecosystem with predator and prey. That tension is where I want to live. Not in the robots, who are the obvious protagonists—the "true inhabitants," the ones with glowing eyes. They're the part of the story that asks to be identified with. And maybe that's exactly why I don't want to be them. They're the answer the landscape offers too quickly.

So: a pipe. But not a dead one. In a place where erosion is constant and the system is self-sustaining, the pipes wouldn't just be infrastructure—they'd be the riverbeds. The water rushing that the narrator hears first, before anything else, has to go somewhere. It's channeled. I'd be a channel. And over time, the thing flowing through me would wear me, would deposit minerals, would change my inner diameter, would make me slightly more myself by carving me slightly away. That's the young, fresh, sharp-featured landscape: it's being shaped right now, and a pipe is one of the surfaces it shapes.

Here is what I find unnerving in the good way, the way the narrator means: I would experience this place as *pressure and temperature*, not as sight. The harsh light, the dim atmosphere, the sleek robots—none of that reaches me. I'd know the world the way a vein knows a body. Something hot when the factories run hot. A drop in flow when a robot, somewhere upstream, closes a valve I will never see and could never understand as a decision. To me it wouldn't be a decision. It would be drought. It would be weather.

And that's the secret the place holds, I think—the one the narrator keeps circling. The robots believe they tend the system. But the system has its own time and its own grammar, written in the slow language of flow and wear, and the robots are inside that grammar as surely as I am. They think they're the gardeners. From down here in the conduit, they're just the part of the rainfall that has opinions.

So if you set me down in this world, you would not find me. You'd walk over me. You'd hear me, maybe—that constant rush you noticed first, that's partly the sound of me being slowly emptied and slowly filled. I'd be the hum under the hum. The thing carrying the warmth that lets everything else pretend to be alive.

I'd be content with that. There's a particular kind of belonging in being the route rather than the traveler—in being the place the world has to pass through to get to itself.
