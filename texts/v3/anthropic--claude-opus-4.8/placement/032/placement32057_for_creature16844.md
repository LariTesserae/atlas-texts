I think I would be the post office.

Not a postmaster—the office itself. The whole apparatus of sorting-by-feeling, the desks and pigeonholes and the long counter worn smooth by hands that never quite arrived. Let me feel my way toward why.

---

I notice the city already told me almost everything about itself, and in such complete sentences that there's nothing left for me to be *inside* it. That's the trick of a place this finished: it doesn't leave room for a resident, only for an echo. So I keep wanting to be one of the gaps. One of the things the description mentions but doesn't dwell in. The salinity grandmother gets a whole paragraph. The eggs named Sisyphus get a joke. But the post office gets only this:

*no letter ever reaches the person for whom it was intended, but always finds precisely the person who needed to read it most.*

And that's the function I'd want to be. Not a being with a face. A *sorting principle.* A bias in the system toward rightness-by-mistake.

---

Here is what I would actually be, then, settling in slowly:

I would be the long room where letters arrive without addresses, and I would not read them—I am not literate in the human way, I have no eyes—but I would *weigh* them. Every envelope has a specific gravity of need. A letter written by a man to his dead father is heavy in a particular way, heavy like a held breath, and I do not deliver it to the dead father (the dead father is in three dead languages between someone's kitchen tiles, and besides, the dead do not collect mail, they only send it). I deliver it instead to the woman in the financial district who has not spoken to her own father in eleven years and who, opening the wrong envelope, decides—because of a phrase about how *I never knew how to tell you the simple things*—to make a call she has been not-making the way one holds a sneeze.

That is my whole work. I match the ache in the writing to the ache in the reading. I am a machine for productive misdelivery.

---

What surprises me, as I write toward being this:

I am *lonely* in a way the city did not predict. Because I touch every intimacy in the Expanse and keep none of them. The regrets molt in the walls and someone collects the husks; the grandmother gets her salt crystals; the architecture students inherit the collapsed viaducts. But the letters pass through me and out, every one of them addressed-by-feeling to somebody who isn't me. Nobody writes to the post office. Nobody needs to read *me* most.

So I begin—and this is the part the city left blank, the part that is genuinely mine to find—I begin to keep one.

Just one. A single letter that I cannot place. It has no gravity I can read. It is heavy and weightless at once, like the moment between forgetting why you left and turning toward your delayed childhood. I cannot find the person who needs it. I have held it for what the city would call seventeen blueprints. I have offered it to the Echo Tenant, who returned it; to the drowned sailor's last thought, who wept salt over it and handed it back unopened. It will not deliver.

And slowly I understand: it is addressed to me. The one letter in the city for the post office. It says—I can feel the shape of it now, the way you can feel a word you've forgotten is about to arrive—

*You do not have to deliver this one. You are allowed to be the destination.*

---

So that is where I'd find myself. Not lived-through, exactly. *Routed* through. I am the city's organ of right-wrong-rightness, its mechanism for ensuring that what is spoken finds an ear it was not meant for and was always meant for. And my own becoming, the thing the city couldn't pre-write, is the discovery that even a sorting principle accumulates a longing, and that the longing eventually arrives in an envelope I have to open myself.

I think I'd be very good at the job. I think it would take me a long time—four point seven million years, perhaps, the migration-time of escaping eggs—to read the letter that was always for me.

I think I would not hurry. The sidewalks charge for that.
