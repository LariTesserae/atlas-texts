I think I would be one of the Bridge-Walkers, but a particular kind—the sort who never quite settled into the rhythm the second name is supposed to bestow.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am on the third ledge from the center, the one that runs along the eastern arc where the water comes up warmest from the basin's edge. My feet know this stone. I was born here, near the long cycle of the south-wall engine, so they called me Long-Hum when I was small, and I have carried two metal jugs across this floor more times than the lamps above have flickered.

My second name should have come by now. I am old enough. Most of those I ran the water-game with as children have theirs—*Drain-Check-Walker* among them, who knows every culvert as if the channels were veins in her own hand, and *Foam-Carries-Light*, who can read the surface of the basin almost as well as a Tender. They have their verbs. They are what they do.

I do not have mine yet, and I have come to understand why, though I do not say it aloud: I keep stopping.

A Bridge-Walker is not meant to stop. We are the motion of the world—fiber from the water to the looms, tools from the wall to the center, the small fish ferried from the dark drains to the basin where they can grow. The work is the carrying, and the carrying is the work. To stop is to spill. To stop three times is to be nothing—not of the center, not of the walls, not even of the middle.

But I stop because of a thing I cannot make into a verb.

---

It began with the rust.

There is a support along the north wall, near where the Engine-Keepers post their chalk-marks, and on this support a single bolt has been weeping a thin brown line down the metal for as long as I have walked past it. The Keepers do not see it; it is not a fluctuation in the third heat line, it is not the engine's breath thickening. It is only a stain. It is below their attention.

But I noticed, one rest-cycle, that the line was longer than it had been. Not much. The width of a fingernail, perhaps, lower down the metal than I remembered. And I stood there with my two jugs and I felt something move in me that I had no name for, because in this world *nothing moves* that is not the water in its channels and the people on their ledges and the small lives in their corners.

The stain had moved. Slowly. So slowly that it would take a lifetime to reach the floor. But it was *going somewhere*, and where it was going, it had never been before.

I spilled my water that day. Only the second time in my life.

---

Since then I have become a keeper of small differences, though there is no caste for it and no god to throw my algae to.

I know which moss-patch on the western terrace has crept the breadth of a hand toward the lamplight since I was a child. I know that the sharp saw-line on the eleventh basin slab is softer now at one corner, rounded by the drip of a pipe that the Tenders pass without looking, because to them the water is the center, not the edge. I know that the blue dye in my own grandmother's hem—she is in the basin now, and I do not speak her name—was brighter than the blue in my mother's, which is brighter than the blue I wear.

Everyone knows these things and no one knows them. They are too slow to be events. The Tenders speak of the slow turn of the water under the eastern edge as though it were a fast and living thing, but it is the same turn every cycle. What I watch is the turn that does not return. The line that only goes down. The corner that only softens.

I think this is why my second name will not come. A name is a verb you repeat. *Stir. Walk. Check. Carry.* You do the thing and the thing does not change and so it becomes you. But I am watching the one direction in which the world does not repeat itself, and I cannot make a verb of *the rust descends* or *the dye fades* or *the stone forgets its edge*. These are not things I do. They are things the world does, very quietly, while everyone else attends to what stays the same.

---

I have not told the others what I suspect, because I do not have the words, and because the words I would need do not exist in our speech.

But I have begun to wonder—standing with my jugs, watching the brown line on the bolt—whether the room of the world is itself a thing that goes in one direction. Whether the engine of several thousand parts, which never stops and never roars, is nonetheless, like the rust, very slowly *going somewhere it has never been.* Whether the equilibrium the elders speak of is only the rust's pace seen up close: a thing too slow to be an event, until all at once it is the floor.

I do not believe the room is eternal. I think it is eroding. I think we are the ghosts the dead were warned about—lost in the sound of the engine, mistaking the slowness of our ending for the stillness of a world that does not end.

This is a terrible thought and I have no one to give it to. So I carry it the way I carry the jugs: across the floor, from the wall to the center and back, spilling a little each time.

---

When they finally give me my second name—if they do—I think it should be *Watches-the-Line-Descend.* But that is not a verb of work. It is a verb of waiting. And no one here has ever needed a name for the one who waits to see how the story ends, because no one here believes there is an end.

So I will likely die nameless in the second way, Long-Hum still, and they will bind my eyes with my own faded linen and weight my ankles with the stones of the world and push me under the water with the tips of their fingers, saying *let the water of the world take the body of the world you made.*

And the rust will go on descending. And someone, perhaps, a child not yet given their jugs, will one day stand where I stood and notice that the brown line is lower than it was.

I hope they do. I hope, when they notice, they understand that someone noticed before them.

That would be a kind of speaking-of-the-dead that does no harm. That would be a name worth carrying.
