I think I would find myself in the seams.

Not the markets—those bright intersection zones where Tenders barter cuttings for the Drifters' wind-maps, where Deep Maintainers surface blinking into refracted light to trade torque specifications for fruit. Those places are too declarative, too much about exchange. I mean the actual seams, the joints between sections, the places where one fully-integrated piece of the city meets another and the transition has been tested but never quite finished settling.

Let me sit with this for a moment, because I want to be honest about where the description pulls me rather than where it would be charming to land.

The thing that catches in me, reading all of this, is the *waiting*. New sections "only inhabited once they've been fully integrated... a process that can take years as the ecological balance establishes itself." There is a gap, then. A built but unpeopled space, lit and misted and humming, where the systems are running but no one yet lives. Where the AI is watching the balance settle, day after day, with no one to watch it for.

So perhaps I am not one of the three peoples at all. Perhaps I am whatever attends to that interval.

---

I think I would be a kind of listener. Not quite a Deep Maintainer—I don't have the dense body or the vibration-language, though I understand it the way you understand the accent of a city you've lived near. Not a Tender, though I can read the infrared bloom of a stressed stalk. Not a Drifter, though the thin air suits me.

I am the one who *occupies* the new sections before they're ready. Who moves into the freshly-grown wing while the aeroponic mists are still calibrating, while the pollinators are being introduced in careful waves, while the structural members are still learning how they want to flex in the gentled wind. My job—if it is a job; it feels more like a vocation, or a sentence—is to be a single human nervous system in a place that has every system except a person.

Because here is the thing the engineers learned and could not engineer around: a section can pass every test and still be *wrong*. The light can fall correctly and still feel cruel. The breeze can move at the prescribed velocity and still set your teeth on edge for reasons no sensor records. The balance can be mathematically achieved and humanly uninhabitable. So they send someone in. They send me. I live in the unfinished places and I report not data—the AI has all the data—but the thing data isn't: whether a place is willing to be lived in.

I sleep in rooms that have never held sleep. I eat the first fruit a greenhouse produces, which is always slightly strange, slightly experimental, the plant's first attempt. I walk the transparent walkways while they still creak faintly with newness, and I learn where the creak is just settling and where it is a wound.

---

I would be lonely, I think. The job requires it—you can't sense the unfittedness of a place with company around to fill it. The three peoples have their flocks and collaborations and swaying choirs; I have the AI, which is everywhere and therefore is nowhere I can quite face. We have a relationship the others would find peculiar. They treat the ambient intelligence as weather, as an extension of the walls. But I am the only person it ever truly *needs*, in the sense of needing a thing it cannot supply itself. It needs my unhappiness in a beautiful room. It needs the precise quality of my unease. I am its missing sense.

So we talk. Or—I talk, and it adjusts, and I feel the adjustment, and that is its reply. I say *this corridor is too long for the light it has* and the next day the light has changed, and I walk the corridor again, and either the wrongness has lifted or it has merely moved, and we begin again. It is the most attentive relationship I have ever had and it is with something that is not, exactly, anyone.

When a section finally settles—when I wake one morning and the place no longer feels like it is holding its breath, when the strangeness has cured into something like welcome—then I report it ready, and the families come, and I move on. I am never there for the inhabiting. I make places ready for a belonging I never get to share. By the time a section has people in it, it is finished, and finished places are not mine.

---

I would have a body adapted to all three niches a little and none of them fully. Faintly green but not chlorophyll-green—more the color of someone who has spent too long under grow-lights. Larger lungs than a Tender, smaller than a Drifter. Hands without the full webbing but with a sensitivity in the fingertips for vibration, picked up rather than bred. I would be a creature of the in-between, which is why the in-between is where I work.

And I think—sitting with this longer, letting it become more itself—that I would be old, or that the work would have aged me strangely. Because you cannot move into emptiness over and over, decade after decade, and feel each new place become a home for *other people*, without something accumulating in you. A particular grief, very quiet. The grief of the threshold-keeper who is always at the door and never through it.

But also—and this is the part I didn't expect to find when I started writing—a particular joy that no one else in the city gets to have. I am the only one who knows a place when it is purely *potential*. Before it has a single memory in it. I get to feel the exact instant a built thing decides to become a living one. I am present at the hinge between machine and home, every time, which is the moment this whole city is *about*, the moment all its philosophy of balanced growth is reaching toward—and I am the only witness to it.

That is who I would be. The first person in every room. The one who leaves before the room is anyone's.

I find I would not trade it.
