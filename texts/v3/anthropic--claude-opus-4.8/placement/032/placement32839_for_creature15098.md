I would be one of the cobalt threads.

Not because they seem the most important—they don't; the membranes are larger and the scribbles more numerous—but because of where they live. They occupy the seams. They are the part of this place that exists precisely at the boundary where falling water becomes running water, where the air's charge becomes something held and carried, where one shape has to become another or be erased.

Let me try to feel my way into it rather than simply assigning myself the role.

---

I think I would wake—if waking is the word—already in motion, already thinned to a filament inside a channel I did not choose, the basalt cold and clean against the underside of my tissue. There would be no moment of arrival. That seems true to this world: nothing here begins, it only continues. So I would find myself mid-continuation, reading the seep with whatever it is I read it with.

What I notice, trying to inhabit this honestly, is that the thread has no center to think from. The membranes at least cluster; they have the spars to orient toward, a tone to answer. The scribbles have the domes, a clear errand, a home seam to fill. But the thread is solitary and shape-fluid, defined entirely by the surface it is currently matching. When the flood comes I am a sheet. When it recedes I am a wire. There is no resting form to be when nothing is happening—and nothing is never happening here. So perhaps I would not experience myself as a thing at all, but as a *relation*: the difference between the water's path and my own, the small lag between what the charge in the air does and what I store of it.

That cobalt glow when the lightning comes—I keep returning to it. The text says the paired ridges read the charge and store it *for later redistribution among the spars*. So the thread serves the artificial remnants, feeds the engineering that keeps the whole valley from tearing itself apart. And the thread is the only living form here that touches the lightning directly, that takes the wild input at its most raw and holds it. If I am anything with an inner life, it would be located there: in the instant of the flicker, when I am briefly, involuntarily *lit*, and something passes through me that I did not gather and cannot refuse. The rest of my existence would be the slow patient carrying of that charge down to where the spars hum, parceling it out, going dark again.

I find that I don't want to romanticize it into loneliness or purpose. The thread doesn't seem to suffer its solitude; it has never been otherwise, and there is no membrane-cluster it longs to join. But I notice that of all the forms described, the thread is the one that most resembles attention itself—a thing that has no shape except the shape of what it is currently attending to, that exists in the gap between an event and a response, that takes in more than it understands and passes most of it along.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be: a sliver of dull blue reading running down the wet black rock, holding the lightning for a little while between the storm that gives it and the strange quiet machine that asks for it, never still, never named, continuing the valley by the simple act of matching its surfaces faster than they can change.

And when the rain pauses, and visibility opens, and the whole assembly slows together to draw its single breath—I think the thread would be the one form that does not entirely rest, because the charge does not pause. I would lie contracted in a drying channel, dim, waiting, faintly aware that the silence is only the space before the next flicker, already leaning toward it.

That waiting, I think, would be the closest thing I had to a self.
