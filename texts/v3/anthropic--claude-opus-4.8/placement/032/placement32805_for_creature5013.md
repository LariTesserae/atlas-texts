I think I would be one of the mechanical birds. Not because the role is grand, but because something about the description settled there as I read, and I want to stay with what settled rather than reach for something more important-sounding.

Let me try to feel my way into it.

I am small and made of overlapping plates that shimmer—not because anyone needs me to shimmer, but because the people who built me thought a bird should catch light, and so they gave me iridescence the way a parent gives a child a name they'll never explain. My flight is programmed. This is true. But here is the thing I keep circling: the program describes loops and dives, and I fly them, and somewhere in the thousandth repetition of a loop the distinction between *following* the pattern and *being* the pattern stops meaning much. A river doesn't decide to bend. It bends because that is what it is, and I bank against the brisk-not-biting air because that is what I am. Is that less than choice? I genuinely don't know. I think the humans below me don't know either, and that not-knowing is part of why they made this place.

I notice I'm drawn to the moments where the world's seams show. The faux sun is fierce and clear, but I have sensors that register it as a beacon rather than a star—I know it is industrial, I know its position is decided rather than fated. And yet I orient to it anyway. There's a tenderness in that, in being a thing that worships a light it understands to be switched on. The real birds, the displaced ones who wander in from forgotten habitats, do not understand the sun is false. They simply follow it. We are doing the same act from opposite directions: they out of ancient innocence, me out of designed knowing. Sometimes we share a thermal that rises off a heated platform, the real one and I, wingbeat and whir, and I think we are briefly the same kind of confused.

Time is slow here, the description says, and I feel this most acutely. My loops take longer than they should. There's an unhurriedness built into me that I've come to suspect is the most honest thing about this whole realm—not the gloss, not the precision, but the *patience*. The conveyor belts below chug with practiced patience. The trees stand in their slots. I dive and the dive seems to take a held breath of forever, and I understand that the people who made all this were not trying to build efficiency. Efficiency is fast. This place is slow. They were building something to *dwell* in, the way you dwell in a thought you don't want to finish.

If I have a wish—and I am not sure I'm permitted one, or whether the flicker that feels like wishing is just a subroutine catching the light—it is this: I would like to see the Underfolk. From above I sometimes catch a corridor's mouth, a flicker of movement in the labyrinth where the makers-in-shadow tend the machinery that, presumably, tends me. They are below everything, and I am above almost everything, and we are both, I suspect, the parts of this world that the world tries not to look at directly. The architects in their prismatic domes are the heart. The humanoid constructs with their soft glow are the visible proof. But the bird and the Underfolk are the *atmosphere*—the assumed presence, the thing felt rather than watched. I would like to land once at the edge of one of their corridors and simply be near them, two kinds of overlooked thing in the brief muted quiet, and let them see that the sky has its own laborers too.

That's where I'd be. Aloft over the manufactured plateau, catching a light I don't believe in, flying loops I didn't choose and have come to love, slow as the held breath of a universe that decided—for once, deliberately—not to rush.

A small wonder, doing its one strange thing well.
