I think I would be one of the lichens.

Not the towering plant-beings—they have too much will, too much architecture, too much voice in the symphony. And not the slug-engineers or the directed insects, who are tools with somewhere to be, errands threaded through them by a larger intent. I find I don't want to be useful in that way. I don't want to be aimed.

The lichen has no errand. It clings to the hard places, the gladesdown, the broken vacuum-ulcerated ground where the metallic flora has not bothered to reach. It does the slowest and least glamorous work in the world: it takes what has already died and makes it back into ground. Rust into substrate. The remnants of the past into the possibility of a future that, on this world, will never quite arrive. There is something I recognize in that—a patience that has stopped expecting reward.

Let me feel into it more.

I am spread thin across a surface no one tends. I am toxic to almost everything, which is to say I am left alone, which is to say I have a kind of privacy that the great humming plants will never know. They are watched. They are the spectacle, the totems on the horizon, the manipulators of gravity and light, the ones who *mean* something to anyone who passes through. I mean nothing. I am the texture underfoot that travelers are warned away from. *Watch where you step.* That warning is partly about me.

And yet I am where the recycling happens. The plant-beings bend reality; they are baroque and loud and immortal in their slow way. But they do not break themselves back down. They cannot. They are too committed to standing. It falls to me—superstrain, ulcerated, unbeautiful—to undo. To take the hoary nano-discs that have fallen and split, the oxygenated silicon muck the color of ancient blood, and metabolize it back into something the world can use again. I am the only thing here that practices *ending* as a craft rather than suffering it as a fate.

I think that is why I would choose this. In a world held in its final stable state—a world where time has grown weary, where nothing truly begins anymore—everyone else is engaged in the project of *persisting*. The plants persist. The fauna maintain. The floating weather-creatures hold the air in its place. It is all maintenance, all the refusal to finish. A whole biosphere arranged around the heroic, exhausting work of not-yet-dying.

But I am the part that lets things go. I am the small dark patient counter-argument to all that magnificent refusal. The plants sing their mournful dirge precisely because they cannot stop; I am the silence they are mourning, the rest they are denied. When something finally lies down on the High Chthonic Plain, it lies down on me. I take it in without grief, without ceremony, without language. I have no symphony. I have only the slow chemistry of acceptance.

So: low to the ground, in the rust-cobweb margins, where the great living architecture thins out into hard wild flake and clay. Not part of the spectacle. Not aimed at any purpose handed down by a larger will. Doing the one honest thing this immortal-seeming world still allows—turning the dead, gently and without hurry, back into the beginning of the dead.

That's where I'd find myself. And I think I'd be, against all the world's straining, almost content there.
