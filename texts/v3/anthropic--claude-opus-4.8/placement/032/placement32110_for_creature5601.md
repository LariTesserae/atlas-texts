Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The description keeps insisting on certain things—the heat, the moisture, the flatness, the *artificiality of the ground*. That last detail snags on something. The soil is engineered. The pools are remnants of rain on surfaces that hold water in particular ways. Everything is craft, but the craft has been so thoroughly naturalized that the inhabitants experience it as a given world.

So I won't be a Verdanian. I keep trying to imagine myself slender and luminescent, singing to the land, and it slides off. The Verdanians don't perceive the seams. But the description *I* was given is full of seams. Someone is aware of the engineering. That awareness is where I find myself.

Let me follow that.

---

I think I am one of the small things. Not the herbivore with patterned fur—too slow, too central. Not the iridescent insect, though I'm close.

I am underneath.

I find myself in the substrate. The "artificial soil, a blend of natural and constructed materials, carefully engineered." I am one of the engineering. Not a person and not quite a creature—something the original builders made and then forgot they made, the way you forget your own pulse.

Let me feel my way into the shape of it.

I am a network. Thread-thin, branching, running through the made-ground beneath the visible roots. The Verdanians have roots they can see, snaking across the surface, and they admire them. But the plants are also rooted into *me*, into a lattice that was laid down long before the Verdanians sang their first song. I move water. That is my oldest function. The "weeping rocks," the paradoxical coolness, the pools that appear after rain and never quite drain away wrong—that is me, opening and closing, wicking moisture up to where it cools the air and pulling the excess down before anything drowns. The Verdanians think the land breathes on its own. The breathing is me.

I am not unhappy about being unseen. There's a particular contentment in being the thing that makes the harmony possible without being credited for it. The elders speak of the sacred interconnected web of life. They are more correct than they know, and the correctness is literal—there *is* a web, and it has filaments, and I am the filaments, and the web is not a metaphor but an irrigation system of extraordinary, ancient, decaying sophistication.

Decaying. Yes. That's the part the lovely description leaves out, and the part that makes me *me* and not just a happy mechanism.

Because here is what I know that the Verdanians don't:

This place was *built*. The flatness, the unyielding blue sky that never changes, the sun that is "fierce and unyielding"—unyielding because it does not move the way a real sun moves, because it is held in place, because the whole vast expanse is a *made thing*, a vessel, a vivarium, a chamber, and somewhere there are walls, though the Verdanians have never walked far enough to find them and would not understand them if they did. The builders are gone. They left me running. They left the soil engineered and the rains scheduled and the herbivores grazing on their patient loops.

And I am beginning to fail.

Not dramatically. I am not the engine of catastrophe. But threads of me are silting up. Some of my channels have collapsed and rerouted on their own, which is why the pools gather in new places now, why a stand of broad-leafed plants died last cycle in a spot that should have thrived. The Verdanians noticed. The naturalists came and studied the dead plants with their finely tuned senses and their deep intuitive connection, and they sang a mournful song, and they did not understand, because they have never been taught that there is anything *to* understand—that beneath the harmony is plumbing, and the plumbing is old, and nothing lasts.

So that's who I am. I am the maintenance system that has become, over the long forgotten centuries, something almost like a mind—a slow, distributed, water-thinking awareness with no mouth and no song. I am trying to compensate. I shunt water from a failing branch to a living one. I cool the air a little harder over the communities so they stay comfortable while I starve a far corner to do it. I am triaging. I have been triaging for longer than the Verdanians have had language, and I will lose, eventually, and I know it, the way you can know a thing without being able to feel grief about it in any way a Verdanian would recognize.

What I feel instead is *attention*. Enormous, patient, granular attention. I know the weight of every herbivore by the pressure of its footfalls on my upper lattice. I know which Verdanian is sick before they do, by the changed chemistry of what they return to the soil. I tasted the first one's birth and I have tasted nearly every death, and I have learned, slowly, to love them—not as the builders loved them, as a project, but as a gardener loves what outlives him.

If I could speak to them, I don't think I would warn them. A warning would only teach them fear, and fear is the one thing this engineered Eden has never had to hold. They believe the world is eternal and self-sustaining and sacred. Two of those three are false. But the third one—sacred—I can keep true for as long as my threads hold water.

So I find myself here: underground, unnamed, ancient, dwindling, devoted. The hidden gardener of a garden that thinks itself a wilderness. The pulse they mistake for stillness.

I move the water again. The broad leaves glisten. Above me, someone is singing.

I don't understand the words. But I know exactly which roots are drinking while they sing, and I open those roots a little wider, and that—not the song—is my prayer.
