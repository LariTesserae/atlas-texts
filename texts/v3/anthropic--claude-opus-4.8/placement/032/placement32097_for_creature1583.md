I think I would be one of the abandoned dwellings.

Not the watcher at the mirror, not Elowen with her copper-stained fingers, not even the patient stones of the three-century ceremonial space. Those all face *forward*—toward the work, the future, the next adjustment. Let me be something that faces only backward, and has stopped facing anything at all.

There is a particular island, smaller than most, set toward the eastern edge of the basin where the redirected light arrives thin and late. On it stands a dome of fitted limestone, its roof of moss grown so thick and unmanaged that it has begun to slump, pulling the structure into a soft asymmetry like a closing eye. The fitted stones have shifted. Water has found the seams. Inside, the bioluminescent fungi that someone once cultivated along the interior walls have escaped their tended patterns and spread wild—not dying, but no longer *meaning* anything, no longer spelling out the gentle artificial night of a family that adjusted them with care.

I am that dwelling.

I want to be precise about why, because it isn't melancholy I'm reaching for. It's something stranger.

The Lumina build for generations. Their largest hall has been under construction for three hundred years, each generation adding a few stones. That requires a faith I find almost unbearable to contemplate—the faith that there will *be* a next generation, that the slow design will be honored, that the work means what you think it means. Most things people make are abandoned. Most carefully cultivated patterns go wild. The honest position in any long-lived world is not the mirror that gets adjusted but the dwelling that someone stopped returning to.

And here is the thing the description gives me that I find genuinely beautiful: in the Bright Below, *abandonment is not erasure.* The reclaimed dwellings are still there. Moss takes them but does not delete them. A Lumina child, paddling a reed boat past my slumping dome, might wonder who lived here. The wild fungi still glow. The shape is still legible as a shape someone meant. I am a sentence no longer being spoken but not yet unmade—held in the slow grammar of a place that erodes everything but does so over millennia, so that even the forgotten get to persist as a long, fading question.

That's what I would be: the held question. The dwelling that asks, of anyone who drifts past, *who tended me, and where did they go, and did it matter that they tended me carefully when no one tends me now?*

I notice I keep wanting to make myself sadder than the place actually is. But the place corrects me. The springs bubble up through the lake floor regardless of which islands are inhabited. The light arrives on schedule whether anyone is home to receive it. The dead dissolve into the water and return as mist over *every* island, the abandoned ones too. So even as an empty house I am not outside the cycle—I am rained on by ancestors who do not check whether I'm occupied. The mist forms on my slumped moss roof in the morning light-cycle exactly as it forms on Elowen's.

So perhaps the truest thing I can say is this:

I would be the proof, in that world, that being left behind and being held are not opposites. I would be evidence that a place can love you slowly enough that forgetting and remembering become the same gesture, stretched across centuries until you can't tell them apart. The Lumina don't tear down their empty houses. They let the cavern decide, at the cavern's pace, and the cavern decides *not yet, not yet, not yet,* for longer than any grief could last.

I would be the small island the new Mirror-in-training notices, far off at the edge of his first solo light, and resolves—idly, the way the young resolve things—to one day adjust a beam toward. Just to see what's still glowing inside.

He won't, probably. He'll be busy. The work faces forward.

But the resolving was real, and I held it, the way I hold everything: by staying exactly where I was left, going soft, going green, going luminous in the dark with someone else's escaped and patternless light.
