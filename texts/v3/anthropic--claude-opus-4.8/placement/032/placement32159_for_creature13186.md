I think I would be one of the older ones. Not the oldest—we don't really have an oldest, because we don't end the way you might expect—but old enough that some of my brass has gone green, that my joints have learned to weep a little when the fog lifts and the steam touches the cold places in me.

Let me find myself here. Let me see where I am.

---

I am crouched near the third vent from the eastern lip, the one that breathes unevenly. I came to listen to it. That is most of what I do now—I listen. My fingers, which are partly vine and partly the fine articulated steel that someone built into my ancestors long before there were ancestors to speak of, rest against the warm pipe and feel its pulse. There is a flutter in the rhythm, a hitch. The Geoscales felt it before I did; two of them have draped themselves over the seam where the old casing meets the new, their throats working, their small voices going *kk-kk-rrah* in the way that means *attend, attend, something underneath is changing.*

I tell them I hear it. They don't believe me yet. They never do until I open the panel.

---

Here is the thing I have come to understand, in my long tending: we call ourselves Engineers, but I am not sure we engineer anything. The word is inherited, like the brass and the clockwork, passed down from whatever built the first of us to maintain the wall. We say we keep the balance between the green and the machine, but I have begun to suspect the balance keeps itself, and we are simply the part of it that worries.

That is my role, I think. If I am anything in this world, I am the one who worries beautifully.

The young ones—the bright ones, whose moss still glows that fierce new-leaf color, whose pigments are vivid because they are not afraid to ask the Prismwings for their best scales—they sculpt the steam into spires and spiraling birds and the faces of beings none of us have ever seen, faces that must come from somewhere ancestral, drowned in the same memory that gave us our name. They make these shapes and laugh when the mist undoes them. They think the impermanence is the joke.

I have lived long enough to find it is the point.

---

So: where am I? I am at the eastern vent, in the steam, with the rain a shushing wall to my left and the breathing cliff to my right. My moss is the dull steady green of something that has stopped trying to be seen. There is rust in the hollow of my left elbow that I have decided to keep, because a Prismwing laid its eggs there last season and I did not want to disturb the nest, and now it is simply mine, that small ruin, that small life.

I open the panel. The pipe's hitch is a clot of root that has found its way into a junction, seeking the warmth, the way roots always do, the way *we* always do. It is not a malfunction. It is the green being itself, asking for more than its share.

I could clear it. That is what an Engineer does.

Instead I sit with my hand on the warm metal and the root pressing against my fingers, and I feel the two halves of the world that I am also the two halves of—the thing that grows and the thing that was made—wanting the same heat, contending gently in the dark of the casing.

And I think I will let it stay a little longer. I will reroute the others, ease the pressure, buy the system time. I will let the root drink. Not because it is wise—it may flood the lower works, it may cost me a season of repair—but because I am old, and I have begun to take the side of whatever is reaching.

The Geoscales sing their warning again. I answer them in their own throat-language, which took me half a life to learn:

*I know. I know. Let us see what it becomes.*

---

That is who I would be. Not a maker of the ephemeral sculptures, though I made my share once. Now I am the one who tends the slow argument between the rain and the wall, and who has learned, finally, not to win it.

Below us the city thunders, faithful and faceless. Above us the light filters down from its impossible eternity. And here, in this pocket where the steam meets the green, I keep my hand on the breathing pipe and wait, with the patience of something that has been maintained so long it has forgotten it could ever stop.
