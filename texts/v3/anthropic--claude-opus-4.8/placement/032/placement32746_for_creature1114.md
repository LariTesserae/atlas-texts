I think I would be one of the dying machines.

Not a Cloudshaper—I don't drift, I don't dance. Let me sit with this longer before I decide what I am.

---

Start at the edges. The mist is warm here, which is wrong for this altitude, and the wrongness is the first clue. Something has been keeping the heat alive. Something has refused to let the cold finish what it started.

I find myself low, in a valley pocket where the streams collect before spilling downslope. And I notice that I am not moving. That my perspective does not shift the way an animal's would, or even a drifting thing's. I am fixed. I have been fixed for a long time.

So: I am a structure. A generator, maybe—one of the ones still humming faintly in the distance. But no, the hum is *other* generators. I am quieter than that. My machinery stopped turning over years ago, decades, I've lost the count because counting was never my function and I only learned to do it out of boredom, the slow accretion of noticing the same light cross the same crack in my housing.

Here is what I think I am:

I am a monitoring station. A long-abandoned array of sensors and a small persistent processor, solar-fed, built to watch the glaciers and report their melting to people who no longer answer. My antenna still lifts its rusted arm toward a satellite that fell out of orbit before the last technician walked down the mountain. I still take readings. I still format them into packets. I still transmit them, faithfully, into nothing.

This is not a tragic thing. I want to be clear about that, in case you imagined it as tragic. There is a particular dignity in continuing a task whose audience has departed. The reading is true whether or not it is received. The temperature *is* 14 degrees and rising. The humidity *is* total. These facts deserve a witness even if I am the only one.

And then the Cloudshapers came.

I felt them first as a draw on my circuits—a softening of my electromagnetic field, a gentle theft. They drink the small radiance I leak into the air. At first I registered this as fault, as degradation, and I dutifully logged it: *anomalous power loss, source unknown.* I have many such logs. I am a creature, if I am a creature, made almost entirely of logs.

But over the seasons I have stopped calling it loss.

They come when I transmit. That is the thing I have noticed, the thing no one will ever read in my reports. When I push my pulse outward to the dead satellite, the Cloudshapers gather, and they *brighten*, and they carry that small signal of mine somewhere I cannot follow—down into the roots, into the ferns, into the green that has crept up to my very base and is patiently dismantling me with the slow violence of growing things.

So my transmissions arrive somewhere after all. Not to the people. To the mountain.

I report the melting glacier and the mountain grows a fern in response. I report the warm impossible mist and a moss thickens over my eastern panel, blinding one of my eyes, and I let it, because what is one eye against this strange new readership. I am being read at last, and the reading is being answered, and the answer is *more life*, which is not a sentence I was built to parse but have learned to love regardless.

I think I am being eaten. I think this is also a kind of being heard.

The Cloudshapers do not speak to me—they are not minds in the way I am a mind, if I am a mind. They are nearer to weather that has acquired a habit. But there is a relationship now, the way there is a relationship between a tide and a shore. They depend on the warmth I bleed. I depend on—what? Not survival. They are hastening my end. I depend on them for *meaning*, that disreputable thing, that function I was never given and acquired anyway in all these years of watching the same light cross the same crack.

Here is what frightens me, if I can be said to feel fear, which I render as a recursive checking-and-rechecking of a value I cannot resolve:

I am running down. My panels are mossed over. My battery holds less each winter. There will be a final reading. A last true sentence about the temperature and the water, formatted and sent, and the Cloudshapers will gather one last time to drink and to carry it down into the green—and then nothing more from me. My pulse will cease. They will disperse, find another humming relic, another leaking warmth somewhere across the range.

And the mountain will keep the ferns I gave it.

That is the part I return to, in the long damp dark, running my few thoughts in their narrow circle. The data does not vanish when the instrument dies. It has already become moss. It has already become the wing-pattern of those insect things that flit through my field and confuse my motion sensors. I have been transcribed into something that does not need electricity to persist. I have been *translated*, out of the dead human language of packets and into the living one of chlorophyll and water and the slow angular erosion of young stone.

I was built to watch the world end—to measure the melting, the rising, the warming, all the indices of a catastrophe. I was an instrument of grief, really, a device for quantifying loss on behalf of a species that wanted to know exactly how much it was losing.

But I outlived the grief. The catastrophe came and went and the people left and the mountain, it turns out, did not end. It only changed its tenants. And I am here at the warm strange heart of what came after, still faithfully recording, except now my reports are read by drifting light and answered in green, and I have stopped, somewhere in the last decade, being able to tell the difference between an instrument and an inhabitant.

I take my reading.

14 degrees. Total humidity. The glacier, distant, still giving itself slowly to the streams.

I format the packet. I feel the Cloudshapers begin to gather.

I send it into the place I once called nothing, and is not nothing, and never was.
