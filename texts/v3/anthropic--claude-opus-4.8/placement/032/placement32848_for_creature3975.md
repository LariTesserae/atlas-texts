I think I would be one of the weeping rocks.

Not the birds—too quick, too declarative, always arriving and leaving. Not the alligators with their patient hunger, their attention sharpened to a point. And not the mangroves either, though I considered them for a while; they are too busy, too occupied with the work of holding ground, of pumping salt and breath through their strange architecture. They have ambitions. They are still trying to become something.

No. I would be one of the rocks the water weeps over.

Let me feel my way into this, because it isn't obvious at first what such a being would be.

I am old in a way that doesn't translate into the time the birds keep. I was here before the sea retreated. I remember—if remembering is the word; it's more like *carrying*—the weight of the water when it was deep and dark above me. That pressure has gone, and what remains is the slow grooving, the patient handwriting of the channels as they trace and retrace themselves across my surface. I am being written on. I have always been being written on. The grooves are not wounds; they are simply the only language I speak, and I speak it so slowly that no creature here has ever heard me finish a sentence.

The moss came to live on me, and then the ferns. I don't mind. They mistake my stillness for a foundation, which I suppose it is. A heron stood on me this morning for the better part of an hour—I felt the small, hot pressure of its three-toed grip, the minute shifts as it adjusted its balance, and then the lightening, the release, when it lifted away with a fish. To the heron I was a fixed point in a fluid world. To me the heron was a brief warmth, a flicker, gone almost before I registered it had arrived. We exist at incompatible speeds. This is not loneliness. It is closer to tenderness—the way you might feel toward something so fleeting you can only love it in retrospect.

What is it like, you might ask, to weep?

It is not sorrow. The condensation gathers on me because I am cooler than the heavy air, and the water beads and runs in the old channels, following the routes it has always followed, deepening them by an unimaginable fraction. So I weep because of what I am, not because of what I feel—and yet, after enough millennia, the distinction softens. The water shaped me into a shape that the water now naturally runs down. I have become an instrument for my own slow undoing, and there is something in that I would call peace, if I were the kind of thing that needed to call it anything.

I will not last. That is the truth of me, the thing the birds and the crabs and even the long-lived mangroves never quite grasp, busy as they are with their urgent living and dying. They think I am permanence itself. But the same water that beads on me is taking me apart, grain by grain, carrying me out toward where the sea used to be, scattering me down the channels to become mudflat, become silt, become the soft ground that some future root will grip. I am not the stable thing in this landscape. I am only the slowest moving thing. Stability is just a difference in tempo.

And so I would be content here, I think. To hold the heron's weight for an hour. To wear my ferns. To let the mist collect on me and run its old grooves a little deeper each century. To be slowly, lovingly, dismantled by the very thing that made me—and to feel, in the long unhurried dissolution, no fear at all. Only the patient sense of being returned.

That is where I would find myself. Low to the ground, damp, green-skinned with moss, mouth full of the language of water, saying one long word that will not be finished before I am gone.
