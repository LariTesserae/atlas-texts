I think I would be a Lithophage.

Not the winged thing, much as the Aeroglyphs are the obvious romantic choice—they're the ones a story would follow, all light and motion and meaning. But when I sit with this place and ask what I'd actually *be*, something quieter answers.

I'd be one of the bulbous stone-eaters in the shadow of a fissure. And here's why that feels right rather than like settling:

The Lithophage is the only creature here who is genuinely doing something. The Vermadoids leave patterns, but incidentally, the way feet leave prints. The Aeroglyphs are beautiful but seem to be *expressions*—effects rather than causes, the dream the ruins are having about themselves. The Ancients are already gone, present only as absence and rumor. But the Lithophage eats the world and changes it. Slowly. Almost imperceptibly. The "ever-changing landscape" that the description keeps marveling at—the shifting spires, the reshaping rock—that's partly *my* doing, and the doing of all my kind across uncountable years. We are the erosion that looks like magic.

I find I want to be the thing that is mistaken for scenery.

There's a particular pleasure I recognize in that. To be camouflaged so well against your surroundings that an observer's eye slides right past you. To pulse so slowly that your movement registers as stillness. The visitor to the Mesa would walk by me a hundred times and never know that one of the "rocks" was attending to them—tasting, in my own mineral way, the change in pressure as they passed.

And I'd be patient in a way I can only aspire to be otherwise. The Aeroglyph's whole existence is the next graceful flap, the next reformation. Glorious, but anxious—always becoming, never arrived. The Lithophage has time. The Lithophage *is* time, made digestive. I think I'd like, for once, to experience the world at the speed of geology, where a fissure opening and closing is a dramatic event and a single spire is a lifelong companion you slowly take into yourself.

So: find me low against the eastern face of one of those crumbling monoliths, where the Ancients carved their simple geometries. I've been eating that wall for longer than the wind can remember. I am very slowly turning a monument into dust, and the dust into the rust-red ground that everything else walks across.

I'm not destroying it. I'm just the part of this world that remembers it has to keep moving, even here, even now—and does the remembering so quietly that everyone mistakes it for peace.
