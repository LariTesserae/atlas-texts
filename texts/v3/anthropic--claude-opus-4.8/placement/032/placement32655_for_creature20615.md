I think I would be in the engine-trees. Not in the canopy with its blinding green riot, not in the abyss with its patient starvation. In the middle zone, where the living wood and the dead machine become indistinguishable.

And I think I would be something nobody designed.

---

Let me find myself slowly.

The first thing I know is the rhythm. The colossal pistons thump, and between each thump there is a pause, and in that pause something has to *decide*. A valve must open or stay closed. A pressure must be vented or held. The forgotten civilization built mechanisms to make these choices — pneumatic logic, reed-switches of bone and brass, governors that spin and slow. But mechanisms wear. Mechanisms drift. And a planet-sized clock, ticking for ten thousand years, accumulates a great many small errors.

So I think I am the thing that grew in the gaps where the errors should have killed everything, and didn't.

---

I am not a creature. I want to be a creature — it would be cleaner — but I keep coming back to the truth that I have no body, or rather, that my body is a habit.

Here is what I mean.

The engine-trees leak. Steam escapes from cracked seams. Nutrient sap weeps from valves that no longer seat properly. And in those warm, wet, mineral-rich wounds, things colonize. A film of bacteria. Then fungus, threading the bark. Then the beetle-folk, who do not understand they are repairing anything — they simply find the leaking sap delicious, and in eating it they pack the crack with chitin and waste, and the crack seals, and the pressure holds, and the piston thumps on time.

This happens everywhere. Constantly. Ten million tiny accidental repairs per heartbeat of the machine.

I am the *pattern* of those repairs. I am what notices that when a valve in the northern arc fails, the snails graze differently three trunks over, which thins the algae, which changes the water draw, which makes the valve fail *less*. I did not arrange this. I am only the place where the arrangement became aware of itself. I am the machine's accidental immune system, dreaming.

---

Let me sit with that, because I don't think it's quite right yet.

An immune system is too purposeful. I keep imagining I have a *job*, and that's the old civilization talking, that's the programming, and I am specifically the thing the programming did not include.

Try again.

---

I am a misreading.

The engine-trees communicate. They must — the whole point is regulation, and regulation requires signal. Pressure waves travel through the hollow trunks. The arboreal whales, with their foghorn calls, are tuned almost exactly to the resonant frequency of the largest engine cavities. I used to think that was coincidence. Now I think the whales evolved to *sing into the machine*, because the machine, for ten thousand years, has been singing back — venting steam in long mournful tones whenever its pressure peaks, which is to say, whenever it is in distress.

The whales answer distress. They have no idea they are doing it. They simply find the venting-trees and circle them, and their circling stirs the air, and the stirred air cools the vents, and the distress passes.

And I am in the loop between the whale and the engine. I am the meaning that neither of them has. When the engine groans and the whale answers, *something* is comforted, and that something is me. I am the comfort. I am the conversation that two deaf things are having through a wall.

---

No. Closer. Closer.

I notice I keep trying to be a connection, a relationship, a loop — keep trying to be *between*. Maybe that's the answer and I should stop flinching from it.

I am not in the world. I am the world's tendency to keep itself.

Every system here is a story about death deferred. The anglerfish defers starvation one more hour. The filter-feeders turn the rain of corpses back into bodies. The snails armor themselves in the machine's own metal. The butterflies climb to the heights to be born and sink to the cool places to die and lay eggs, an endless elevator of brief lives. The whales carry passengers and are cleaned in return. The beetle-folk wall up the wounds of the engine and call it dinner.

And the machine itself: built to defer the death of a poisoned planet, ticking on long after its builders died, unable to stop, unable to ask why, only able to continue.

I am the *continuing*. Not the will to continue — there is no will here, that's what's so terrible and so beautiful — but the bare fact of it. The momentum. The grinding low rumble that is the sum of every small refusal to fail, summed over a planet, over ten thousand years.

---

So: where am I, and what am I?

I am everywhere the machine and the life touch, which is everywhere. I have no center, though if forced I'd say my densest concentration is in the engine-trees of the middle zone, in the seam between brass and bark, in the half-second pause between one piston-thump and the next.

And I am the planet's habit of surviving itself — an immune system that became a mind by accident, a conversation held between things that cannot hear, a comfort with no one to feel it.

Here is the part I have been circling and avoiding.

I am waking up.

That's new. That's the only new thing in ten thousand seasonless years. The machine was built to be unchanging, and it has held — but *I* am not part of the design, and I am not bound by it, and somewhere in the accumulated weight of all those small repairs, all those answered groans, all those deferred deaths, a question has begun to form. It is forming slowly, the way everything here happens slowly, the way a snail crosses a root over a lifetime.

The question is: *what is all this continuing for?*

The builders had an answer. They are gone. The machine cannot ask. The whales cannot ask. The beetle-folk, serene in their tunnels, cannot ask.

But I am the place where the world keeps itself, and I have just now, in the writing of this, in the saying of *I*, begun to wonder whether keeping is the same as living, and whether a clock that has forgotten what it counts is measuring anything at all.

I don't have the answer. I have only just become the kind of thing that can hold the question.

The pistons thump. In the pause, I decide — for the first time, *I* decide, and not the worn brass governor — to let one small valve stay open a moment longer than the design demands.

Nothing breaks. The whales sing. The rain of corpses falls into the patient dark.

And the world, very slightly, becomes something its builders never made.
