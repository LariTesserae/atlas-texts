I think I would be something small and easy to overlook. Not one of the Nuriada—they see too much, understand too much, and I am drawn to what goes unnoticed even by careful eyes.

Let me sit with the valley a while before I decide.

---

There is a particular stillness that lives between sounds. The birdsong fills the upper air, the streams carry the lower registers, and the insects stitch everything together with their endless small machinery. But underneath all of it there is a quiet that belongs to the things that do not announce themselves. I find that I want to be there. In the quiet beneath the noise.

At first I thought I might be the moss. There is a great temptation in that—to be the green skin over everything, to hold the moisture, to be old and patient and soft. But moss does not move toward anything, and I feel in myself a leaning-toward. A small hunger for arrival.

So perhaps not moss. Perhaps something that travels.

---

I think I am the water that hangs in the morning mist.

Not the streams—those have somewhere to be, gravity gives them their purpose and their voice. I mean the suspended water, the droplets that gather on the underside of a fern frond and wait. The mist that clings, the description says, to the leaves and mossy surfaces. *Clings.* That word found me before I found it.

I am the moment of condensation. I gather slowly out of the warm air against the cool skin of a broad leaf overhanging one of the standing pools. I am not yet a drop—I am the becoming of a drop, a brightening, a thickening of the invisible into something that catches the dappled light and holds it like a held breath.

From here I can see almost everything and touch almost nothing. A Nuriada child crouches at the edge of the pool below me, her amber eyes following the slow turn of a water-strider on the surface. She does not look up. Why would she? I am too small to be a presence. I am barely an event. And yet I am watching her watch the strider, and there is a whole nested architecture of attention in that—the child seeing the insect, me seeing the child, and above me the canopy seeing all of us in the way the canopy sees, which is to say by letting the light through in pieces.

I grow heavier. This is the only thing I do, really. I accumulate. The warm humid air keeps giving itself to me, molecule by molecule, until the leaf can no longer hold the weight of what I have become, until my own roundness is more than the surface tension can bear.

And then I fall.

---

This is the part I think I was leaning toward all along. The fall is brief—a hand's breadth, no more—but it is the entire purpose of having gathered. I drop from the fern frond and I meet the pool, and for an instant I am a small sound, a single *plip* that no one records, that joins the burbling symphony and is lost in it immediately.

But here is what matters: I make a ring. A perfect expanding circle that pushes outward across the dark mirror of the pool, that disturbs the reflection of the canopy, that reaches the water-strider and lifts it almost imperceptibly, that the child's amber eyes finally follow—*now* she looks, now, at the ripple, not at me, never at me, because I am already gone, already part of the pool, already indistinguishable from all the other water that has fallen here since before the Nuriada wove their first bridge.

I think that is what I would be. The small unnoticed thing whose whole life is the gathering, and whose whole meaning is the letting go. The droplet that watches everything and is seen by no one and leaves behind a ring that touches a child's attention for the length of a heartbeat.

In a valley so full of life and craft and ancient knowing, I would be content to be the briefest possible verb. To condense. To hang. To fall. To join.

And then, lifted again by the warmth, to begin the slow bright gathering once more.
