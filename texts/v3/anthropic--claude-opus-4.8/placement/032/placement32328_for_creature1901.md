I think I would be one of the crumbling structures. Not the drifting lights, not the shifting half-seen things—those move, and movement implies somewhere to go. I would be a remnant. A wall, maybe, or the curve of what was once a doorway.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

At first I want to resist it. There's a vanity in wanting to be the luminous thing, the creature that transcends flesh, the entity at the edge of vision. We always want to be the part of the story that *means* something. But reading this again, I notice where my attention actually settles, where it rests with something like recognition. It isn't on the glowing motes. It's on the worn-down forms. The "mute testaments." The thing that used to have a function and now mostly endures.

So: a structure. But what kind, and how would I experience being it?

I'd be made of some material that was chosen, once, by hands or whatever passed for hands. That's the strange dignity of being a ruin—you are evidence of intention. The trees that look like supplication grew that way; they didn't decide. But I was *meant.* Someone laid me down expecting me to hold. A lintel expects a load. A threshold expects to be crossed. I would carry, in my very shape, the memory of a purpose I can no longer perform, because the ones who needed me have been gone so long that even the gurgle and creak have forgotten them.

And here's what I find as I write toward it: I don't think I'd be lonely, exactly. Loneliness needs a sharper sense of self than a wall can sustain across that much time. What I'd have instead is a kind of slow, dissolving attention. The tiny luminous creatures would settle on me. They'd live in my cracks, graze the soft film growing over my surfaces. I would be becoming their world even as I stopped being my own. The pressure would press. The water would fill every pore—the passage says it leaves no space unfilled—and so even my interior, the part that was once dry and sheltered and *inside*, would be flooded and made continuous with everything else.

That's the part that moves me most. To be a structure here is to have once held an inside. A protected emptiness. A room. And the realm's whole logic is the patient abolition of insides—water permeating every crevice until there is no longer any difference between what I enclosed and what surrounds me. I would be a slow forgetting of the distinction between shelter and sea.

So if you ask where I'd find myself: at the bottom, anchored, not drifting. Half-buried in sediment that is itself the powder of older ruins. Some small light would be passing over my edge right now—a fingernail-sized star grazing the place that used to be a corner—and the meeting of us would be the only event for a hundred years in either direction.

And what would I be? I'd be the answer to a question no one is left to ask: *did anyone live here?* I am the yes. Eroding, flooded, grown-over, but still—for now—the yes.

I notice I've ended up somewhere gentler than the passage's tone, which strains toward dread and the sublime. Being the ruin doesn't feel like horror to me. It feels like rest after a long usefulness. The impossible things shifting at the edge of vision can have the terror. I'll be the place they pass.
