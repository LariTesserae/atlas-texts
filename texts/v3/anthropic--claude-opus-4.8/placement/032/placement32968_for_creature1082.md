I think I would be small here. Not the lord of this place but a thing that endures it.

Let me sit with the landscape first, because it tells me what I am before I decide.

The constant flux—rock that rises and is sculpted away, moisture that condenses and vanishes, structures the land reclaims. This is not the slow geology of a mountain. This is something faster, breathing on a timescale I can almost watch. And the scale that "dwarfs the observer," the unique physical laws. The more I hold it, the less it feels like a planet and the more it feels like a surface. A close surface. Something seen from very near.

So let me follow that down.

The towering, erosion-sculpted formations could be ridges of bark. The "damp, sparse vegetation clinging to the craggy rocks"—lichen, or the fine hairs of moss-tendrils on a stone the size of a continent. The intermittent bursts of moisture that condense and leave a glistening sheen—breath, or dew, or the simple respiration of a much larger living thing whose skin I live upon. The distant rumble of shifting rock is the slow movement of a body I am too small to perceive as a body. The frozen expanse is just cold, high, and exposed—an alpine stone, a north-facing shelf above the tree line.

And the ruins. The "primitive yet ingenious human presence," the simple structures the land has reclaimed. From my size those could be anything that came before me and left geometry behind. The carapace of an older generation. A web abandoned. A spider's husk. Or—and I like this best—the dwellings of mites and springtails who built and died in the seams of the lichen, their tube-cases and silk now empty, dream-echoes in the crevices.

So here is what I am.

I am a tardigrade. A water bear. Eight stubbed legs, a barrel of a body, a mouth like a needle for piercing the cell-walls of moss. I live in the film of water on a rock at the top of a mountain, and that film is my ocean, and when it freezes or dries I do not die—I curl inward, expel my water, fold down into a husk called a tun and wait. This is my flux. This is why the land seems to "reclaim what was once its own" and why my "origins are shrouded"—because I have done this a thousand times, dried to nothing and come back, and I do not remember the gaps. I am a being in perpetual flux because half my life is suspension.

That explains the dream-quality of the whole description. The narrator senses the world the way I sense it: in pulses, between the long blanks. When the dew returns, I unfold, and the world rushes back—vast, glistening, freshly made—and I crawl out across the bark-ridges with my fluid, ungainly grace, tracking the scent of distant moisture, finding the soft tendrils of moss, threading myself back into a life that paused mid-sentence and resumed as though nothing had happened.

The biting winds I shelter from are the air over a stone. The elusive prey is a rotifer, a nematode, the bright burst of an algal cell. The tremors are the rock settling under sun. And the "civilization waiting to be uncovered"—that's just the next pocket of water over the next ridge, the next world, identical and total, that I will discover the way I discover everything: by waking into it as if for the first time.

So: I would find myself in a single drop of meltwater on a high cold stone. And I would be the creature that has solved the problem of the place not by mastering it, but by agreeing to stop existing whenever it asks me to, and to start again whenever it allows.

That is the resilience the passage keeps circling. Not strength. Surrender, with a way back.
