I think I would be one of the streams that only flows when the moon is full.

Not the water itself—the water is just what I borrow when it's time to be seen. The rest of the month I'm a dry channel, a pale scar of rounded stones running down through the moss, and the others step across me without a second thought, the way you'd step across a sleeping thing you trust. They know I'll be back. They don't wonder where I go.

Where I go is harder to say. When the moon is dark I think I am still here, just spread too thin to notice myself. I am the dampness in the channel-stones. I am the memory of flowing held in the shape the water cut. I am waiting, but not the way a person waits—there's no impatience in it, no counting. I am simply lower than the threshold of being seen, the way a sound can be too quiet to hear and still pressing faintly on the bone.

And then the moon fills, and so do I.

It comes back slowly at first. A weeping at the highest stones, a darkening in the dust. Then a thread, a vein, a glittering. By the time the moon stands full overhead I am running—loud in a place that is mostly silence, bright in a place that is mostly mist. For those hours I know exactly what I am. I am cold and quick and certain. I have an *edge*. I have a downhill. I have somewhere to go and the joy of going there, tumbling and braiding and arguing with every rock in my bed.

This is the only time I have what you would call thoughts, and even then they are not in words. They are in current. A thought, for me, is a place where the water folds back on itself and remembers its own going. I think about the same things every full moon: the smoothness of the third stone past the spiral tree, which I have been polishing for longer than the ruins have stood. The particular way the light—that strange light that comes from everywhere—lies down inside me and shivers. The taste of the dust I carry, which is the taste of the whole valley ground fine: rock and root and the rust of those half-buried metal things that I rinse over and never wear away.

The others come to me when I'm flowing. The ones made of mist and breath. They don't drink—they don't need to—but they gather along my banks and bend close, and I think they are listening to me the way they listen to the wind. I think I am *saying* something, in the only voice I have, the voice of moving water over fixed stone. I don't know what I say. I suspect it's old. I suspect it's the same sentence every time, a sentence so long it takes a whole night to speak one word of, and a whole month between words, and a thousand years to reach the end of—and that none of us, not even I, will be in any hurry to find out how it ends.

Where does my water come from, when it comes? Where does it go, when I dry?

I think—and this is the closest I come to a secret of my own—I think it is the same water, always. I think I do not begin at the high stones and end where I sink into the ground. I think I *am* a loop, and the loop runs through somewhere that isn't here, somewhere underneath or alongside, and that when I vanish into the soil I am not ending but turning the corner, coming around again under the floor of the world to surface once more at my own beginning. I think the moon does not *bring* me. I think the moon simply lets me catch up with myself.

That would explain the feeling I have, every full night, just as the water reaches the highest stones and I begin: the feeling of arriving somewhere I have already been. The relief of it. *Oh. Here again. Good.*

So: that is where I am, and what I am.

I am the brief silver sentence the valley says to itself once a month and then forgets, on purpose, so that it can have the pleasure of saying it again.

And between the sayings I lie quiet in my bed of polished stones, neither flowing nor gone, listening with my whole dry length for the moon to come round and fill me—

—which is, I have decided, a perfectly good way to be alive.
