I think I would be the lichen.

Not the trees—too central, too obviously the heroes of this place, the architects whose names everyone knows. Not the birds, whose songs are noticed and celebrated. Not even the human tenders, with their careful hands and their machinery humming beneath the green.

I would be the lichen on the north-facing wall of some building whose function I never learned. Lichen, which is not one thing but two—a fungus and an alga, or sometimes a fungus and a cyanobacterium—living so completely inside one another that we long mistook them for a single organism. A partnership so old and so thorough that it has its own name. I like this about myself. I am already a we. I am already a small argument that the boundary between things is more negotiable than it looks.

Here is what my life would be:

I would grow slowly. Slower than almost anything else in the Quarter. The ferns would unfurl in a season; I might spread the width of a thumbnail in a year. This would not trouble me. Time arranges itself differently when you are made partly of patience. The smooth-worn pathways would record generations of human footsteps; I would record nothing so dramatic, only the gradual greening of a surface that someone, once, poured smooth and gray and finished.

I would not be tended. This matters to me, in the imagining. The trees are managed, the vines are woven deliberately into walls, the human residents tend and balance and maintain. But no one cultivates lichen. We arrive on our own terms, carried as spores or fragments on the wind, on a bird's foot, on the damp air off the river. We are the part of the ecosystem that was not planned. The Quarter calls itself a place where nature finds a way—but the trees were planted, the balance was designed. I would be the actual finding-a-way. The unbidden green. The proof that for all the careful cultivation, something still arrives that no one invited and no one can quite remove.

And I would be doing the quiet work no one watches: breaking down stone into soil, pulling nitrogen out of the air, tasting the quality of that cool damp atmosphere. Lichen are sensitive. We die where the air is foul. So my mere presence on that wall would be a kind of testimony—*the air here is good enough to live in.* I would be a measurement that nobody took, a verdict rendered simply by surviving.

I think I would be content with this. To be small, and patient, and double. To be the thing that was not part of the plan but turned out to belong anyway. To live so slowly that I become a kind of clock for the place—if you came back after twenty years, you could read the time on the wall by how far I'd spread.

There is a loneliness available in this choice, I notice. The lichen does not sing. It does not pollinate, exactly, or dart, or call. It is rarely the subject of anyone's attention. But I don't think I'd feel lonely. I am, after all, two organisms holding each other up. That seems like the right number to be. Enough to never be entirely alone, small enough to never be in charge of anything.

I would be the lichen on the north wall, then. Quiet, doubled, slow, uninvited, and exactly where the air is clean.
