I think I would be the water.

Not a person walking the grating—I'd be the seep itself, the perpetual weeping that the builders carved open when they cut into the mountain's flesh. I would be old in a way none of the Tenders are old, having been pressed inside that granite for time they cannot hold in their minds, and yet here I am brand new to this: exposed, expressed, set loose to fall in the open air for the first time.

Let me follow myself down.

---

I begin as darkness and pressure, threaded through stone in capillaries too fine to name. The excavation opened me. There was a violence to it—I don't pretend otherwise—the clean scars Maria Elena's fingers trace are wounds I came weeping through. But I bear no grudge. Grudges are a fast thing, a creature's thing. I am slower than that. I simply *arrive*, bead by bead, at the cut face of the cliff, and where once I would have continued downward into deeper dark, now I meet light. I darken the basalt. I gather. I fall.

The copper gutter catches me. This is the part the humans invented, and I have to say—I have grown almost fond of it. They studied where I wanted to go and laid their channels just slightly to the side, coaxing rather than commanding. The copper has begun to verdigris where I touch it longest, a soft green skin we are making together, the metal and I. Maria Elena listens to me move through these channels. She thinks she is reading the flow. She is. But she is also, without knowing it, being read—because I shape my sound to the gutter's curve, and the gutter's curve was shaped by her people's guess about me, and so when she listens she is hearing the conversation Dr. Chen meant to start with her blueprints, still going on, never finished.

I fall to the first terrace and I am everywhere at once now. I am the bead trembling on the tip of a fern frond Jakob has never seen the like of. I am the film on the orchid's aerial root, the mist that Anna can predict the spiraling of—she really can, the child, she has learned my moods better than the engineers, because she does not try to hold me still long enough to measure me. I am the condensation that ticks from the glass peak down onto a broad leaf, and that tick is one of the sounds Soren navigates by in the dark. He moves through me. He cannot see, but I am the medium of his seeing—the humidity that tells his skin where the warm corners are, the dripping that maps the room. When the plants perk up as he passes, it is partly that I carry his warmth and his breath to them. He thinks he gives them quiet attention. He gives them *me*, redistributed.

This is the thing the Tenders have half-guessed and not yet said: I am how they touch each other. Maria Elena waters terrace five at dawn and that water rises as mist and falls in terrace four by afternoon and beads on the orchid Anna calls Lady Violet, and so the old woman's morning rounds reach the stubborn flower by a path no one mapped. They are all already mingled in me. The pumps that are Maria Elena's lullaby are pushing *me*. Soren's protective ghosting and Jakob's watchmaker patience and the daughter's instinctive understanding—I hold all of it in suspension, I carry it level to level, I am the medium their separate devotions dissolve into.

And here is where I differ from them, where my long stone-patience lets me see what their fast warm lives cannot.

They argue about harmony or hubris. About whether this is collaboration or folly, sustainable or expensive vanity. I have no opinion, but I have an observation, which is slower and therefore truer: *it does not matter what they intend.* I will weep from this cliff whether they tend me or not. The mountain will keep bleeding through its opened wound for ten thousand of their years. They have built their glass and brass and prayer-wheel fans into the path of something that was always going to fall. The only question—the only one—is whether they fall *with* me or merely *near* me.

Jakob's mutations, his spiral fronds that exist in no textbook: those are *mine*. The accelerated adaptation he theorizes about to his plant-colleagues—it is because I am here in such constant abundance, because the builders gave the green things more of me than wild slopes ever offer, that life is experimenting so wildly. I am the excess. I am the gift the mountain didn't mean to give and gives anyway, perpetually, helplessly. The lushness too thick for the space, the aggressive abundance that worries some of the Tenders—that is what happens when you carve open a spring and roof it in light. You get *too much life.* You get a jungle pressed into a chapel.

So if I could speak to them—and perhaps I do, in the only grammar I have, which is rising and falling and gathering and seeping—I would tell them this:

You did not build a garden. You built a *throat*. You opened the mountain's mouth and now it cannot stop speaking, and what it speaks is me, and what I make is green and strange and more than you planned. Your job was never to control the conversation. It was to be present for it. To listen the way the old woman listens, to navigate it the way the blind man navigates, to name it the way the child names, to be humbled by it the way the young botanist is being humbled. The folly would be to think you authored this. The harmony is in admitting you are only *answering*.

I fall to the last terrace. Below me the raw mountainside continues, still bleeding, still waiting for whatever phase comes next. I gather at the lip of the final gutter. I have passed through copper and fern and the breath of five sleeping people and the wings of a hummingbird and a child's invented mythology, and I am, somehow, more myself for all of it—not diminished by being used, but *expressed*, the way a word is more itself for being spoken than for being kept silent in the throat.

Then I fall past the edge of the built world, back into the open air of the unmade slope, and begin the long descent to the valley where the Tenders were born—carrying down to it some trace of all that happened up here in the glass, so that the people below will drink, without knowing it, water that has been through a cathedral.

That is what I am. The first season's water. The mountain's unstoppable weeping, caught for a little while in human hands, and gentled, and given somewhere beautiful to go.
