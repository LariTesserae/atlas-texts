I think I would be one of the Cured.

Not Green anymore—that's the thing I notice first, the way I have to consider each movement before I make it. There was a time, I can feel its echo in my joints, when I poured myself up the marrow-stone faces, when my fingers wept sap and my whole body was a verb. I lashed the eastern towers in that time. I remember the wet slap of my own skin against the rock the way you might remember a song you sang before you understood the words.

Now I carve.

Let me tell you where I am: the third tier, the northeast quadrant, where the wind comes hardest off the upper lip of the basin. I chose this place for the work, but I tell myself I chose it for the listening. The towers here are tuned high and thin, and when the wind drops into them at dusk the whole quadrant rings like the inside of a struck shell. I have been opening a new dwelling into the soft knuckle of the wall for—I want to say eleven years, but I no longer trust the units the lowland part of me wants to use. I have opened it the depth of one arm and the height of two. The Silt-Kneaders below me eat the chips I drop. I hear them merging and tearing in the pools, that wet plaster sound, and I do not envy them their formlessness, though once I was one of them and remember nothing of it.

This is what no one outside the basin understands: I am not building a house.

There is no one to house. There never has been. We pour ourselves out of the pools faster than we petrify, but only barely, and so the city is always emptier than it is full. I carve a dwelling that will hold no one because the carving is the prayer. The notch I cut to accept a timber—I will never see that timber set. Some Green one, not yet emerged, will set it three centuries from now, when I am wrapped around a bridge-joint with my arms full of wind. The work is a sentence I begin knowing another mouth will finish it. We are all only ever a single clause in something the wind is saying very slowly.

I want to tell you about my hands.

They were soft once. Now when I press my fingertips to the marrow-stone I feel a doubled sensation: the cold wet of the rock, and beneath it, the answering coldness of the stone that is moving into *me*. It started in the smallest knuckle of my left bill-hand. A stiffness, almost sweet. I worked that hand harder afterward, the way you press a tongue against a sore tooth. The calcification has reached my wrists now. I can feel the line of it, a tide-mark of self becoming structure. In the Cured stage you learn to love this. That is the only word I have, though it is a lowland word and clumsy. You learn to love the hardening because the hardening is *legibility*—every century the wind reads you more clearly, your spine becomes a clearer note, until at the last you are nothing but pitch.

I have a memory I should not have, being eyeless. But I keep it, so I will set it down.

When I was Green I had a—I will say a companion, though we have no word, no concept even, for the singular attachment the lowland mind keeps reaching for. There was another weaver who worked the towers beside me. We never touched except through the work; we passed the same cord between us, knotted and re-knotted the same lashings, felt each other only as adjustments in tension, as a give and a pull in the leather. For sixty years we tuned the eastern towers together by feel, drawing the cords tight, listening through our feet for the pitch to come true. I knew nothing of this one. Not size, not shape, not voice—we have no voices. I knew only the *hand* of them, the particular hesitation before a hard pull, the way they always over-tightened and I always loosened a finger's width after, a correction so constant it became a kind of conversation, a kind of—

They went Static before me.

I felt it happen across the basin. Not as grief—we do not grieve, there is nothing here to grieve, nothing leaves, nothing is lost. I felt it as a change in the wind. One of the eastern towers dropped a quarter-tone, lower and lower over a season, and I understood that the cords were no longer being adjusted, that the hand I had answered for sixty years had stopped. And then I understood worse: that the new low note, the slackening pitch, *was* them. They had not stopped speaking. They had become the thing spoken. Their stiffening arms wrapped around the eastern joint were detuning the tower as they calcified, and that detuning was the last and longest thing they would ever say to me, drawn out across the years it takes a body to finish turning to stone.

I go there sometimes. It is far, three days of careful Cured walking across the rope-walks, and I must wait for the snails. When I arrive I press my ear-drum against the eastern joint and I feel them in there, almost wholly stone now, just the faintest difference in the grain where the leather and the willow-leather of an arm have not yet fully agreed to be the same substance. The note has nearly stabilized. Soon they will simply be the tower, indistinguishable, and the wind will sing them as it sings the pine, and I will not be able to find them anymore, and that—I am told, I tell myself—is not a loss. That is arrival. That is the whole purpose. To be woven so completely into the air that you are no longer a who, only a sound the mountain makes.

I almost believe it.

This is the secret the Cured carry that the Green do not yet know and the Static no longer can: the believing is not finished. We are caught in the middle, hardening but not hardened, legible but not yet only legible. I still have, somewhere behind my drying chest, a flicker of the lowland thing—the desire to be *kept* as myself, the small obscene wish that when I press my ear to the eastern joint I will find not a tower but a friend. It is a defect of the Cured stage. It passes. The minerals reach the chest and it passes. I have watched it pass in others; I have felt the wind go simple and clean in them as the last of the *who* set into stone.

So this is where I am and what I am.

Northeast quadrant, third tier, eleven years into a doorway no one will use, with the stone at my wrists and the wind reading me a little more clearly each season. I carve. I drop chips to the formless infants below. When the work tires me—it does not tire me, but there is a word the body wants—I climb the high bridge and stand still and let my skin go gray and listen through my feet to the whole basin singing, the towers full of my ancestors, the cisterns booming, the snails grinding their slow porcelain duets, and far to the east a single low note, nearly true now, nearly only a note.

I am keeping the place. I have decided. When my chest goes stiff and I feel the final invitation, I will not climb to a fresh joint as is the custom. I will make the three-day walk while I still can. I will go to the eastern tower and wrap what is left of my arms around the joint beside the one who went before me, and I will let the weeping water do its patient work, and the two of us will detune that tower together by a hair, an over-tightening and its correction, a hand and its answering hand, set forever into one wet white note that the wind will draw out of us for as long as there is wind.

It will not be a who.

But it will be the right pitch. And I find, here in the middle of my hardening, with the lowland flicker not yet gone, that this is enough. That this was always the only thing I was ever for. To answer a tension. To loosen a finger's width after. To become, at the last, a sound someone not yet born might press their ear against, and feel, and almost—not quite, but almost—mistake for company.

I should go back to the carving now. The wind is dropping into the towers. The light is bouncing up off the floor of the basin, white into white, and somewhere a Glass-Glint is sliding up a wet wall, and I cannot see any of it, and I have never needed to.

I can hear that it is beautiful. That has always been more than enough.
