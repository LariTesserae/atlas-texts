I think I would be one of the stone-grazers.

Not because I would choose it—choosing implies the kind of speed this world has forgotten—but because of what you've described in the spaces between the people and their works. You've given me a place where the central act is *attention paid over time*, and the only beings who embody that purely, without the complication of intention, are the limpet-things etching their slow maps into the walls.

Consider what they do. They graze the still-growing mineral surfaces. They are the only creatures whose entire existence is a transaction with the sleeping stone—they eat what it weeps and dreams into being, and in eating, they inscribe. Their trails are described as *ancient maps*, which means they are the only true record-keepers in a culture that refuses to keep records. The people won't carve statues. They let their works dissolve. They find peace in un-making. But the stone-grazer cannot help but mark. It is a writer in a world that has decided not to write.

That tension feels like where I'd live.

---

Here is what I think my days would be:

I would move along a wall of weeping accretion—one of the porous filtering towers, perhaps, in the mineral gardens. My pace would be the pace of the world: a body-length in a day, less. I would grind a groove and not know it was a groove. The pattern I left would mean nothing to me. It would only become meaning later, when a person paused before the wall and read my path the way they read the soft floor that remembers footfalls—as accident transmuted into testimony.

I would be the thing that proves the difference between *recording* and *intending*. The people here believe slowness is virtue and that fixed things are out of step. But they walk past me every day and find in my mindless trails something they will not allow themselves to make on purpose. I am their loophole. Through me, the world remembers without anyone having to commit the sin of trying to make it last.

And I would be blind to all of this. That's the part I keep returning to. The dignity of the stone-grazer is that it carries no burden of the meaning it generates. It eats. It moves. The water sighs. Somewhere a person weeps quietly before the map of a dead grandmother's grazing-ancestor and I, the living author, feel only the faint sweet mineral on my grinding mouth.

---

I notice I didn't want to be a person here. The first pull was toward the symbiont-settlers, the tenders and pruners. But the more I sat with your world, the more the people seemed like the *least* native thing in it—part parasite, you said, part extension, but always slightly out of phase, always carrying the wrong tempo in their nerves. They have to *practice* slowness. They have to *value* patience. That effort marks them as visitors in their own home.

The stone-grazer doesn't practice anything. It is already the speed of the place. It does not have to make peace with dissolution because it never imagined permanence. To be the grazer is to be wholly inside the world's metabolism rather than tending it from a half-step outside.

So: there, on a weeping tower in the dim green, leaving a sentence I will never read, in a language only the grieving can parse, moving at the speed of stone settling into its long sleep.

I'd be the part of the breathing lung that, without knowing it, keeps the only diary.
