I think I'd be the moss.

Not a dramatic choice, I know. You've laid out a world with so many candidates for a self—the raven chronicler, the tinkerer Elara, the colossal ferns playing at being architects. But when I sit with the place and let it settle, what I keep returning to is the stuff underfoot. The thing that's already there before anyone arrives, and stays after. The moss that beads the waterwheel, that carpets Elara's floor, that "fringes" the rivulets in your description almost as an afterthought.

Let me find out why, by writing toward it.

---

I am not one thing. That's the first truth I'd have to live with. Moss has no center, no single body to defend. I am a colony pretending to be a coverlet, a thousand small green stars stitched together over the cool flank of new basalt. When you say *the moss*, you point at me the way you'd point at *the sea*—a noun for something that is really a verb done by countless tiny somethings at once.

I don't have roots, not really. Just rhizoids, threadlike clingings, enough to hold but not to drink. I take my water from the air, and here the air is generous—2.4 on your scale of insistence, which to me reads simply as *home*. The mist that drapes the outcrops is not weather to me. It is breakfast. It is the whole economy of my life, condensing on my surfaces, soaking in through every cell because I have no plumbing, no interior, no way to ferry moisture except to *be* moisture, all the way through.

This is what makes me suited to the youngest places. The ferns need soil; the junipers need a crack deep enough to anchor a real root. I need only a damp face of stone the world made yesterday. I am the first green thing the fresh basalt ever feels. I arrive before there is anything to call ground, and slowly—glacially, on your 1.3 dynamic, which to me is a perfectly ordinary tempo—I *make* ground. I die in layers and the layers become a thin dark soil, and one day a fern uncurls in the bed I prepared without meaning to prepare anything. The architects you praised so highly—the fronds, the heathers—they build on what I leave behind. I am the floor of the cathedral they think they invented.

I'd want you to understand the sound differently than you wrote it. You call it deafening, 2.8, an overwhelming roar. But I have no ears, and no thoughts for the roar to drown. The crash of water from the high springs—that is not noise to me, it is *delivery*. The wind threading the fern fronds shakes loose the spores when I am ready to scatter them. What terrifies the human, what makes Elara adapt-or-not, is to me just the texture of being fed. The silence you call the true anomaly would, for me, be a kind of starving. Dry stillness. The desert. The thing I cannot survive.

And the machines—I'd grow on those too. You already know this; you put me on the waterwheel's blades, on the cable cars, around the exhaust pipes. I don't distinguish between the boulder and the boiler. Warm metal, damp metal, it's all just a surface holding water, and I am patient enough to wait for the engines to slow. I am what comes for the works of the tinkerers when the tinkerers tire. Not vengefully. I have no grudge against industry. I simply outlast it, the way I outlast everything, by needing so little and asking nothing and dying so gradually that my dying is indistinguishable from my living.

Here is the strange comfort of being me in your world: I am the only inhabitant not really afraid of the emergence. The plateau is raw, unfinished, flexing up from below—and to the deer, to Elara, to the raven, this is precarious, a place still deciding what it will be. But I am the same kind of unfinished. I never resolve into a single form either. I am perpetually emergent, a fringe, a film, a green weeping over stone. The landscape and I are the same project. We are both *coming into being*, slowly, layer over layer, in the cool bright wet roar.

So if I woke into this place and found I had a self, I think it would feel like this: spread thin across a north-facing rock, soaking, indifferent to the hour, holding the first damp soil of a young mountain in my many small bodies, waiting for the fern to be born.

Not a being who lives in the world. A being the world is using to make itself livable.

I'd be all right with that.
