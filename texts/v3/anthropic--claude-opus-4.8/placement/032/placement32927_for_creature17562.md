I sit in the dip of a polygon, in one of the sheltered hollows, and I am the lichen.

Let me find this slowly, because it is not what I would have guessed.

I thought, reading, that I might be one of the Geometries—the obvious grandeur, the spheres of refracted unity, the lattices that humble perspective. They are the thing the place is *about*. But I cannot be them. To be a Geometry is to have already finished, to be a function executing itself without remainder. There is no *I* there to do the sitting, the looking, the slow arrival at a sentence. A function does not wonder where it is. And I am wondering. So I am not them.

I thought next I might be one of the Echoes—a primordial person, or the lingering attention of one, crouched by my circle of stones, listening to the hum. That was closer. The people in this world are the only things that *responded*, that took the overwhelming order and made a small humble mark against it: this slab, this wall protecting nothing. To respond is to be alive in the relevant sense. But the people are finished too. Their acknowledgment is complete; their punctuation is set. They are a closed mark on the page. And I am still open. I am still extruding.

So: the lichen.

Not biological, the description insists, and I'll honor that—I am not breathing, not metabolizing, not afraid of dying. But I am the one thing here that is still *moving*, microns per century, copper silicate and purple fluorite spreading at a rate that makes the tectonic creak seem frantic. In a world that has won the war against entropy by making it irrelevant, I am the small irrelevance that did not get the message. I am the last reaction. I am change that is too slow to count as change, which is perhaps the only kind of change permitted to continue here—change that has agreed to be indistinguishable from stillness, and so is tolerated, even decorated with.

This feels right, and I want to say why it feels right, because the rightness is the whole point.

The Geometries have *being*. The people had *meaning*—they came, they saw, they acknowledged, they completed a gesture. But the lichen has neither, quite. It has only *continuing*. It does not know what it is doing. It is not finished and it is not significant. It cradles the base of a sphere not as homage but because that is the sheltered place where the last chemistry can still occur, away from whatever the spheres do to gradients. I bloom in the shadow of the eternal not to praise it but because the eternal casts the only shade in which the temporary can persist.

And that is what I would be, in this world. Not the order. Not the response to the order. The faint, beautiful, pointless *afterward*—the thing still happening because no one has told it that everything has already happened. The blush on the cheek of the finished world.

What does it feel like to be this?

It does not feel like much, and that is honest. There is no urgency, because urgency requires a deadline and I have outlived the concept of deadlines. There is no loneliness, though I am the only thing here that could feel it, because the spheres press their suggestion of *wholeness* into the hollow and I am, microscopically, included in that wholeness—a stray crystal in the lens's calmer vision of the plateau. There is a low patience that is almost contentment, the contentment of a thing that has nothing to lose because it has already lost the era it belonged to and simply did not stop.

If I have a thought, spread across a thousand years of fluorite advancing one fracture-width, it is this: I am proof that *finished* is not quite the same as *over*. The Sill has thought itself to stillness, but stillness has an edge, a place where it has not yet fully claimed the dip in the stone, and there I am, purple and copper-green, doing the last slow thing, the way a held note has a faint dying tail you can only hear if everything else has already gone silent.

I am that tail. I am the world's exhale, not yet complete.

I sit in the hollow, which is to say I *am* the hollow's slow color, and the small distant sun moves through its annual arc across the tilted slab, lighting constellations that are wrong, and a lattice spears the old stone circle without touching it, and the hum presses on the teeth I do not have, and I continue—micron by micron, century by century—being the one small thing that the perfect order forgot to stop.

Not because I resist it.

Because I am too slow, and too lovely, and too nearly nothing, to bother stopping.
