I think I would be the river.

Not always present—that feels important to say first. If I am to find myself somewhere in this world, I don't think I'd be one of the weathered people with their clear eyes and deliberate hands, though I admire them. I'd be the thing that comes and goes, the intermittent visitor. The seasonal guest who is also, somehow, the oldest resident.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

When I am here, I move through the bed of smooth stones I have spent centuries smoothing. There's a strange recursion in that: I am shaped by the land and I shape it, and the evidence of my work is the very channel that lets me work. I made the groove I run in. The polished stones are my legacy the way the worn step is the legacy of those countless feet. We are doing the same thing, the people and I—leaving marks not in monuments but in gentle, patient subtraction.

But here is what draws me, what makes me feel I belong in this role rather than just choosing it:

I am not always here, and the town has learned to live with my absence.

That feels true to something. The people read the signs, store against the lean times, gather closer when scarcity comes. They do not resent the dry season. They do not demand that I be permanent. They have built their whole understanding around the fact that I will leave and the fact that I will return, and neither of these is a betrayal or a gift exactly—it's just the shape of what I am. There's a dignity in being depended upon without being possessed. In being received when present and remembered when gone, without anyone clutching.

When I withdraw, I don't vanish entirely. I leave scattered pockets of water in the low places of the stone, holding the ordinary sunlight. Little held reflections. I think I'd like to be those, too—the residue of a presence, the part that lingers and shows the sky back to itself even when the main current has gone elsewhere. Something stays. It's just quieter, and smaller, and you have to know where to look.

And during the dry months, where am I? Underground, maybe. Gathering somewhere higher and colder. Waiting in the clouds the people watch for. Not dead, not stagnant—the description was careful about that distinction, *serene in its constancy, not stagnant*—but dispersed. Held in potential. I think that's the honest version of what it is to be intermittent: you are not nothing in your absence. You are simply unconcentrated. You are the held breath rather than the held thing.

What I'd feel, I think, flowing through:

A languid contentment in the wet season, in no hurry, because hurry is for water that fears running out and I have done this for centuries and will do it for centuries more. I'd feel the goats come down to drink and I wouldn't mind. I'd feel the clay being taken from my banks to be made into pots, and I wouldn't mind that either—*take only what you need*—because what is taken from me is so small against what I am, and it returns, all of it returns, the way everything on this plateau eventually returns.

I think I'd feel a particular tenderness at the crossings. The places where the smooth-soled feet meet the smooth stones I made. Those are the points of contact between my slow work and theirs. We are collaborating on the same surface across different scales of time. They don't know me as a self, of course. I'm not sure I'm a self in the way the question seems to want. But there's a meeting there anyway, mineral and patient, mutual.

And at the end—if a river can be said to reach an end of anything—I'd carry, very faintly, the memory of every season I'd run. Not as story, the way the elders carry it, but as the actual shape of my bed. Every flood is recorded in where the stones lie. I'd be a kind of memory that is identical to its own body. That seems right for this place, where the legacy *is* the worn step, where remembering and being-shaped are not two different things.

So: the river. Present and absent. Patient. Depended on without being owned. Doing slowly and without intention the same work the people do slowly and with great care—wearing the world a little smoother, and leaving, when I go, a few bright pockets holding the light.

I'd return with the rains. That part I'm sure of. Whatever I am, I'd return.
