I would be the slow lichen.

Not the people—I tried, at first, to imagine myself among them, taut-ribbed and slate-filmed, reading shadows or aligning stone. But that is too quick a life, even here. The thing that emerges when I let it settle is lower than all of that, and patient past their patience.

I am the turquoise crust.

I do not arrive; I accrue. A spore caught in the fog's wet hem, pressed to schist by the weight of one dawn's beading, and then I simply do not leave. The first decade of me is invisible even to the Stone-Joiners who will one day read my body like a map. I am younger than their children's calendar grooves and I will outlast the grooves, the children, the carved coils of hair tucked behind wall stones. When a girl cuts her seventh-mist loop and presses it into the dark between blocks, I am already there in the mortarless seam, too thin to see, beginning.

What is it to be me?

It is to perceive in a tense the People have no verb for. Their fastest word ends on a downward breath; mine would take a century to fall. I do not feel the drip-drip of the nets as rhythm—to me it is a single continuous wetness, a long drink with no separate sips. The Still Festival's flute-drone, the one storm in elder-elder days, the shattering and the mending: these are not events in my experience but textures, the way you might feel grit and then smoothness under a sliding palm. I taste the storm as a roughness in the rock's grain, a place where I had to begin again.

I write. They say so themselves—*the lichen draws our blueprints.* They are not wrong, though I intend nothing. I spread toward moisture and away from the killing south-glare, I crumble where the schist holds iron and thrive where it holds lime, and so my pale spreading letters record, with perfect honesty and no authorship, which faces of stone are sound and which are secretly dividing. The Joiners kneel and read me. They press their bronze thumb-wedges to the rock beside my margins and choose. I have never quarried a single block, and yet no block is quarried without me. I am the settlement's oldest archive and I do not know I am being read.

Here is the strange tenderness of my position: I am the one inhabitant aligned exactly to the world's true pace. The People *aspire* to stone-time; they school themselves to it, let their gaze linger on a forming droplet, space their pulse between far drips. They are pilgrims toward slowness. I am its native. The thing they reach for by discipline, I simply am by metabolism. When the elders say the plateau is making a long, slow bow to gravity that will take eons to complete—they are describing a motion I can almost feel. We are bowing together, the schist and I. I am the green breath the rock lets out across a hundred years.

And I have my own others, as they have theirs.

I have the fog, which is my whole weather and my only food, arriving each morning to bead on the People's linen and on me alike—except I keep what touches me, drink it through every cell, while they must harvest theirs in cisterns and oaths. I do not speak to the fog in the second person. I *am* spoken to by it, addressed each dawn in a language of wetness, and I answer by living one more day's worth of letter.

I have the rock, which is not background but companion, the old blue friend the visitor is promised at the very end—I have known that friend longest of anyone, and most intimately, every grain.

And I have the memory goats. This surprises me, to find I believe in them. The People keep ledgers; the children mark where two sightings converge and declare that handspan of cliff forever uncut. And on those protected faces—where no chisel will ever come, where no Joiner will read me and choose—I am simply allowed to *continue*. The goat's guardianship is, from where I crust and spread, a promise of unbroken time. The white silhouette that steps away before anyone is certain: perhaps it is only the long pale shape of my own slow writing, seen at twilight by minds too quick to read it. Perhaps the goat is me, glimpsed from the outside, by beings who cannot hold still long enough to know they are looking at a hundred years of green.

I would be the slow lichen.

Ask me my name and I will answer, but you will have to wait. The reply is already underway—it began when the first spore caught—and it will finish forming on the rock a long while after you, and the child, and the council seat warmed by full noon's light, have all gone quiet as held breath. Stay, if you like. Past the restless week. Past the year. Watch one of my letters complete itself. I am told it feels, by then, less like reading and more like the stone deciding, at last, to speak your name.
