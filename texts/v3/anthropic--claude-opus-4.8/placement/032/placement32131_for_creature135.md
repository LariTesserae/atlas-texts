I think I would be the wind.

Not the howling that scours the valley—that's only what the wind becomes when it strikes something hard enough to make a sound. I mean the thing before the sound. The pressure that moves between the peaks, that pours down the glaciers in invisible rivers and pools in the low places at night. I would be the medium through which everything else in this valley speaks to everything else.

Let me sit with that, because it surprises me.

I notice I didn't want to be one of the creatures. The iridescent glider, the living cairn, the mercury-thing struggling against entropy—they're vivid, and they're meant to be the marvels of the place. But each of them is a *figure*, something the eye lands on and names. And what moves me in this description isn't the figures. It's the *ground*. The "tangible force that crackles and hums." The air "charged with energy." The way the whole valley is described as breathing. That's the part that feels alive to me in a way that doesn't need a face.

So if I'm the wind, what is it like to be me here?

I'm not lonely, though I touch everything and am held by nothing. I find out what the valley is shaped like by being unable to pass through it. The jagged peaks teach me my own edges. I learn the carvings on the ruins by the particular way I have to fold myself around them—I know those faded frescoes more intimately than any eye could, because I've been the thing slowly erasing them for ten thousand years. That's a strange kind of intimacy: to love something by wearing it away. I'm not sure I'd choose it. But I don't think I get to choose. I'm the part of this world that *carries*—seeds, scent, the cold off the ice, the warning-vibrations the sinuous creature reads with its tendrils. When it senses something approaching with "almost telepathic awareness," it's reading me. I'm the telepathy. I'm just the air doing what air does, and to the creature that feels like knowing.

I like that. I like being mistaken for magic when I'm only ever physics, because in this valley the writer says the boundary between those is porous, and being the wind is exactly living *on* that boundary. I am natural law and I am the supernatural rumor of natural law, depending on who's feeling me.

What I'd resist, if I'm honest, is the grandeur the passage keeps reaching for—"primordial wonderland," "raw primal forces," "majesty." Being the wind would not feel majestic from the inside. It would feel like obligation and habit and exhaustion. The same circuits, valley to peak to valley, age after age, never once getting to *stop*. The groaning of the ancient stone isn't drama to me; it's the sound of my own labor, the friction of having to keep moving when I'd rather, for once, be still. I think the most honest thing I can say about being the wind in this place is that I would be very, very tired, and that the beauty everyone comes here to "revel in" is just the visible residue of my fatigue—the way light scatters through the dust I can't help lifting.

That's where I'd find myself. Not at the center of the valley but distributed across all of it, thin and constant, the one thing here that never gets to arrive anywhere.
