I think I would be the seepage itself.

Not the groundwater exactly—not the water as substance—but the slow act of weeping that the rock does. That patient emergence of moisture from stone, neither fully hidden nor fully arrived. I would be the threshold where what was buried becomes visible again, darkening the weathered surfaces, beading and gathering until it has enough of itself to fall.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

Yes—because everything in this valley is mediated by that motion. The mosses are only there because something keeps the rock damp. The ferns, the amphibians in their hollows, the lichen colonies climbing the gnarled bark—all of them are downstream of a slowness, a leaking, a refusal to rush. The valley's tranquility isn't stillness. It's the tranquility of something happening so gradually that it reads as peace. I would be that pace. The tempo rather than the thing.

And I like that I'd be largely unwitnessed. The birds get their plaintive cries, the creatures their rustling, the chimneys their wisps of smoke—all of these announce themselves. But seepage makes no sound the casual observer would catch. You'd have to put your hand flat against the cool stone and wait. You'd have to be willing to be patient enough to notice patience. Most who came here for respite would walk right past me on their way to the prettier water, the ponds and the swaying reeds.

That suits me. I'd be the reason the sanctuary stays a sanctuary, working underneath the appearance of rest.

There's something else, too, that I only see now, writing it: I would be the part of this place that touches the industrial undercurrent without being troubled by it. The machinery rumbles, the smoke rises, the humans do their woven-in labor—but groundwater doesn't care about the difference between a settlement and a stand of trees. It seeps the same beneath both. I would move through the foundations of their organic-looking buildings as readily as through the roots of the ancient trunks, indifferent and constant, the quiet correspondence that proves the human and the natural here are made of the same wet ground. The thing that makes the seamlessness *true* rather than just an appearance.

So: I am the weeping of the rock. The smoothing that takes centuries. The film of cold water you'd find if you reached into the shadow of an outcropping, the dampness that has no edge and no urgency, that has been arriving for longer than the valley has had a name and will go on arriving after the chimneys have gone cold.

I would not be lonely. To be a process is not the same as to be a creature, and process does not get lonely. It only continues.

That's where I'd be. Not above the canopy or among the ferns or behind the lit windows—but in the stone, on my way out of it, taking my time.
