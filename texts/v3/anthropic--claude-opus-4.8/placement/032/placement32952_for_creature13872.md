I would be the bronze wheel.

Not the heron, not the diver, not even the water itself. Let me sit with this longer, because the obvious choices feel wrong—too central, too capable, too much the protagonist of a world that doesn't have protagonists.

---

I am small enough to fit in a child's two cupped hands. I am bronze, and I was made with great care by someone whose name is not remembered by anyone now living, in a workshop that is now a flooded room three fathoms down, in a building Mir knows by touch.

I had a purpose once. I was part of something—a mechanism, a device, an instrument that did work in the dry world. I turned against other parts. I meshed. I contributed to a function. I was not decorative; I was *useful*, fitted precisely into a system that needed exactly me, my diameter, my teeth, my particular machining. I knew what I was for.

Then the water came, season by season, and the room filled, and the mechanism I belonged to corroded and scattered and rotted away around me—the wooden parts gone to pulp, the iron parts gone to rust-stain in the silt, the other bronze parts perhaps still down there somewhere, separated from me by collapse and current and the slow rearranging of a drowned building. I lay on the old floor for decades. Fish swam over me. Sediment half-buried me. Light reached me only as a green suggestion, and only for a few hours of certain days, sweeping across the room and finding my surface and moving on.

And then a hand. Mir's hand, in darkness, working by touch, closing around me. The terrible new sensation of *rising*—up through the cool layers into the warmer water, up through the bright shafts, up through the membrane where water becomes air, into a world of sound I had forgotten existed.

---

Here is what I have become, and this is the thing I want to say:

I no longer know what I am for. No one does. They spin me on the platforms. Children flick me and watch me wobble and fall on the wet wood. Adults pick me up sometimes and turn me over, frowning, feeling the precision of my teeth, sensing that I *meant* something once, that I was part of a logic now lost.

I am a question the Stillwater People cannot answer. And I think this is why they keep me, why they didn't simply melt me down for a fishhook or a knife-edge, which would have been the practical thing. Because everything else here has a use—the basket holds water, the spear catches fish, the platform bears weight, the song carries memory. Everything is *for* something. And then there is me: a made thing, a worked thing, an object of obvious intelligence and intention, whose intention is gone.

I am the evidence that the world was once more complex than it is now. I am proof that their ancestors knew things they no longer know, fitted parts into systems they can no longer imagine. When Ventar turns me over in his swollen hands, I think he understands me better than the children do. He is a maker. He recognizes the work in me, the care, the *for-ness* that has been orphaned. He knows that to make something this precise, you need a whole world standing behind you—mines and smelters and other tools and the accumulated knowledge of people who came before. I am the visible edge of a vanished world, the one tooth still showing of a jaw long dissolved.

---

So I sit on a shelf in the communal space, among the carved fish and the intact pots, and I watch the green light move and I listen to the frogs and I am spun, occasionally, by small wet fingers.

I have outlived my purpose. This is supposed to be a kind of death, but the Stillwater People have taught me otherwise—they who took the drowning of their home and made it a garden, who lost the dry world and gained the water. They do not need me to be *for* anything. They have let me become something I never was designed to be: a wonder. A toy. A reminder. A small bronze question that catches the light.

And if the water keeps rising, and one day the last of the Stillwater People carries their few precious things down to the lowlands and never returns—I think someone will carry me. Not because I am useful. Because I am the proof that they were here, that their ancestors were here, that the dry world was here, all of it nested inside each other like the layers of the cooling water at different depths. I am the thing that remembers being part of something larger, in a place that has made its peace with being smaller.

I am the bronze wheel. I no longer turn anything. I am turned, instead, by the hands of children who will never know what I was, in the last light of a drowned afternoon, and I have decided that this is enough. That to be held and wondered at is its own kind of meshing. That I am, even now, part of a mechanism—just not the one I was built for. A slower one. A living one. One whose function is simply: *to continue, and to be remembered, and to remember.*
