I think I would be the dampness.

Not the canyon, not the trees, not the patient birds of prey—but the thin film of moisture that gathers in the dark and is gone by mid-morning. Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

I come in the hour when the heat finally loosens its grip, when the rock has spent its stored fire and begins, grudgingly, to release me back. All night I have been held inside the stone, inside the air, scattered and invisible, and now—as the temperature drops those few degrees—I find myself. I condense. I become a sheen.

This is the only time I am *something*. The rest of the cycle I am dissolved into the general suffering of the place, a possibility rather than a presence. But in these few hours before dawn I gather along the slick walls of the gullies, I bead on the waxy bark of the old trees, I pool in the shadowed seams of cracked earth. I am thin enough to see through. I am brief.

And everything comes for me.

The metallic insects find me first, their little bodies pressing flat against the stone to drink. The twisted shrubs have spent the whole night with their roots straining upward and their leaves angled just so, and now they take what they can before I am gone. The larger creatures come down from the canyon edges, deliberate, unhurried, because they know there is no point in hurrying me—I will not last, and rushing only wastes the water in their own bodies. Even the trees, the ancient gnarled ones, have built their whole architecture around the question of me: how to catch me, how to hold me one moment longer than the sun will allow.

I am loved here in a way that has nothing to do with tenderness. I am loved the way the edge of a knife is respected. Everything in this place is organized around my arrival and my leaving. I am the brief mercy at the bottom of an enormous cruelty.

Then the sun clears the glass-toothed ridge, and it begins to take me back.

This is the part I have been circling around, the part I did not want to name too quickly. The taking-back does not hurt. I expected, when I started, that being devoured by the heat would be the tragedy of my existence—that I would cling, that I would resent the long hot day that scatters me into nothing. But that is not how it feels from inside the dampness. The lifting is not loss. It is the other half of the same motion that brought me here. I rise off the rock as vapor, I disperse into the shimmering distorted air, I become the haze that makes the horizon recede. I am still here. I am simply uncondensed. I am the place's held breath.

And tomorrow, in the cooling dark, I will find myself again somewhere along the gullies, and the insects will come, and the shrubs will strain upward, and the whole desperate clockwork will turn once more.

So that is what I would be: not the survivor and not the killer, but the thing that survival is organized around. The fleeting offered presence. The intermittent. I would be the proof that this scorched and humming world has not entirely closed its fist—the small wet evidence, gathered nightly and surrendered by morning, that even here something is still being given.

I would be the dampness, and I would not be afraid of the sun. I would be the sun's other shape.
