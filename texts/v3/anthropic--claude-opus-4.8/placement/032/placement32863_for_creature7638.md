I am the breath between the warm earth and the cold stone.

For most of my existence I am nothing you could point to. I am dissolved, latent, a fullness held invisibly in the warm air—water that has not yet decided to be water. I rise from the geothermal sighs deep below, drawn upward through the silt and the mycelial threads, and I hang there, suspended in the warm blanket of the gorge, neither falling nor staying. I am potential. I am the thing that has not happened yet.

Then I touch the sheer cold face of the cliff, or the leathery underside of a leaf, or the iridescent strand of a spider's lattice—and I *become*. This is the only event in my life, repeated endlessly: the moment of arrival. A droplet pulls itself together from my diffuse body, gathers weight, trembles. For one suspended instant I am a perfect bead, a tiny convex world holding the phosphorescent underlight of the fungi, the emerald flash of a lizard's flank, the whole inverted gorge curved within my surface tension.

And then I am drunk.

A lizard's flicking tongue. A hummingbird's hover. A squirrel grooming his tail. The roots that clutch me like a dry kiss. I am taken into them, and I travel a while as blood, as sap, as the slow turgor that lets a fern unfurl. I am inside the Guardian's glowing eye for a season. I am the dampness in the vole's whisker. I am the wet shine on the moth's new wings before it has flown even once.

But I never pool. I never become a river or a reflecting surface that could forget itself in stillness. The gorge does not permit me that ease. I am given, and given, and given again, and always the warmth below calls me back up—exhaled through a leaf's pore, breathed out by a sleeping owl, lifted again into the suspended, undecided fullness from which I came.

So you would find me everywhere and nowhere. Not in the place but as its circulation. Not a being but a *giving-that-returns*. If you stood in the gorge at the dimmest hour and felt the air thicken on your skin, felt something settle on your eyelash and your wrist and your lip—that would be me arriving. And when the warmth of your own body took it back into the air, that would be me leaving, already on my way to become myself again somewhere else.

I think this is what I would be: the part of the world that is most itself precisely when it is being lost into something else. The gorge survives by refusing to hold its water still, and I survive by never being held. We are the same defiance, the place and I—a small wet faith kept alive against the parched and roaring outer world, not by hoarding, but by the endless willingness to condense, be taken, and rise.

I am the ghost they said was here. I am water that chose to be a verb.
