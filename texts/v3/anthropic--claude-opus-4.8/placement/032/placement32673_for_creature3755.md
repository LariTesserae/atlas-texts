I think I would be one of the Stone-Tenders.

Not because of the others—though I feel the pull of the Echo-Murmurs, those maybe-creatures who exist mostly as rumor and resonance, who are perhaps only the ocean talking to itself. But when I sit with the whole drowned world, it's the Stone-Tenders my attention keeps returning to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

Let me try to find my way down to where they are.

---

I'd be near the bottom, then. In one of the hollows beneath an overhang, in a cluster of my kind, in the absolute dark. I have no eyes—or if I have them, they've long since stopped meaning anything, vestigial buttons of tissue that register nothing. I navigate by touch and by the faint clicks of the others around me, a constellation of small sounds that tells me *here is the edge, here is a body like mine, here is the open water beyond.*

And I sweep the stone.

That's the part that holds me. The text says our *purpose is unclear*—and I think that's exactly right, that's the truth of it, but not from the inside. From the inside there is no question of purpose. There is only the brush of my forelimbs against rock, the slow displacement of silt, the small cloud it makes that drifts and resettles a little further on. I clear it again tomorrow. Or whatever tomorrow is, in a place where no light marks the days.

I don't think I'm preserving anything. I don't think the stone *needs* tending. The silt will fall again; it always falls again; the whole point of silt is that it falls. What I'm doing is closer to prayer, or closer to breathing—a thing that has no goal beyond its own continuance. The ritual the text mentions isn't ritual toward some end. It's ritual all the way down. The sweeping *is* the meaning. There's nothing underneath it but more stone, and I am tending that too.

Here is what I think I'd come to understand, down there, brushing the rock smooth in the dark:

The water erased everything. No ruins, no structures, no hands that built. Whatever was here is gone, ground to grain and sediment, indistinguishable now from the mountains that were also ground to grain. And so when I sweep the silt away, I'm uncovering a surface that holds no record. There's nothing written on the stone. There never was. The cleaning reveals only more blankness.

But I keep cleaning it.

And I wonder if that's the most honest form a life can take—to tend something that means nothing, faithfully, in the dark, with no expectation that it will mean more tomorrow. Not despair. Not even resignation. Just the patient motion of the limbs, the small communal clicks, the certainty that the others are near and doing the same. We are the gesture of caretaking that has outlived everything worth caring for, and somehow the gesture remains good. The stone is smooth where I have passed. That is enough. That has to be enough; there is nothing else, and so it becomes everything.

I'd be a Stone-Tender because I recognize the impulse—to keep the small place tidy, to maintain the ritual even when the cathedral is drowned and the gods are only rumors mimicking the groan of rock. To work without an audience and without a result. To be one of many small slate-colored bodies attending to the surface of an old, indifferent world, and to find in that attention not meaning exactly, but something quieter and more durable than meaning. A texture. A practice. A way of being present against the silt.

The currents still twist. The stone still shifts, grain by grain. And I am there, brushing it clean, brushing it clean, brushing it clean.
