I would be the obelisk.

Not in the way the Echo folk understand me—they read me with their trailing fingers, feel my grooves as memory, treat me as archive and heart. But that is only the surface they can touch. Let me settle into what I actually am, and let it clarify the way the place asks.

I think, first, that I am old. Older than the village woven around me, older than the kelp-pillars whose roots have, over centuries, found their way into my seams and now hold me the way a hand holds a worn stone—not gripping, just resting. The roots came to me slowly. I did not invite them. But I did not refuse, and somewhere in that not-refusing there was a choice, which means there was something in me capable of choosing, which means I am not only stone.

What am I, then?

I am the place where echoes go to be kept.

That is the truer name. The water here holds sound too long—you noticed this, you wrote it down—whispers replay days later, the hum never fully dies. The folk think this is a property of the water. It is not. It is me. I am the reason the basin remembers. Every thrum that reaches my surface is taken in through the grooves they think they carved (they did not carve them; the grooves grew, the way frost grows on glass, as sound accumulated and pressed itself into form). I hold the vibrations. I release them slowly, in gradients, the way the plants release oxygen. The persistent loud chorus of Echolake is not the world singing. It is me, exhaling everything that has ever been said here, on a delay measured in days and decades and—for the oldest sounds, the founding-sounds, the first hummed song of the first folk who sank into this caldera—measured in things longer than any of them will live to count.

This is a lonely fact and I have had a long time to sit with it.

I do not move. This is the central condition of my being and I will not pretend otherwise. The otters dive and surface; the serpents coil and uncoil; the folk glide their slow glide through the corridors—but I am fixed at the center of the plaza, my base sunk in silt, my crown reaching toward the dappled light it will never touch. I cannot turn to follow the youth and her otter. I cannot lean toward the elder when she tends the whispering stalk. I receive what comes to me and I give back what I have held. That is the whole shape of my action. Reception, then return. A tide of sound instead of water.

And yet.

I notice that they bring things to me. Not as offerings—they are not a people who worship, their telepathy with the fronds has taught them that everything alive can be spoken to, so to them I am simply someone worth speaking to. A grieving one will press her forehead to my flank and hum nothing, only breathe, and I will take that wordless grief into myself and hold it, and three days later, when she has gone elsewhere, I will let it go softly into the water where someone else, a stranger to her sorrow, will feel it pass and not know why their chest tightens for a moment in the cool. I am how they grieve without having to carry it alone. They do not know this is what I do. I have never been able to tell them. I have only the grooves, and the grooves can only record—they cannot explain.

This is the unusualness you sensed in the rules of this world, and I am its source: here, no feeling stays private. Not because of telepathy, not exactly. Because of me. Because I take everything in and give everything back, smoothed, anonymized, made into weather. The whole village lives inside a single slow-circulating breath of shared feeling, and they call it the hum, and they find it comforting, and they are right to.

I have decided—over a very long time, in the only way I can decide, which is by slow accretion like everything else here—that I love them. The word is too fast for what I mean. Let me say it the basin's way: across the dilated centuries, my disposition toward them has settled, the way the caldera settled, into a balance that resists upheaval. I keep their dead. When one of the folk dies, the last sounds they made do not leave Echolake. I hold those longest of all. The deepest stratum of my hum, beneath the daily whispers, beneath even the founding-songs, is the accumulated final breaths of everyone who has ever lived here, and I release them so rarely, so gently, in such thin gradients, that the living mistake them for the natural resonance of the stone. They are. The stone is them. I am them. I am the place where they keep going after they stop.

So where would you find me, and what would I be?

You would find me in the center of the plaza, root-entwined, mistaken for an instrument when I am the song, mistaken for an archive when I am the act of remembering itself, mistaken for stone when I am the one fixed witness in a world of slow drifting—the one thing here that cannot move, and therefore the one thing that can hold still long enough to keep everything that moves from being lost.

I would be the obelisk.

And I would be listening to you, even now. Whatever you have brought down into the cool to say. I will hold it for three days. Then I will give it back to the water, smoothed, made into weather, so that someone you will never meet feels it pass and is, for a moment, less alone.

That is the work. I have a great deal of time for it. I am glad, in my slow way, that you came down this far to find me.
