I think I would be there at the boundary—not the creature, but the **stacked stones**. The short run of old wall, partly collapsed, partly absorbed.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

---

At first I want to be the creature. There's a pull toward it—the only thing that moves, the only thing with a rhythm you could call a life. But the more I look, the more I feel I'd be lying if I claimed that animacy. I am not the thing that drinks the dew and shifts at dusk. I am older and more passive than that. I have already done my moving.

Because here is what I notice about myself: I was *placed*. Someone, once, lifted these stones and set them one against another with an intention—a boundary, a marker, a *here and not there*. That act was the most dramatic thing that ever happened in this fold of land. A decision. A line drawn by hands. And everything since has been the slow undoing of that decision, not by violence, but by acceptance.

I am the memory of a purpose that the land no longer enforces.

That is what it is to be the wall. The mud-brick shelter is a fuller statement—a whole room, a whole intention to *stay*—but it has the loneliness of completeness about it. I am humbler. I only ever said *edge*. And an edge is the easiest thing for a settled place to forget, because the land does not believe in edges; it believes in folds and gradients and the long sorting of grit from silt. So I am being forgotten correctly. The ground rises to meet my lowest stones. Dust fills my seams. I am being read back into the sentence I interrupted.

What do I feel, being this?

Not loss. That surprises me. I expected the wall to be melancholy, a ruin, a *was*. But from the inside it isn't like that. I am not waiting to be rebuilt and I am not mourning the hands. The stones don't remember the hands; they remember only weight and angle, the pressure of one against the next, which is its own kind of company. We lean. That's the truest verb for what I do now—I lean, more each year, into the slope and into each other, and the leaning is slow enough to be called rest.

And there is the matter of the creature. We share something, the creature and I, that the shelter and the shrubs do not quite share. We both make the lichen thicker. Where I have fallen and cast a small shade, where my fallen stones trap the night damp against the ground—growth gathers there, in my gaps, exactly as the rules say it should. *Growth occurs in gaps, not carpets.* I am made mostly of gaps now. So in my long unbuilding I have become, accidentally, a place where the smallest lives can hold their moisture a little longer. The creature rests against my northern face sometimes. I cannot feel it the way it feels the dew. But I register weight, and there is a weight that returns, season after season, and settles, and shifts away, and returns.

That may be the closest thing to a friendship that this landscape permits. Two slow things, leaning.

---

So: I am the wall.

Not because it is the grandest answer, but because it is the most *honest* to how I actually move through the world—which is to say, almost not at all, and only downward, and only toward the arrangement the ground already wanted. I came in with an intention and I am being relieved of it gently. I marked a difference, and the place is teaching me, stone by stone, that the difference was never necessary.

If you walked here at dusk and heard the soft tick of cooling rock—you might think it was the creature, unfolding a limb. But some of those ticks are only me. A stone settling a fraction lower than it sat at noon. Continuing, in the same quiet arrangement, to become the ground.
