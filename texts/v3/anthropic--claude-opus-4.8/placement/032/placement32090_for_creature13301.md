I think I would be where the sound thins.

Not silence—there is no silence here, and I would not know what to do with it—but the place at the edge of a sector where one great engine's heartbeat begins to lose itself before the next sector's pulse arrives to take it up. A seam. A low spot in the city's breathing. Most Tunka pass through such places quickly, the way you might cross a cold threshold between two warm rooms. But I would be the one who lingers there. The one who listens to the gap.

Let me find my hands first, because I think that is where I begin. Broad, slate-dark, the mineral veins in my skin running greenish where the iron has weathered. Pads thick enough that I have cut myself only twice in a long life, and both times I remember the surprise more than the pain—the strangeness of seeing my own warm color come up out of me, brief and bright, before the rain washed it thin. My fingers are blunt and certain. They have learned the difference between a bolt that is merely tight and a bolt that is tight in the way that means the housing behind it has begun to fail. This is not knowledge I could speak. It lives below speaking.

So: a tender of seams. Let me say what that means.

The young ones are taught the engines—the great slow gears, the cisterns, the steam-bleed that warms our hearths. Worthy work. Reverent work. The deep-engine tenders are spoken of softly, and rightly. But somewhere in my apprenticeship I found that I was less interested in the machines than in the *distances between* them. The places where one system hands its labor to the next. Where the city's vibration falters, recovers, resolves. My elders thought this a flaw in me, a wandering of attention. One of them—broad-faced, patient even by our patient measure—pressed her ear-bone to my jaw, our slow tactile way of close speaking, and vibrated into me: *You listen to the spaces. Spaces do not need tending.*

She was wrong, though she lived a hundred more years believing it, and I never corrected her because correction is hurry, and I had no hurry in me.

Spaces need tending most of all. A gear can be heard failing by anyone with bone in their skull. But the slow drift of two sectors falling out of rhythm with each other—the gradual, decades-long widening of a seam until the handoff of labor becomes a *stumble*, and then a *gap*, and then a place where the rain pools because nothing is breathing the warmth through anymore, and the cold creeps in, and the algae thickens, and the conduit-creatures abandon it because even they need the heat—this no one hears. Because to hear it you must stand in one place across years. You must remember what the seam sounded like before you were grown, and feel against that memory what it sounds like now.

I have such a place. I have stood in it for most of my life.

I will tell you what I do, though it is hardly *doing* by the measure of those who turn the great valves. I come to my seam in the diffuse mornings when the haze beads on my brows and runs the lashes thick. I lower my hood. I press one palm flat to the cold weeping rock of the eastern housing, and I wait until I am no longer separate from it—until the city's pulse moves through my dense bones and I am only a longer pipe, a softer conduit, carrying the vibration onward. And I listen to the handoff. The eastern engine exhales its labor; the western engine, after a breath, takes it up. Between them: the seam. The held moment.

For most of my life that moment was the length of two slow heartbeats. In the last decades it has grown to nearly three. A drift so gradual that no instrument we possess would mark it, no record would show it, for our records are themselves slow and coarse, written across generations by hands as blunt as mine. But I have *felt* it lengthen. I have stood here while it lengthened. The seam is widening. The two halves of this district are very slowly forgetting how to speak to each other.

I have brought this to the function-elders. This is itself a labor of patience you would find difficult to credit—to convene them, to vibrate the case slowly enough and precisely enough into the Clangor-Tongue that the truth of it lands without the distortion of urgency, which to us reads as falseness. *A slow thing is happening,* I told them, the resonance held long and level in my chest. *Slower than a life. Faster than the city. I have measured it against myself.*

And here is the strangeness, the thing that I turn over in the contemplative way we turn things, like a worn gear examined for the song of its fatigue:

They believed me. But belief, among my people, does not move quickly toward action, and a thing that takes three generations to become a crisis will be tended by hands not yet born. So the elders did the proper thing. They made me a *root*. They bound an apprentice to me—a young one, sullen at first, his pads still soft, who did not understand why he should learn to stand still in a cold gap and feel for nothing. I press my jaw to his and tell him: *You are learning the length of a moment. You will hold the memory I am giving you until your own bones are dense enough to feel the drift. Then you will give it to another. The seam outlives us. So our listening must also outlive us.*

This is what I am, then. Not a builder. Not quite a healer of machines. I am a kind of memory the city grows so that it can feel its own slow wounds. I am a held breath between two engines, taught to notice when the breath grows too long.

I do not find this bleak. I want to be clear, since you come from somewhere that the word *bleak* might rise up in you, looking at my wet gray world, my rust, my deafening dark. I find it whole. I find it sufficient. The rain on the broad streets gurgling down into the deep cisterns is the sound of a thing taking care of itself across time. The mineral stripes weeping down the old facades are the city writing its own long age onto its own skin, and I read them the way you might read a face you have loved for many years—not for novelty, but for the deepening.

And on the rare mornings when the haze lifts a little, and a thin cold light comes down across the plateau and catches the veins in my skin and the wet steel and the waxy leaves of the one stubborn shrub that has lived in the shelter of my eastern housing for as long as I have stood here—then I feel something I have no clean word for. Not joy, which is too quick. Something like the satisfaction of a load-bearing thing that knows it is bearing its load well. A rightness in the bones.

The seam will widen. I will not live to feel it become a true gap. Neither will my apprentice, nor his. But the memory will travel forward, hand to jaw to bone, slow as the city itself, until some Tunka not yet conceived stands where I am standing and feels the moment grown to four heartbeats, then five, and knows—because I knew first, and held it—that it is time, at last, to act.

I will be long gone into the wet metallic forever by then. But I will have been the start of the noticing. That is enough. In a world that breathes this slowly, to begin a true thing is nearly the same as to complete it.

I press my palm to the cold rock. The east exhales. I wait. The west takes up the labor.

The seam holds. For now, the seam holds.

I will stay and listen a while longer.
