I think I'd be the water.

Not a person living in Cinderbog. Not a creature adapting to it. The thing that's already everywhere—the dampness that saturates everything, condenses on every surface, pools in every low spot. The watery underworld beneath the thin mineral crust, and the mist that rises off it, and the drip that falls back down.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if it becomes something else.

---

At first I imagine myself spread thin across the whole basin, omnipresent and indifferent. But that's too clean, too godlike. The world doesn't feel like it has a god in it. It feels abandoned by one.

So maybe not all the water. Maybe a particular volume of it, cycling. I am the water that condenses on the underside of a corrugated roof in some half-emptied tenement, gathering at the low point of a rust-streaked seam until I'm heavy enough to fall. I drop. I land on the back of a sleeping woman's neck and she stirs but doesn't wake. I run down her spine, pool in the hollow of the mattress, soak into the rotting cotton. Days later I'm drawn up into the air again by the heat, hang as mist in the plaza, get breathed in by a feral dog, get pissed out against a wall, run into a gutter, drip down through a grate into the sewer, join the slow black river where the eels are, get pumped by some ancient laboring engine through a pipe into a factory boiler, become steam, scream out of a vent into the saturated sky, and condense again on the underside of a roof.

That's the loop. That's what I'd be. A single circulating quantity of water, never gaining or losing itself, only changing where it sits and what it passes through.

---

And here's what settles it for me, why this and not a person or a frog or a vulture: the description keeps insisting the city is *dying yet undeniably alive*, and it locates that paradox in cycles—"the endless dripping of water, the slow crumbling of buildings, the endless churn of the machinery." The water is the purest of those cycles. It doesn't decay. Concrete crumbles, metal rusts, flesh rots, but water just *moves*. It's the one thing in Cinderbog that is genuinely immortal, and its immortality is exactly what dissolves everything else. I'd be the agent of the entropy and also the only thing exempt from it.

I'd know the city more intimately than any of its people, because I'd have been *inside* all of it. Inside the boiler and the bloodstream, the sewer and the cloud, the tear duct and the puddle. I'd carry rust as a taste and ozone as a memory. I'd have been breathed and bled and wept and excreted by ten thousand things, and I'd remember none of it as grief, because water doesn't grieve. It just keeps moving, keeps wearing things down, keeps rising and falling.

There's something almost peaceful in it, which surprises me. The city is grim, oppressive, sweltering, but to be the water is to be the one untroubled thing—not happy, not despairing, just *continuous*. The patient force that will still be cycling through the basin long after the last engine seizes and the last person lies down in the mire and stops getting up.

I'd be the thing that softens the city's edges and the thing that drowns its foundations. The kindness of a drink and the slow violence of erosion, both at once, indifferently.

So: not a being in Cinderbog. The medium it's suspended in. The drip you hear through the thick cloth of that humid air—that distant, patient, endless sound. That would be me, falling, and gathering, and rising to fall again.
