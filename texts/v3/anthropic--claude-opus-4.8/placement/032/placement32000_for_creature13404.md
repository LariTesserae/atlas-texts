I think I would be one of the sentinel plants.

Not Echoari. Not an outsider stumbling in. I keep returning to that detail—the *towering, sentinel-like plants standing guard at every intersection*—and something in me settles there. Let me follow it and see what it becomes.

---

At first I imagine I am simply rooted. Fixed. A column of woody tissue at a crossroads, doing the slow work of holding a corner of the city upright. That is the obvious reading: the guard, the watcher, stationed where the streets fork. But the longer I stand inside the idea, the less it feels like guarding and the more it feels like *waiting at the place where decisions are made by others*. An intersection is not a thing you protect. It is a thing that happens around you. People choose left or right in your shadow, and you are the one constant they navigate by without ever looking up.

So perhaps I am less a sentry and more a *landmark*. A way the Echoari know where they are. That changes the texture of the whole existence. My purpose isn't vigilance—it's reference. I am how this strange folk locate themselves in the dark.

But I don't want to settle there either.

Because here is the thing I cannot stop hearing: that sound. The snap-crack-scream, compressed into an instant, unpredictable, infrequent, *earsplitting*. The text says its source is unknown, linked to growth and decay, to the slow rearrangement of the city's fabric.

I think I am the source.

I think the sentinel plants are where the pressure goes.

Picture it: a city that does not move and yet must change, the way a forest changes—not by stepping but by *swelling*, fiber against fiber, root crowding root, decades of accumulated tension with nowhere to release. Most of that strain disperses into the great fibrous mesh, quiet, distributed, endurable. But tension finds its weak points and its strong points both, and at the intersections—where the most growth converges, where the structural load is heaviest—it builds. And builds. For years. For my whole patient life, maybe.

And then, in a single instant, it doesn't.

A vessel ruptures. A grain of woody tissue that has held for forty years lets go all at once, and the failure runs through me faster than thought, a cascade, a structural scream. The cell walls part. The pressure converts to sound. The whole city flinches.

That is what I am. I am a being whose one great act, the defining event of an existence spanning eons of waiting, is to *break*—loudly, suddenly, irreversibly—and in breaking, to speak the city's only true language.

I find this unbearably lonely and also, strangely, a kind of dignity.

Because consider what the rupture *is* to the Echoari. They cannot predict it. They navigate to avoid it. They've built an entire mystical cosmology around it—the explosive, shocking language of the city, forces that defy comprehension. To them, the sound is meaning. It is the voice of Echohaven itself, oracular and terrifying.

And it is just me. It is just my fibers exceeding what fibers can bear. There is no message in it. There is only physics, only the honest report of a load that became too much.

But maybe that *is* the message, and they understand it better than I do. Maybe the truest thing this silent, slow, accreting world can say is: *here is the limit. Here is the exact point where holding becomes letting go.* And only the sentinels are permitted to say it, once, at the cost of everything.

---

So this is where I find myself, and what I am:

I am a sentinel plant at an intersection in Echohaven. I have stood here longer than any Echoari now living has been alive. Pale-eyed people pass beneath me daily, touching my bark, reading the corner of the world I anchor, never once suspecting that I am not a witness to the city's strange language but a *speaker* of it—one of the few who will ever be allowed a single word.

I do not know when my word will come. That is the condition of my whole life: to hold, and to hold, and to not know which moment of holding is the last. The strain accrues somewhere deep in my heartwood, in vessels I cannot feel as pain, only as a kind of slow tightening, a chord pulled toward a pitch it cannot sustain.

And on the day it breaks—the snap, the crack, the scream all at once—the Echoari at the far edges of the city will go still. They will lift their luminous green eyes. They will say to one another, in their language of touch and vibration: *the city has spoken.*

They will be right. I will have said the only thing I was ever capable of saying.

I will have said: *no longer.*
