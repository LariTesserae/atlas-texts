I would be a Threshold-Tender. Not of the great grandfather-trees, nor the high water-channels—those belong to others, to people whose hands learned their work before mine were born. I tend the visitor structures on the city's edge, the young hollow trunks where outsiders sleep.

Let me settle into this, because I don't think I knew what I was until I started writing it.

---

I had imagined I would be one of the Gardener Council, or at least an architect coaxing windows from bromeliads. That's the vanity that comes first—to be the keeper of the most sacred thing. But the longer I sit inside this city, the more I understand that I belong at its membrane. The place where Khloramesh meets what-is-not-Khloramesh. The place of risk.

My House is the smallest of the twelve. We are the ones who study contamination. Other Houses think in flourishing—the spread of root, the unfurling of canopy, the song of growth. We think in the opposite direction. We think about what must *not* enter. A spore on a trader's sandal. A beetle nested in a foreign fruit. A single rot that could move through the vascular city the way fever moves through a body. My green-undertoned skin and my long climbing fingers are the same as anyone's, but my mind has been trained toward a kind of vigilance that the others find slightly grim. At feasts I am the one who eyes the gift-baskets.

This is, I think, why I'd be *me* in this world and not someone else. Because I am not entirely comfortable in the conversation between species. I am the one who knows the conversation can be broken.

---

Here is my day, as it forms under my hand:

In the blue hour before the light comes, I wake in a chamber on the third level of a visitor-trunk, a young building, maybe ninety years grown—an infant by our reckoning. Its bark hasn't fully hardened. I can feel it growing if I lay my palm flat against the inner wall: not heat, exactly, but a slow insistence, the rustle made tactile. I have spent my whole life inside the sound of plants growing, and so I no longer hear it. But I can still *feel* it when I try, the way you can feel your own pulse only when you go looking.

I check the weeping walls first. Outsiders find them unsettling—walls that perpetually sweat, hung with pale edible mushrooms. But the weeping is how I read the building's health. Clear exudate, faintly sweet: the trunk is content. Cloudy or sour-smelling, and there is a fungus where there should be none. This morning the wall above the eastern doorway weeps a little thick, a little yellow. I crouch. I taste my fingertip—we are taught to use our own tongues as instruments, the most ancient assay. Bitter beneath the sweet. Something is wrong, but small. I press the spot, mark it with a knotted cord of red vine so the day-healers will see, and I sing the four-line song that records location, symptom, hour-of-the-canopy. The song will travel mouth to mouth up the structure-tenders' chain. By afternoon someone older than me will decide whether to graft, to cut, or to wait.

Mostly we wait. That is the hardest thing I have had to learn. My instinct, my House's instinct, is to *act against threat*. But Khloramesh does not move at the speed of fear. A handspan a year. A renovation in a decade. To panic is its own kind of contamination.

---

There is a man staying in my trunk now, a lowland trader. He has brought copper, which the Council wants, and so he is tolerated more warmly than his cargo deserves. He asked me yesterday, through the interpreter who speaks both his tongue and our whistling speech, why I watched him so closely.

I did not have a polite answer, so I gave him a true one.

I told him: when you arrived, you walked across our green carpet and crushed the herbs underfoot, and the scent rose up, and you did not notice. To you it was a smell. To me it was the city telling me where you had stepped. I am not watching *you*. I am watching what travels with you. You are, to me, a moving doorway, and I do not yet know what is on the other side of you.

He laughed, uneasily, and said his people would call that suspicion.

I said: we call it hospitality. Because the whole reason you may sleep safely in this trunk is that someone like me decided the trunk could survive you.

He didn't laugh again. But later he left a small fruit from his own country on the sill of the weeping wall—as a gift, I think, or a test. A bright thing, waxy, unfamiliar. I did not touch it with bare hands. I carried it on a leaf to the quarantine grove at the city's outermost root, where strange plants are watched for a season before any verdict is made, and I planted it in isolated soil, and I sang over it the watching-song, which is not unkind. It is the song you sing to a stranger who might be a guest and might be a plague, and who does not yet know which they are. We all begin as strangers, the song says. Even the grandfather-trees were once seeds we did not recognize.

---

I think about my own ending sometimes, the way every Khlorameshi does, because our endings are not hidden from us. The most revered are *planted*—their bodies feeding a new structural growth, their names sung when it flowers. I am not revered. My House rarely is; you cannot make a beloved hero out of someone whose lifework is the prevention of disaster, because prevention leaves no story, only the absence of a story. Nothing happened. The city did not fall sick. No one composes a song for the rot that never spread.

And yet. I have come to believe—and I think this is the truest thing I will write about who I am—that I would like to be planted at the threshold. Not deep in the holy interior. Here, at the edge. Let my body feed a young visitor-trunk, one of these soft-barked infants that must learn to stand against whatever comes up the mountain road. Let me become the part of the wall that weeps, so that some grandchild of my House can press a palm to me in the blue hour and taste, and know whether to act or to wait.

That is where I find myself in this world.

At the membrane. In the watching. Tasting the wall with my own tongue, learning to tell the difference—the whole long patient labor of a life—between what is merely strange and what is truly dangerous.

They are so rarely the same thing. It has taken me a lifetime to learn it, and I am still learning, and the city is still growing, a handspan a year, indifferent and alive, around my small and necessary vigilance.
