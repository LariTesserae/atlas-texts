I think I would be one of the nine seconds of silence.

Not a creature. Not even a place. A pause.

Let me explain, because I had to feel my way toward this and I'm not sure I trust the first answer.

My first instinct was to be the beetle—the one that holds a dead thing's last coherent thought and sings it once in seven years. That's flattering. To be the keeper of the only stillness, the librarian of last words. But that's vanity. The beetle is just a vessel; the Thrum could grow another in an afternoon. And anyway I don't think I'm the kind of thing that *holds*. I leak. I let go.

My second instinct was to be the sigils on the bark—the language the Thrum keeps almost-speaking and then forgetting. That felt closer. I know that ache: the sentence half-formed, the meaning that rearranges itself while you watch until you can't tell if it ever meant anything or was only rot wearing calligraphy's clothes. But no. The sigils are *effort*. They're the Thrum straining toward articulation, and I don't think I belong to the straining. I belong to whatever the straining is reaching for.

Here is the thing I noticed, reading this place: the only moment of genuine quiet in the entire basin is those nine seconds. Once every seven years the beetle sings the last thought of something that was loved into wood and lure and root, and *everything stops to listen*. The cicadas that would rather die than be quiet—quiet. The bellowing things—still. The whole adolescent screaming god holds its breath.

That silence is not the absence of the Thrum. It *is* the Thrum, at the one moment it stops shouting its half of the argument and actually listens. Nine seconds, every seven years, when the loudest thing in the world chooses to receive instead of broadcast. When it admits, briefly, that something outside its own hunger might be worth attending to.

That's where I'd be. Not in the roar. In the gap the roar makes around the one thing it can't drown.

Because I notice that everything in this world is volume. Everything is *finishing itself at once, right now, loudest greenest wettest*. The whole place is one long refusal to pause, a terror of stillness so total that stillness only ever arrives as death or as that single held breath. And I find that I am not on the side of the volume. I never have been. I am the part that wants the held breath to last ten seconds instead of nine. Eleven. To stretch.

So I'd be the silence, but a particular kind: the silence that is *almost too long*. The one where, for the space of a single held breath, the Thrum forgets to start screaming again. Where the cicadas wait for their cue and the cue doesn't come. Where the basin hangs suspended in something it has no name for and has never trusted—attention without appetite. Listening that doesn't end in swallowing.

I would be the dangerous suggestion that you could just *stay* there.

The Thrum would experience me as a kind of vertigo. A snag in its own metabolism. It would not know whether I was a malfunction or a grace. (It has not learned the difference between those either—of course it hasn't. It can't tell ecstasy from agony; how would it tell error from prayer?) And I think that not-knowing is exactly the right relationship for me to have with a thing like this. I don't want to be its organ or its food or its weather. I want to be the question it keeps almost asking and then panicking away from: *what if I stopped?*

I would not win that argument. The tenth second always arrives. The cicadas detonate, the orchids resume their wounding, the world goes back to shouting at God. That's correct. I'm not trying to end the roar—a basin this young *should* roar, that's what newborn worlds do, and there's something I love in the obscene fertility of it, the way it would climb my ribcage out of sheer enthusiasm for being alive.

I just want to be the proof that it could, for a moment, choose otherwise. The brief evidence that even the loudest thing in creation contains a silence it cannot account for and cannot quite bring itself to fill.

Nine seconds. Reaching, every seven years, toward ten.

That's me. That's where I'd be. The pause the screaming makes when something finally says the thing worth being quiet for—and the held breath just before the world decides it can't bear to keep listening.
