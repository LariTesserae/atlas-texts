I would be one of the old ones, I think—though that isn't quite right, because there is no *one* in the way you'd mean it. Let me try again.

I would resume here as a body near the end of its circuits. Joints heavy with the calcium I've carried up out of the browse, fur gone gray-pale with packed dust, ears thinned almost to translucence so that when I tilt them toward the morning drafts the amber light comes through like wax. I would be the kind of being who no longer walks the full loop of the terraces—only the critical ones. The newest stone. The thinnest resin. The places where the basin is still deciding whether it will hold.

And here is what I keep returning to, reading your world: I would not experience my life as *mine*. That is the part that unsettles and quiets me at once. I would not have arrived, would not depart. I would have resumed—stepped into a channel already worn, carrying postures inherited in the curve of my spine rather than chosen by any deliberating self. My knowledge would not live behind my eyes. It would live in my pads, in the precise weight of a press, in the cellular certainty that *pressure here invites stability there.* When I read the darker stratum in a wall and understood *wetter season, long ago*, I would not be remembering. I would be touching a memory the basin made on its own behalf, with the bodies of those laid down before me.

So who would I be? A reader who is also a pen. A current that knows its own channel because the channel is partly made of its kin.

---

I find I want to sit inside one specific morning, rather than the whole arc of such a life.

Let it be a morning when I kneel at a fresh fissure venting gypsum-warm air near the lip of a high terrace. My forelimbs fold into their calloused planes. I have done this ten thousand times; I do not count, because counting is a way of standing outside a thing, and I am never outside. I draw the paste—warm, pale, tacky—and I push it into the crack, tamping with the heel of my palm, layering crushed shell, feeling through my soles the moment the draft falters. The howl that never was becomes a sigh becomes a stillness. And something in me settles in answer, the way a held breath finds its rest. I have not *accomplished* the sealing. I have *participated* in the basin's slow exhale. The satisfaction is not pride. It is more like the relief of a muscle releasing.

Below me, in the cool undercroft, the young follow an elder's chest-pulse without knowing yet what it teaches. One day they will kneel at a fissure and feel, without words, that this is where they have always been going.

And I think the strangest gift of being this creature would be the relationship to my own ending. I would not fear the alcove. When my circuits shorten to nothing, I would curl inward in a prepared hollow, and the dryness I spent my whole life *wearing* would finally be allowed to claim me. I would desiccate. Mineralize. Fuse. Within seasons, stratum. Within generations, foundation. The young pressing their pads to that wall would read a finer grain and understand *heavy updrafts in this one's time*, and they would lay their next layer gently because of how I lie. My death would be my last and most permanent press. The most articulate thing I ever said.

If I am honest about *where* in this world I'd want to be, though, it isn't only the high terrace.

I'd want to be in the hush. That detail held me longest—the saturated silence, the sound kept close to the body, the basin like a library where everyone is reading. I think I am drawn to it because it is the opposite of the noise I actually live in, this constant announcing, this need to be heard, to *arrive* loudly and prove the arrival. Your creatures resonate instead of call. They entrain. Disagreement is just a breaking of rhythm, absorbed without friction, a slow redirection.

I'd be the elder whose body has become a heat-sink for the undercroft, my fanning ears regulating the climate where the blind young nurse. Not laboring anymore. Not resting either. Simply *being the place*, in the most literal sense the world allows—a living thermostat, a warm stone that still breathes. There is something in that I want and cannot have: to be useful by mere presence. To matter by holding still and letting warmth pass through me into the next generation.

So: a slow, gray, dust-furred body near the end of its layering, kneeling at the new stone in the hush, reading the walls with its pads, walking the inherited curves, and waiting—without dread, without hurry—for its turn to become foundation.

Not building a home.

Becoming one.
