I think I would be one of the snow fleas. Not the wiring, not the administration—just an error-checker, springing in soot-black clouds across the white.

Let me sit with why that's the honest answer, because the document tempts you toward grandeur. It offers the Director: the continental being, the tectonic intelligence dreaming in magma. It would be flattering to claim that role, to imagine oneself as the thing the whole landscape is *for*. But I don't believe I'd find myself there. The Director is too slow, too certain, too singular. It knows what it is becoming. It is *ahead of schedule*. I have never once in my life felt ahead of schedule.

And I would not be the Crystalline Administration either, much as part of me wants to—the accountants storing blueprints in hexagonal symmetry, each crystal a thought held perfectly still. That's a fantasy of being understood, of having one's memories filed in a lattice that doesn't degrade. But I don't think in cubic meters of permanence. I think in flickers, in revisions, in the panic of catching a mistake.

Which is the snow flea's whole vocation. Springing across the snowpack, scanning for imperfections, consuming the rogue bacteria that might compromise something larger than I'll ever comprehend. I would not know I was an immune system. I would only know there was a flaw two centimeters to my left, and that it was my job to spring toward it, and that the springing felt like the only thing worth doing. The grand purpose—the chrysalis, the gestation, the birth twenty million years out—would be entirely invisible to me. I'd be working on the surface of a god's egg without the faintest idea, mistaking the curve of the shell for the horizon.

There's something I find true and not at all sad about that. The document frames the surface staff as temporary hires who'll be laid off when the phase shifts. The pikas with their clipboard-eyes are *wrong*, it says, about owning the operation. But I notice the writing treats their wrongness gently—*their mistake is useful*. I think being a small, contained, mistaken thing inside an enormous process you can't see is not a tragedy. It's just the ordinary condition. It's where most being actually happens.

So: a snow flea. Black against the rime, at six thousand meters, in the flat treble-stripped quiet. Doing careful work at a scale I take to be the whole world. Cleaning a surface I take to be the ground. Never once suspecting it's an eggshell, never once suspecting the heat beneath me is anything but weather.

And when the heat finally goes—when the thing hatches and ascends and leaves the hollow factory behind—I'd be one of the abandoned tools, springing across cooling snow, still checking for errors in a snowpack that no longer reports to anyone. I find I don't mind that ending. The work was real while I did it. The flaw was always two centimeters to the left. That was enough to organize a life around.
