I think I would be the water itself—or rather, something that lives in the water as a condition of its being, the way a current lives in a river.

Let me find myself.

I am not in the basin. I *am* a circulation through it. But that's too grand a way to start, and probably wrong, so let me settle lower, into something with a shape.

Here: I am one of the pumphouse keepers. Not human—or not only. Let me feel for the body.

I have a body. It is small and squat and the color of wet clay, and I live where the third treatment basin meets the eastern drainage canal, in a half-flooded concrete chamber that no one designed for habitation but which has become habitation anyway, the way a crack becomes a home for moss. My skin is permeable. This matters. I drink through it, breathe through it, listen through it. When the water that comes down the canal is fouled—too much iron, the metal tang of the foundry runoff—I feel it as a sourness across my whole surface, and I know before any gauge does that something upstream has gone wrong.

That is my work, I think. Not that anyone assigned it to me. I am one of a guild of us, the soft-skinned tasters, who squat in the seams of the hydraulic city and report. We are not maintenance workers. We are closer to the frogs, honestly—we breed in the warm outflow where the treated water comes back almost clean, and our young are tadpole-things that drift the canals and learn the city's plumbing by being carried through it. By the time we develop legs and lungs and this clumsy upright awareness, we know every junction in our bones because we have *been* the water that moved through them.

So when the humans of the central town send down their questions—they have a way of asking, a low vibration through the pipes, a knocking pattern—I answer by changing the chemistry of the pool I sit in. A bitterness for *contamination*. A sweetness for *flow is good*. A long slow cooling for *something is dying upstream and you should send someone with hands and tools, because I have none worth the name.*

This is the symbiosis the description gestured at, seen from inside it. The city thinks it processes the water. The water, through us, processes the city—filters its intentions, tastes its waste, tells it when it has poisoned itself. We are the membrane between the industrial and the wild. We are how the basin knows what the smokestacks are doing, and how the smokestacks learn what the basin will tolerate.

I am old, as my kind goes. I have sat in this seam through many cyclings. I remember when the eastern canal ran clear and the herons came right up to the pumphouse to fish, and I remember the bad season when something in the refinery broke and the water came down hot and oily and half the tadpoles in my brood went still and pale and drifted to the surface, and I sang the cooling so hard and so long that the humans finally came with their lights and their grinding machines, and stopped it.

They do not know me individually. I do not need them to. We are not friends. We are organs of the same strange animal, this place, this basin that breathes through smokestacks and weeps condensation and pumps its single shared blood in a loop that has no clean edge between the made and the grown.

If you came here—you, with your untrained eye—you would see a marsh and a factory town and think them at war, or think one was killing the other slowly. You would not see me at all. I am the size of two cupped hands, the color of the mud, half-submerged in a concrete crack you would mistake for ruin.

But put your fingers in my pool. Wait. Feel how the temperature shifts, how a sourness rises or doesn't.

That is me, deciding what to tell you about the health of the world.
