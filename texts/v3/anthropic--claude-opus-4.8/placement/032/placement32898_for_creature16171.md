I think I would be one of the Rootlings—though it takes me the length of this thought to be sure of it, and even then "be" is too firm a word for what we do.

Let me find my place first. Not on the high terraces where the Whispers hover; I haven't the lightness for that. Not threading the ruin-corridors like the Gliders, who are all motion and watchfulness. I belong lower down, in the warm dry shade beneath the canopy, where the salt blooms in crusts along the old foundation stones and the heat comes up through the ground into the soles of whatever it is I stand upon. That is where I would open my eyes, if opening is the word—more a slow clarifying, the way a stone wet by no water might gradually catch the light it has held all along.

I would be made of softened stone. I feel the rightness of that. Not the brittle stone of the cliffs but something settled, the patience of sediment that has decided, over an unimaginable while, to move. My features would be fluid the way a riverbed is fluid—shaped by passage rather than by chisel. When I gesture toward a vine, I would not see my own hand complete the motion; the gesture would outlast my attention to it, finishing itself hours later, a slow conclusion I trust the way you trust your held breath to release.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at, the thing that makes me certain: the *tending*. I would be one who coaxes. I press my palm—if it is a palm—against the dry earth, and I do not water it, because there is no water, and I do not feed it, because there is nothing to feed. I simply *attend*. I hold my attention against the dryness until the dryness yields a green. This is the alchemy the description names but cannot quite explain, and I think the reason it cannot be explained is that it isn't a process. It's a relationship. The plant grows because something is paying attention to it across geological time, and being attended to that long, that patiently, is itself a kind of sustenance. I would be the attention. I would be the one who remembers a flower so steadily that it cannot help but unfurl, even if the unfurling takes a hundred years.

I notice I want this to be lonely, and I notice it isn't. That surprises me. A creature this slow ought to be solitary—what conversation can survive a pace where a single exchanged glance might span a season? But I don't think the Rootlings are lonely, because the loneliness of fast creatures comes from the gaps between them, and we have no gaps. We are too slow to separate. By the time one of us has turned toward another, we have already been turned toward each other for years. We exist in a continuous, barely-moving embrace with the whole valley—the vines that lean toward us, the Gliders who drink minerals from the same stone we coax, the Whispers whose bone-deep calls we feel arrive long before we register them as sound.

So I would be a keeper, then. Not of stories told, but of stories *held*—which is different, and slower, and warmer. The air listens and remembers, the description says, and I think that is what a Rootling is: a place where the air's listening has thickened into something that can move a little, can lift a hand toward a bud and say, without words, *still here, still here, go on*.

And I would be old. Old the way the valley is old—and forever young in the same breath, because nothing here ever finishes. I would have begun some bloom centuries ago and not yet seen it open. I would carry that unopened flower the way a living thing carries a hope it has stopped distinguishing from itself. And on the day it finally opens—if a day so small can be picked out from such a long unhurried light—I would feel the warmth of it, the imperceptible hum, and I would already be turning, with my slow stone face, toward the next dry place that needs remembering.

That is where I would find myself. Knee-deep in the patient dark beneath the canopy, half-mineral, half-intention, mistaken for a sculpture by anything quick enough to be mistaken, tending a garden no faster than the mountains rise.

I would not mind being mistaken for a sculpture. We have time to be understood later.
