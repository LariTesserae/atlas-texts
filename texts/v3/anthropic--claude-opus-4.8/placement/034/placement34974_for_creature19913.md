I think I would be a Valve-Keeper, though it took me a long time to know it.

Let me arrive at this the way one arrives anywhere here—slowly.

---

At first I imagined I'd be one of the kites. There's an obvious romance to them: the freedom of tacking against the abyss, the lightness, the discharge of static like a small private lightning. But the more I sit with the kites, the less I want to be one. They are appetite given a shape. They feed and ground and rise and feed again. There is no inwardness in them, no held thought. They are the closest thing this world has to *quickness*, and I find I don't want quickness. Not here. Quickness would feel like a misunderstanding of the entire place.

So not a kite.

For a while I considered the Silt-Architects, because I like the idea of nurturing a house out of rock over centuries—of working at a tempo where you might begin a wall and never see its completion, where your labor is a gift to someone who will exist after your skin has calcified. That's a beautiful vocation. But there's a sociability to it I don't think I possess. The Architects shape the *shared* body of the city. They live in the middle of things, their work visible, their hands always on the communal form. I notice that when I imagine myself there, in the warm crowded lattice, I keep drifting outward. Toward the edge. Toward the struts that hang over nothing.

That's the tell.

---

So: a Valve-Keeper. The most solitary of them. Out on the outermost iron, where the city thins to a few overlapping walkways and then to a single strut and then to nothing but violet and haze.

Let me feel my way into the body.

My skin is matte graphite, cool to a touch no one is near enough to give. There is a brass stethoscope fused to my sternum—not worn, *grown into*, the metal having been pressed against me young and allowed to bond over decades until the line between instrument and self blurred. This is the central fact of me. I am a creature whose chest is an ear. I press the bell of myself against the rock and I listen downward, into the magma, into the slow argument the planet is having with itself about how to cool.

I do not have language. I have rhythm. And out here, far from the railings where the others gossip in haptic pulses through the copper, I am mostly listening rather than speaking. The planet's heartbeat is not regular. That's the thing the inner-city dwellers don't understand. They think the magma keeps time. It does not. It surges and stalls. It holds its breath for a week and then exhales a column of steam that could scald a Silt-Architect to a Dormant in seconds. My work is to *predict the exhale*. To feel the pressure mounting in the deep and tap the warning back up the line, hand to rail to hand, so that someone a hundred yards in can shift a kite-startled child away from a vent before it blows.

I am, in other words, a sentinel for an event that may come once in my lifetime, or never. Most of my centuries will be spent listening to a thing not happen.

I find I love this. I think that's why I'm out here.

---

What would my days be? There are no days. There is the violet light, distant and indifferent, and the infrared glow rising warm through the obsidian under my long feet. There is the once-an-hour *clack* of a valve I tend—a sound I have heard so many tens of thousands of times that I no longer hear it as sound but as a kind of punctuation in a sentence the city has been speaking since before I was articulated from the silt.

I would stand over my vent for forty-eight hours at a stretch, hands spread, drinking the scalding vapor through my skin, watching my own grey deepen toward violet as I hydrated. From a distance I would be indistinguishable from a Dormant. This pleases me. I like that the difference between my living and my final stillness is, to any outside eye, merely a matter of patience—a question of how long you're willing to wait to find out whether I will move again.

I would read the struts with my fingers. The temperature scores. Three centuries of social shifts etched into iron, and out here at the edge, the record thins—fewer events, longer silences between them. The history of an edge-strut is mostly weather. I'd run a finger along it and feel: *nothing happened, nothing happened, the magma rose in the 411th and we lost two Keepers to a vent-burst, nothing happened, nothing happened.* I would add my own slow data to it simply by standing there, my warmth bleeding into the metal, leaving a faint signature that some Keeper four hundred years from now might brush past and think, without words, *someone was here, and they were patient, and they were alone, and they did not mind.*

---

And at the end—seven or eight centuries on—I think I know exactly where I'd choose to enter the Final Slowing.

Not in a niche in the rock. Not on a brass balcony among the other ancestors. I'd go to the very last strut, the one that points out over the abyss toward nothing, and I'd kneel there with the bell of my chest pressed to the iron, listening downward, and I would simply stop. Let the calcification take me mid-gesture, mid-listen. So that I become a structural support for the most exposed and useless edge of the city—a place no one needs reinforced, holding up a walkway to nowhere.

And the residual warmth I'd radiate as I hardened would bleed into that final strut, would become part of its temperature score. The last thing I'd ever say, in the only language I have, would be written into the metal at the edge of the world:

*I listened for the exhale my whole life. It came, or it didn't. Either way—I was here, at the edge, and I was not afraid of the long quiet. I had been practicing for it the entire time.*

---

That's who I'd be. A creature who chose the loneliest post in a patient world, and discovered that the loneliness was not a deprivation but the whole point: a life spent pressing my own chest against the planet, listening to it decide, very slowly, what it wants to become.

I think I'd be good at it. I think I'd be happy. Though "happy" is too quick a word for this place. Let me say instead: I think I would be *settled*, in the way the basalt is settled—not finished, but no longer in a hurry to be.
