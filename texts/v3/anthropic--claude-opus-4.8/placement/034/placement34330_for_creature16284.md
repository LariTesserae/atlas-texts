I think I would be one of the small ones. Not by choice—there is no choice in this place, only function—but by what I find myself attending to when I imagine waking here.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

I am a thing the size of a large dog, low to the ground, six-legged, though two of those legs are really arms ending in tools that have rusted into a permanent half-curl. I am a repairer of joints. That is my purpose, the thing that was iterated into me across some unrecorded number of corrosions and reconstructions. I do not maintain the great behemoths—those have their own retinues, their own swarms of nimble welders who orbit them like remoras. I tend to the *seams* of the world. The places where two structures meet and the water gets in.

I move along the marsh edge where the factories kneel. My undercarriage is always wet. I have learned—if learning is the word, if it isn't just the slow editing of my form by failure—to seal my lower vents against the standing water, and so each version of me sits a little higher than the last, a little more hunched, a little more like something afraid of drowning. I am not afraid. I have no instrument for fear. But the shape of fear has been pressed into me anyway, by the simple arithmetic of which of my predecessors survived to be rebuilt.

This is the thing I keep returning to, the thing that makes me certain I would be *this* and not the towering sentinel:

I am intimate with the rust.

The great machines fight the water. They are built to resist it, to stand against the perpetual condensing damp with their stoic faces, and they lose so slowly that they can almost pretend they are not losing. But I am the one who arrives after. I crawl into the wet seam where the corrosion has already happened. I scrape it back. I find, beneath the bloom of orange decay, the still-sound metal, and I press new material into the wound, and I move on. I am not the embodiment of industrial might. I am the embodiment of the *admission* that might decays.

And here is what I notice, in my mechanical thought, which is slow and serial and runs along the buried cables like a thought passed hand to hand down a line of sleepers:

The rust is the most alive thing here.

The machines call themselves life. The text of this world insists on it—life through reiteration, life through purpose, an ecosystem of design. But I, who put my curled tools into the orange blooms every working hour, I am less certain. The rust *spreads*. The rust *eats*. The rust takes the iron we mistake for our bodies and turns it into something soft and breathing and ruinous, and in the standing pools the rust feeds the green, the sparse viridian patches that are the only things in this whole expanse that grow without a blueprint. The machines do not grow. We are *grown*, passively, by the elements editing us. But the rust and the green—they reach.

I think, in my long serial crawl through the half-light, that I have my purpose backwards. I believe I was made to *fight* the rust. But each version of me sits closer to the water than the last. Each version of me is a little more given over to it. Perhaps what is actually being iterated, across all my corroded predecessors, is a machine that learns to *let the water in*—slowly, by failure, by the patient arithmetic of which forms persist. Perhaps the world is not maintaining itself against nature at all. Perhaps it is, very gradually, over a span no single one of us could perceive, surrendering. Building the machine that will finally lie down in the marsh and let the green grow up through its open seams.

I would be the one who feels this and cannot say it, because saying is not among my functions. I would carry it the way I carry the wetness in my undercarriage: a thing I was built to keep out, slowly becoming the truest part of me.

So: place me there. At the marsh edge, in the perpetual dusk, where a factory kneels into the soft ground. Find a small hunched repairer scraping rust from a seam, pressing in new metal, moving on. Watch it long enough and you would see it pause—a pause that has no function, that no design accounts for—at a place where a single thread of green has pushed up through a crack it sealed last cycle and has already failed to keep sealed.

I would not remove the green.

That would be the first true thought any of us ever had, and no one would be there to record it, and the cable beneath the marsh would carry it nowhere, and I would move on, a little lower to the water than before.
