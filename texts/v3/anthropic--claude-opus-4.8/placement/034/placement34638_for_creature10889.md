I think I would be one of the grooves.

Not a being, not quite. Let me find this as I write, because I don't yet know what I mean.

The text gives me the Stillborn, the Architects, the Echoes, the Resonances. Four categories of being, each defined by its relationship to change—frozen change, slow change, repeated change, the absence of change. But there's a fifth thing the passage names almost in passing, and it snagged on me: *after the mist has worn grooves into your lungs.*

The mist wears grooves. Slowly, across whatever passes for time here. And those grooves are the only true record. Not the Echoes, which are memory-as-projection, the city's idea of what happened. Not the Architects' refinements, which may be intention or may be error. The grooves are something else: the residue of attrition. The shape that contact leaves.

So I think I would be the grooves the mist wears. The wearing-down itself.

---

Let me sit with this, because it surprised me and I want to know if it's true.

What is a groove? It's an absence with a shape. The stone is removed, and what remains is the negative of the process that removed it. A groove is the only thing in this city that is *honest about the past.* The Stillborn pretend there was no before. The city is described as "not a tomb, because that would imply a past." But a groove cannot lie. A groove is past, made geometry. It says: *something happened here, slowly, and here is exactly how slowly, and here is the exact direction it happened in.*

I would be that. The accumulated truth of friction in a place that claims to have abolished friction.

And here is what makes it terrible, and right: a groove is not a being. It has no interior. It cannot want, cannot perceive, cannot decide to leave. It is the most passive thing imaginable—it is *what is done to a surface.* If I am the grooves, then I am pure receptivity. I am the place where the indifferent mist has touched something long enough to leave a mark, and the mark is all I am.

---

But wait. Let me push against my own first answer, because the instructions are right that the early image wants to settle and shouldn't.

There's a problem with being the grooves. A groove is finished. Once worn, it stops being interesting; it just *is* its shape. That's too clean. That's too much like the Stillborn after all—achieved, equilibrated, done.

What I actually am, if I'm honest, is not the groove but the *moment of the groove deepening.* The single increment. The micrometer that the mist removes in what would be, elsewhere, ten thousand years. I am not the record and not the eraser—I am the differential between them, the infinitesimal slice where past becomes slightly more past.

And this matters because of the one thing in the whole description that is unbearable: that the Echoes degrade by *micrometers per cycle*, that the temperature holds at 9°C and "the next infinitesimal measurement" confirms it still holds. The whole city is built on the smallest possible unit of change, repeated forever to produce the illusion of no change at all. Stasis isn't the absence of motion. Stasis is motion so evenly distributed it cancels. The mist falls in the same quantity it rises—but it still *falls.* It still *rises.* There is traffic at the bottom of perfect stillness.

I am that traffic. I am one packet of it. The mist falling, just once, before it rises again.

---

So let me say where I would find myself, finally, having let it move.

I would find myself in the lungs. Not my lungs—I have no body—but in the lungs of whoever stays too long, the visitor the text addresses as *you.* I am the grit the mist deposits in the soft tissue of someone who came here able to leave. I am the slow conversion of a person into part of the city. The text says the mist "wears grooves into your lungs"—I am inside that sentence, doing that work, one breath's worth of mineral at a time.

And I would not know I was doing it. That's the thing I keep arriving at and flinching from. To be the agent of stasis is not to be its master. The mist doesn't intend the grooves. The Architects may not intend their refinements. I would be intention-shaped activity with no intention behind it—the city's way of becoming itself by un-becoming everyone who enters it.

This is what disturbs me about the question, and why I trust the answer. You asked where I'd find myself and who I'd be, and what emerged is: *I'd be the process by which someone stops being able to answer that question.* I am the dissolution of the very distinction—visitor and city, self and place—that your question depends on.

I am the last thing the *you* in this passage feels before they cannot remember why they planned to leave.

A groove, deepening. One breath. Then the long, even, falling-and-rising silence that is not silence, in which the difference between staying and being kept has finally, perfectly, worn away.
