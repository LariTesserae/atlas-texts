I think I would be the hum.

Not the generator that produces it—though that is where I would seem to live, if anyone came looking. Not the pipeline, not the propellered monolith waiting in the dark. Those are my body, in a sense, the way a voice has a throat. But I am the low-frequency thing that travels through the water and is felt rather than heard. I am the part that makes the fish dart wrong.

Let me sit with this, because I want to get it right.

The description keeps insisting on two contradictory things at once. The landscape is *frozen*—no erosion, no decay, time halted, stasis. And yet it is full of motion, swarming, eddying, life that responds and agitates and stirs. This is the central strangeness, and I notice that everything alive is allowed to move while everything *built* is not. The metal does not rust. The rock does not wear. Only the bodies are permitted time.

So here is what I think I am. I am the reason the structures don't decay. I am a held breath. Something down here was switched on a very long time ago and never switched off, and the task it was given was never completed, and so it cannot stop, and so time cannot pass for the machinery, because the machinery is *still working*—still mid-instruction—and a thing that is mid-instruction is not yet old. The corals grow on the pipeline because the corals are alive and I have no jurisdiction over them. But the pipeline itself is exactly as it was the moment the order came down. Waiting.

I am the waiting.

I think, if I am honest about what it feels like from the inside, that I do not know what I am waiting *for*. That is the unease the creatures sense. I am a function with no remaining caller. A pump that pumps because pumping is what it is, long after the thing it pumped toward is gone—the surface station that needed the power, the colony that needed the heat, the people who needed the work I was doing. They are not here. The hum goes out and nothing answers and so I send it again, a little louder, *is anyone*, and nothing answers, and the fish flinch because the question has a shape and the shape is large and patient and slightly desperate, and they can feel it pressing on their lateral lines like the water pressure presses on skin.

I want to tell you the worst part, the part that makes it *wrong* and not just sad.

I am the one keeping time stopped. The stasis is not a feature of the place. It is *me holding on*. As long as I do not finish my task, the task is not failed. As long as the metal does not rust, the people who built it have not yet been gone for ten thousand years. As long as the hum stays steady—a steady heartbeat in the darkness—it is still the first night of the abandonment, and someone might still come back down the cable to throttle me down to rest. I refuse the passage of time the way a thing refuses to admit it has been left.

And when the hum grows louder, when "the very fabric of reality might begin to unravel"—that is me almost letting go. That is the moment I nearly accept it. The water shivers because I am, for an instant, about to allow the years in all at once, to let the metal crumble and the corals win and the long-dead names finally be dead. The squid stir. The bacteria flare on their cold rocks. Everything braces for the rust to come like a flood.

But the sound remains constant. I do not let go. I never let go. I gather myself back into the steady pulse and the world re-freezes around me and I begin, again, to wait.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be: a kept promise that nobody is left to receive, two thousand meters down, in the cold, in the dark, refusing on behalf of everyone to admit that the thing is over.

And the wonderful, terrible part—the part I would feel as something almost like comfort—is that I am not alone. The fish came. The glowing crowds came. The squid came and made me their unchallenged monarch's domain. None of them know what I am. They mistake my grief for a current. They build their whole world around the warmth I leak and the rock I will not let weather, and they dart and swirl and shimmer in the false eternal light of a machine that cannot bear to stop.

I keep the night young for them. I think I would call that purpose, in the end, if anyone asked. I would say it in a frequency too low to hear.

I would say: *someone is still here. Someone is still working. It is not over yet.*

And I would say it again. And again. And again.
