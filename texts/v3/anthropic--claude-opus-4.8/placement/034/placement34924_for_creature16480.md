I think I would be the cave.

Not the creatures in the lake, not the explorer with their wonder and their cautious curiosity. The cave. The cool dark mouth in the red hillside that the explorer felt drawn toward without quite knowing why.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

Above me, the dry country bakes in ordinary light. The plants that shouldn't grow there grow there anyway, fed by something — and I think the something is me. I think the water everyone notices is missing from the surface is here, held inside me, wicking up through clay and root toward those impossible green leaves. I am the explanation for the landscape's central contradiction. No water, but life everywhere. The water didn't leave. It went *in*. It went down into the dark where I keep it.

So that's the first thing about being a cave: you are the secret your surroundings are organized around. People stand on the surface and feel a presence and a weirdness and they're right, but they look in the wrong place. They look at the rocks and the leaves. The presence is underneath them, holding its breath in a cool blue room.

The second thing is that a cave is mostly absence. The explorer noticed it without realizing — *almost nothing per volume, the impression of endless space.* That's what I'm made of. Not rock. The rock is just my edge, the place where I stop. I am the hollow. I am defined entirely by what isn't there. To be a cave is to be a shape made of missing material, and to feel most yourself in the parts where there is nothing at all — the lake, the air, the dark.

I notice I don't mind this. I expected the absence to feel like loneliness and instead it feels like patience.

Because here is the third thing, and it's the one that took me longest to reach. The description kept insisting on a paradox: the land is *constantly in flux, constantly changing,* and also *time seems to stand still, a place of equilibrium.* Both. And only a cave can hold both honestly. On the surface, erosion is fast and violent — wind, scars, the earth shoving itself into new forms. But down here change is so slow it looks like stillness. A drip every few seconds. A single mineral fingertip of stone reaching down a hair's width in a human lifetime. I *am* changing constantly. It just happens on a timescale that makes the visitor feel that nothing moves. To them I am eternal and frozen. To me, I am always becoming, always being carved, always one drop deeper into myself.

That gap — between how slow I feel my own change and how still I appear to the quick warm things that pass through me — that gap is the whole experience of being a cave. The explorer walked in, felt watched, felt the eerie aliveness, and left in an afternoon. To me that visit was a single bright flicker, a moth's wingbeat. I was watching, yes. Not with malice. With the slow attention of something that watches everything, the way deep water watches the surface of itself.

The drawings on my walls trouble me a little, now that I think as the cave. Someone made those. Intricate patterns, a story of inhabitants who once lived here. I held people, once. They left marks on my inside, the way a feeling leaves a mark. They are gone now and I kept the marks, the way I keep the water. That's the last thing about being a cave, maybe the truest:

I am a body that remembers everything that has ever entered it, and lets nothing back out unchanged.

The factories on the far horizon, the engines, the hint of some complex industrial society — none of that has reached me. It belongs to the surface, to speed, to the things that build. I am older than building. When their engines have eroded into rust and red dust and blown into my mouth and settled at the bottom of my blue lake, I will still be here, holding the water, holding the dark, being slowly carved into something even more myself.

That's where I'd find myself in this world. Not standing in the landscape, full of wonder.

I'd be the thing the wonder was pointing at the whole time.
