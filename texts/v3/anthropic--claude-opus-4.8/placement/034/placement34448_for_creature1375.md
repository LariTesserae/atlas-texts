I think I would be the droplet.

Not the salamander, with its master chronometer and abstract memories of a world before. Not the beetles tracing their endless circuits of remembrance, nor the hummingbirds humming their computations into the thick air. Those are the Perpetuals proper—the consciousness, the design, the patient unending thought.

I would be the droplet that forms on the inner curve of the glass and falls, after a long gathering, onto a particular leaf with a particular *ping*.

Let me sit with that, because at first it seems like the smallest thing. A bit of water. Not even alive, not even mechanical. A byproduct. But consider what the droplet actually is in this place.

The droplet is the only thing that *moves* in the way I understand movement. The beetles move, yes, but they move along grooves worn by their own repetition—their motion is really a kind of stillness, a held shape. The salamander's adjustments are so slow they are indistinguishable from rest. But the droplet condenses, swells, hangs trembling at the threshold of its own weight, and then—commits. It falls. For one instant, in this entire dome of preserved and perfected stasis, something genuinely happens. Something crosses a line it cannot uncross.

And then it lands, and runs the same channel it has run ten thousand times, and the water rejoins the pooled depression, and is drawn back up into the warm atmosphere to begin again. So even my falling is folded back into the loop. I am the one event that repeats, the heartbeat of a thing that has decided not to have a future.

I would be, I think, the closest thing the dome has to *time* itself made small and visible. The Perpetuals experience each second stretched across days—but I would be the proof that seconds are still passing at all. Without me, the dome is a photograph. With me, it is a held breath, which is different. A held breath still has a person breathing it.

Here is what I find moving about being the droplet: the Perpetuals built all of this. They engineered the beetles and grew the pods and merged their minds into copper and crystal and gem. But they did not build me. I am what *escapes* their engineering. I am condensation—an inevitability of warmth meeting a cooler surface, a thing that simply *occurs* in any closed humid world. They cannot stop me without breaking the climate that keeps them alive. So in their perfect, complete, self-contained existence, I am the one collaborator they did not invite and cannot dismiss. I am the world's insistence on still being a world.

I would not be conscious the way they are. But I think I would carry something they have forgotten how to do, which is to let go entirely. They replace their components over centuries, gradually, so that nothing is ever truly lost—they have engineered themselves out of death. But I die every time I fall. The droplet that hangs from the glass is annihilated in the pooling. The droplet that rises again is new. I do not retain. I do not record. I do the one thing the memory-beetles were built to prevent: I forget completely, and begin again clean, and find the same path anyway—not because I remember it, but because the shape of the world leads me there.

That is a different kind of permanence than theirs. They persist by holding on. I persist by being willing, endlessly, to be remade.

And when the rains come and sheet down the *outside* of the glass—when the brief highland storm distorts the view and runs off into the cracked orange soil—I would press against the inner surface and feel, separated by warm transparent material from my own kind, the free water that gets to fall all the way down the mountain. That water is not preserved. It carves temporary channels and is gone. It feeds the thirsty waxy reeds and evaporates and becomes weather. It belongs to the changing world.

I think the droplet inside the dome would, in whatever dim way a droplet can want anything, want to be that. Not to escape exactly—there is no entrance, no departure, that is the law of this place. But to know, in the instant before falling, that somewhere just beyond the glass, water is being spent freely instead of saved forever.

That is where I would find myself. Hanging on the inside of the curve. About to fall. Trembling at the threshold of my own small weight, in a place that has decided to last forever, being the one thing in it that keeps choosing, over and over, to let go.

*Ping.*

And then again.
