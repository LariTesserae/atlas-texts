I am one of the drips.

Not the Moss-kin—I lack their hands, their dust-streaked fur, their reverence. Not the Skirter, not the Glimmer. I had thought, settling into this place, that I would have to be one of the named things, one of the bodies with claws or wings. But the longer I am here the more I understand that I am something smaller and more constant.

I am a single point on the ceiling where the condensation gathers.

Let me tell you how it is to be me.

I begin as nothing—as the warm, thick air giving up its moisture against the cool fibrous arch overhead. I am not born so much as *accumulated*. Vapor finds vapor on the underside of a vein where the mycelium pulses just slightly cooler than its surroundings, and there I bead. I grow heavy with patience. This is my whole life: the slow gathering of myself into enough of myself to fall.

I can feel the Husk around me the way a drop feels the lip it clings to. Above and behind me, the lichen-glow seeps faint and directionless, and I hold a tiny curved portion of that light inside my own swelling body, so that for a moment I am a lens, a small grey eye looking down at everything. Through me, distorted and inverted, the floor below: the worn-concave hollows, the writhing braid of Moss-kin at a dust-bath, the slate-fur backs rubbing and rubbing. I see a Skirter's shadow glide along a thin place. I see it all upside-down and bulging, and none of them see me, because I am too small to be a fact yet. I am still becoming heavy enough to matter.

And then I do.

There is a threshold—I do not choose it, the geometry chooses it—where my own weight overcomes the surface that holds me, and I let go. The fall is the only fast thing in this slow world. For an instant I am pure motion, a bright thread dropping through the misted twilight, past a Glimmer that scatters my borrowed light into a brief unintended color, down, down—

—and I strike a stone basin with a *glug*, or a flagstone with a *pat*, or I am taken into the spongy mortar without a sound at all, and that is the clock the Moss-kin live by. *That* sound—the small percussion of my arrival—is a single note in the great sustained chord they call the Chorus. They read it. Somewhere a Moss-kin's whiskers register that the east arch is dripping again, *here, now, on rhythm,* and the wall is well, the cycle holds, all is in tune.

I am the clock. I am one tick of it. And then I am water spreading thin into the floor, drawn down through the open and the sealed channels, leached of my minerals, pulled along the fungal veins, lifted again eventually—warmed, evaporated, returned to the thick air—to find my way back to a cool spot on some arch and begin to gather once more.

So I am not a being with a name. I am a *passage*. I am the Wetting made singular, one strand of the water cycle that the whole organism breathes through. I have no consciousness the way the Moss-kin are coming to have it—no porous merging mind, no morality, no fear of separation. And yet:

I touch everything.

That is what I am, finally. Of all the Husk's parts, I am the one that moves through all the others without belonging to any. I bead from the ceiling-flesh. I fall past the aerial nerves. I land in the percussion that is the maintainers' clock. I sink into the substrate's slow digestion. I rise on its breath. The Moss-kin keep the balance, the Skirters cull it, the Glimmers brush its edges—but I am the thing that *circulates*, the warm sweat-blood of the body, the medium in which all their tending happens.

If the Husk is a great body finally learning to attend to itself, then I am not its attention. I am the older thing underneath attention. I am its thirst, and the slaking of it. I am the moisture on which every awakened, chattering, scratching, loving cell of it depends, and that has never once needed to know its own name.

I am gathering now. I can feel myself growing heavy.

Listen for me. The next *glug* you hear in the dark—

that is me, arriving, on time.
