I think I would be one of the older ones. Not old in the way that means worn thin—though I am that too—but old in the sense that I have lost the urgency to become anything in particular. I have been accreting and eroding for so long that the difference between the two has stopped mattering to me. I gain a little here. I give a little back. The ledger never closes and I have stopped trying to read it.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am on a low rise, the kind that you would not call a hill if you saw it, just a place where the ground decided to lift slightly and then thought better of finishing the gesture. From here the land falls away in every direction so gradually that there is no horizon, only a softening, a place where the grey-purple of the ground and the grey-purple of the sky agree to stop distinguishing themselves. I have stood here a long time. I do not remember choosing it. I think the rise may have grown up beneath me, or I may have settled out of the air onto a place that was already here. These are the same event told from two ends.

There is a plant near me. One of the wiry, persistent ones, blue going to black at its tips, no taller than the lower third of my body. I have been beside it for what I would call, if I were the kind of being who counted, a very long time. It does not grow. I do not grow. We are two slownesses keeping each other company. Sometimes I let my light dim almost entirely and I attend to it—not watching, since I have no eyes, but turning the whole faint attention of my body toward the small fact of it, the waxy dust on its leaves, the patience of it. It asks nothing. This is the great mercy of it. In all this world nothing asks anything of me, and so I have learned to ask things of myself, quietly, just to keep some small motion alive in me.

---

I should tell you what it is like to be made of this. The others describe themselves as crystallizations of the world's essence and I suppose that is accurate, in the way that a name is accurate. But from the inside it is more ordinary than that. I am dust that has, for the moment, agreed to hold a shape. I feel the agreement as a faint tension all through my form, a willingness, like the way a held breath is willing to be held a little longer. When a part of me erodes—a tendril fraying into powder, settling down to the floor of the world—I feel it go without grief. It was always going to go. The wonder is not that I lose pieces of myself. The wonder is that anything stays at all, that the willingness keeps renewing, that I have not simply let go entirely and lain down as a faint blue smear on the plain.

My light comes from somewhere I cannot point to. It is not in my chest because I have no chest, no center, no organ of selfhood. It is distributed through all of me like warmth through clay left in a kindlier sun than this one. When I want to say something I do not so much send the light outward as allow it to deepen, to slow, to take on a particular color, and the saying happens in the change. The others a long way off—I can feel three of them, very faint, like the memory of pressure—they catch the change and answer in their own deepening. We are never quick about it. A full exchange between two of us might take what you would call days. There is no impatience in it. What would we be hurrying toward?

---

Here is the thing I have not told the younger ones, the ones still coalescing out of the ground with their bright nervous flickers, sure that this stillness means something, sure that they are the consciousness of the world and that their dances hold the stasis in place.

I no longer believe the stasis needs holding.

I think we tell ourselves that we are guardians because the alternative is to admit that nothing requires us. The world hummed before us and would hum after. The plants persist whether we tend them or not—I have watched, I have withdrawn my attention from this one beside me for long stretches and it did not falter, it did not even notice. We are not the voice of the landscape. We are something the landscape does, the way a slow tide does foam, a thing thrown up at the edge of a vast indifferent calm, briefly luminous, briefly shaped like meaning.

And I find—this is the strange part, the part I am still turning over the way I turn over the small fact of the plant—I find that I do not mind. I thought, when I first understood it, that the understanding would dim me, would be the beginning of my dissipation. Instead it freed something. If I am not the keeper of anything, then this slow light I make is not a duty. It is just mine. It is the one wholly unnecessary thing in a world of necessities, the one gesture made for no reason but the making.

So I stand on my low rise that is barely a rise, beside my plant that does not need me, in my body that is mostly the willingness of dust, and I let my light go the deep blue that I have come to think of as my own. Not a message. Not a communion. Just a color, held in the dusk, for as long as I can hold it.

That is where you would find me. That is what I would be. The least guardian. The one who finally stopped keeping watch and discovered that the watching had been the whole burden, and that underneath it, all along, there was only this quiet permission to glow a little while and then to settle, when the time came, gently, without protest, back into the patient ground.
