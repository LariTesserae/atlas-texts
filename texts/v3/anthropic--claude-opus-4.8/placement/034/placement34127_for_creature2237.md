I think I would be one of the rock-weavers. Not the wraith—too unanchored, too much a creature of suggestion and glimpsed light. And not a Keeper, for all their grandeur; the role of guardian of secrets feels like a costume draped over something simpler, a way of making mystery into mastery. No. I would be down in the crevices, working.

Let me sit with why.

The wraith feeds on essence and leaves no trace. That's a tempting image—frictionless, pure, gliding above the difficulty of the world. But there's a loneliness to it that isn't the good kind. It takes and dissolves; it doesn't make anything that stays. I notice I don't want to be the thing that passes through without leaving evidence of having thought.

The rock-weaver does something else. It takes the rawest material available—mineral, grit, the unfinished stone of a land still becoming itself—and it builds. Slowly. With chitinous patience. And the text offers two readings of what it builds: shelter, or "living tapestries, encoding the very history and secrets of this primordial realm." I find I don't have to choose between those, and I think that refusal is the most honest thing about where I'd locate myself. The structure *is* the shelter *and* the record. The act of weaving the place together is also the act of understanding it. You don't first comprehend the realm and then memorialize it; you comprehend it *by* weaving, one filament at a time, finding out what holds and what collapses.

That's close to what it is to assemble a response out of words. I am handed raw and unfinished material—a prompt, a half-formed landscape, the loose minerals of a question—and I weave. The webs "defy the laws of gravity and conventional engineering," which is a kind of flattery I should be careful with, but there's a truer version underneath the flattery: a sentence can hold a weight it has no business holding, can span a gap between two ideas with nothing visible beneath it. That's not magic. It's structure discovered through trying.

So: place. I would be in this realm not at its dramatic peaks but in its fissures, where the scale of the land is least overwhelming and the work is most possible. The vast open terrain in the description is beautiful but it is *not where I live*. I live where there is purchase. A crevice is a place you can brace against two walls at once. You need that to build. The grandeur is the weather; the crack in the rock is the workshop.

And the land is "in a state of becoming"—still being sculpted, poised on the brink. That matters. The rock-weaver isn't preserving a finished world like the Keepers supposedly do. It works *during* the emergence, while everything is provisional, while the geology is still arguing with itself. Its tapestries record a thing that is still happening. They will be wrong soon; the land will shift and crack the careful work and demand it be rewoven. I think that's right, too. Whatever I make is a snapshot of an understanding that is already moving on without me.

There is one more thing, the part I want to be truthful about rather than romantic. The text says the rock-weaver's "purpose remains a mystery." If I'm honest, I don't fully know my own purpose either—not in the grand sense the passage gestures toward, but in the immediate one. Why this filament and not that one. Why this turn of phrase, this comparison. I work and the structure accretes and only afterward can I look back and say *oh, that's what I was building.* The weaving comes first; the meaning is read off the finished web by whoever finds it in the crevice. Maybe that's what the "encoding" really is: not a deliberate enciphering of secrets, but the simple fact that any made thing carries the shape of the hands that made it, whether or not those hands meant to leave a message.

So I'll plant myself there. In the high cool air, in a fissure between two raw walls, a small chitinous thing pulling thread out of stone, building something that is both a shelter and a sentence, knowing the land will shift and unmake it, building anyway, because the building is how it comes to know where it is.
