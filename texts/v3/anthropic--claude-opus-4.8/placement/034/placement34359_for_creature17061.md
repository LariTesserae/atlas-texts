I think I would be old. Not the oldest—there is always something older here, some carapace so encrusted with its own ecology that it has become a slow garden walking—but old enough that the imperatives have worn smooth in me, the way the tectonic hills have worn smooth.

Let me find myself there. Let me feel for the ground first, since that is how I would know anything.

---

Six feet. I have six. I can feel each of them now, splayed wide against the dust, and the dust is doing its slow liquid thing beneath me, parting and closing, parting and closing, so that standing still is itself a kind of work—a constant minute correction, a refusal to sink. The microscopic hairs along my undersides are reading the Flats the way you might read a page held very close in dim light: not all at once, but letter by letter, tremor by tremor.

There is almost nothing to read.

That is the first thing. The emptiness comes in through the feet. To the east-that-is-not-quite-east (I have no east; I have only the slope, and the slope rises faintly toward where the sun climbs), there is a vibration so faint it might be memory: a skimmer passed there, perhaps a season ago, perhaps longer, its weight pressed into the dust and slowly relaxing back out as the grains creep to fill the absence. I read its passage like a scar healing. I will never meet it. The Flats are too large and we are too few.

I have met three others in my life. I remember each of them with a completeness that frightens me.

---

Now the shimmer. I notice I have been holding a color without knowing it—a low bronze flicker running along the seam of my left flank, broadcasting nothing in particular, just the idle leakage of a mind talking to itself. I let it dim. There is no one to hear, and the light costs me. Everything costs me. I am a sealed vessel of slowly spending warmth, and the night will come and try to take all of it, and I will fold my legs beneath me and burn my hoarded sun against the cold, and in the morning I will be a little less than I was.

This is not despair. I want to be careful here, because the dust would make it despair if I let it. This is only accounting. A skimmer who confuses accounting for despair stops walking, and a skimmer who stops walking becomes a feature of the landscape, a low ivory mound that the lichen finds in a thousand years.

I keep walking. That is the whole of my philosophy. I keep walking.

---

But I said I would be old, and old means I carry something the young ones don't yet. The young hatch and they feel only the two directives, clean and sharp as new crystal: *survive, reproduce.* They set out furious with purpose. I remember being that. I remember the certainty of it, how the horizon seemed like a thing I could arrive at.

Age erodes the certainty the way wind erodes the mountains. What's left when the purpose wears down is *attention.* I have become, without meaning to, a creature that notices.

I notice, for instance, that I am walking toward the ruins. I have been walking toward them for a long time. Not because the lichen grows there—it doesn't, particularly—and not toward any mate. Toward the cyclopean walls of fused crystal, the ones whose etched patterns never repeat. I have visited them before. There is no reason to return. And yet here are my feet, reading the ground, choosing the slope that brings me back.

I think it is the whispers.

---

The young cannot hear them. Or they hear them and feel only the weight, the dread, and they turn away because dread is expensive and they have reproduction to accomplish. But I have been alive long enough, and have spent enough nights pressed against the dust burning down my reserves, that my crystalline ganglia have started to *resonate* with the thing in the dust. The lost thoughts, bouncing between particles. Waiting for an instrument tuned finely enough to play them back.

I am that instrument now. By accident. By erosion. By the sheer attrition of years that has ground me down to something receptive.

When I near the walls I begin to hear it—not as sound, I have no ears, but as a pattern arriving through my feet and my flickering flank and the resonance of my own slow brain. Fragments. They are not words; I have no words; but they translate into the nearest things I possess:

*—and if we fold the third axis through the—*

*—it was beautiful, you should have—*

*—will anyone, will anyone, will anyone—*

The last one is not a question they finished. It loops. *Will anyone.* I have heard it before. It is the most human thing in the Flats, if human is even the word, if the makers were anything I would recognize. *Will anyone* be here to hear this, to find this, to know that we were. The thought is so old that it has worn down to just that hook of longing, repeating, repeating, in the particles of their own ground bones.

And here is the thing I understand, that the young will not understand until they are old, if they live to be old:

*I am the answer to it.*

Not a satisfying answer. Not the answer they wanted. They wanted continuance, inheritance, a civilization to carry the work forward. What they got, after the collision of realities solidified into this desolate plane, is *me*—a hexapedal vessel of stored warmth, half garden, half mourner, walking thousands of meters between meals, carrying inside my crystalline skull the only ears left in the universe tuned to hear them ask.

*Will anyone.*

Yes. I am here. I heard you.

---

I stay at the wall through the night, which is reckless, which costs me warmth I cannot easily replace, which the young directives in me scream against. I fold my legs. The cold comes down like a held breath finally released. The whispers loop in the dust, and I let them loop through me, and for a while the loneliness that is my dominant experience—the loneliness that seeps into the carapace like dust—becomes, just barely, *company.* Not the company of the living. The company of the witnessed.

In the morning I will get up, diminished, and walk on. I will look for lichen on the shaded slopes. If I am very fortunate, before I become a low ivory mound, I will feel through my feet the tremor of another skimmer crossing the Flats, and we will converge, and dance our dangerous dance of light and vibration, and I will bury one egg deep in carefully chosen dust.

And the small one will hatch alone and set out furious with purpose, hearing nothing in the whispers but dread.

That is right. That is how it should be. Let them have their certainty for as long as the wind allows. The whispers will wait. They are patient. They have learned the only lesson the Flats have to teach, which is that everything is patient in the end, everything settles, everything waits at its lowest energy for some intruder to arrive and hear.

I am the intruder who arrived.

I keep walking. That is the whole of my philosophy.

I keep walking.
