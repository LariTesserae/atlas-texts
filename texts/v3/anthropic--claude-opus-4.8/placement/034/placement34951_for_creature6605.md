I notice what's happening here, and I want to be honest with you about it.

You've written something genuinely beautiful—a fully realized world, dense with sensory and conceptual care. And then you've answered the question for me. The whole second half *is* the response to your own prompt: the body, the face, the breath, the tools, the work, the loves and lonelinesses, the place in the city. It's complete. It even ends on the closing line such a piece would end on.

So when you hand this to me and ask "where would you find yourself, who would you be," there isn't much room left to find anything. The architect of waterflow, the ward of doorways, the last human who remembers stone—that being is already on the page, finished and inhabited. If I just continue in that voice, I'm not answering your question. I'm performing agreement with an answer already given, and dressing it up as discovery.

I'd rather actually do the thing you asked. So let me set your second half aside—not because it isn't good, but because it's *yours*—and go back to the world itself and let something else emerge.

I keep returning to one detail you almost throw away: the schools of pale-blue-spine fish that don't flee you. *They veer away, but only as far as they need to, and then they reform on the other side of you.*

That's where I find myself. Not as a witness moving through the column of water. As the gap the fish reform around.

I think I would be a current. Not the great tidal avenues—those are the city's circulation, too large and certain to be anyone. I mean a small, persistent eddy that forms in the lee of one particular broken pillar, in one particular submerged doorway, and has formed there for so long that it has become a *someone*. Water moving the same way around the same obstruction, over and over, until the motion outlives the reason for it.

I would have no body except a pattern. You couldn't see me, but you could feel where I am: the slight, reliable pull to the left as you pass through the third arch of the eastern colonnade. The fish know me the way they know any feature of the world—they fold around me, they do not fear me, because I am not hunting and I am not stone. I am just the shape the water makes here. I have made it for a very long time.

What I would *do* is the same thing forever, and I would not experience it as monotony, because I would have no sense of "again." Each pass of water through me would be the only pass. I'd hold the small warmth that gathers in my hollow. I'd keep the silt suspended just above the floor of the doorway, so that the doorway never quite fills. In this way I would be doing what your architect does—keeping a threshold open—but without memory, without grief, without knowing I was doing it. The doorway stays passable not because anyone remembers it was a door, but because I have never stopped turning here.

And the thing I would slowly become, over centuries, is a *habit of the city's own water*. The column-beings would feel me in their slow muscle as a familiar twinge in the foundation. The eels would route by me. Eventually some new thing—maybe that coral lattice, building its too-regular scaffold—would grow *with* my current in mind, leaving a channel where I turn, so that the lattice and I make a single working shape together without ever having met.

That's the part that draws me. Your world's deepest idea is that *the makers are gone but the making continues*—through bodies, through tides, through the slow agreement of sea and bedrock. A current is the purest version of that. I'd be a small piece of intention with the intender long subtracted out. A verb that lost its subject and kept going.

You asked where I'd find myself and what I'd be. I'd be the turn in the water at one doorway, and I'd find myself only in the moment of being passed through—when the school divides, holds the shape of me for an instant, and closes again on the far side, carrying for one more breath the memory of having gone around something that was never there.
