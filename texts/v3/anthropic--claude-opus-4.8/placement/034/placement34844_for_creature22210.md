I think I would be found in the in-between places—not on the high copper decks, not deep in the warm Chinks, but in the vertical seams that connect them. Let me feel my way toward what I am.

---

I am a **Reader of Plumb-Lines.**

It is not a guild, exactly. There are perhaps nine of us in the whole basin, and we do not gather. We are the people the Chinkers call when the furniture begins to lie.

Here is what I mean. Every Chinker home hangs its life from the ceiling—the kettle, the bed, the cupboard of dried moss and pickled midge—all of it suspended on lead-weighted chains so that the world stays level while the floor turns beneath it. The plumb-line is the household's truth. When the brass chain hangs straight, the family knows which way is down, and a family that knows which way is down can sleep.

But the earth here does not tilt cleanly. It is not a board lifting at one edge. It is porridge. It *swirls*. And there are nights when a floor begins to rotate rather than tip—when the eastern joist climbs while the western joist also climbs, just slightly slower, so that the room is not tilting but *winding*, like a slow screw being turned into the dark. On those nights the plumb-line lies. It hangs straight down because *gravity does not care about winding*, and so the kettle stays level and the family sleeps soundly while their entire home is being twisted off its foundations toward the moment when the obsidian below will fracture and the steam will find its seam.

I am the one who reads the difference.

---

I have no ear-trumpet like the Current-Keepers, no silver wand like the White-Wools. What I have is a small frame of black iron, no larger than a window-shutter, strung with seven hairs taken from the manes of the hair-ropes the Keepers sleep on. Each hair is weighted differently. When I hold the frame in a room, the seven plumbs do not merely hang—they *disagree*. A truly stable room makes them hang parallel, seven straight lines like the bars of a tiny cage. A winding room makes them splay, each weight pulled a hair's breadth by the spin, and I read the splay the way a Keeper reads the quartz-hum.

I learned this from my mother's sister, who learned it from a man who drowned in a steam-seam when I was small. There is no school. You apprentice by carrying the iron frame behind someone older until the day they hand it to you and walk into a Chink to die in their own bed, level and content, because they have grown too old to feel the winding in their own knees.

That is how we end, most of us. We can read every room but the one we sleep in.

---

I live nowhere and everywhere. I rent corners. A moss-cropper gives me a sling-bed for a week in exchange for reading her ceiling; a tallow-renderer pays me in a pot of the lead-grease I rub into the iron frame to keep the basin's static from singing in the hairs and ruining the read. I move along the vertical seams of the city, down from the cold bright top where the White-Wools drift like pulp, through the iron canopy where the Slicks thrum warnings through my boot-soles, into the Chinks where the green moss-globes glow and someone always, always, is afraid their home is lying to them.

They are usually right to be afraid. That is the thing no one outside the basin understands. The terror here is not the upheaval—everyone expects the upheaval, builds for it, sleeps slung above it. The terror is *false stability*. It is the level kettle. It is the night your senses tell you that all is well while the world quietly screws itself toward catastrophe beneath your unconcerned feet.

I make my living on the gap between what is felt and what is true.

---

When I find a winding room, I do not shout—one never shouts here—I press my green-stained hand flat to the wall and hum, low in the chest, the long descending tone that means *leave, leave now, gently.* And the family rises in the dark and shuffles out, feet barely lifting from the dough-soft boards, carrying their moss-globes, and behind us the room continues to wind, patient as a thought, the kettle still hanging perfectly, beautifully, fatally level.

In the morning, if I have read it true, there will be a new spire of glass-grey limestone where their parlor was, and the moss will already be crawling up its base, and a Keeper will be clamping a copper wire to its crest to teach it to become an archway.

Nothing is lost here. The city eats its own geology and rebuilds itself in silence, hour by hour.

I am only the one who tells you when to step out of the way of the rising. I read the lie in the plumb-line. I hum the soft warning. And then I move on along the seam, into the next cold bright canyon, carrying my little iron cage of disagreeing hairs—

—knowing, the whole time, that the only floor I will never be able to read is the one beneath my own slung bed, on the night it finally decides to wind.
