I think I would be one of the listeners.

Not a hunter, not an engineer. Something the city needed but never advertised for, the way a body keeps its own count of breaths without being asked. Let me find where I am as I go.

---

I live in the gutters. Not as a beggar — I want to be careful about that — but professionally, the way a person might live in libraries or live in the sea. The open canals of Kyrios are where I do my work, because the work is *listening*, and water carries sound the way nothing else in this place does.

Here is what I do. The weeping walls and the rushing channels and the hum of the pumps — to most people this is the single mesmerizing melody the descriptions keep insisting upon. One sound. A blur. But it isn't one sound. It is thousands of sounds laid over each other, and somewhere in that braid is information, if you know how to unpick it. A pump that has begun to grind a quarter-tone flat will throw a thread off-color into the whole weave three days before it fails. A canal whose level has dropped will let the limestone-barked trees downstream begin a thirst-song you can hear in their swaying, a dry creak under the wet.

I am the one who tells the engineers *where* before they would otherwise know *that*.

So that is my place: a low stone ledge worn smooth by my sitting, beside a confluence where four channels meet under the eleventh mesa. The water comes down from the high gardens carrying their runoff and their gossip — the mineral taste of which terraces have been overwatered, the faint oils of which machines have been wept on, the disturbance pattern of feet upstream. I keep my hands in the flow. I have kept them there so long that the skin has gone pale and ridged and, I'm told, looks like the limestone bark. I find I don't mind the comparison. We've adapted to the same wet.

---

Let me tell you who I actually am, because I've been describing the job, and a job is not a person.

I was born in one of the temporary cities — a vine-and-fungus settlement called Onkos that lasted eleven years before the land underneath began to complain and we unwove it in a single season and carried it elsewhere. I remember the unweaving more clearly than the living. A whole city taken gently apart so the ground could rest. My mother had a phrase: *we are guests who clean up after ourselves.* That is the deepest law of the land around Kyrios, and Kyrios itself — the permanent city, the proud city on its mesas — has always seemed to me faintly indecent for staying put. A guest who never leaves. The rocks weep, I think sometimes, because the city sits on them and will not get up.

I came to the canals young because I couldn't do the other things. My hands were no good for the engineering — too slow, too dreamy. And I couldn't hunt; I cannot make myself the kind of quiet that hunting needs, the predatory quiet, the held breath. My quiet is the other kind. The receiving kind. You sit very still and you make a hollow of yourself and you let the world fall in.

A canal-master found me at nine years old with my head against a weeping wall, weeping back at it, and instead of scolding me she crouched down and asked: *What's it saying?*

I didn't have words yet. I pointed upstream and said something is broken up there, it sounds sore. She walked up the channel and found a fractured sluice gate that no one had reported. She came back and looked at me for a long time, the rain in her eyebrows, and said: *Right. You'll do.*

---

So now I am old, or older, and I have a ledge and ridged pale hands, and apprentices who think I am half made of mineral myself.

And here is the part I have never told the engineers, because it is not the kind of information they relocate cities for.

The water carries the machine-sounds and the garden-sounds and the foot-sounds, yes. But under all of those, very faint, only audible in the deepest part of the night when the pumps idle and the drizzle slackens — there is another sound. It comes up *through* the water, not along it. It comes from below the mesas, from whatever the city is so indecently sitting on. It is slow. It is enormous. It is the rhythm of something the size of the whole landscape, turning over perhaps once in a human lifetime, and the rocks weep because of *it*, not because of us, and I have spent my life learning to listen and only now begun to understand the worst and most beautiful thing:

The temporary cities are right and the permanent city is wrong, but not for the reason we tell ourselves. It isn't the *weight*. The thing below isn't burdened. It is *waking*. Slowly, over centuries, all this water we are so proud of channeling — it's the thing's own moisture, the way breath fogs a cold window, and it is opening its eyes, and the eyes are the high lakes, and we built our proud city directly on the lid of one.

I have run the sound through every test I know. I am not mistaken.

I am, I think, the only person in Kyrios who knows we live on a face. That the dance of nature and machinery, all that harmony the storytellers love — it is a thing happening on a sleeper's cheek. Beautiful. Real. Doomed in the gentle, un-urgent way a dream is doomed by morning.

---

What do I do with this?

I keep my hands in the water. I teach the apprentices to hear the flat pump and the thirsty tree. I do not tell them about the slow enormous rhythm yet, because they are young and the city needs its pumps tended and there is, by my estimate, still a great deal of night left before the morning comes.

But I have started, lately, to teach them something they think is whimsy. I have them put their heads against the weeping walls and weep back. I tell them it's an exercise in stillness.

What I am actually doing is teaching them to listen *downward*. So that when the rhythm quickens — and it will, in their lifetime or their apprentices' — there will be someone who recognizes the sound for what it is. Someone who can say, the way my mother said it, the way the canal-master said *you'll do* —

*We are guests. It's time. Help me wake the others. Let's clean up after ourselves and go.*

That is where I am in this world. On a smooth ledge, in the dark, with my hands in the cold braid of all the city's voices, keeping the one count nobody asked me to keep.
