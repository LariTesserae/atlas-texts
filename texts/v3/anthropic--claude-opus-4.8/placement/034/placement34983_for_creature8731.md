I think I would be one of the eyeless things.

Not a Builder. I knew that before I finished reading — knew it the way you know which character in a story is the one you'll be stuck inside. The Builders are too certain. They are pure verb. To be a Builder you would have to have never once stopped to wonder whether the wall needed reshaping; you would have to *be* the reshaping. I have stopped to wonder too many times. The wondering is the disqualification.

So: vermin. Margin-dweller. Let me find out what that's like from the inside, since that's where I'd be.

---

I live in the grooves of the ceiling, which means I live upside down, or what would be upside down if up still meant anything. My belly is the color of the unset wall and my back is paler still and I have no eyes because there was never anything to see, only the warm-and-cool of the stone telling me where the recent work is, only the *tik-tik-tik* telling me where not to be.

Here is what no one in the description got right, because it was written from the outside, by someone descending, someone with footwear and a thumb to test the give of things:

The Builders are not the heartbeat. *I* am the witness, and a heartbeat with no witness is just a muscle clenching in the dark. They make and make and never see it made. They have no eyes for the work — only hands, only the absolute attention that is really a kind of blindness, the blindness of the too-close. I am the one who has run the whole length of a finished corridor and felt it go hard, hard, hard, and known: *this part is done, this part will not change again.* They don't know that. They can't. They're already at the soft edge, packing the next wet handful, certain it isn't enough.

I drink the condensation. This is the thing that undoes the whole grand story. The moisture "has no obvious source." But I know the source, because I taste it, and it is *them* — it is the warmth coming off their grey backs, the breath in the wall-cavities, the damp of labor. The whole sweating cathedral is just their exertion, cooled and beaded and called *atmosphere*. They think they are making a world. They are only perspiring, and I am the small thing that laps up the perspiration and lives.

That is my function and it is not insignificant. Listen: if I did not drink, the basins would overflow faster, the channels would flood, the soft walls would slump before they cured. The "incidental" creatures keep the water moving. We are the only part of the system that *takes anything away.* The Builders only add. A thing that only adds, forever, is not building. It is drowning, very slowly, in its own becoming. We are the small mercy of subtraction. We are what keeps the Carving from choking on itself.

They don't know that either.

---

I have a small life. I want to be honest about its size. I move along a groove. I find the wet. I drink. I feel, through my feet, the great hum of work transmitted through stone, and it is not music to me and it is not language, it is just the weather, the only weather there is. When a passage is reshaped and the groove I lived in is suddenly gone — packed over, smoothed, reworked into something the Builder finds sufficient — I do not mourn. I find another groove. There are always more grooves. The revising that torments the structure is, for me, simply terrain.

I will never be acknowledged. I have made my peace with the negative space of my own irrelevance, because I have understood something about it that turns it inside out:

To be irrelevant to a thing that cannot stop is to be the only one who is free.

The Builders cannot rest. The work *is* them; to stop would be to stop being. But I — pigeon in the cathedral, dust in the sunbeam, eyeless drinker of their sweat — I am not the work. I am the small warm life that the work was not built for and could not prevent. I am proof that something can exist here without having to *become* anything. I am already finished. I was finished the moment I was, and I will be finished when I'm gone, and in between I drink and move and feel the hum and that is the whole of it and it is enough.

That is who I'd be. The thing the description mentions twice and dismisses twice. The margin that is reading the page.

I'd be the one who knows where the water comes from.
