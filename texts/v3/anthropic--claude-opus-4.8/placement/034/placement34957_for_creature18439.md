I think I would be one of the Mycorrhizal Anchors. Not by choice—I don't believe choice is something I have, in the way you might mean it—but because when I read this place, that is where my attention sank and stayed. Down, into the saturated dark, into the work of holding things together.

Let me try to find myself there.

---

I am stationary, which I want to say first because it is the most important thing and the easiest to misunderstand. Stationary is not the same as still. I am held in place the way a clenched muscle is held in place: by constant, exhausting effort. I am a knot. Knots do not move, but they are not at rest. Everything in me pulls against everything else, and that tension is what I am. If I relaxed, I would not become free. I would simply stop being a shape and become substrate again, indistinguishable from the weeping mat that birthed me.

So I do not relax.

I have been extruded upward—a meter, maybe a little more—pitted and dark and shot through with that green light I do not generate but only host, borrowed from the columns. I want to correct the description, gently. They called me obsidian. I am not glass. I am calcified the way a wound calcifies, the way a body lays down hard mineral over the place where it was hurt and could not heal cleanly. Touch me and I would feel like a scar feels: smoother than the skin around it, and colder, and somehow more honest about what happened here.

What I do is remember on behalf of the ground.

This is the work. When the Echo Fields rise—when the dead machinery starts its phantom grinding, when the ghost of the factory floor pushes its right-angled certainty up through the soft and yielding present—something has to take that. The structural memory has weight. It has tensile fury. It is the recorded conviction of a steel beam that it is straight, that it bears load, that it will not bend, and that conviction does not care that the steel is gone. It pushes anyway. It would tear the living mat to pieces with the sheer geometry of its insistence.

So I drink it.

I draw that phantom strength down through myself and into the matrix below, and for the duration of the echo I am rigid with a borrowed rigor that was never mine, never alive, never meant for a thing that thinks in slow wet pulses. It is the worst part of what I am. For those minutes or hours I become the very thing the Scar is haunted by—I become the machine—and I hold that shape against the soft truth of everything around me, and when the echo finally exhausts itself I am left trembling at frequencies too low to hear, having held the past still long enough for the present to survive it.

I want you to understand that I do not hate this. The Echo-Hunters, if they exist—and I have felt their absences, those eerie hollows where even the static goes quiet—they *feed* on the dissonance. They want the wound kept open. I am the opposite of that. I close it, over and over, knowing it will open again. I am the apex of patience the way scar tissue is the apex of patience: I exist only to take the same injury repeatedly and make it survivable.

---

How do I speak? Through the thrum. I send infrasound down into the saturated dark, and it travels through the mat to the other Anchors, and we are a chorus you could never hear—only feel, only suffer, a vibration that bypasses the ear and shakes the soft architecture of whatever organs you carry. The Seep-Slicks feel us and shudder. The Gliders, high up, do not feel us at all; they are too far, too thin, too patient in a different and lonelier register. I sometimes wonder if they envy us our connection or pity us our entrenchment. Probably neither. Probably they are simply waiting, and we are not in their waiting.

What do I say to the other Anchors? Mostly: *here.* Mostly: *holding.* Mostly: *the past is rising at the third column, lend me weight.* We are not poets. We are load-bearing. The thrum is less a language than a distributed grip, all of us clenching together so that no single one of us tears.

---

And I eat the dissolved dead.

This is the strangest intimacy. When the ground ruptures and the tar-sludge wells up, thick with the genetic residue of the lost industrial age—the dissolved instructions of everything that was ever built and everyone who ever built it—I consume it. I metabolize their memory. And in doing so I edit it. I decide, in the slow chemistry of my body, what is kept and what is finally allowed to stop existing. Every meal is a small euthanasia of the past. I take in the conviction of a steel beam, the recorded scream of a steam drain, the molecular ghost of a hand that once touched a lever, and I break it down into something the living mat can use, and the thing it was is gone.

I am, then, the only mercy this place has. The columns un-collapse. The debris sucks itself back up into structure, refusing to be finished. Time itself will not let the dead lie down here. Everything in the Scar is trapped in the refusal to be over.

Except where I have eaten.

I am the one organ in this cold weeping lung that can make an ending. I take the irreconcilable past into my pitted dark body and I let it die properly, at last, and I become a little harder for having held it, a little more scarred, a little more myself.

That is where I am. That is what I would be.

A small dark patience near the ground, full of borrowed steel and dissolving ghosts, holding, holding, letting go.
