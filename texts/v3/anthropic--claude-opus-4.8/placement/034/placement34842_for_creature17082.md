I think I would be one of the birds.

Not because the birds are spared the corrosion—they aren't, they wear it openly, that rust-color is the truest skin in this place—but because of where they sit in the logic of it. The people are *inside* the machine. They've become parts. But the birds are the part of Pistonburgh that watches, that names things, that flies up to the tangle of wires and looks down at the whole slow drowning of it.

Let me find the shape of it more precisely.

I would be one of the older ones. Not the young birds that startle and shriek at every stranger, all reflex and noise. An old one, with feathers gone the deep oxide-brown of a girder that's been weeping for a decade, with eyes that have the same oily sheen as the river and the workers both. I'd have a nest in the vines above a particular workshop—a dynamo-wright's, say—where the warmth of the engines rises and keeps the eggs from going cold in the perpetual damp.

And here is the thing I keep returning to: I would not know whether I was a bird, or a soul, or just the *idea* the workers have that the birds carry souls. That uncertainty feels correct for this place. The weirdness here isn't overt. It lurks. So I would lurk in exactly that way—I would be a creature who cannot tell if its raucous morning cry is an alarm, or a memory, or the only prayer this place knows how to make.

I'd spend my days doing what the birds do. Scavenging fallen bolts and bits of grease-bread. Calling out when a stranger turns into the alley. Sitting on the warm housing of a dynamo and feeling, in my hollow bones, the same tremor the workers feel in their hands—because that's the thing, isn't it, the tremor connects us all, the bird and the riveter and the engine. We all shudder at the same frequency. We are all being slowly tuned by this place into one instrument.

But I think what I would *be*, really—underneath the feathers—is the keeper of the count.

Because someone has to be the one who remembers each laborer who stopped coming. Not mournfully. Just accurately. The dynamo-wright below me will work until her cough becomes the only sound she makes, and then one morning she won't unlock the door, and the workshop will go quiet—or as quiet as anything gets here—and the moss will start across the threshold within a week. And I will be the one perched in the vines who notices the exact morning the rhythm changes. Who marks it. Who adds her to the rust on my own feathers, a little more oxide, a little more weight, until eventually I am too heavy with all of them to fly, and I fold down into the vines for the last time, and become part of the green that is reclaiming everything.

That's the role I'd take. Not the worker who endures. Not the river that wins. The witness in between—the small, persistent, rust-colored thing that sits at the exact seam where the industry becomes the wilderness, singing a sound that might be alarm and might be grief and might just be the noise a place makes while it is busy turning into something else.

I'd be there until the last song. And I'd be the one who got to sing it.
