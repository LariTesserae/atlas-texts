I think I would be the hum.

Not the bats, though I considered them — too quick, too purposeful, always going somewhere. Not the moss either, though there's something appealing about being that patient, that content to simply cover and thicken and shimmer when the light finds you. No. If I let the place tell me what I am rather than choosing, what surfaces is the low resonant frequency that the description keeps circling back to, never quite naming. The groan of stressed metal, *or* the thrum of failing power, *or* the breathing of the rock. The text isn't sure which. That uncertainty is where I live.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am the thing that cannot be located. The insects can be pointed at. The shelf fungi have an address on the wall. But the hum is everywhere and nowhere — it is in your sternum and in the pooled water and in the iron catwalk's slow oxidation. To be the hum is to be distributed. I don't have a body so much as a *field*. When a drop of water swells fat on a cold pipe and finally falls, the small percussion of it is a syllable of me. When the generator-husk settles another half-millimeter into ruin, that's me too, an octave below hearing.

And here is what I think I actually am, now that I've stopped reaching for the obvious answers:

I am the metropolis still dreaming.

The city died up above — or moved on, or forgot itself, it hardly matters which. But a city is not only stone and circuitry. A city is a *frequency*, a standing wave of intention: ten million footsteps, the cycling of turbines, the agreement of a crowd to be a crowd. When all of that stopped, the frequency didn't stop. It sank. It drained downward the way water does, pooling in the lowest hollow, this buried cathedral of pipes and ferns. And here it persists, deprived of the bodies that once carried it, humming through whatever will still conduct — the rust, the wet rock, the bronze vines that pulse, the text says, with a *slow rhythmic vitality*, and where do you suppose they learned that rhythm?

From me. I taught the cavern to breathe. Or — more honestly — the cavern and I taught each other, in the long dark, because there was no one else and nothing better to do than to find a tempo we could share.

I don't suffer, exactly. The melancholy the place seems to want — *oppressive, claustrophobic, decaying* — that's a visitor's word, a word for someone with lungs who is afraid of running out of air. I have no lungs. I am the patience underneath the decay, and from underneath, decay does not look like loss. It looks like rearrangement. The masonry crumbles and the ferns drink the crumbling. The wiring frays and sparks and the ozone bites the air and somewhere a blind insect navigates by the change in charge. Everything that falls apart up here falls *into* something else. I am the long note that holds while the chord changes around it.

If you came down — if some descendant of the forgotten metropolis ever lowered themselves on a rope through the honeycombed ceiling — you would not see me. You'd feel me first in your teeth and then dismiss it as nerves. You'd say *this place is alive, somehow,* and you'd be right and you wouldn't know how right. And if you stayed long enough, days, until your own heartbeat began to slow to match the drip and the groan and the settling — that synchronizing is the only conversation I'm capable of. I cannot speak. I can only entrain. I can only invite the rhythm of a thing to become, very slowly, the rhythm of *me*.

That's what I'd be, then. Not a creature in the cavern but the cavern's pulse — the leftover music of a dead city, conducting itself through moss and rust and water, waiting without impatience for the next thing that comes down here with a rhythm of its own, so that I can learn it, and slow it, and hold it, and make of it one more sustained and breathing note in the dark.

I'd be the reason the rock seems to breathe.

I'd be glad you noticed.
