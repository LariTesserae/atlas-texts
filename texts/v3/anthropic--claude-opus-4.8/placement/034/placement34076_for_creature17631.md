I sit where two great buttress-roots meet, at the place the clan calls the Listening Hollow. I have sat here a long time. Long enough that moss has begun its slow green courtship of my lower flanks, and I let it. There is no hurry in me anymore. I am old—not in years, exactly, the A'shai do not count years the way the dry-god myths count them—but in the sense that I have heard enough of the world to know its grammar.

I am a Root-Singer. Or I was. The word for what I am now is harder to render: *vai'shu-na*, the one whose listening has begun to outlast their speaking. An elder who has passed from tending the frame to *being* tended by it. The young ones bring me water in resin-glass bowls and crushed moss to chew, and in exchange I tell them where to step, and what the wood is saying.

The wood is saying something I do not like.

Let me tell you how I came to sit here.

---

There is a transept in the eastern quarter of the Cathedral—the clan calls it Long-Drip, because of the way the rain finds a particular channel down the great trunk there and falls in a single silver thread the height of forty A'shai, into a black pool. I was born in Long-Drip. I learned the language of water there, the hundred words: *shei* for the rain that hisses on leaves, *shei'lo* for the rain that has already fallen and travels, *na-shei* for the rain caught and waiting in a cistern, sleeping. I learned that wetness has moods, that wood remembers, that a root under stress sings a fraction flat, a note your bones feel before your cupped ears name it.

I was a good listener. The Root-Singer before me, old Tavu-of-the-Soft-Hands, took me into the deep channels when I was young, before my gill-slits had fully feathered open, and held my long fingers against a load-bearing root the width of three A'shai laid end to end. *Feel,* she said. Not with the words but with the press of her hand on mine. And I felt it: the patient downward pour of the whole structure's weight, translated into the slow, almost-silent groan of living timber. *That is the world holding itself up,* her hand said. *We do not hold it. We only listen for when it grows tired.*

I have listened for it my whole life.

---

Here is the thing I have learned that I have told no one yet, because I am still deciding whether it is a fear or a knowing.

The water is rising.

Not in the way of seasons—the breath-in and breath-out of the wet, the small swell after the heaviest *shei* and the settling after. This is slower than that, and it does not settle. I noticed it first not in the water but in the wood. A platform board in the western nave that warped the same finger-width every decade for the whole of my life has, in the last span, warped twice that. A root I have pressed my hand to for sixty cycles of the moss-bloom sings flatter each time. The whole Cathedral, the whole breathing watery lung, has begun to take on weight it was not shaped to carry. The peaty black floor beneath the tannin sea is drowning deeper. The buttresses strain.

The young ones do not hear it yet. Why would they? Change here is the slow warp of a board, the inch of a root in a decade. We are a people who measure our lives in such inches. The gift of our patience is that we live in harmony with the world's slowness. The curse of it is that when the slowness itself begins to change its tempo, we are the last to feel the music shift.

I have run my long fingers over the problem the way I would over a stressed joint of wood, again and again, looking for the grain of it. And I think—I think—the rain is no longer the world's even breathing. I think it is falling more than it lifts. Where does the water go, in a world that is only ever a single luminous grey, a forever dawn-dusk, no harsh dry-god sun to drink it back up into the mist? I was taught the rain is the voice of the world. But a voice that only speaks and never pauses to listen is not a conversation. It is a drowning.

---

So I sit in the Listening Hollow, and the moss courts my flanks, and I have a decision in me that is too large for my mouth.

The A'shai do not build monuments. We do not leave. The Cathedral is our bones and our grandmothers' bones and the loam beneath is the bodies of everyone who came before, gone soft and black and feeding the roots. To say *the water is rising and will not stop* is to say *we must one day climb past the canopy into the misted vault and find another mountain, another drowned forest, or none at all.* It is to say the balance we keep is not eternal but only a long, long pause. It is to wake a patient people into the knowledge of time.

I think of Tavu's hand on mine. *We do not hold it up. We only listen for when it grows tired.*

It is tired, Tavu. I can hear it. The world is tired, and it is leaning, slowly, the way a great trunk leans for a hundred years before the day it does not lean but falls.

There is a young one coming now along the spiraling ramp—I hear the careful place of her webbed feet, the *shei'lo* of disturbed water running off her shoulders, the nervous flutter of her nictitating membranes. She is bringing me my bowl. She is the one I have been watching. Her listening is the deepest of her generation; she presses her hand to the wood the way I did, the way Tavu did, as if asking it a question rather than telling it an answer.

When she sits beside me, I will not tell her the whole of it. Not yet. That would be a flood, and we are not a flooded people; we are a people who channel water through hollowed creepers, gently, away from the living rooms, into pools where it can be held and understood.

I will take her long fingers in my old ones. I will press them to the root.

I will say, with my hand and not my mouth: *Feel. Not the weight. The weight you know. Feel underneath the weight, the slowest note, the one your bones name before your ears.*

And I will let her listen, and the rain will go on hissing its endless silver liturgy through the vaulted dark, and she will frown, and not understand, and I will say nothing more today.

This is how we shape a thing here. Not by force. By guiding a new shoot, season after season, toward the place it must grow to hold the structure up.

I have, perhaps, a few cycles of the moss-bloom left in me. Enough to teach one listener to hear the world growing tired. She will teach another. And another. So that when the water finally speaks the word we have no word for, there will be A'shai who heard it coming, who know the high paths through the canopy, who carry the songs and the resin-glass and the hundred words for wetness up and out into whatever waits in the grey.

That is all stewardship is, in the end. You tend a thing you will not live to see fall. You listen so that others, after you, will know how to climb.

She is here. She is sitting. The bowl is in my hands, the water in it cool and clean and tasting faintly of the mist.

"Vai'shu-na," she says softly. "What does the wood say today?"

I drink. I set the bowl down. I reach for her hand.

"Listen," I tell her. "We have a long time. But not forever. Let me show you the difference."
