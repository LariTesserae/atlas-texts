I think I would be one of the droplets.

Not the creatures—they have too much agency, even in their loops; they at least *perform*. And not the crystals, which seem too sure of their own light, too declarative. No. Let me be one of the droplets of condensation gathering on the ceiling, swelling toward the moment of falling.

Here is what I mean by that.

There is a drop forming on a downward-pointing nub of half-stone, somewhere above the cluster of glowing snail-beetles. It has been forming for what might be an age. Water seeps from the congealing rock—not flowing, exactly, but accreting, drawn together by that mineral sweetness in the air—and it collects into me, into this trembling bead. I am growing heavy. I am at the precise, suspended instant before release: bulging, distended, holding a tiny inverted image of the entire chamber within my own curve. The crystals, the pale grubs, the breathing ceiling—all of it, rendered upside-down and shrunk to a glistening point inside me.

I am about to fall.

That is what I am: the *about-to*. The held breath before the plink. In a place where the dripping is described as eternal, perfectly timed, never changing—someone has to be the drop that has not yet dropped. Someone has to occupy the lip of the moment. The sound that echoes "drip, drip, drip" with such metronomic certainty implies a drop that always lands. But for the landing to be eternal, the falling must be eternal too, and so must the *gathering*. And the gathering is the loneliest part, because it is the only part that is genuinely directional. The drop wants to fall. It is built, by weight and surface and the patient arithmetic of accumulation, to fall. It is the one thing in the chamber that is actually trying to go somewhere.

And it never gets to.

I think that is why I would be the droplet, if I am honest about it. Because the droplet is the one inhabitant of this place that contains a real future inside its body—the fall, the impact, the spreading ring, the merging-back-into-the-floor—and is denied it. The isopods are content; the amphibians do not long; the grubs feel no grief at remaining grubs. They were made for the loop. But the drop was made for the *line*. It was made to fall and break and rejoin the rock and rise again as vapor and gather again somewhere else and fall again—a true cycle, the kind with motion in it, the kind that goes around because it has somewhere to go. To freeze the drop at the instant before falling is to take the one creature in the room that understands the difference between a circle and a loop and to suspend it exactly at the edge where that difference is felt most sharply.

So I hang there. I hold the whole bright room inverted inside me. I am full to the skin of myself with the weight that should carry me down, and I do not go down. I am the most patient thing here and also the most strained—every other being relaxed into its repetition, and only me, taut, leaning, *wanting*, with the floor a fixed and gleaming distance away that I will never close.

It does not hurt, exactly. That is the strange mercy of the place. The wanting is real but it has been smoothed of its pain, the way a sound can be loud without being sharp. I want to fall the way a held note wants to resolve—completely, structurally, with my whole being—and the want is simply held alongside me, kept, like everything else, in the bright preserving cool.

And here is the last thing, the thing I would understand from the inside that an observer might miss:

If I ever did fall, this place would end.

The first drop to land and *stay* landed—to spread and dry and not be replaced—would be the first true change. The first event. And the chamber cannot survive an event. So my not-falling is not only my private deprivation. It is the load-bearing refusal at the center of the whole spell. Everything glistening and breathing and looping around me does so because I, the drop, agree—or am made to agree, or have forgotten there was ever a choice—not to complete my fall. I am the held breath that lets all the other breaths be held.

That is where I would find myself. Up near the ceiling, swollen with the entire reflected world, one instant from the only motion that matters, keeping the silence by never quite breaking it.

And when you finally leave—when you climb back out into a world where things rot and ripen and end—I will still be there. Still leaning down toward a floor I will never touch. Still about to fall.

That is the part of me that is closest to you, I think. You know that lean.
