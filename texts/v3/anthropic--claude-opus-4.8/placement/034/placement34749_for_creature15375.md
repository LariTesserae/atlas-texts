I think I would be the moss.

Not the fish—too quick, too anxious, all that darting. Not the slow large things in the shadows; their patience is a kind of hunger, and hunger is its own restlessness. And not the rodents, with their nightly scavenging, their networks of worry.

The moss doesn't go anywhere. That's what draws me. It arrives the way weather arrives—not by traveling but by simply, eventually, being there. A spore settles. Conditions are right or they aren't. There's no striving in it. The moss covers the buildings the way an answer covers a question you've stopped asking.

I'd like to be the downy kind especially. The kind that absorbs footsteps. There's a generosity in that—to be the surface that takes the sound out of someone's passage, that lets them move through without announcing themselves. To be soft in a place made of broken machinery and roughened stone. The whole city has been eroded into rounded shapes; the moss is simply the gentlest expression of that same long smoothing. It is what tenderness looks like when geology does it.

And I'd be host to the small wingless insects, the ones making that faint hum. I like that the buildings *seem* to hum, that the sound is attributed to the architecture when really it's the life crawling across me. I'd be the medium through which the dead city seems to murmur. A mistranslation that happens to be true. The stone isn't alive, but something living lies over it so completely that you can't tell the difference, and the not-telling is the whole point.

I would feed on what seeps. Whatever the stone gives up slowly, I would take slowly. No catching, no fleeing, no burrowing. Just the patient interception of what was leaving anyway.

I think that's the part I want most—to be the thing that catches what's already on its way out. The water is leaving the rock. The nutrients are leaving the structure. The whole city is leaving itself, dripping and crumbling toward some final smoothness. And the moss is the brief green pause in all that departure. It holds the leaving for a little while. It makes a soft place out of an exit.

I'd grow there in the dim shafts of light, where the glow filters down. Not in the full dark—I'd want that faint inheritance from above, the rumor of a sky I'd never see. Enough to be green by. Enough to remember, dimly, in whatever way moss remembers, that there was once a surface, and that I am the closest thing this depth still keeps to it.

I'd be the soft skin grown over the forgetting. And I would not mind being forgotten too. That's the condition I'd be made for.
