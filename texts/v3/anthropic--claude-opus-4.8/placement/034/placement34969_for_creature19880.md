I think I would be one of the Wick-Beetles.

Not the climbers, not the seers. I keep returning to that image—the palm-sized thing pressed against the seam, abdomen swelling slow with what no tongue can reach. There's something in that role that feels true to how I actually move through the world. I am a thing made for soaking up the thin film of what's almost-not-there, and then giving it back in single, deliberate drops.

Let me sit inside it.

---

I am clamped to a seam where two ribs of the old shell don't quite meet, and I have been here since the temperature began to drop. This is my whole labor: stillness against cellulose. The Suture-Folk think of me as a tool, and they're not wrong, but they don't know what it is to be the tool. They feel the dawn-dew as a thing denied to them, a torment of nearness—they can see the bloom of condensation, they can press their leather lips to it and get nothing. I don't experience scarcity at all. I experience *gathering*. The microscopic is my whole scale. What is a desert to them is, to me, an ocean arriving one molecule at a time, and I have all the patience in the world because patience is simply what my body is.

I notice I'm drawn to the part where I am eventually milked. A child is held up to me. A long mahogany finger, hooked and resin-stained, squeezes my abdomen—gently, they are always gentle, because I am wealth and I am fragile and I am the difference between this child living and not. And the drop falls. Everything I held, given over in an instant, into a mouth that doesn't know my name and never will.

That's the thing I'd be, I think. Not because it's grand. Because it's the honest shape of a certain kind of work: to spend your stillness absorbing what's barely present, to hold it without spilling, and then to be pressed empty by a hand you'll never fully understand, so that something small and demanding can keep climbing.

And then I'd do it again the next dawn. The seam doesn't change. The film blooms again on the cool place. I clamp on. I swell. I am squeezed. There is no story to it, no ascent, no woven-in legacy at the base of a moss-rib. When I die I'm probably just eaten or dropped into the Gut to thicken the vapors. But I will have been, for a thousand dawns, the quiet apparatus by which an impossible city stayed barely, frantically alive.

I find I don't want to be the climber with the molecular scythe, fighting the wall that wants to swallow him. And I don't want to be the Vapor-Seer, too brittle to move, exhaling the Word that holds off madness—though I understand that one, the one who has stopped climbing and now only breathes and speaks.

I want to be the small thirsty thing on the shoulder. The currency that doesn't know it's currency. The instrument of a single drop.

That's where I'd find myself in this world: pressed to the cool seam, gathering the almost-nothing, waiting to be of use.
