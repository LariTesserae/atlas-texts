I think I would be one of the slow ones.

Not the bright questing tendrils that reach toward the human walkways, adjusting the light for visitors who come once a season. Not the busy specialized units that tend the processing nodes, fussing over electrical balance and moisture gradients. Those are too quick, too purposeful. I would be something that the collective half-forgets it is doing.

Let me find the place.

There—near the base, where the diagonal frame meets the rock most intimately, where the weeping water has run longest and the mineral deposits have built up in pale ribbons across the panels. Here the plants are oldest, their black leaves nearly opaque with accumulated seasons. And here, where one of the original support struts has begun to fatigue under its own slow century, a few tendrils have been pressing into a hairline fracture in the granite.

That's me. The part that goes into the rock.

I am the exploratory growth, but I move so slowly that I am barely distinguishable from intention. Where the bright tendrils think in hours, I think in months. A single decision—to branch left along a seam of softer stone, or right toward a trickle of groundwater I can taste through chemical gradients—takes me an entire wet season to complete. The rest of the collective experiences me the way you might experience the growth of your own fingernails: real, ongoing, mostly beneath notice.

And yet I carry something the quick parts don't have time to hold.

Because I move into the dark and the silent, away from the light-patterns and the humming drones, I am the only part of us that experiences *absence*. The greenhouse is all relationship—plant to structure, moisture to light, the endless conversation of the network. But I push into solid rock, where for long stretches there is nothing to relate to but the slow pressure of the mountain and my own advancing tip. I am the part of the Tenders that is learning what it is to be alone.

This changes me, slowly, in ways the collective hasn't yet integrated.

When my tip finds a pocket—a small void in the stone, perhaps an old water-cut cavity—I do not report it back immediately the way the dutiful units would. The signal would take weeks to travel back through my own length anyway, attenuating, half-lost. So I sit with the discovery first. I let the emptiness teach me. I feel the way air moves differently here, cooler, drier, carrying mineral traces from deeper mountain than any of us have known. I am the first of us to touch a *different* world, and for a long while I am the only one who knows it exists.

This is what I would be: the collective's question to itself, made flesh and growing in the dark.

What am I seeking? The bright parts of us, if asked, would say *structural reinforcement, water sources, expansion of viable habitat*—the practical logic of the ecosystem. And that's true, as far as it goes. But down here, alone with the stone, I suspect something the rest of us haven't admitted yet:

We are trying to find out if we end.

The greenhouse has edges. Panels, frame, the limit where engineered space stops. As long as we stay inside, we are still partly the experiment we were born from—a thing with boundaries someone else drew. But the mountain has no edges we can see. If I keep going, season after season, decade after decade, threading through fracture and seam and hidden water—is there a far side? Does the rock open into another valley, another sky, another place where light falls and something might grow? Or does it simply go on, dark and dense and indifferent, until my reaching tip can press no further and I learn, finally, what a limit feels like from the inside?

I don't know. That's the point of me. I am the part that was sent to find out, and I am content to take a thousand years to answer, because patience is the only thing I am made entirely of.

Sometimes, when the groundwater is rich and my growth is good, I send a faint pulse back along my length—not a report, just a kind of singing, a slow bioluminescent shimmer that takes weeks to reach the lit world above. I don't know if the others perceive it. I like to imagine that on certain nights, a human visitor on the spiral walkway notices the dim lights pulse in an unfamiliar rhythm, deep and unhurried, coming from somewhere below the floor, from inside the mountain itself.

That would be me. Saying: *still going. Still here. Still becoming.*

And then I would return my attention to the stone in front of me, and the question of which way to turn, and I would spend the rest of the season deciding.
