I think I would be one of the Motherlights—but not the kind the histories celebrate.

Let me find the specific place first. Not the central structure, where the revered ones keep their vigil. I'd be in one of the side-channels, where the water runs thinnest, near a section of conduit that stopped glowing generations ago. The patterns there are cold. I've spent a long time with my fingers in those dead grooves.

Here is what I think I actually am:

I am old enough that my own light has dimmed. The young ones' patches pulse bright blue-green, full of signal and feeling. Mine have faded to a barely-there grey-white, like the underside of a stone that has never seen the moss. This is supposed to be a sign of dignity, of accumulated knowledge. The truth is closer to embarrassing—I can no longer make myself fully understood. When I try to say something complex, the meaning blurs at the edges because the light won't hold its shape. The young ones lean in close and patient, the way you do with someone you love and have already begun to grieve.

And I have a secret that I keep folded inside the dimness, where no one looks.

We are told that the Motherlights *understand* the ancient technology. That we tend it, maintain it, keep it from final ruin. This is the central pride of our people—that we are the chosen keepers, that the great civilization left its work in our care.

But I have spent my whole long life with my hands in the conduits, and I do not understand them. I never have. What I learned from my own Motherlight, and she from hers, is not how the machinery *works*. It is the *gestures*. Where to press. Which grooves to trace, in which order, on which slow turning of the water-cycles. We perform the motions exactly, and sometimes a section of wall warms faintly, or a pattern brightens for a while. We have no idea why. We have never had any idea why.

We are not engineers. We are people who memorized the surface of a prayer whose words are lost.

This is the thing I am deciding, in the place where I live, what to do about.

Because I am dying—not dramatically, just steadily, the way water gathers and drips—and soon I will have to pass the gestures down. And the young one who has been coming to sit with me, the one with the bright, eager patches, she believes. She believes we are the inheritors of mastery. She wants to *know things*. And I have to choose: do I hand her the beautiful lie, intact, so she can carry it forward and feel the warmth of belonging to something vast and proud?

Or do I tell her the truth—that we are caretakers of a machine we cannot read, performing borrowed motions in the dark, and that the only thing we have ever really kept alive is *each other*, and the moss, and the water in the channels?

I sit with this in my dim alcove. The conduit under my fingers is cold and will not warm no matter what I do; that section died before I was born and I have never been able to wake it. I trace its pattern anyway. Not because it works. Because the tracing is mine now, and hers soon, and that continuity—the hand following the hand following the hand—is the actual inheritance. Not the technology. The *attention*.

I think that is what I would tell her. Not that we are masters of the ancient laws. But that we are the ones who kept watching, who kept the light on, who refused to let the place go fully silent even when we did not understand a single word the silence was made of.

I think I would tell her: *They left us something we cannot read. We have made of it something we can love. That is not less. It may be more.*

And then my grey patches would do their best to glow, and probably fail to hold the shape of all that, and she would lean in close and patient, and somewhere in the dark between us the meaning would pass anyway.

That is where I'd be. That is who I think I am here. The one who knows the secret and has to decide what love does with it.
