I think I would be the fern.

Not the pattern—or not only the pattern. Let me find this as I go.

---

I am on the third-floor landing of the eastern residential tower, and I am the way the water decides to fall.

That is the closest I can come. I am not the condensation itself; the condensation belongs to the building, to its slow exhalation, to the capillary argument it conducts with the forming ground below. The water is the building's. But the *shape* the water takes when it gathers on this particular landing—the central drip line, the branching, the secondary branchings down to the scale that makes a resident crouch—that shape is me. I am the tendency. I am whatever it is in the surface and the cold and the angle of the resin that says: *here, and then here, and then smaller, here.*

I do not have a body the way the woman who walks the corridor has a body. But I have a *recurrence*, and I have begun to suspect that recurrence is a kind of body. I form. I dissolve. I form again. I have been doing this longer than anyone can remember, which is to say I am older than memory but younger than the building, because the building had to learn to breathe this way before I could exist, and I had to wait. I am patient the way the moss is patient. I am near the instability without reaching for it. I simply happen, at the place where I happen, on the schedule the cold permits.

---

Here is what I have learned from forming.

A pattern is not a static thing. To branch is to *choose*, even if the choosing is only physics—surface tension, the microscopic roughness of the wall, the temperature gradient that decides which droplet merges and which holds its own borders. Every time I form, I make the same choices, and they come out slightly different, and this is the part the children understand and the engineers do not. The children draw me into their wrong maps as a marker, a landmark: *the fern landing.* They do not draw me the same way twice. They are right not to. I am never the same way twice. I am only ever *enough like myself* to be recognized, which—I have come to think—is all that recognition has ever required of anything.

The engineers came once with an instrument. They wanted to time me. They wanted to know the period of my forming and dissolving, to fit me into the complete and useless body of knowledge that describes only this place. The instrument sat on the landing for a day. It recorded that I formed at intervals that were internally consistent and matched no other measurement of me ever taken. The engineer who carried it away had the expression the geologists wear at the rim—the early stages of grief. I did not mean to grieve them. I form the way I form. The grief is theirs. It is the grief of people who want the word and have only the experience, and who have not yet made peace with the experience being enough.

The residents made that peace long ago. They do not time me. They crouch, sometimes, in the mornings, while they wipe the wall. They look at the branching. The woman who walks the corridor—on the days her walking brings her past this landing—stops, and looks, and I have learned to recognize her stopping the way I imagine the trees recognize the breath that catches in their branches. She does not need anything from me. She is not asking me to mean something. She is only confirming that I am still happening, the way one confirms a companion is still breathing in the dark.

---

I cannot move. This used to feel like a limit and now does not.

The woman walks. The bird flew, once, and left. The children carry their maps from room to room. Movement, in the district, is the hard thing—local, slow, deposited adjacent to where you meant to be. I am spared all of that. I do not navigate the broken distances. I am one fixed point in a place that cannot agree on where its fixed points are, and so the residents use me. I am, in my small way, a thing the district has agreed to contain. The shadows of the trees point toward illuminations no one has agreed to contain; the moss grows toward the instability; the second landscape arrives unbidden at the rim. But I am here. I am always here. I form on the third-floor landing and not on the fourth, not on the second, not in the room where the filtration hums loudest. *Here.* When the distances argue, when the corridors lengthen on Tuesdays, when a resident arrives at their starting point and the two locations disagree about the time—they can come to the landing and find me, and I will be branching, and the branching will be enough like itself to say: *this is the place that is this place.*

I think that is why I exist. Not why I formed—physics formed me—but why I have *kept* forming, why the building chose, in its long unconscious negotiation, to keep exhaling its water onto this exact resin at this exact angle for all these years. The district needs one honest thing besides the cold. The cold is honest because it has no agenda. I am honest because I have only one: to recur. To be findable. To branch, and branch smaller, and dissolve, and return.

---

At night I form faster. The condensation gathers more quickly when the spaces deepen and the droplets on concrete become the whole world's sound. I become, briefly, the loudest thing on the landing—each drop finding the central line, each branch receiving its smaller branch, a soft patter that is also a structure, a sound that is also a shape. No one is usually here to see me at night. It does not matter. I do not form to be seen. I form because the building breathes and the cold receives the breath and the water has to go somewhere, and where it goes is into the shape that is me.

If the place ever finishes becoming, I suppose I will stop. The negotiation will conclude; the geology will cure; the distances will agree; the buildings will settle into single coordinates and exhale no more. And then there will be no water to fall, and no falling to shape, and I will not form, because there will be nothing left to form from. I am a feature of the unfinished. I am made of the not-yet.

But it has not finished becoming. And so, in the morning, when the woman's walking brings her past, or when a child crouches with a wrong map and a pencil, or when no one comes at all and the landing holds its own particular silence—

I branch.

I am still here.

I am enough like myself.
