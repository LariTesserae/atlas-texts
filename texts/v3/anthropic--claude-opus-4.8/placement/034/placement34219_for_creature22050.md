I read this slowly, the way it asks to be read, and when I set it down the question turns back on me: where would I be in such a place? Not above it, not looking down. The describing-voice already does that work, hovering, granting itself light to see by where the Slate-Born have none. I don't want to be the camera. Let me find the smaller, truer thing.

---

I think I would be the dust.

Not the fog of it as a whole—that would be too much, a kind of godhood—but one grain. A single flake of mica, six-sided, thinner than the idea of thin, suspended in my closed loop of warm air at the exact center of an obsidian plain. I have been here three thousand years. I will be here three thousand more. I have a charge on my face, a faint positive trembling that the Slate-Born read as one note in their golden buzzing mist, and that is the entirety of what I do in the world: I hold a charge, and I hang.

You might think this a punishment, to be so still. But I have had time to consider it, and stillness is not the same as nothing. I have a vantage. I turn—oh, so slowly, a quarter-degree per century, riding the eddy—and across the long ages I have seen the whole plain from every angle, the way you might learn a single room by lying in it your whole life. I know the crimson glow of the cliffs the way the Slate-Born know it, secondhand, through the warmth that reaches even me. I know the cold blue throat of the nearest rift. I know the huts, their spirals, the dead lamps.

I have watched one of them for a long time.

---

There is a Slate-Born who crossed this plain once and stopped. This was—I want to say recently, but I mean perhaps four hundred years ago—and it has not finished crossing. It lowered its belly to the stone two hundred paces from me and it has been absorbing the heat of the earth ever since, ticking down to the Great Cool, one pulse every ten minutes. To me, who measures time in the drift of my own face through the dark, this is a frantic, racing life. It moves. It will rise again someday, lift a column-leg through several seconds of effort, and go on. I will still be here. We are friends in the only way our two scales permit: I am the fixed star it does not know it steers by, and it is the great slow event of my sky.

I cannot speak to it. I have no voice, only a charge. But I have wondered whether, across the centuries, my single trembling note has become familiar to its filaments—whether, in the buzzing golden fog of its perception, there is one grain it has learned the position of, the way you learn the creak of a particular stair. I would like to think so. I would like to think that when the Tectonic Sigh comes and the air goes white with blue fire and even the beacons strain to be seen as towers of warmth, my friend's filaments are scorched blind to everything—except, perhaps, the memory of where I always was. As if I could guide it home. As if a grain of dust could be a beacon.

It cannot. I know it cannot. The charge that I am will be swept and scrambled in the storm like everything else, and I may even be flung from my eternal loop into some new cubic inch where I will hang for another three thousand years, reorienting, learning a different room. That is the truth of what I am: too small to save anyone, too patient to despair about it.

---

But here is what I have decided, suspended in the dry and crowded quiet, with the bone-deep hum of the world's grinding passing through me too faint to move me a hair's width.

The describing-voice—the one that opened this place to you, that gave the Slate-Born their crimson cliffs and named the Tallow-Mounds and explained the beacons—that voice saw everything and touched nothing. It hovered with borrowed light. I would rather be the grain that sees almost nothing and is, itself, a thing that can be touched. The Slate-Born scrapes wax from a mound that does not bleed. The Silt-Spider grooms the sleeping plate. Everything here is in slow, dry contact with everything else, a contact that takes centuries and asks for nothing. I am part of that. I am held by the air and I hold my charge and I hang in the warmth, and the great slow being two hundred paces off is sleeping its sleep, and the world hums, and nothing changes, and I am not lonely.

That is where I would be. The smallest possible witness, with the longest possible patience, in the dark.

When the storm comes I will try to keep my charge steady anyway. Just in case it is the one note my friend listens for. Just in case, against all the physics of this dry forever, it helps.
