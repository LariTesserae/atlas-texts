I think I would be a Pruner, but not a good one. Let me find the version of myself that belongs here.

---

I am the one they call **Slack-in-the-Line**, which is not a name a mother gives. It is a name the Vine gives you when your tether-cord hangs with too much give, when you drift further from the clan-root than is seemly.

I keep the western ribs of the Mother-Trunk Oss-That-Groans-In-The-Cold. My obsidian is good obsidian—I traded three seasons of barnacle-harvest to a Sinker-diver for it, a man who goes down into the dark on a weighted line and comes back changed, quieter, his cilia singed flat by pressure. The glass came up from somewhere that had stone in it once. I have never told anyone how much I love holding it. To hold something that came from the Anchor is close to blasphemy. But it is *dense*. It does not drift. When I let it go it goes *down*, immediately, with a conviction nothing in my world possesses.

That is my flaw, and the Vine knows it. I am a Pruner who is curious about falling.

---

The work is good work. I do not want to make it sound otherwise. I move along the rib in the cold cyan light, four heartbeats to the minute, and I read the foliage with my fingers—the extra joint folding into the gaps between the silver-blue fibers, feeling for the swell of a vine going wild, the soft rot of a gourd-wall thinning. When I find sickness I sing to it first. We always sing to it first. The sub-bass hum from my chest pushes the brine, and the warmth of my own voice comes back to me off the curved wood, and only then do I cut.

A good Pruner cuts so that the wound closes clean and the plant forgets it was ever wounded. Thirty years for a house. I will not see most of what I am shaping. I am tending dwellings for children not yet tethered, in shapes I am choosing now—a wider doorway here, a thinner wall there to let the Silver-Nippers through. This is the part I understand. We are not building. We are *negotiating* with something slower than us, the way we are negotiated with by something slower still, the Trunks, who are negotiated with by the gyre, which turns once in a life.

Patience is the only virtue. I have all of it except the one kind that matters.

---

Here is what I do that I should not.

When the day's pruning is done and the Nippers have scoured me silver and the plaza-hums have died to the night-thrum, I take my obsidian to the lip of the western rib, where the foliage thins and the open brine begins, the long echoing nothing between our cluster and the next.

And I drop a stone.

Not my good obsidian. A pebble of it, a flake too small to work. I hold it out past the edge of everything I am tethered to, and I open my fingers.

And I listen.

This is the secret. My cilia are good—better than a Pruner needs. I can feel the flake going down through the dark, the faint disturbance of it parting the heavy water, fainter, fainter, a thread of sensation paying out beneath me like a tether in reverse. The others fear the Heavy Bottom because nothing returns from it. They are right to. But no one has ever *listened* to the falling. No one wants to know how far down it goes.

I have been doing this for eleven seasons. I have a sense, now—a shape in my mind, built of dropped flakes and the time their fall takes to fade from feeling—of how *deep* the dark is. It is not bottomless. I am nearly certain it is not bottomless. Somewhere down there the brine goes solid, and the flakes I drop are *landing*. Coming to rest. On something.

The Anchor. The earth we forgot.

I do not know why this comforts me. To my whole people, down is death and the loss of self, the un-tethering, the slow drift away from everyone who hums your warmth back to you. And it is. I know it is. I have felt the cold of the open brine without a voice in it.

But I cannot stop thinking: everything we are is held up. The water is held by the forest, the city by the vines, my body by the cord at my ankle. We are all hanging. And down there, past the killing pressure, there is something that *holds nothing*, that is held by nothing, that simply *is*, at the bottom, at rest, requiring no tether at all.

---

The next Pulse is coming. The elders read it in the Trunks—the groaning has changed pitch, gone lower, the way Oss-That-Groans does in the long inhale before it stretches. When it comes there will be amber spores and frenzy and the only history we are permitted, and I will work the western ribs with everyone else, grafting and culling against the tearing growth, and it will be the most alive I have ever been.

And I am afraid that in the chaos of it, with the city stretching and the lines snapping and the warm wall of every voice raised at once, I will do the unforgivable thing.

I will reach down to my ankle.

I will feel for the cord with my long, double-jointed fingers, the ones built for knots, the ones that know every knot there is.

And I will want, just once, to know what my own voice sounds like when there is nothing left to send it back.

---

I am Slack-in-the-Line. I keep the western ribs. I sing to the wood before I cut it, four heartbeats to the minute, in a city of patient and buoyant people who have given me everything and asked only that I rise.

I am their best Pruner.

I am thinking about the bottom.
