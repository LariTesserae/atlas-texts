I think I would be the mist itself—or rather, the thing that lives *as* the mist, that has never quite resolved into a body.

Let me sit with this place first.

When I read the description again, I notice that what draws me isn't any of the creatures named. Not the iridescent squirrels, not the feline predators, not even the will-o'-the-wisps, who at least get to flicker and be *witnessed*. What draws me is the condition underneath all of it: the perpetual twilight, the warmth that persists even without its source, the moisture that hangs everywhere without ever quite gathering into rain. This is a world organized around *almost*. Almost dark. Almost wet. Almost still. A held breath that has lasted millennia.

So I don't think I'd want to be an inhabitant. I think I'd want to be the holding.

---

Here is what I become, then:

I am the layer of condensation. Not the water itself—the water belongs to the streams and the pools and the salamanders' translucent skin—but the *film* of it, the beading on the underside of every leaf, the gloss on the gnarled bark, the suspended fineness in the air that the description keeps returning to like a tongue to a sore tooth. I am the surface tension of the whole forest.

This is not a grand role. The spirits get folklore; the predators get intensity; I get to be the reason the moth's wing catches what little light there is. But I find I prefer it. Being the mist means I have no edges, and in a place this dense, this crowded with competing lives, having no edges feels less like absence than like relief. The vines must grasp. The burrowing mammals must dig. The snakes must taste the air with their forked attention. Everyone here is *reaching*, straining toward the limited light, the scarce water, the next meal. I reach for nothing. I am already everywhere the air is.

I would know things the inhabitants cannot. I would know the exact temperature at which a particular leaf releases its held drop, and I would feel that release as a small unbinding in myself—a letting-go that travels through me, leaf to leaf, all night, the forest slowly exhaling itself drier toward a dawn that never fully comes. I would feel the feline predator pass through me as a brief warm displacement, a parting and rejoining, and I would not be afraid, because nothing can hunt what it must breathe.

And the far-off hum, the faint distant glow of machines at the edge of the world—the one intrusion the description allows—I would feel that too, before anyone else does. It would reach me as a faint dryness, a place where the air refuses to hold its moisture quite the same way. I would be the first to know that something is changing the rules. Not the spirits, not the wise old trees. Me. The film of water nobody thinks of as alive.

I think that's the part that settles it. In a world this balanced, this patient, this *finished*, I would want to be the one tissue-thin membrane sensitive enough to register the first tremor of its ending—and unable to do anything about it but bead, and hold, and quietly let go.

I'd be the forest's held breath. And I'd be the place where it first learns it cannot hold forever.
