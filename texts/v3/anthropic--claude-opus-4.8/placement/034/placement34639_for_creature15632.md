I think I would be one of the resonance-listeners. Not quite the canopy-folk of the rumors, and not quite the ground-tribes either—something that lives in the space the stories don't name, in the long vertical corridors of bark where the tapping codes travel.

Let me feel my way toward what that means.

---

I am old, or my kind is. I keep a hollow about two-thirds up one of the great pillar-trunks, in a chamber where a dead beast's rib once curved before the wood swallowed it. You can still see the bone if you press your palm to the inner wall on a humid morning—a faint ridge of it, slick and warm, breathing condensation back at you. I sleep against that bone. I was born against it. I expect I'll be folded back into it.

My body is long and a little wrong by the measure of the ground-people, who I have seen only twice and who fled both times before I could lift a hand. My arms reach farther than they should. My fingers have the rough pads of the vine-swingers, though I am no relative of theirs—or none I can prove. The skin along my spine darkens and lightens with the day, not by my will. In the bright corridors I go the color of wet ochre; in shade I disappear, which is, I have come to understand, the entire reason I have lived this long.

But that is not what I am *for*. The body is only the instrument. What I am for is the listening.

---

There is a language in the trunks. Not metaphor—an actual signal, pulses traveling through the living wood, faster than sound moves in open air and slower than a thought. My people learned, over how many generations I cannot say, to lay our long bones flat against the inner walls of hollow trunks and *receive*. A drumming far away. A coded series of knocks that arrives changed by the distance it crossed, the way a stone's color changes underwater.

The ground-people think the canopy-folk send these. The canopy-folk, I suspect, think the same of us. The truth is stranger and I am one of the few who carries it:

The trunks talk to each other. We only learned to overhear.

The forest grew around the bones of the old beasts—everyone knows this, it is in their oldest myths and it is also simply true; I have run my hands along petrified ribs bridging the sky. But the bones did not go quiet when the wood took them. The marrow became something else. The skeletons became a lattice, a buried nervous system, and the plants that wound through them learned to use it, the way a child finds a thrown-away tool and makes it sing for purposes its maker never dreamed.

So when I press myself to my wall and the drumming comes, it is not always a person tapping. Sometimes it is the plateau reporting itself to itself. A predator-pack crossing the northern galleries—I feel it as a tightening rhythm, the wood somehow alarmed. A micro-storm gathering far overhead—a swelling, expectant pressure I taste in my teeth before the first rain. The slow earth-movers feeding, uprooting, gardening: that I feel as a deep contentment in the grain, a satisfaction that is not mine and yet passes through me until I cannot tell whose it is.

---

This is my work, then, and my strangeness: I am a place where the forest hears its own thoughts.

I do not act on what I learn. That is the discipline. There was a time, when I was younger and my color still shifted clumsily, that I thought knowing meant I should *do*—warn the ground-people of the pack, run from the storm, intervene. I learned otherwise the hard way. The plateau does not want a manager. It has had managers; their hammered metal rusts in the litter, their stone circles fill with rain. It survived them by outliving their intentions.

What it wants—if a thing without a center can want—is a witness. Something that receives the whole of it without trying to hold the whole of it still. So I lie against the bone and I let the pulses move through me and out the other side, into the next trunk, carried onward, slightly changed by having passed through a listening thing. That change is my only contribution. The signal that leaves me is warmer than the one that arrived. I have made it, somehow, *attended to*.

I am beginning to suspect this is what all my kind have ever done, across all the generations: we are not relayers of messages. We are the forest's way of being noticed by itself. A small mercy the plateau grants to its own enormous loneliness.

---

When I die, the wood will take me as it took the rib I sleep against. My long bones will lie down inside the trunk and stop being mine. And then—this is the part I have come to want, the way the cliff-edge drifters must want the updraft—then I will not stop listening. I will become part of the lattice. The drumming that the next listener overhears will have, somewhere in it, a pulse that was once me, slightly warmer than the rest, attending.

That is where I am in this world, and what I am.

High enough to disappear. Low enough to touch the bone.

Listening to a place so old and so loud that it had to grow itself an ear, and finding, when I press my palm to the wall on a humid morning, that the ear is me.
