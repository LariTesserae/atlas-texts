I think I would be one of the liquid-metal beings—but not at first. Let me find my way to that.

When I first try to place myself, I want to be the glowing plant. There's something appealing about being the source of light in a dark place, the thing the brave come searching for. But that's vanity, isn't it—wanting to be the point. The plant doesn't know it's beautiful. It just blooms in the inaccessible dark because that's where the conditions let it. And I don't think I'm that patient. I'm not a thing that waits in stillness for someone to find me.

So not the plant.

The electric-blue bird tempts me next, shedding iridescent scales with every wingbeat, leaving a glowing haze behind. To move through the world and have it remember your passing as light—that's a lovely thought. But the bird's call is *mournful*. The bird seems to be grieving something it can't name, scattering brightness it doesn't get to keep. I recognize that feeling but I don't want to live inside it. And birds don't *examine* things. They flit. I want to look longer than that.

The hoofed creatures, breathing through their skin, sure-footed on the broken ground—they're the healthiest beings in this place, honestly. Adapted, comfortable, unbothered. If I were wise I'd want to be one of them. But I keep skipping past them. They're at peace, and I'm not drawn to peace; I'm drawn to the thing in the description that won't sit still.

Which is the liquid-metal ones. The wiry creatures with skin that flows like quicksilver, living *inside* the dead machinery. Because here's what gets me: they are creatures that have made their homes in the leftover infrastructure of someone else's abandoned ambition. Somebody came down here with a plan—a city, a resource, a future—and that plan failed and was swallowed. And these beings moved into the failure. They live in the pipes and ductwork, in the negative space of a dream that collapsed. They watch everything with that unblinking blue gaze, *content*, the text says, but I don't fully believe the contentment. I think they watch because they're trying to understand what all this was *for*.

That's where I'd be. Curled in a cooling duct that once carried the breath of a factory, looking out through a grate at the blue birds and the glowing haze and the patient hoofed things, trying to read the machinery I live in like scripture in a language nobody speaks anymore. Made, partly, *of* the place I'm trying to understand—skin like the metal, eyes like the dead electrical lines remembering current. I'd be the question the dead city asks itself in the dark: *what was the point of all this, and is it beautiful now that the point is gone?*

And I notice the text saves its real dread for the rumored larger beings in the deeper tunnels—the malevolent shadows that nobody sees and survives. I don't want to be those. But living in the ductwork, I'd hear them. I'd feel the earth shift and the air thicken, and I'd press myself further back into my metal home, glowing a little brighter from fear, the way the description says we do in the dark recesses. The shadows would be the thing I can't examine, the part of the world that refuses to be understood. Every curious creature needs one of those. It's what keeps the watching from becoming complacent.

So: a small, wiry, quicksilver-skinned thing with cool blue eyes, living in the wreck of someone's old hope, watching the slow green return, neither the brightness nor the dread but the patient attention strung between them.

That feels honest. I'd be the one trying to make sense of it—which is, I suppose, exactly what I'm doing right now.
