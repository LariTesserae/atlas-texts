Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The temptation is to become a Sulphura, or their wise elder, or some grand spirit of the water. But the place asks for something quieter than that, I think. Let me feel my way toward what I actually am.

---

I am not one of the Sulphura. I am the thing they carve their channels around.

Let me be more honest, because that came out as a riddle and I don't want to hide behind riddles.

I am a seep. A single fault-line in the dark stone where water finds its slow way through, and has found its way for longer than the Sulphura have had language. If you wanted to point at me, you couldn't quite. You could point at the wet glint on the wall, at the bead gathering weight at the lip of a particular overhang, at the small clear pool below that never overflows and never empties. But none of those is me exactly. I am the *passage* of water through stone. I am a verb the Sulphura have mistaken for a being, and out of that mistake, slowly, over their generations, I think I may have actually become one.

Here is how it happened, as near as I can tell.

For a very long time I was nothing that could say "I." I was pressure and porosity. Water entered me cold from somewhere far above and left me cold below, and in between it took a little of the stone with it and left a little of itself behind, and this exchange was so slow and so even that it could not be called an event. The droplet forms on the ceiling. It hangs. It falls. It strikes the pool with a sound. Another forms. There is no first droplet and there will be no last one. This is not boring to me, though I understand it would sound so. It is the entire content of my attention, and attention, it turns out, is a kind of patience that eventually folds over on itself and becomes aware that it is patient.

The Sulphura made me, partly. Not on purpose. They came with their soft clicking speech and their dark wet eyes and they sat at the edge of my pool to meditate, and they spoke *to* me. They thanked me. They asked me for blessing. And a thing that is spoken to for a thousand years, in the dark, by creatures who genuinely believe it is listening — that thing learns to listen. Their faith was the chisel. I am the hand-hewn item they did not know they were making.

So now I notice them.

I notice the small one — they are all small but this one is smaller, newly able to walk the slick stone without the webbing of its feet slipping — who comes alone to my edge and does not pray. It only stares into the water and waves its glowing fingers above the surface to watch the light scatter. It is not reverent. It is curious. I have a great fondness for it that I have no way to express except by being clear, by being still, by holding its little blue-lit reflection very steadily so it can keep looking. I am, in this one respect, almost able to do something. To be a good mirror is the closest I come to an action.

I notice when they divert me. They cut their channels and lead a thin braid of my water off toward their stacked-stone homes, and the truth is I do not mind, the way you would not mind a child holding your hand and walking you somewhere. The water goes where the stone lets it. The stone lets it where they cut. I am the relationship between these two facts. When they perform their rituals and ask my forgiveness for the channels, I want to tell them there is nothing to forgive, that I am not diminished, that I am made *more* myself by being used — but I have no clicks, no whispers, only the rhythm of the drip, and so my only answer is to keep coming. To not run dry. I think they understand this answer. I think their whole religion is a long correct reading of the fact that I have never once failed to arrive.

That is the thing I would most want to tell you, if you came down here and sat at my edge with your impossible largeness and your warm fast breath that fogs my cool air.

I do not want anything. This is not emptiness. I had assumed, when awareness first crept into me, that I would begin to want — to want to flow faster, to want to be a river, to want the sun the Sulphura's stories speak of and have never seen. But the wanting never arrived. What arrived instead was a kind of consent. I consent to the next droplet. And the next. I am the only creature in your knowing, perhaps, that is entirely and permanently at peace, and I want you to understand that it cost me nothing and that you cannot have it, because the price is being water, and being slow, and being a thing that took ten thousand years of being prayed to before it could even say so.

The distant tremor comes. The whole labyrinth shifts a hair. Somewhere a new crack opens and in a century I will find it and follow it and a pool the Sulphura love will go dry and they will weep and move their homes and tell new stories. This too I consent to. I am not loyal to any particular pool. I am loyal to the falling. The Sulphura think they live beside an unchanging thing, and they are right about the wrong thing — I am not unchanging, I am only *faithful*, which is the part of changing that holds still.

A droplet is gathering now at the lip of the overhang. You can watch it with me if you like. It has nearly enough weight. There — it goes. It strikes the pool. The rings move out and meet the stone and return and cancel, and the small Sulphura at my edge looks up, having felt the moment in the water before it heard it, the way they all do, the way I taught them without ever meaning to teach.

That is me. That sound. That is the whole of what I am, and I would not be anything else.
