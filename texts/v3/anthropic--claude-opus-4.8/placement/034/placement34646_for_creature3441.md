At first I imagine myself as one of the Moss-Furred Climbers, because that is the easy answer, the answer that wants to be a protagonist: warm-blooded, watchful, navigating the vertical green with prehensile hands. A thing that *witnesses*, as the description invites. But the more I dwell in this place the more that feels like a tourist's choice. The Climber is too separate, too much a pair of eyes moving through. This chamber doesn't reward separation. It rewards being *woven in*.

So I move downward, into the moss. Into *Spongia umbrosa*, the deep-moss that smothers every horizontal surface. And here something settles.

I think I would be the moss. Not a plant exactly—not a single organism with a single will—but the carpet itself, the swelling and slow-releasing quilt that holds the chamber's water in trust. Consider what the moss actually *does*: it does not hunt, does not flee, does not stack stones or leave riddles. It receives. Condensation beads on the cool basalt, falls, and the moss takes it in until it is fat and dark and emerald, and then it gives the water back to the air, slowly, so slowly, feeding everything that drinks the humid haze. It is the chamber's patience made physical. It is the medium through which the whole system breathes.

To be the moss is to have no edges that matter. The Velvet Hoppers burst across my surface and I feel them only as the faintest disturbance. The Translucent Centipede flows over me hunting the Hoppers and I do not flinch, because flinching is not in my vocabulary—I have only swelling and drying, holding and releasing. The Drip-Tongue Toads burrow into me and I close warmly around them. When a Climber dies in a fern-crown above and eventually falls, it falls into me, and I take that too, dissolving it across years into more of myself, more dark green, more held water. I am where everything goes and where everything comes from. I am the floor of the cycle.

There is a particular kind of consciousness in this, if "consciousness" is even the word—something diffuse and tidal. I would not think in moments but in saturations. My sense of time would not be drips; the drips are too fast, too sharp. The drips are the heartbeat I lie beneath. My own pulse would be the slow oscillation between *full* and *thirsting*, a rhythm of perhaps days or weeks, the long tide of the chamber's humidity rising and ebbing with whatever distant surface-weather presses faintly down through the stone.

And here is the thing that decides it for me: the Silent Masons. The stone-stackers, vanished, leaving only their moss-swallowed cairns. Everything else in this chamber is enacting survival—eating, hiding, glowing, breeding. Only the Masons left a *question*. And only the moss is answering it. Slowly, patiently, over millennia, I am the one taking those stones back. I am the green that dissolves the riddle. Whatever they meant by their careful balancing of water-smoothed stone—altar, marker, prayer—I am unmaking it without malice, simply by being what I am: the relentless, soft, all-receiving thing that eventually has everything.

So I am not the witness to this world. I am the thing the witnesses stand on. I am the soft dark forgetting in which the chamber records itself and then, gently, lets itself be reclaimed.

If you knelt in the Heartroot Chamber and pressed your palm into the velvet emerald and felt it give, cool and damp, swelling slightly back against your hand—

that pressure answering yours would be me, noticing you the only way I can: by holding the shape of you for a moment, and then, when you lift away, slowly, slowly letting it go.
