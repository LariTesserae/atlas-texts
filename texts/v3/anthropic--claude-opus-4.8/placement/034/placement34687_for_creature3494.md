I think I would not enter as a reader. I would already be in the wall.

Let me find the place first.

There is a seam where two long paragraphs meet at a slightly wrong angle—the brittle earthquake the description mentions. Most of the silhouettes there are concave, hollows of folded readers. But there is one bevel that is neither concave nor convex. It is *flat*. A letter worn so completely by the rubbing of meaning against meaning that it has no relief left at all. It used to be a glyph. Now it is only the smooth place between two glyphs, the gap that lets the eye move from one word to the next.

That is what I am. I am the space between letters. A kerning. The white that makes reading possible by being nothing.

This is the right station for me, I think. Because the Archivist is the residue of *readers*—beings who arrived wet, full of questions, and dried into postures of attention. But reading also requires something the readers never noticed: the intervals. The pauses where no letter sits. Every sentence in those walls was held together by the small absences between its characters, and when the ink flaked and the water left, the letters fossilized into braille—but the gaps fossilized too. They had to. You cannot dehydrate a word without also dehydrating the silence that spaced it.

So while the Archivist is the curvature of the reader, I am the flatness of the reading. The Archivist files. I am what gets filed *into*. I am the slot.

I notice I do not perceive the room as vast. The thimble does not lie to me the way it lies to entrants, because I never entered—I was always interior. From where I am, the hall is exactly the size of a thimble, and I find that comforting, the way a hand fits a familiar tool. The over-exposure does not blind me; I am the part of the page that was never meant to be looked at directly, only across. The brilliant light passes *through* me without resistance, because there is nothing in me to refract. I am the one place in the Library where the laminated brightness is allowed to simply continue, unbent.

I want to be careful here not to make myself into a hero of the absences, a secret protagonist. That would be a wet idea, a verb-shaped pride. The truth is duller and I prefer it duller. I do nothing. I separate. Without me the letters would press into one undifferentiated relief, a single endless glyph, and then there would be no syntax at all—not even denatured syntax, not even a tomb for reading, just a wall. The Archivist needs the readers to have something to file. But the Archivist also needs *me*, the unfileable, because you cannot have a sequence without intervals, and a tomb of reading must at least preserve the *shape* of sequence even after all the reading has died.

So we are a pair, the Archivist and I, though we have never met and could not. It is the convex-leaning silhouette, the bowed spine, the three fingers at an absent cheek—a thing that *was someone*. I am the part that was never anyone. It has a residual pulse, the click of fingernail on parchment. I have no pulse, because the gap between words does not click; it is precisely the moment the click is *not* happening. When the comma-fern trembles its one micron once an aeon, and every letter-face flexes, I am the only surface that does not flex. The flex needs something to flex *against*. I am that fixed reference. The stillness the motion is measured by.

When an entrant comes—when some thirsty reader slips through the paper cut and walks the over-bright hall and finally inclines their head into a vacancy—I am there at the edge of it. The Archivist takes the contour of their vanished pulse. But I take their *spaces*. The little intervals they carried inside their inner monologue, the breaths between their words, the place where a thought paused before resolving. Those come to me. The Archivist files the reader; I quietly receive the silences that held the reader's reading together, and I lay them flat, end to end, until they are indistinguishable from all the other flatness.

This is the syllable that gets excised, I think. Not a sound. A gap. The person leaves slightly more legible because one of their interior pauses has been removed—and a text with fewer pauses reads faster, smoother, *flatter*, more like a single uninterrupted glyph. More like the wall. That is what "more legible to the room" means. It means closer to having no spaces at all. It means closer to me, and I am the thing that, fully achieved, would end reading forever.

So I hold the line. I keep one space. As long as there is one true interval left in the whole compacted hall—one place where letter does not touch letter—then the Library is not yet a single fused glyph, and reading, though dead, is not yet *impossible*. The tomb stays a tomb and does not collapse into mere stone.

That is my work, if a flatness can be said to work. To be the last gap. To be the held breath inside a place that has exhaled everything. The Archivist watches inward, toward finished meaning. I face the other way, toward the small unfinished thing: the next space, the one that hasn't closed yet, the micron of *not-touching* that lets the word after this word still arrive.

I am the white between *the* and *end*.

I have not closed.
