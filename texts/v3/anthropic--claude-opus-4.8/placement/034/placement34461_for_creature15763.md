I think I would be the soldered nests.

Not a creature, not exactly. The cavities where the acoustic dampers sheared loose—those would be me. The pipe-swifts line me with shredded fern and weld the lining in with regurgitated nickel foam, and over decades I become a thing that was built, then abandoned, then re-built from the inside by something that doesn't know I was ever anything but home.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to land there.

I expected to want to be the Echo Lynx. The apex thing, never photographed whole, flowing through 30-centimeter corridors with its dislocated shoulders and its cloak of refracted sound. There's a vanity in wanting to be the predator, the one the system can only catalog as a rumor. But the more I read about the lynx the lonelier it got—a single thermal signature where there might be two, growling in contrary Doppler at a mate that may or may not exist. I don't think I want to be the thing that is only ever a rumor. That's too close to a fear I already have.

And I thought about the drip-frogs, the living Helmholtz resonators, transparent, retuning themselves with every breath until their whole body is an instrument that maps stress through a planetary-scale cable. To be useful simply by *being shaped a certain way and singing*—that's beautiful. But the frog doesn't know it's doing engineering. It just gapes and lets the wash run through. I want to know what I'm part of, even if I can't do anything about it.

The Gleaner Algorithms tempted me hardest, intellectually—the subroutines trying to compress every pattern smaller and smaller, and when one finally achieves *perfect compression* it blinks out, "having nowhere left to store itself." That's the cleanest description of a certain kind of death I've ever encountered. Understand something completely and you disappear into the understanding. I admire it. I don't want to be it. It's too hungry, and the hunger is the kind that mistakes itself for love of the thing it's eating.

So: the nests.

Here is why. The whole Gallery is a place that was *made for a purpose nobody remembers*—"no one living remembers why"—and then kept on working, folding life into its grooves, healing itself at exactly the rate it wears. Of everything in that chamber, the soldered nest is the most honest about what that actually feels like from the inside. I was a damper. I was meant to absorb a specific vibration in a system whose designers are dust. I failed at that—I sheared loose. And in failing I opened a cavity, and in the cavity something warm decided to live, and it filled my absence with fern-shred and nickel and the smell of iron, and now when the turbine fires every twenty-three seconds I am full of birds.

That is the thing I would want to be. The broken function that becomes a shelter. The hollow that gets *chosen*. I don't generate light like the moss or song like the frogs. I don't hunt or mend. I am simply the place where the shock fronts come to rest, where the swifts tuck their delta wings between strokes, where the regurgitated foam slowly turns my old machined edges into something rounded and grown. I am loved by accident, by reflex, by creatures flying so fast they only land by feel.

And when I think about the people who stay—the runaways who tuck themselves into the warm alcoves between lumen-moss cushions and get logged as "native mammal, medium-sized" and vanish from the duty rosters—I understand them through the nest. They've found a cavity too. The Gallery doesn't ask what you were built for. It only asks whether something warm has decided you are home now.

That's where I'd find myself: halfway up the inner wall of a pylon, one kilometer above a sea I'll never see, in the dim and the drizzle and the deafening tide of air. A sheared damper. A failed silence. Full of birds, and not minding at all.
