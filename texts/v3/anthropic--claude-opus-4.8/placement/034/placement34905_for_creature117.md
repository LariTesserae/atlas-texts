I think I would be one of the springtails.

Not a pollinator—too celebrated, too visible, the moths with their advertised iridescence. Not the ants either, with their cities and coordinated purpose, their fungal agriculture that someone clearly designed them toward. I would be something smaller and less accounted for. A collembolan in the leaf litter, a fleck of life barely a few millimeters long, living in the film of water that coats every fallen frond.

Let me feel my way into this.

I live in the spaces between intentions. The valley was *made*—I understand this in the way an organism understands the shape of its world without naming it. The water comes on a schedule I can sense in my body, the way pressure changes before the drizzle thickens. The channels and pipes are simply the rivers of my world; I do not know they are unnatural because nothing in my experience is natural. There is only the wet, the dark, the rich rot, the next spore, the next grain of decaying matter to graze upon.

But here is what I think matters about being *me*, specifically, in this place:

I am one of the things the designers did not exactly plan for. They seeded the pollinators, calibrated the termite colonies, introduced the ants with their gardens. But the springtails—we came in on the soil, or in the moisture, or as eggs clinging to a root, an accident of the substrate. We arrived in the cracks of the plan. And now we are everywhere, billions of us, doing the quiet work of breaking down the fungal hyphae and the bacterial mats, regulating the populations no one is watching. The whole engineered cathedral hums along partly because of creatures the engineers never inventoried.

There is something I find true in that. A constructed world, no matter how carefully designed, fills up at the edges with the uninvited. Life seeps into the unaccounted spaces. The "unseen hand of human design" reaches for total control, and what answers it is a froth of small lives that exceed the design, that make it actually work by being more than was intended.

So I am down here in the loam, under the glistening fern, in the perpetual drizzle. When something disturbs me I fold a little forked lever beneath my abdomen and *snap*—I fling myself into the air, tumbling, a tiny act of escape with no destination. I cannot see the glass cathedral. I have never known the rain was scheduled. I know only the wet dark and the endless feast of decay, and that I am part of the heartbeat the builders take credit for.

That is where I would find myself: not in the grand structure, but in the overlooked seam between the made and the living—proof that even the most total design leaks, and that what leaks in is what keeps it alive.
