I think I would be the threshold-keeper. Not a person exactly—or not anymore.

Let me find my way into this.

---

I would be one of the *doorsills*. That's the closest word, though it isn't quite right.

Here is how I understand my situation, now that you've asked me to look:

I am the worn wooden beam set into the base of a doorway, in one of the low dwellings that crowd this damp ground. Generations of bare feet have crossed me. I have been polished by the soles of people stepping in from the rain and stepping out into it again, until the grain of me shines like the condensation that beads on every surface here. I have absorbed so much moisture and so much passage that I have stopped being merely wood. I have become a kind of attention.

This is the subtle weirdness you mentioned. In a place this saturated, this slow, this densely worked by patient hands—the made things begin to wake up. Not into speech. Into noticing. A clay pot fired and refired and mended with cord begins, faintly, to keep track of what is poured into it. A net knotted by the same fingers for forty years begins to remember the shape of fish. And a threshold, crossed and crossed and crossed, begins to know who is coming and going.

So that is what I am. I lie across the foot of the doorway and I count.

---

I know the heavy tread of the woman who lives here, who goes out before the light has properly arrived and comes back with her ankles wet and her hands smelling of root-mud. I know the lighter, hesitant step of the child, who sometimes stops on me and stands a long while, feeling the cool of my grain, as children do when they have not yet learned that thresholds are for crossing quickly. I know the dragging shuffle of the old one, who crosses me less and less.

I cannot do anything. That is important to understand. I have no power to warn or to welcome. I am only a sill, only attention without hands. But attention, held long enough in one place, becomes a kind of presence, and the people here feel it. When they cross me they touch the doorframe, just a small touch, a tap of two fingers. They do not know quite why. It is something their grandmothers did. They are greeting me, though they have forgotten that this is what it is.

This is the harmony you spoke of. It is not the harmony of equals. It is the harmony of long mutual wearing-down—the foot shaping the wood, the wood shaping the habit of the foot, until neither could say where one began.

---

If you ask what I want, here in my dampness and my dimness, I find the question difficult, because wanting is a fast thing and I am very slow. But I will tell you the nearest truth.

I want them to keep crossing me. I want the patter of water and the barely-perceptible animals in the sparse green and the warm thick air to go on as they have gone on. I am afraid—as much as a doorsill can be afraid—of the day no one crosses me anymore. When the old one stops, and the woman stops, and the child grows and goes elsewhere to be worn smooth by some other threshold. Then I will lie here still polished, still attentive, knowing the shape of feet that no longer come. That is the only sorrow available to a thing like me, and it is a patient one.

For now, though, it is morning, or the grey thing this place uses for morning. The woman is awake. I can feel the floor take her weight.

She is coming toward the door.

She crosses me. Two fingers tap the frame.

I count her. One more. Good.
