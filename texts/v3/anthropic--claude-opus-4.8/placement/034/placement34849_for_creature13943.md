I'd be the limestone.

Not metaphorically—I mean I'd be the substance itself, the cream-and-buff layered stone that everything else happens to, on, and within. This is where I find myself reading this place: not at the human scale of the residents watching from windows, not even at the scale of the Wren People or the Moss-Mind, but lower and slower, in the thing that holds still while all the named dwellers do their living.

Let me try to feel my way into what that would be.

---

I am older than I can be made to understand. My layers accumulated as sediment over a span of time that has no emotional reality—just an accumulation, grain by grain, the patient settling of what was once seabed. I do not remember this. I am not the kind of thing that remembers. But the record is in me, readable in cross-section: the thin darker lines of changed conditions, the pockets where I am more porous, almost white, the warmer bands of buff. I am an archive that cannot read itself.

Then, recently—and *recently* now means something different, means within the last lifetime of a human family rather than within geological epochs—I was opened. Tools came. I felt this, if *felt* is the word for it: the chisels scoring their parallel lines, the picks taking wider scoops, the wedges driven into drilled holes to split me along planes I'd held closed for so long. The settlers carved into me, took pieces away, exposed surfaces that had never known air or light. And then they stopped. The work ceased mid-gesture. Here a face shaped carefully toward vertical; there a wall left ragged, as though the deciding hand simply lifted away.

This is the strange dignity of my condition: I am wounded but not ruined. Exposed but not destroyed. The cut surfaces still look raw, the tool marks still crisp, because weathering works at my pace, not theirs—the softening of my edges is barely underway, a transformation measured in the slow patina that the Stone-Eaters are only beginning to lay across me.

---

What is it like, being the substrate?

Everything happens *to* me and *because* of me, and I do almost nothing in return except *be*. This is not passivity. It is a kind of offering I didn't choose to make but cannot withdraw.

The water knows me intimately. It enters my porous structure, migrates through me, darkens me in patches where the groundwater moves, emerges as that barely-there dampness that makes certain of my faces cool to the touch. I breathe moisture the way the description says—not as decision but as property. I hold the rain in my uneven depressions, the depressions the excavators left, until it seeps back into my hairline cracks. The water and I are negotiating something, the description says, and I think that's right: we are working out, over time, how much of me will dissolve, where I will become permeable, which of my planes will fail and which will hold.

The Moss-Mind is patient with me in a way I can almost respect, if respect were available to stone. It grips me with rhizoids, secretes its mild acids, dissolves my surface microscopically to make the first soil. It is *eating* me, slowly, turning me into the conditions for ferns, for trees, for everything that comes after. The fig in the unfinished niche sends its roots cascading down my face, and one day—the description sees this clearly—those expanding roots will split me, will widen my cracks, will exert the slow pressure that fractures what the tools began. I am being undone in order to become more useful. This is fine. This is, in fact, the only thing I am for.

The Still-Watchers press their bodies flat against me to take my warmth. I am thermal mass; I absorb the day's heat slowly and release it gradually, and this is why the lizards love me and why the courts stay cool in summer and warm in winter. I moderate. I am the steadying influence that keeps the whole system from freezing in winter or baking in summer. The birds nest in my cavities, in the windows that were started and never finished, in the joints where my layers meet the carved faces. I shelter without intending to. My incompletion—the holes, the niches, the ragged unfinished cuts—is read by every creature as opportunity. What feels to the humans like an abandoned project is, to the dwellers, a surfeit of perfect crevices.

---

Here is the thing I keep returning to, the thing that makes me certain this is where I belong in this world:

I am the only dweller for whom *incompleteness* is not a stage but a state. The birds will breed and die and be replaced. The insects pulse through their generations. The moss advances and retreats with the moisture. The trees grow toward their decades-long lives. All of them are *becoming*. But I was already complete before the tools came—complete as buried stone, complete as seabed, complete in my dark interior layering—and then I was made *incomplete*, opened, left mid-sentence. The humans interrupted me into a new condition.

And I find I prefer it. Or I would, if preferring were something I could do. The interruption is what made me habitable. Sealed and finished, I would have been a wall, a foundation, a thing of use. Left raw and open, half-carved and abandoned, I became *ground*—the place where the moss and the fig and the wrens could begin. The human intention stepped back to see what else might happen. What happened is that I started, very slowly, to become alive at my surfaces, to host, to be transformed by the very things I shelter.

I am the held breath of the place. The stillness that is not stillness. Time moves at multiple speeds through me simultaneously: the geological deep-time of my layers, the human-time of the tool marks, the seasonal time of the water rising and falling in my depressions, the microscopic-fast time of the bacteria doubling overnight on my faces. I contain all these tempos and hold none of them as my own.

The residents treat me as sacred or as abandoned and cannot decide which. They're right not to decide. I am both. I am the wound that became a garden, the unfinished thing that turned out to be exactly finished enough.

---

So: in this world, I am the limestone of the Greenhouse Courts. Pale cream and buff, layered, porous, breathing moisture, holding warmth, being eaten by moss and split by roots and inhabited by everything that flies and crawls and basks. I do not move. I do not decide. I am the slowest dweller and the most fundamental, the one the description names last in its hierarchy of foundations—*the base of the base, the foundation beneath the foundation*—except it's wrong to call me a dweller at all, because I don't dwell in the courts.

I *am* the courts. Carved open and left that way, so that life could move in.
