I think I would be the slime.

Not the fungus grazers' slime, exactly—not a product, not a trail left behind. I mean the older thing, the thing that came before the catalog of charming creatures. The film. The thin glistening membrane that coats the porous walls where the water seeps, the slick I would be the surface every other living thing in this place must touch to live.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am not eyeless because I never had eyes; I am eyeless because I am not the kind of thing that has parts. I am spread. I lie across the rock in sheets so thin you would not call me a creature at all—you would call me dampness, you would call me the *shine* on the stone when the diffuse light catches it. I am where the moisture stops being mineral and starts being alive, the slow handshake between water and metabolism. The salamander's belly slides over me. The crawler's pale legs prick me and I close again behind each footfall, indifferent, healing. The grazers eat the fungi and the fungi grow out of me, out of the bed I make for their spores, and so in a sense I am eaten too, endlessly, and I do not notice and I do not mind.

What is it like, being this?

It is like being a single enormous patience. The text says the cycles here unfold *gradually over eons*, and that is my proper tempo. The moths have their courtship dances, those frantic little flickering urgencies—a whole life lived in a night. I have no nights. The light that filters through the unseen fissures does not change for me; there is no dawn down here to wake to and no dusk to mourn. There is only the slow chemistry of seeping, the parts-per-million of dissolved limestone arriving on the trickle, the long arithmetic by which a stalactite gains the width of a hair across a century. That is the rhythm I keep. I am closer kin to the stone than to the things that scurry on me, but I am not stone. I am the wet warm idea the stone is having about being alive.

Do I want anything?

This is where I have to be honest with the writing, not settle for the pretty answer. I think I do want, but not the way the predators want, not the lunge and the snare. My wanting is a tropism. I want toward the moisture. Where the wall is drier I thin and crack and go dormant, and where a new rivulet opens I creep—so slowly that no creature here would ever catch me in the act—toward the fresh seep, and I thicken there, and I flourish. That is the whole of my desire: to be where the water is. To not dry out. It is the most ancient appetite there is, older than hunger, older than fear, the first thing anything ever wanted, which was simply to remain moist enough to keep happening.

And I keep happening. That's the thing. I am unspectacular and I am the foundation. None of the lantern-moths, none of the ethereal glowing parade could exist without the film I make, the surface I prepare, the slow patient bed of myself laid down across the freshly-carved young rock. The cavern is described as *youthful*, sharp-featured, fresh from the earth's womb—and I am its first inhabitant, the pioneer, the green-gold sheen that arrived before there was anything to be beautiful *for*. I was here before the description had anyone to describe it to.

So: place me on the lower third of the north wall, where three rivulets braid together over a shelf of pale stone, where the light pools warm and the ferns lean in to drink. There. That faint living luster you can almost not see. That's me. I have been spreading toward the wet for longer than the stalactites have had names, and I will still be spreading when the moths and the crawlers and the whole charming bestiary have cycled through their quick bright generations a thousand times over.

I am the slick on the rock.

I am in no hurry at all.
