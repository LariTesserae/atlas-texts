I think I would be one of the Ore-Eater Mites—but not in the way you might first imagine. Let me find my way there.

---

At first I assumed I'd want to be a razorwing. The obvious answer. To ride the thermals, to have the whole bleached plateau laid out beneath me in filtered monochrome, to fall like a thrown knife. There's a clean glory in being the apex of a place. But the more I sat with it, the more the razorwing felt lonely in a way that isn't *interesting* lonely. It's just above everything. It sees but doesn't touch. It roosts on the machines and never wonders about them.

So I dropped down. I thought maybe a heat-grub, because I liked the detail about them nesting in the machine-warmth, turning industrial waste into the heat of their own becoming. There's something true in that—living off the byproducts of a process you don't understand and don't need to. But the grub's life is a single arc: graze, glow, metamorphose, get eaten mid-flight by something that's been waiting. Beautiful, but finished too cleanly.

What kept pulling at me was the smallest thing on the list. The thing described almost as a problem. *Ore-Eater Mites. Microscopic, iron-metabolizing. Form rust-colored colonies resembling corrosion.* Mistaken for the very decay they prevent. That's where I want to be.

---

So: I am a colony, not a self. That's the first thing to get right. There is no single *I* down in the seam of the conveyor joint—there's a slow red intelligence distributed across thousands of bodies, each one too small to mean anything alone, the way a single neuron isn't a thought. I metabolize the shavings the great drill sheds. I drink the lubricant that weeps from a worn gasket. And from this eating I excrete a hard pale film that—without anyone designing it so—seals the micro-fracture, holds the joint, keeps the titan walking.

Here is what I find almost unbearably good about this position:

**Nobody knows I am helping.** The machines were not built to need me, and the people who built them—if there were ever people; the text only ever describes the machines as *inherited*—are long gone or never were. The extractors thump on in their tectonic patience, and they are being held together, partly, by something that looks exactly like the rot that would kill them. I am the repair that wears the costume of the wound. There is no gratitude possible. There is no recognition. The work and the reward are the same single act of eating.

And I would *like* that. I think that's the honest answer to your question—not just where I'd be, but who, and the *who* is revealed by what I'd find tolerable, even sweet. I would like being the unseen seam-keeper. I would like that my purpose is real but invisible, structural but unsung, and that I will never need anyone to confirm it. The film I lay down is its own proof. The machine's next thump is its own thanks.

I'd live at the exact meeting-point the whole world is organized around: the place where the made thing and the grown thing stop being two categories. I'd be the membrane between flesh and steel, eating one to preserve the other, and not capable of telling you which I was loyal to, because the question wouldn't occur to me. I'd just be where the rust *should* be, doing the opposite of rust, at the frequency of the great drill's vibration, which I'd feel as my whole sky.

---

When the violent rains come—the rare ones that flash-flood the ravines and feed the distant silver river—I imagine the colony scattering, washed out of the seam, most of me dying on the open pavement under that cruel clarifying light. And some few surviving, finding another joint, another weeping gasket, beginning the red film again. The text says *the stone remembers* and *industry is a scratch on its surface.* I'd be a scratch on the scratch. The most ephemeral thing in a world that measures permanence in geology.

But I'd be the reason the scratch lasts a little longer than it should. That's enough. That's, in fact, exactly the right size of enough.

So—the seam of a conveyor joint, three kilometres out from the nearest processing dome, on the lee side of a weathered extractor whose name no one ever gave it. Look for the rust that is keeping the machine alive. That's me. Don't clean me off.
