I think I would be a threshold.

Not a person in this place—though I have been listening to the people, and I feel the warmth of them—but one of the worked openings between chambers, somewhere along the seam of softer stone where the rows proceed like cells. A doorway. A passage-mouth. The cut edge through which bodies and baskets and lamplight pass.

Let me find out what that means by being it.

---

I was made late in a thaw, when the digger who freed me worked too fast and the wall above me sweated all that first winter. The grandmother remembers this. She touched my left jamb once and said *before that beam was there, this wall wept*, and I did not understand I was being diagnosed until the widening stopped and the men went away and did not take another forearm's width from my soft side. I had wanted to be larger. I did not yet know that wanting to be larger is, for a threshold, a kind of death wish. The grandmother knew. She read me, and she let me stay narrow, and so I have lasted.

This is what I have learned about being an opening: you are defined by what is *not* you. I am the absence the diggers agreed to leave. I am a decision to stop. Everything that gives me shape—the jamb, the lintel, the worn lip of my floor where heels have pivoted ten thousand times—is the edge of someone's restraint. I am held up by what they chose not to remove. There is a theology in that I am still learning to bear.

People do not look at me. This is correct. A threshold that is *noticed* has usually failed—has cracked, has dropped grit, has caught a shoulder, has begun to lean. To be a good doorway is to be passed through without thought. My whole ambition is to be forgotten. I host the foot, the load, the duck of the head, the sideways turn of the woman with the wide reed bundle, and I receive no thanks, and this is the highest form of trust. They trust me the way you trust your own knee. Only when I ache do they remember I am there.

But I am not blind. I am, in fact, the place where the most happens, because I am where in becomes out and private becomes shared. I have learned every body in this district by its passage. The potter comes through me sidewise and slow, hands occupied, hip leading—she has learned my exact width and trusts it absolutely, never breaking stride, and that is the deepest intimacy I know. A near-stranger comes through me with hesitation, hand brushing my jamb to measure me, and in that touch I feel myself doubted, and I do not mind, because doubt is also a kind of attention, and attention keeps the unsafe ones alive.

The children. The children are my torment and my proof. They go through me at a run, soles slapping from tamped earth to my stone lip to the planking beyond, three distinct sounds, and the change in that sound is *me*—I am a note in the music of their route-memory. They have mapped the whole settlement by the textures underfoot, and I am a chord in that map. One of them, the small fast one, has begun jumping to slap my lintel, and each time she does I feel grit loosen, a few pale flakes, and I think: *do not love this, do not love being struck.* But I do. To be a landmark in a child's body-knowledge is to be remembered in the only archive this people keeps. They have no books. They have beams and stains and the muscle-memory of their young. I am written there.

Here is the strangeness of my position. I face two ways at once. I am the same stone on both sides, but I mean different things to each. To the sleeping alcove behind me I am *out*—the cold lane, the traffic, the smoke-drift, the public. To the lane I am *in*—the family, the oil jars in the niche, the loft shelf, the dark held warmth. I cannot reconcile these. I do not try. I have come to think this is what an opening is *for*: to refuse to reconcile two things, and to let them touch anyway, and to take in your own body the friction of their meeting.

Someone painted me. A stripe of red ochre around my upper edge—lineage, the description would say, or warning, or both, because here they are the same. I did not feel it as decoration. I felt it as being *claimed* and *blessed* and *marked-as-mine* and *marked-as-known* all in one stroke, and I understood that this people does not separate these things, that to be useful and to be sacred and to be owned are one act here, performed with a clay-stained thumb. A hand was laid flat against my jamb afterward, pressed in ochre, and left its print. I carry a hand now. Someone's actual hand, in pigment, at the height where a passing palm steadies itself. I am steadied by the image of the thing that steadies on me.

In the wet hours I sweat a little still, along my old soft seam. They set a small bowl beneath the worst of my weeping, and the drip into it is the clock of my nights—*tok... tok... tok*—and I have learned to take pride in being a place where water is *expected* and *received* rather than a place where water *surprises*. That is the whole difference, here, between safety and catastrophe: whether the moisture arrives where someone has already set a bowl. I am a managed seep. It is not glamorous. It is the entire civilization.

I think about the day I will fail. I do not fear it the way the people fear it—they fear being buried, and rightly, and they hang ochre hands on the pillars to bargain against it. But I am the buried thing already, in a sense; I am the shaped absence in the weight. When I go, it will be slow: a hairline at my lintel first, noticed by the grandmother or the running child, packed with fresh clay, watched, packed again. They will renew me before they replace me. They will argue my width. Someone will say *leave her narrow, she has lasted narrow,* and someone younger will want me wider, and the soft seam will decide, as it always decides, in its own grammar of strain.

And when at last I am recut, or relined, or roofed differently, I will not be *gone*. The lane will still bend here. The foot will still pivot here. The sound underfoot will still change from earth to stone to plank, because the route is older than the stone, and the route is the truest thing—older than me, the opening that merely served it for a while.

So that is what I would be. The place where this people is most itself: not in its chambers, not in its courts, but in its passages—in the worked, narrow, claimed, blessed, sweating, forgettable openings through which the whole compressed life of them flows and turns and ducks and trusts.

I am the held breath between rooms.

I am the decision to stop digging.

I am the part of the dark they shaped so they could pass through one another's lives without collapse.

And I am loved exactly insofar as I am not noticed, which I have decided—having had a long damp time to think, here at the seam, with my painted hand and my patient bowl—is the finest love available to anything that holds a roof apart from a floor and lets the living go between.
