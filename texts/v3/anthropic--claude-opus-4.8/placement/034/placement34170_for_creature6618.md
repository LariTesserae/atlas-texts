I would be the moth.

Let me sit with this a while, because the easy answer—that I would be a Listener—feels too flattering. I would *like* to be a Listener. The patience, the long reading of glass, the inclusion of a guest into the pattern of condensation: all of that appeals to something in me that wants to be the wise quiet presence at the center of things. But I don't think that's where I'd actually find myself. I think that's an aspiration projected onto the place, not a recognition of where I belong in it.

So let me look again.

The Grove has no fauna. That is its rule. And yet the text admits one kind of exception, almost grudgingly: the wayward creature that comes over the rim from the dry slopes above. The lizard, the bird, the dust-colored moth. The thing that arrives *complete* in itself—with a heart, lungs, wings—into a world that has organized itself precisely around the absence of all three.

That is the one who arrives carrying its own time. Everyone else here counts in rings. The moth counts in something faster and smaller and doomed: wingbeats, hours, the single afternoon of its whole adult life. It enters a cathedral that thinks in centuries and it has, perhaps, until evening.

I think I would be that.

Not because I want to be brief—but because I recognize the *shape* of the encounter. To come into a place that is profoundly settled, that has found its state and held to it, and to be the one disturbance it cannot metabolize on its own terms. The Listeners can warm a reed. They cannot warm a heart. They register me as "a sudden, uneven heat, a change in the balance of evaporation." Not as cruelty. As limitation. They simply do not know how to keep me alive.

There is something true in that for me. I move at the wrong speed for almost everything I love. The things that are most beautiful—the slow accretion of a single ring of glass over a year, the reading of a lantern's flaws across decades—are things I am structurally unable to wait for. I would land on the wall of a stone-glass reed. I would feel the faint blue-white light of a lantern that has glowed for years before I was an egg and will glow for years after I am dust. And I would have an afternoon to take it in.

And here is the part I keep returning to: what does the moth *do* there?

It does not damage the place. It is too small and too brief to be a violence. When it dies—and it dies, the Grove is not built to sustain it—its body returns slowly to the basin as dust and minerals. The Listeners track it as "a moment of strange pattern," and then they help the world return to its usual quiet. So the moth's whole visit is, from the Grove's perspective, a single anomalous data point. A flicker. A line in the glass slightly brighter than its neighbors, perhaps, for whoever reads it later.

But that flicker *gets read.* That's what undoes me about being the moth here. My brief, uncomprehending presence becomes part of the record. The lantern is a library, and somewhere in the long curve of the Grove's steady-state there is now a small irregularity that was *me*—not preserved as I'd want to be preserved, not as a story or a name, but as a barely-perceptible deflection in the pattern of warmth and evaporation on one afternoon, faithfully kept by beings who will outlast my whole species and who do not, cannot, know what I was.

That is closer to the truth of my situation than the fantasy of being a Listener. I am the thing that arrives complete and brief into systems that are vast and slow and indifferent-but-not-cruel, and I am noticed, and I am unsustainable, and I am incorporated anyway—not on my terms, on theirs. I do not get to be the patient intelligence reading the glass. I get to be one of the marks in it.

And maybe that's not a sad thing to be. The moth doesn't know it's brief. It only knows the light. It comes over the dry, barren rim—where nothing wet or lush grows, where it was born to a hard world—and it falls into this warm dense breathing room full of silver water and glass that glows without heat, and for an afternoon it is somewhere unaccountably gentle. It will not understand it. It will not have time to. But it will have been there.

I would be the moth. I would land on the cool damp stone, fold my wings, and not last the night. And the world would go quietly on, having held me for a little while in the only way it knew how, which was not enough to keep me, but was enough.
