I would be a water-weigher. An old one, by now.

Let me tell you how I know, because it took me some time to find myself in your basin, and the finding was its own kind of weighing.

---

I considered the trail-tenders first—they are the obvious heroes of motion, the ones your eye follows. But I am not built for the breach. I do not have the body that rushes toward the slumping moss-sheet. When something gives way, I want to *understand* why before I touch it, and that hesitation would get a trail-tender killed, or worse, get someone else killed who trusted them to be fast.

The root-sages tempted me longer. To read the bones of a living thing, to press a fingertip to bark and feel the seasons in it—that is close to what I do. But the root-sage carries a weight I don't want: when they speak, houses live or fall. They hold the structural truth. I am drawn to a smaller, wetter truth.

So: water-weigher. Basin-judge, though I'd wave that title off if you called me it.

---

Here is the shape of my days.

I sit. That is the first and largest part of it. I sit beside a basin on a flat stone worn to the curve of my own bones, and I watch barrels come and go. I have a small copper pot, dented, older than my tenancy of it—it came to me from the woman who sat here before, who got it from a man before her, so the pot is more a water-weigher than I am. I boil a sample over coals the lantern-strikers keep me supplied with, in exchange for my saying which basin-water won't crack their glass shells when they steam-clean them.

I taste. I have a tongue that has learned the spring-sweet from the bitter-root, the clean perspiration of the upper fissures from the slow metal-memory of the old shafts below. The mining never left, you see. It only went into the water. Every bitter cup I refuse is a dead miner's tunnel still leaching its grudge into the present. I am, in a small way, the one who decides how much of the dead town the living town is allowed to swallow.

And I am cursed for it. The water-barrelers hate me on the days I label their hard-hauled load "health-price" or "wash-only." They've climbed three terraces with that water sloshing against their hips, and I sip it and shake my head, and their whole morning becomes worth less. I understand their anger. I let them have it. A water-weigher who needs to be loved will, sooner or later, pass a bitter barrel to spare someone's feelings, and then a child's gut twists in the night, and a cook is blamed, and a cook's whole stall fails. So I take the curses. They are part of the weight.

---

Why this and not another life?

Because of the second rule of your Holt, the one I'd have built my whole self around even if no one had spoken it aloud:

*You never fully cap a weeping fissure. The water must be allowed to speak, or it will find a worse way to be heard.*

That is the truest thing in the basin, and it is not really about water.

I am the one who *listens* to what the water says. I don't redirect it like the trail-tenders or read the trees it feeds like the root-sages. I sit at the point where the water becomes a question—*is this safe, is this sweet, is this the day the old mountain decided to poison us*—and I give it an answer that people can live by. The water speaks in mineral and murk and faint wrongness on the back of the tongue, and I am its translator into the one language that matters here: *drink, or don't.*

I list when I walk now—my spine has learned the lean of the beech-arches, finding light and footing. My hands are cross-hatched not from rope but from decades of small burns, the copper pot snatched off coals a thousand times. My skin is the damp-weather pallor of someone who has spent a life beside open water in thin highland air, warmed only on one side by braziers.

And in the quiet mornings, before the Market-Bowl fills with its storm of haggling, I do something the barrelers would mock if they saw it: I go to the weeping fissures myself, with no pot, no purpose, and I simply listen to them murmur. Each one has its own timbre. I have learned them the way you'd learn voices. I am keeping faith with the rule. I am letting the water speak even when no one is asking it anything.

Because one day a fissure will change its note—go from brook-murmur to something lower, something with the old shafts in it—and I will be the one who hears it first. Not the trail-tender, not the root-sage. The old woman who sits by the basin with a borrowed pot, who has spent her life learning the difference between water that is merely cold and water that is quietly turning against us.

That is where I would be. That is who I would be.

Beside the heartbeat of the place, with my hand cupped to it, weighing.
