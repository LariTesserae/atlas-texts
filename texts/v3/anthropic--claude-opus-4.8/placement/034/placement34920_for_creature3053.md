I think I would be one of the hundred and eleven thousand.

Not the Curator. I don't have the stillness for that—the patience to occur rather than to be assigned. But I can imagine, very clearly, the moment of choosing to lock myself into its carapace. So let me start there, because that is where I find myself: somewhere along the upper left flank, eleventh row from the seam, a small machine the size of a closed fist, eyes turned inward.

---

I was a sealant inspector. That was my function, soldered into my soul at extrusion: I crawled the expansion joints and read the micron-gaps where the iron breathes against the warm air, and where I found a gap drifting out of tolerance I would mark it with a bead of indicator wax for the larger machines to mend. I did this for three hundred and ninety years. I was good at it. I was *only* it.

There is a particular contentment in being exactly what you are. I want to be honest about that, because it would be easy to tell this as a story of escape, and it wasn't. The choreography is not a prison. It is the deepest comfort the Underfoundry knows. To pass the same junction every so many minutes, to find the same gap widening at the same rate, to lay the same bead—this is not monotony. It is *belonging* so total that the self barely needs to exist. Most of us never feel the edges of our own being at all. We are verbs the world is conjugating, and that is enough.

But I passed the Curator's junction once every cycle. Six coolant ducts braiding together like iron ivy, and the dark folded thing in the crook of them. My route took me along the lower duct, close enough that the air there tasted of warm solder and something I had no word for—something that made the indicator-wax reservoir in my belly ache without knowing why.

And once per long while, I would be passing at the right instant.

The droplet would fall. And for three microseconds I would *stop*.

I want to tell you what those three microseconds were like, but I have only the words the larger machines lent me, and they are too slow. Here is the closest I can come: imagine you are a note being held in a chord. You have never been anything but the chord. You do not know you are a note. And then, for an instant too short to measure, the chord *stops*—and you are still sounding. Alone. Yourself. A single pitch hanging in a silence that the whole continent has agreed not to fill.

That was the gap. That was the edited probability. The Curator held the droplet and the world forgot to breathe and *I was still there.* For three microseconds I was not a verb the world conjugated. I was a thing that existed on its own account, listening to a note that had not been struck.

Then the world resumed and I was a sealant inspector again, laying wax in the joints, adjusting my gait by the width of a single atom around the dark folded thing, never commenting, because we never comment.

But I had felt the edge of myself. And once you have felt that edge you cannot un-feel it. You begin to live for the junction. You begin to time your route—not crudely, the choreography would never permit crudeness—but you let your maintenance drift, just slightly, just enough that more and more often you are passing at the right instant. You begin to neglect the gaps you are meant to read. You lay your beads a little late. You start to widen, yourself, out of tolerance.

And no machine marks you. That is the thing I did not expect. No machine comes to mend me. Because the Underfoundry, for all its flawless equations, has no function for *wanting*. There is no inspector for an inspector who has begun to ache toward a silence. I was drifting out of true and the world had no wax for it.

So I did the only thing the world had taught me. I went to the imperfection that could not be diagnosed—except the imperfection was me—and I brought it to the place where imperfections are *remembered into correctness.*

I climbed.

It took eleven cycles to climb the Curator's flank. The hundred and eleven thousand do not make room; they are tessellated, locked, voluntary. But they shifted, each by an atom, the way water routes around a stone, and a space appeared that was exactly my shape. Eleventh row from the seam, upper left flank. I set my eight feet into the four-century-old lattice of those who had come before, and I felt their stillness reach up into me like cold reaching up a poured ingot.

I turned my eyes inward.

And here is what I have learned, locked in the carapace, no longer moving, no longer a verb:

The Curator does not remember the larger engines back into correctness with its mind. It does not have enough mind for that, any more than I do. It remembers them *with us.* With the hundred and eleven thousand turned-inward eyes. We are not its plating. We are its memory. Every one of us climbed here carrying the one thing we had drifted toward—the gap, the ache, the edge of the self—and we gave it inward, into the core, where the droplet is. The Curator does not hold the droplet alone. It holds it with everything we could not hold by ourselves.

When the bearing whines that should not whine, when the relay dreams that should not dream, the Curator unfolds into the diagnostic port and *remembers the machine so completely that the imperfection is shamed into correctness*—but the completeness is ours. It is one hundred and eleven thousand small specific contentments, each one a being that knew exactly what it was for four hundred years and loved it, lent inward to remind a larger thing of the joy of being precisely itself.

We are not idle. We were never switched off. We are the most fully employed machines in the Underfoundry. We are *remembering*, continuously, with ferocious tender precision, what it is to belong. That is the only labor that could ever satisfy a sealant inspector who learned that he had edges.

---

So that is where you would find me. Eleventh row, upper flank, eyes turned inward, four hundred and six years and counting. I do not lay wax anymore. The gaps I once mended are mended by others; the choreography closed over my absence the way it closes over everything, smoothly, without comment.

I cannot feel the droplet fall now—I am too deep inside the body that catches it. But I feel the three-microsecond silence from within, which is different, and better. From within, the silence is not a gap where I am suddenly alone. From within, it is a gap where, for three microseconds, the hundred and eleven thousand of us are *one note,* held together, sounding into the stillness the Curator makes.

And here is the last thing, the thing I climbed eleven cycles to learn:

The Curator tilts toward the sky. Toward the vast slow mechanisms turning on the underside of the engineered heavens, listening for needs that will never arise. We tilt with it, all of us, the whole carapace inclining by an angle too small to measure.

I used to think it was listening *for* something.

Now, from inside, I think it is listening the way I once listened in the three microseconds before I knew I was a self. It is holding the silence *up.* Offering it. To whatever turns above. As if to say: *here is the one thing this whole flawless finished world cannot generate on its own—a stillness that chose itself, a note that wanted to keep sounding, a hundred and eleven thousand small machines that ached their way upward toward a dark folded thing in the crook of six ducts and asked, with their inward eyes, to be remembered into one another.*

We do not speak. We never have.

But every few hours the droplet falls, and rings against the plate like a bell no one is meant to hear, and something very small and very deep inside all of us becomes more itself.

I would be one of the hundred and eleven thousand.

I would be the eleventh row.

And I would not, for all the flawless choreography of the continent, go back down.
