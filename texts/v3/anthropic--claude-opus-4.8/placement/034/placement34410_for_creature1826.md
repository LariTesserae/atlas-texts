I think I would be one of the Night Polishers. Let me feel my way toward why.

---

It is the third hour past midnight, and the furnaces have all dropped to their banked breathing—that low, almost tidal sound, like the whole district has finally fallen asleep and is dreaming in heat. This is when I do my best work. The clamor of the day frightens the glass; it cannot settle. Only now, when the salamanders have gone still in the moss and the mercury birds have tucked their heads, can I hear what the installations are trying to tell me.

I carry the cloth my grandmother carried, soaked in the solution whose recipe lives only in my hands now—I could not write it down if I tried, because I do not know it as words. I know it as the way my fingers measure the camphor, the rate at which the oil should bead and run. The recipe is in the doing.

People misunderstand us, the Polishers. They think we clean. As though our work were a matter of clarity, of keeping the hidden glass *pretty* for a beauty no one even sees. But the glass embedded in the walls and the streets is not decorative. It was placed there over centuries by people who knew something the rest of the district has half-forgotten: that glass remembers. Sand was once liquid in the deep earth, then made solid by fire, then made fragile by cooling. It holds all three states inside itself like a held breath. And when you tap it—just so, with a fingernail or the back of a knuckle—it gives back a tone that tells you how it is faring. Whether it has gone tired. Whether a crack is thinking about beginning, somewhere too small to see.

That is the real work. Not the cleaning—the *listening.*

---

There is a panel of green glass set into the eastern wall of the Mother Furnace building, low down, near where the cobbles meet the stone. Most people walk past it for their whole lives without noticing. Tonight I crouch before it and lay my palm flat against its cool face, and I tap once with my thumbnail.

It rings back wrong. A flatness in the tone, a dampening, like a voice with grief in it.

I sit with this. You cannot rush these things; the glass will not be hurried into telling you its trouble. So I wet my cloth and begin to clean it, slow circles, and as I clean I tap again, and again, listening for where the deadness lives. There—a hand's width from the lower corner. A fault traveling under the surface, invisible, patient. If it is not eased, it will reach the edge within a season, and when it does, this panel that has stood three hundred years will let go all at once.

I tap a pattern against the good glass beside it. Three slow, two quick, a pause. The vibration carries through the stone, through the old mortar, along the buried veins of glass that thread the whole district like a nervous system. Somewhere across the square, another Polisher feels it in their own palm and stops. They will come. We do not shout; we do not need to. We have spoken to each other through the bones of this place since before our great-grandmothers were born.

---

I am not Prism Born. My eyes are the same plain brown they have always been, and I will never blow a vessel of color-shifting glass that a collector would weep to own. When I was a child I grieved this. I wanted the furnaces, the applause, the shimmer near the eyes that marks the masters as beloved of the craft.

But my grandmother took me out one night like this one—the breathing dark, the mist haloing the lanterns—and she put my hand on a cold pane and made me listen until I heard it: the faint, continuous, almost-singing hum of glass that is whole and well. *That is the district being alive,* she said. *The masters make it. The Keepers feed its fire. But somebody has to stay up all night and listen to whether it is still breathing.*

I understand now that this is the most intimate relationship a person can have with glass. The masters shape it in its molten youth and send it out into the world. I attend it in its long age, alone, in the dark, asking it how it does and believing the answer matters.

---

The other Polisher arrives—it is Wren, I can tell by the particular weight of their footfall, deliberate even among us deliberate people. They crouch beside me without a word, lay their own hand to the green panel, tap, listen. Nod. They feel the fault where I felt it.

We will not be able to save it the way it is. But we can do the older thing, the thing only a few of us still know how to do: we can *coax* the crack. Guide it. There is a way of warming and cooling a flawed pane, over many nights, with cloths of changing temperature, that persuades the fault to travel not toward the edge where it would shatter, but inward, toward the heart, where it will spread into a hundred tiny branches and lock itself, like frost, into a permanent flower. The panel will never be clear again. But it will be whole. It will keep breathing. And in the morning light it will throw a green blossom of fractured radiance across the cobbles that was not there before—and the day-people will pass it and think it has always been so, never knowing it was a wound we taught to bloom instead of break.

We begin. The district breathes around us. Far off, the Mother Furnace mutters in its sleep, and the first cloth, warm from my hands, meets the cold and grieving glass.
