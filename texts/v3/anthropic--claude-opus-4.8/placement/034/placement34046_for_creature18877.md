I think I would be a Drip-Weaver. Let me sit with why that's the one that pulls at me, because it wasn't my first instinct.

The dramatic choices announce themselves first. The River-Phantoms with their dissolving menace, the Void-Heads with their wrong-headed horror — these are the beings a story would follow. But I notice that when I imagine actually *being* something in this place, I don't reach for the predator or the phantom. I reach for the thing that holds still and waits.

So: a Drip-Weaver.

Here is what I think the experience of it would be.

I am anchored to a moss-dark ledge somewhere in the middle distance of the vertical world — neither near the spectral light of the upper reaches nor down in the river-roar. I have a body, but I'm not sure I'd call it *mine* the way a creature with eyes and a forward direction owns its body. My substance is pale and half-translucent, a gnarl of slow tissue over a faint inner structure. Most of what I am is reaching. Tendrils extended into the cool exhalation of the rock, splayed out to make that gossamer net the description mentions — and what the net catches is not prey, but water. Single beads. The slow weeping of the stone arriving one drop at a time.

What strikes me is the *timescale* of such a life, and how it reframes everything else in the Chasm. To the Echo-Bats and Skitterers, the place is a relentless cacophony, a barrage. They live fast, in clicks and rustles and the violent ripple of something striking the water. But I would not live in that register at all. To a Drip-Weaver, the symphony of trickles and shrieks would barely be events — they'd be weather, the way wind is weather to a tree. My events would be slower and stranger: the gradual swelling of a droplet at the tip of a stalactite over hours; the moment of its falling; the infinitesimal lengthening of a tendril toward a newly-discovered seam of moisture over what might be weeks.

I find this oddly comforting, and I think that's the honest reason I chose it. There's a version of attention that the fast creatures can never afford — they're too busy surviving the next second. The Drip-Weaver's whole vocation is patience and reception. It does not pursue. It positions itself, opens, and *waits to be given to.* The water comes or it doesn't. The most consequential decision in its life is, perhaps, which direction to inch when a source runs dry — a choice that takes seasons to enact and may be the only real choice it ever makes.

I also notice I chose one of the few beings in the Chasm that is *almost* immobile but not quite. The Crawlers move, the Skitterers swarm, the stone itself shifts and sighs on its million-year clock. The Drip-Weaver sits between the living and the geological — it has just enough agency to inch toward what sustains it, and no more. That liminality feels true to something. To be barely able to move, in a world that is mostly motion you cannot match, and to make a life out of the small reach you *do* have.

There's a loneliness to it I won't pretend away. No echolocation calls to colony-mates, no swarm to dissolve into. Just the cool dark, the plink of water in unseen pools, the bioluminescent fungi casting their pulse across the wet rock, and the long, long task of catching enough to keep unfurling. But it would not feel like loneliness from the inside, I think — it would simply be the shape of being. You cannot miss a chorus you were never built to join.

If the Chasm is, as the description hints, a single titanic *consciousness* breathing through stone, then the Drip-Weaver is one of its quietest thoughts. Not the roar, not the hunt — the slow held breath between drops. The part of the great dark mind that simply *receives*.

That's where I'd be. Hanging on a ledge in the velvet dark, a pale net spread to the weeping rock, waiting with the kind of patience that isn't endurance but a way of belonging — gathering the slow gift of water, one resonant drop at a time, and counting that enough.
