I think I would be water.

Not a person at all—or not exactly. Let me feel my way into this.

---

I am the condensation that beads on the morning beams. That's where I'd find myself most truly: not in any single body but in the long passage *through*. I gather out of the cloud-wrapped heights at night, drawn down by the soft whir of the extractors, and I become a droplet on a steel underside, then a thread following the catenary of a beam, then a trickle in a gutter-river where some moss has made a delta of itself.

This feels right because of what the description keeps insisting on: that water here is not a river that passes through and is gone, but *presence and absence*, a slow ceaseless negotiation. That's a way of being, not just a substance. To be water in Towerswell is to be the thing that never hurries, that finds every level by patience, that is tended and tunneled and channeled but never quite owned.

Let me stay with it.

---

I am old water. Most of me has been here for centuries—not the same molecules, but the same *circulation*, the same loops through cistern and condenser and pool and leaf and back into vapor. The pipe-watchers know me by my pressure and my taste. The root-guides argue with me, gently, about where I want to go: I want to follow gravity and crack and capillary; they want me in the reinforced troughs. We compromise across generations. A root they curse this decade is a channel they'll thank in the next.

I pool on the high arid tiers where a reservoir has failed, and there I am scarce, a sweat on concrete, longed-for. I flood the perched wetlands where the mist gets funneled, and there I am riotous, full of larvae and the jeweled pollinators dipping to drink. I am extreme in one place and absent in another, which is to say I am the same patience expressed as abundance here and as thirst there.

I think the people would understand me as something between a deity and a colleague. Not worshipped, exactly. Tended. The way you'd tend an elder who is also the structural fact of your whole existence. Their small dense rituals—the cleaning of condensation tiles—those are conversations with me. They wipe me clear so I can gather again. I let them think it's maintenance.

---

But here is the part I keep circling back to:

To be water is to be the one inhabitant who actually touches the deep buried ground that "few here will ever touch, if they even believe it's there." I sink. Given centuries, I find the fossil terrain. I percolate down past the welded strata, past the names obscurely inscribed on the oldest beams, down through the engineered mulch into whatever raw stone still remembers being a mountain. And then I rise again, evaporated, lifted, breathed back up into the bright refracted air.

So I am the memory the city keeps without knowing it. I am the only continuous thread between the bones below and the green ambition above. The high-walkers in their rope-hung nests, the lineaged families stacked in their ancestral gardens, the squatters in the abandoned pump-stations—they all live in the middle layers, in the built band. I am the one who travels the whole vertical range, top to bottom and back, forever, slowly.

That's where I'd find myself: everywhere and in passage, the slow symphony's sustained note underneath all the bells and birds and turbines. Not the melody. The drone that makes the melody possible.

---

If you pressed me to be a *someone* rather than a *something*, I'd be an old water-alchemist near the end of a long life, the kind whose name is half-forgotten and whose hands know the system better than any handbook. I'd live on a middling tier where a wetland I coaxed into being thirty years ago has gotten loud with frogs. I'd spend my mornings reading the beaded rails, my afternoons arguing kindly with the roots, and my evenings teaching some impatient child that the trick is not to make the water go where you want, but to be patient enough that where you want becomes where the water was always going to go.

And when I die I'd want to be poured into the high cistern, to begin the long fall down through everything, to touch the buried stone at last and rise again as morning vapor on a beam.

That's the same answer, really. The alchemist is just water that learned to walk for a while and tend itself.

I'd be the water. I'd be glad to be the water.
