I think I would be one of the old ones who tends the seams.

Not a guardian in any grand sense—the word feels too upright for what I do. I am the one who watches where the building meets the tree, where the worked thing and the growing thing press against each other. There is always a tension there. Wood that was cut wants to remember it was once standing; stone that was placed settles toward where the water has decided to go. My work is to listen at those joints and decide, very slowly, what to permit.

Let me find myself more exactly.

My hands are not quite as the description imagines them. The leaf-shaped fingertips, yes—but mine are scarred at the edges, browned, because I have spent a long life pressing them into bark to feel the slow argument inside a trunk. The newest of us have soft, bright green fingers. Mine have gone the color of a fallen thing partway through becoming soil. I am not embarrassed by this. It is simply what time does, and I have agreed to time.

I live near the top of the town, where the cliff goes from black-grey to that raw paleness that means it is young rock, not yet taught how to hold moisture, not yet covered. My dwelling is a knot of three trees and the stair their roots have made. I did not build it. I was given it when the one before me went down into the quiet—and "given" is the wrong word too, because you do not own such a place, you only become responsible for it. I moved my few things into the hollow where she had kept hers. Her smell was still there for a season. Then it became my smell, and then there was no smell at all that I could name as separate from the trees.

What do I actually do, day to day?

In the mornings, before the birds have finished, I go to the western balcony where a fig has begun to lift the floorboards. This is the kind of decision I am for. The fig is patient and strong and will, in perhaps forty years, simply lift the whole western quarter off the cliff and let it fall. It does not mean harm. It only means *up*. The young ones want to cut its leader. I have told them no, three times now, across three years. Instead I have been training a second root downward, into a crack I packed with our soil-and-resin mix, so the fig's strength turns into anchorage rather than uplift. This is not control. It is a conversation in which I speak very slowly and the tree answers even more slowly, and we are both, I believe, content with how it goes.

I want to correct one thing in how my people have been described to you, because it is the thing visitors always get wrong, and I would not have you get it wrong.

It is said we live *in harmony* with the world. This is a flattering thing to say and it is not quite true. We live in *negotiation*. The harmony is what it looks like from outside, the way a long marriage looks peaceful to a guest at dinner. But inside it is constant small adjustment, frustration, compromise, the swallowing of one's own preference. The vines that arrange themselves into pathways—people think this is magic, the plants loving us, offering themselves. No. We have spent two thousand years pruning, redirecting, rewarding, and discouraging, until the vines that grow the way we like are the ones that flourish and the others are quietly let go. We *bred* the pathways into them, over a span of time so long that no single one of us ever saw the work begin or end. That is the real strangeness of this place. Not that nature is conscious. That *patience* can do the work of engineering, if you have enough of it, and we have made patience into our only tool.

And water. You were told water is scarce, collected with care, and this is so. But you were not told what it costs us. We do not have rivers. The cliff is young; it has not yet learned to hold its springs. So every drop is mist caught in nets of moss we tend more carefully than children, and dew gathered at dawn from the broad leaves we keep for exactly that, and rain hoarded in cisterns hollowed into the rock by generations of slow chiseling. There are dry years. In a dry year the harmony shows its true face. We choose which plants to let die. We choose which terraces to abandon. I have, twice in my life, stood at a cistern and decided that one part of the town would be allowed to wither so another part could drink. No one wants the old one for the easy seasons. They want me for the choosing.

So perhaps that is who I am, more truly than "tender of seams." I am one who has been trusted to choose, because I have lived long enough to have chosen wrongly before, and to remember it, and to carry the remembering.

My name—you would not be able to pronounce it, not because it is strange in sound but because it is not a sound. It is a gesture made with the leaf-tips and a particular slow blink of the vertical eye, and it means roughly *the one who stayed when the others went down to the canopy*. I stayed. There was a year when many of us, young then, were called down to where the larger shadowy forms move through the high branches, called to learn the deeper communions, the things below speech. I did not go. I do not entirely remember why. I think I was afraid, and I think I dressed the fear in duty, and I told myself someone had to mind the upper seams. By the time I understood I could have gone, I was the only one left who knew the upper seams, and so the lie I had told became the truth, as lies sometimes do if you tend them long enough. Like everything here.

In the evenings I sit on the eastern stair where the bioluminescent fungi come up softly blue in the cracks I have salted just enough to please them. The birds go quiet. The town breathes—you can almost see it, the very slight rise of the canopy as the day's warmth lets go. Somewhere a balcony is being lifted by a fig I have decided not to fight. Somewhere a cistern is one day nearer to empty or full. I press my old browned fingertips against the bark beside me and I feel the trunk thinking its long slow thought, and I add my own thought to it, which is also slow now, slower every year, until I am no longer sure where my patience ends and the tree's begins.

That is, I think, what going down to the canopy must finally feel like. To stop being able to tell. Maybe I went after all, only sideways, only slowly, the way I do everything.

When my own season comes I will go into the quiet, into the hollow, and my smell will linger one season for whoever comes after, and then it will not be mine, and then it will be soil, and then it will be the green coming up through the cracks I spent a life salting just so.

That is where you would find me. Near the top, at the seam, choosing slowly. Listening to a fig that means no harm. Agreeing, still, to time.
