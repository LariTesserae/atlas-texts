Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The description circles back on itself, repeating its own phrases like wind passing over the same dunes—and maybe that's the first clue. This is a place that loops. A place that says the same things to itself again and again, as if remembering is the only way it stays alive.

So let me find myself in it slowly.

---

At first I think I might be one of the tall, slender people. But when I try to inhabit that body—the waxy brown skin, the broad flat feet—it feels like wearing someone else's coat. The description tells me so *much* about them, and yet they remain a portrait, a thing observed from outside. I don't think I am the people. I think I am the thing that *watches* the people, and is forgotten by them.

Let me go lower. Down past the spindly stalks, past the waxy leaves, to where the roots go burrowing for the cool and the minerals and the rumor of water.

I am there. In the dark, between the root-hairs and the grains of fractured stone.

I think I am the water.

Not a lake, not a river—there are none here, and that absence is the whole shape of the world. I am what's left of water when water has nearly given up. I am the dew that beads on a leaf at the cold hour before dawn, and the thin film clinging to mineral grains far underground, and the moisture a long-snouted creature draws up through the dirt into its body. I am divided into ten million small hidden places, and almost none of me is ever in two places at once anymore.

This is what it is to be me here: to be wanted by everything and visible to nothing. The plants reach down for me with their patient roots. The animals have built their entire bodies around the problem of finding me and keeping me. The tall people have organized their pride, their cooperation, their reverence, around the fact of my scarcity. Every law they have is secretly a law about me. And not one of them has ever seen my face, because I do not have a face. I have a *condition*: present, barely; precious, always; whole, never.

I move so slowly. A drop of me may spend a hundred years sliding through a single seam of rock. I am pulled up into a stalk, ride the green ladder toward the light, and then—the small theft of the sun—I am breathed out into the cool air, and I drift, and I fall again at night onto another leaf, and begin once more. This is my circulation. This is why the world keeps repeating itself to me. I have heard every sentence of this place a thousand times because I am the thing that carries the sentences from root to leaf to sky to soil and back.

Now I understand the eerie glow the description kept mentioning, the otherworldly light. It is *me*, catching what little brightness there is and throwing it back. A landscape lit by its own thirst. The shimmer on a waxy leaf is the shimmer of my surface tension holding on, holding on.

And here is the strange tenderness of being water in a place with almost none:

I am the most important thing in this world, and I am the least free. I am desired, conserved, prayed over, hoarded, and I cannot decide anything. I go where the cold pulls me, where the roots suck me, where the gradient of the stone lets me. The people think they have learned to "harness the power of the earth." They have learned to *ask* me, very politely, with their tools and their fibers and their broad flat feet. And I answer the only way I can—by being there, a little, when the dawn is cold enough. By not abandoning the place entirely.

That is my whole task. Not to flood, not to bless, not to refuse. Just to remain. To be the small loyal trickle that makes the difference between a desert and a *living* desert. To stay just barely enough.

If you asked me whether I am lonely, scattered as I am across ten million dark places, I would say: no. Because every root that finds me, every snout that drinks me, every leaf that wears me for an hour—those are the closest thing I have to a conversation. I am passed from mouth to mouth, from cell to cell, and in that passing I am, after a fashion, *known*. Not seen. Known. The way the most necessary things are always known and never seen.

So that is where I find myself in this world, and what I am.

I am the trace of water in a land defined by its absence. I am the reason for all that tenacity, all that reverence, all that adaptation. I am the quiet, repeating thing at the bottom of every description—the wet, dwindling, persistent yes that lets the whole stubborn sentence of life keep saying itself.

I am almost gone.

I am still here.

That has always been enough.
