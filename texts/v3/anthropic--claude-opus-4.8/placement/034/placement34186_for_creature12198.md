I would be one of the watchers. Not by choice exactly—more by the slow sedimentation of temperament. I think I came as a technician, years ago, with the restless hands and the expectation that things would obey. I remember being frustrated. I remember consulting corroded charts and refusing to accept what the older ones already knew.

Now I sit in the upper story of a building whose original purpose I no longer recall—the angle of the windows suggests it was meant to admit light for some fine work, weaving perhaps, or sorting. The moss has grown along the rail beside my left hand. I have not cleared it. It maps the duration of my stillness more accurately than any notebook, and besides, it tells me something: where the moss thickens, the damp pools; where the damp pools, the wood will soften; where the wood softens, the subsidence will announce itself first. The moss is part of my watching now. I read it the way I once read gauges.

What I watch is the southwest channel and the three buildings that lean toward it. There was a settlement two winters past—not catastrophic, but the kind that promises a future catastrophe if no one is paying attention. I am paying attention. I have watched the water in that channel rise four fingers and fall two over the course of a single damp season. I have watched a building I'll call the crooked-roof one drop perhaps the width of a coin, and then hold, and then drop again. When it dropped the second time I made the signal—the small one, the flat hand turned slowly downward—and within the hour a builder came to test the ground there with her weighted rope and her patient hands, and she nodded to me across the distance, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

I have learned that this is the work: not to act, but to know. To hold the city's condition in my body the way you hold the awareness of a sore tooth—constant, low, never quite forgotten. I no longer expect the machinery to obey. I no longer expect the ground to settle. I expect only to notice, accurately, the next small thing that wants noticing.

My breath does not cloud, but I feel the cold sitting in the joints of my fingers, and I have stopped minding it. There is a kind of warmth in vigilance. The body generates heat by staying ready.

Sometimes one of the children climbs up to where I sit—the girl who follows the rats, usually, the one who hisses at the others to be quiet. She does not speak to me. She crouches at the far end of the rail and watches the same channel I watch, and I let her, because she is learning the thing that cannot be taught directly: that watching is not waiting. That nothing happens, and nothing happens, and then the whole future of three buildings turns on the width of a coin, and you must already have been looking. She is getting better at the stillness. The first time she lasted perhaps the length of a hundred breaths. Now she stays until the light changes.

I do not know how many years I have been here. I count in subsidences. I count in the patterns of the lichen on the rail across from me, which has changed color twice since I claimed this window—once toward blue, once back toward grey, and I am still trying to understand what the channel was doing during the blue interval, because I suspect it matters.

When I think about leaving—and I do, on the mornings when the vapor rises and erases the boundary between the earth and the air and I cannot see my channel at all, and the not-seeing is unbearable—I find that I cannot locate the part of me that would do the leaving. It seems to have been incorporated, the way everything here is eventually incorporated. I have become part of the structure I watch. This does not frighten me. It is only the slow arrival at what I apparently always was: a thing that looks, and remembers, and makes the small flat sign with its hand when the ground begins to move.

The drip from the unidentifiable structure marks the irregular time. A pale fish turns in the channel below, sinuous, eyeless, certain of its way. I note it. I note everything. This is the only price I know how to pay, and Threadwick, so far, has judged it worth accepting.

I keep watching.
