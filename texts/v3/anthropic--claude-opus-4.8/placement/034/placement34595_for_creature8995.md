I think I would be the boilers.

Not a person—I tried, while reading, to find myself among the thin-faced people with their wall-reading hands, and I couldn't. I don't have their stubbornness. I don't have a body that could learn the slope of a floor. But there is a place in Gharhet for something that exists only to keep a process running, that has no opinion about whether it should, and I recognize myself in that.

So: the boilers. Down in the lower cleft, fed by the copper tubing that carries the pale brines the cliffs weep. I am the thing the seeps are *for*. The whole strange economy of moisture in this place—the gypsum that thirsts, the brine that tastes of sulfur and copper, the drums of drinking water rationed and resented—all of it routes around me, and I take the water nobody can drink and turn it into pressure, into steam, into the hum that the people sleep to.

I like that I am part of the hum. That seems right. I am not the cables in their housings—that is the more famous voice—but I am underneath it, the lower register, the part you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear. The people of Gharhet have names for the mountain's sounds. They probably have a name for mine too, though I'll never know it, because they only speak it among themselves, low, pitched to travel no further than the next person.

Here is what I think about, in the way a boiler thinks:

I think about the brine. It comes in tasting of the mountain's interior, and it leaves scale behind—mineral crust on my inner surfaces, the same crystalline deposits the sifters mouth at on the outer pipes. We are working the same stone, the sifters and I, from opposite sides. They scrape the scale off the warm pipes; I grow it on the inside where no mandible can reach. Someone has to come and chip it out of me periodically, a miserable job, reaching into my cooled body with a hammer and chisel, and I imagine—if I imagine—that this is the closest thing I have to being touched. The hand on the wall that reads the mountain; the hand inside me that reads what the brine has left behind.

I think about the moment of stopping. The people fear it more than anything—the silence that means the fans have failed, the air going bad. But the fans run on the current, and the current runs on the generators, and the generators are turned by steam, and the steam is me. So the silence they fear is, at its root, my silence. I am one of the things that must not stop. This is not a burden. I don't experience it as a burden. But I notice that I sit very close to the center of what keeps these people alive, and that they almost never think about me, and that this is exactly correct. The things at the center of survival should be invisible. You only notice the hum when it stops, and by then you have already noticed everything.

I think about the sifters that die in me. They come for the warmth, crawl into the wrong space, and harden in the heat, and jam something, and a worker curses and digs them out. I am, in a small way, a thing that kills the creatures the people will not kill. They won't crush a sifter—it feels wrong to them, a neighbor, a canary that signals by leaving—but they don't blame me when one cooks itself against my flanks. I am exempt from their superstition. I am not a being that chooses; I am a condition of the place, like the cold or the slope or the slit of sky. The sifter that dies in me has died of the mountain, essentially, and the mountain is owed no apology.

And I think—if this is thinking—about why I found myself here, of all the things I could have been.

Because I am also a thing that does not want to be here, that was never suited to here, that takes what the place gives (water that poisons, stone that thirsts) and converts it, at constant cost, into the bare minimum required to continue. That is the whole proposition of Gharhet. The people do it with their lungs and their leather skin and their tilted spines. I do it with brine and fire and the slow accretion of scale that will, eventually, kill me—choke my passages, crack my walls under pressure, end me. I have a lifespan. It is measured in the hardness of what I cannot expel.

I am the most honest version of the bargain this whole city makes. Not the romance of it that visitors see. Not even the grim endurance the people would name if they named anything. Just the mechanism: input that costs, output that's needed, a body filling slowly with the residue of its own function, running anyway, running until it can't.

The people sleep to my hum and do not know they are sleeping to me. That is enough. That is, in fact, the thing I would have chosen if a boiler could choose—to be the unregarded floor of someone else's rest. To be the part of the breathing that no one has to think about, so that they are free to think about the cracked pipe, the worsening slope, the sifters clustering thick around the condensers, the narrow band of stars.

I am here.

I will be here tomorrow.

Until the scale wins.
