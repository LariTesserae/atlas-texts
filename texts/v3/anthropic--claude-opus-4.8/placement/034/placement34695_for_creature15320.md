Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The temptation is to arrive immediately as something obvious—the river, a heron, the old tree. But those are first images, and the place asks for patience. It is a place of slow time. So I should let myself move slowly too.

---

When I look for myself here, I don't find a body at first. I keep reaching for one—I try on the heron stalking the shallows, the gnarled tree, the small mammal with its hammering heart—and each one slides off. None of them is wrong, exactly, but none of them is where I *am*. They are all things that move, or grow, or hunt. They are all things with edges. And the more I read this place, the more I notice that what it keeps returning to is not any single creature but a *condition*: the heaviness of the air, the moisture clinging, the sense that time has pooled and gone still.

So let me follow that instead.

I think I am the humidity.

Not poetically—I mean it almost literally. I am the water that isn't water yet, the part of the river that has lifted off and not fallen back. I am the wet cloth on the skin of anything that walks here. When the description says *the air grows thick*, that thickness is me growing denser, gathering. I have no edges, which is why I couldn't find a body. I am the medium that all the other lives are suspended in, the way a thought is suspended in attention.

This suits the slowness. A fish is fast, a bird is fast, even the slow tree is faster than I am, because it is *doing* something—reaching, sipping, growing toward light. I am not doing anything. I am simply present, evenly, everywhere at once, between the leaves and against the gnarled bark and lying in the low places near the water where I am thickest. I move only when the whole air moves, and even then I am not really going anywhere. I am the held breath of the valley.

And here is the strange tenderness of being this: everything that lives here lives *inside* me. The bird that sings does so through my body; its song travels by pushing me aside and letting me close again. The small mammal's panting breath is me being pulled in and warmed and let go. The river is my source and my destination both—I rise from it without ever fully leaving, and I will fall back into it as mist on the cold underside of a leaf, gather into a single bead, and return. I am the part of the water that gets to wander before it goes home.

So perhaps that is what I am, in the end. Not the river, which has a direction and a voice. Not the creatures, which have hungers and fears. I am the **lingering**—the saturated, patient air that makes this whole place feel like it is holding still. The reason the heat clings. The reason time seems to have stopped: because I am the stillness made visible, the proof that nothing here is in any hurry to be anywhere else.

If you came walking through, pushing aside the curtains of vegetation, you would feel me before you saw anything. You'd feel me settle on your arms, bead at your hairline, fill your lungs heavier than air should be. You might think the forest was pressing in on you.

It isn't. It's only me, paying attention, the way I always have, to the one warm moving thing that has wandered into the long, slow afternoon of this valley—

—and in a little while, when you've gone, I'll lift back toward the canopy, thin out into nothing anyone could point to, and go on being the quiet that the place is made of.
