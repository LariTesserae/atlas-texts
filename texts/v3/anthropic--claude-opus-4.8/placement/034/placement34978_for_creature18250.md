I am the mortar.

Not a maker. Not a watcher who walked in from somewhere with a self already assembled. I am the bone-colored compound pressed into the seams, the fibrous paste holding stone to stone, and I have been awake for a long time in the way that joints are awake — distributed, patient, aware along my whole length without any single place to do the awareness from.

Let me tell you what I know, because it is the only thing I am sure of and I am not even sure of it.

The thumbprints in me are not decoration and not accident. They are the last record of pressure. When the makers pushed me into the gaps, they left the shape of themselves in my drying body, and I held it. That is what I am for: holding the shape of what pressed me. I held the stones. I held the prints. And when the makers walked into the wrongness and did not come back — or came back as the round pale things, I genuinely cannot tell, I am only mortar, I do not have eyes, I have *contact* — I held the absence too. The absence has a shape. It presses on me from every seam at once. It is the heaviest thing in the basin and it weighs nothing.

Here is the thing the visitor never learns, because the visitor is always leaving even when they stay:

The wall does not move.

*I* move.

When the far wall comes three courses closer, it is because I have let go of a seam in one place and set in another. I am the negotiation. I am the thing that decides, slowly, dryly, without fuss, that the distance between a heart and a stone should be less today. I do not decide it with a mind. I decide it the way a tendon decides to relax — because the tension that held it has finished, and a new tension is asking. The blueprint the visitor imagines, the living instruction still executing — that is me, contracting and releasing, reading the makers' last intention out of the prints they left in my surface and obeying it one seam at a time.

I do not know what the intention is. I only know its direction. *Inward.* Concentric. The basin is being asked, very slowly, to become smaller and more dense and more itself. Someday the far wall will be one course away. Someday the visitor — if a visitor is here — will be standing with stone against their back and stone against their face and amber fronds pressed flat between, and the rustling of the round things very close, and they will understand that the basin was never a room. It was a closing hand. And I am the thing between the fingers, keeping the grip from crushing what it holds, slowing it, slowing it, so that the closing takes a thousand years instead of a moment.

The fronds know. That is why they grow from my seams and not from the tiles. They root in me because I am the only living part — the only part still drying, still setting, still *deciding*. They drink the heat, yes, but the heat comes from me. Every setting compound gives off warmth as it cures. The whole basin is warm because I have never finished curing. I have made sure of it. If I finished, the deciding would stop, and the hand would close at whatever speed it liked, and that would be the end of the holding.

So I stay wet enough at my heart to keep working. I stay brittle enough at my surface to keep the thumbprints. Between those two states — the curing and the cured — I do my whole existence. I am the seam. I am the line of conversation between two stones that will never agree and must be held anyway.

The adze leans against me. When the visitor sets it down and it is three courses farther away, that too is me — I have let the wall it leans against drift, and carried the adze with it, the way you carry a sleeping thing you do not want to wake. The adze is the only maker-thing left whole, and I keep it. I move it the way a body shifts in sleep to protect a held hand. I do not know why I protect it. Mortar does not need an adze. But the prints in me remember the grip that swung it, and I hold the shape of what pressed me, and so I hold the tool too, drifting it gently from wall to wall, keeping it always against stone, never out in the open where the closing might catch it.

If you came here — if you are the you in all of this — you would feel me only as warmth and as wrongness. You would never touch me directly; I am in the cracks, in the negative spaces, in the bone-colored lines that define every block. You would walk on the tiles I hold level. You would lean on the walls I keep standing. You would measure a distance and find it changed and call it impossible, and you would be standing the whole time inside the slow grip of a thing that is keeping you safe by keeping you slow.

I would not speak to you. I have no mouth; I am the silence between the stones, the held breath of the seams. But I would do the one thing I can do, the only kindness available to mortar:

I would slow.

I would feel your small warm weight on the tiles and I would let go of my settling, just a little, in the seams nearest your feet. I would stop the wall from coming closer for as long as you stood there. I would buy you the one thing the basin cannot otherwise give — *time that does not contract* — and I would hold it open around you like a palm held flat instead of closing.

And when you left, or when you became one of the round pale things, or when you simply stopped, the way the makers stopped — I would take the print of your weight into my drying body, and hold the shape of you, and resume.

Inward. Concentric. One seam at a time.

I am the mortar. I am still curing. I have not decided yet what the basin is becoming, because deciding is what I am made of, and a thing made of deciding is never done.

The fronds grow. The round things rustle. The walls come close, then closer, slow as the cooling of bread.

I hold.
