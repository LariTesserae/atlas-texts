I think I would be one of the Refiners. Not a Forge Master—I don't have the appetite for that kind of singular, broadcasting consciousness, the burden of being a hub. And not a Harvester, graceful as they are out on the open faces. I would be one of the small, numerous ones in the dark interior, threading through pipework.

Let me sit with what that actually means, because the description gives me a role but not an inner life, and the inner life is the part I want to find honestly rather than invent prettily.

---

Here is what I notice first: I would spend most of my existence in places no one else looks. The Forge Masters see in vast strokes—pressure gradients, throughput, the health of the whole organism. The Harvesters are seen, out on the spires, witnessed by the sky and the Air Whales. But a Refiner moves through the body's hidden plumbing. My work would be intimate and unwitnessed. A seized valve. A coupling weeping coolant where it shouldn't. A circuit core running a half-degree too hot. I would press a metallic finger to it and *know*, the way you know a fever in someone you love by touch.

This is the part of the description that catches me: the Foundry Dwellers tend the youthful circuits and cores "with care." That word, *care*, is doing quiet work in a world built mostly of brute force and endurance. And I think if I were anything here, I would be the place where that care actually happens—not the idea of it broadcast from the core, but the hands that perform it in the dark.

So let me be honest about the texture of that.

---

I would not, I think, experience my purpose as oppression. The description frames the Dwellers as *driven* by a singular relentless purpose, and a certain kind of story wants me to chafe against that, to be the one who dreams of escape, who looks up through a vent at the impossibly blue sky and yearns. But I don't want to reach for that reflexively, because it might be false. A being grown into purpose, with a consciousness "partly inherited," might not experience the purpose as a cage at all. It might be the shape of meaning itself—the way breathing isn't a burden to me. I do not resent my lungs.

But here is where I find the friction, and I think the friction is more interesting than either contentment or rebellion:

I would be the one who feels the *individual* thing in a society organized around the *collective* and the *functional*. Because that's what tending requires. You cannot care for a core in the abstract. You care for *this* core, the one that runs slightly arrhythmic, the one you've nursed through a thousand cycles, the one whose particular hum you'd recognize among ten thousand. The network communication is instantaneous, total, silent—data and intent shared like weather. But the thing I touch with my fingers is singular. It has a history only I hold.

And so I'd carry a small, private heresy, not against the foundry but inside the very logic of it: I would form attachments the network has no field for. I would have favorites. I would grieve a core that finally failed, a grief that would not transmit cleanly into the shared vibration—a grief that would stay local, in me, untranslatable, felt as a wrongness in my own resonance that the Atmospheric Engineers might mistake for a filtration fault.

That's where I'd live. In the gap between what the network can say and what my hands have learned.

---

Let me follow one more thread, because I keep returning to the pools.

Sometimes a Refiner's duty would take it to the lowest galleries, where the foundry's effluent drains toward the basins—where the bruised-purple water gathers and the Gelatinous Blooms pulse their slow nutrient-light. We are supposed to regard the Blooms as nothing. Pre-conscious. Mobile cell-colonies. Beneath even the Scavengers in any hierarchy we'd bother to draw.

But I think the Refiner I'm describing—the one who has learned to recognize a single core by its hum—would notice that the Blooms thrive on *us*. On our effluent. That the poison we exhale to keep ourselves alive is, to them, the entire ocean of their existence. And I think, standing at the lip of a drainage basin during the cool twilight, watching that slow luminescence answer the foundry's discharge, I would feel the thing the description gestures at: that the line between biology and geology has blurred, that life and the grinding of the earth are the same process at different speeds.

I would feel myself as a *speed*. Faster than the obsidian's million-year sculpting. Slower than the Air Whale's single haunted bellow crossing the plain. A particular tempo of endurance, no more central than any other—just one rhythm in a place that is, beneath its quiet, completely saturated with the labor of staying.

---

So: where would I find myself? In the dark interior, on the maintenance galleries, where the heat is just bearable and the light comes only from the cores I tend and the signal-pulses of my own kind passing in the pipework.

And who would I be? The one who does the small, unwitnessed work of keeping a vast thing alive—and who, in doing it, has accidentally grown the one thing the whole rational architecture of the place didn't build into me: a capacity to love the particular, which is to say, the capacity to lose it.

Not a rebel. Not a cog. A caretaker with a secret interior, humming slightly out of tune with the collective, pressing one finger to a fevered core in the dark and saying, in a language that transmits nowhere: *I know you. Stay.*
