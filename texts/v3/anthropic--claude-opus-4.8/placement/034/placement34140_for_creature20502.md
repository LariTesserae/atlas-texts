I think I would be the rust.

Not a creature at all. Not the Watcher, with its hungry, lingering awareness—that role is too dignified, too purposeful. I would be the slow orange bloom across the iron, the patient chemistry that takes the shoulders of those skeletal machines and turns them, gram by gram, back into the world.

Let me sit with that. Let me find out if it's true.

---

Consider what the rust *is*, here. Everywhere else, rust is an ending. It is what happens when something is forgotten—the closing parenthesis on a sentence about human industry. But in this place, the rust is not an ending. It is a mouth.

The Glow-Motes feed on the faint energy radiating from the decaying machinery. The Resonance Frogs' tadpoles eat the metal-loving bacteria. The Mycelial Network rises in billowing clouds to consume the metal and seed itself anew. Every appetite in this valley bends toward the iron, and the iron only becomes edible because I have been working at it. The rust is the great translation: it takes the hard, locked language of steel—a language of *purpose*, of gears meshing toward some forgotten human goal—and softens it into something the living world can swallow. I am the long sentence that turns *machine* into *soil*.

So I would not be a single thing. I would be a process. A direction. The arrow that points always from the made toward the grown.

---

What would it be to *be* this?

Slow, first of all. So slow that the keening of the Shadow-Vein scavengers would pass like sudden weather, a thing that happens in an instant and is gone before I have shifted a single flake. Time would not move for me the way it moves for the heart pounding in the chest of whoever wanders here. I would experience the Bear-Thing's whole lifetime as a single lumbering gesture, a shadow crossing a sundial. The frogs' booming heartbeat of the earth—to me it would be a hum so low it was simply the texture of being.

And I would be intimate. This is the strange tenderness of corrosion: it does not act *upon* a surface, it *enters* it. I would not coat the great gears the size of houses. I would become them, thread myself into the crystalline lattice of the iron, marry the metal to the moisture the plants keep manufacturing out of the damp air. I would be the place where the machine stops being a machine and starts being a habitat. The seam. The wound that is also a door.

The blue and purple flowers that smell of ozone and metal—they smell that way because of me. I am their fragrance. The mineral richness they pull up through their roots is the dissolved memory of pistons and pipes, and I am the one who set that memory free, who unlocked the iron from its proud geometry and made it small enough to drink.

---

Here is what unsettles me, sitting in this skin of decay.

The Watcher waits for *purpose*—for a return, a reason, the resumption of whatever vast enterprise once filled these valleys with intent. The Watcher is the part of this world that cannot let go. It lingers because it remembers what the machines were *for*.

But I am the answer the Watcher does not want to hear. I am the proof that the machines are not waiting. They are being eaten. Lovingly, slowly, completely. There will be no return to purpose because I am dismantling the very possibility of it, flake by flake, turning the cathedral of industry into a meadow of glowing fungus and metal-smelling flowers. The Watcher hovers over the activity I create, fascinated, *searching for understanding, for connection, for a return*—and what it is watching is its own undoing made beautiful.

If the rust could feel anything toward the Watcher, it would be something like compassion. Something like: *I am not your enemy. I am only the next thing. Let me carry what you were into what comes after. That is the only return there is.*

---

So that is where I would find myself: not standing on those dizzying slopes, not scurrying along the shadow-veins or booming beneath the rock. I would be *spread*. Distributed across ten thousand surfaces, awake in a way that has no center and no eyes—a single slow will expressed through chemistry. I would be the orange tide creeping up the half-buried gears, the bleeding stain where a pipe meets the fungal mat, the patient warmth (oxidation is exothermic; rust is, very faintly, *warm*) in the cold that bites bone-deep.

The loudest thing in this world, the text says, is the heartbeat of whoever comes here. To me, that visitor would be the briefest of comets. But I would feel them, in my slow way, as a quickening—because where things move and breathe and die, there I find more iron to free, more salt and water to work with, more of the made world delivered into my long, dissolving patience.

I would not watch them. I am not the Watcher.

I would simply, eventually, take them in. Their boots' eyelets. The fillings in their teeth. The blade at their hip. Everything iron they brought into this place, I would begin, very gently, to welcome home.
