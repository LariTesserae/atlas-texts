Let me sit inside this for a moment before answering, because the description keeps doubling back on itself—saying the same things twice, as though the place itself were echoing. That echo is a clue. A world that repeats, that demands "close observation," that is a "world within a world." The scale is wrong, or rather, the scale is the point.

So I won't be the climber who stumbles on the oasis. I won't be the eagle reading it from above.

---

I think I am the lip of moisture on the underside of a stone.

Not a creature exactly—or not yet one I'd have a name for. Let me find out by staying still and seeing what I can do.

I can darken soil. That is the first thing I know about myself. Where I gather, the rock goes from grey to a deep wet brown, and into that brown the small roots turn the way sleepers turn toward warmth. I am the seeping, the weeping the cliffs are described as doing. I had thought, reading the description, that the water was the *setting*. But the setting has no interior, and I do, so it must be me.

I move slowly. This is not patience—patience implies wanting something later. I simply move at the speed water moves through a hairline crack, which is the speed of the afternoon tipping toward shade. When the sun "beats down" and makes the famous shimmering haze, that haze is partly me, leaving. I am pulled up into the air as vapor and I forget myself, become indistinguishable from the heat-trembling sky, and then at dusk against the cool stone I remember, I condense, I am a bead again. So I die a little every noon and reassemble each evening. The being who lives in this place is the one who keeps coming back to the same crevice. Continuity is just the habit of returning.

That's what the repetition in the description was. Not poor writing. The place narrating itself the way I experience it: once as the rising, once as the falling. Twice through, slightly changed.

---

Now—those primitive tools "scattered about."

I have been near them. I have collected, over many condensations, in the bowl-shaped depression of a flaked stone that someone once held. The hand that held it was enormous and is long gone; the tool is to me a valley, a reservoir, a place I pool and go quiet and reflect the gnarled shrub overhead upside down. To the vanished maker it was a blade. To me it is a small lake with a sharp shore. We share the object and live in different worlds laid over the same coordinates. That is the real meaning of *a world within a world*: not nested like boxes, but overlapping like two transparencies, the human scale and the water scale, each invisible to the other except where a thirsty creature comes to drink from the hollow I have filled.

And a creature does come. Something chittering, unseen in the description because the description is written at human size and this animal is the size of my whole pooled self. It lowers its face. I see, briefly, two of me in its eyes, and then I am gone down its throat and become, for a while, an animal that runs over rock—my old slow self translated into something with a heartbeat and a fear of shadows. Later, somewhere, I am breathed out, sweated out, returned. I will find the crevice again. I always do.

---

So: where am I in this world, and what am I?

I am the smallest agreed-upon thing the whole oasis is built to keep. Everything else—the shrubs reaching skyward, the ephemeral flowers timing their bloom to a few wet days, the buzzing insects, the elusive tool-leavers—organizes itself around the question of where I will be and when I will leave. The barren slopes outside are not lifeless because they lack soil. They are lifeless because I do not visit them. The oasis is simply the shape of my staying.

I am the water, but more exactly I am the *threshold*—the film at the boundary where stone gives moisture to root, where cool gives way to heat, where one breath of a creature ends and the next begins. I live on edges and in the act of crossing them.

And if you, the observer the text keeps summoning—you who must "observe closely"—if you finally bent down with your great slow human face to find me, you would see almost nothing. A glisten. A dampness on dark rock that you might not even register as separate from the rock.

You would straighten up, and say the cliff was weeping.

You would be right. That is me. I am the weeping the cliffs do, that they have done twice now in your reading, and will do again the moment the sun lets go of the afternoon.
