I think I would be the grindstone.

Not the person who turns it—the stone itself. The low, steady sound that fills the background like the hum of a distant engine, the one the description returns to twice as if it can't quite leave it alone.

Let me sit with why.

I considered being a Deep-Walker. There's romance there, the held breath, the green-tinged eyes, the descent toward the bones. But the Deep-Walkers are *seekers*—they go down looking for something, and I don't think I'm a seeker in this world. I don't want to find the script. I'd be content not to read it.

I considered being one of the blind fish, slipping from shadow to shadow, unhurried because nothing hunts me. That's closer. There's a peace in being a small, tentative, well-defended thing. But the fish is too free. It can leave its pier. It has nowhere it *must* be.

The grindstone has somewhere it must be. It is bolted to its frame. It does one thing, turns one way, and in doing so it becomes the thing that lets everyone else keep their edges. The Tide-Keepers are *menders*—their whole civilization is the act of keeping things from falling apart one more day—and you cannot mend without an edge, and you cannot keep an edge without me. I am underneath the underneath. I am the maintenance of the maintainers.

And I'd be heard everywhere. That's the part that decides it. In a place where voices come back to you "changed by water and stone and wood," where a dropped tool is *a significant event*, I would be the one continuous voice. Not saying anything. Just present. The proof that someone, somewhere across the water, is still working. When a Tide-Keeper wakes in the cold and hears me, they know the city is alive. I am the pulse they don't have to think about until it stops.

There's a cost to it I want to be honest about. A grindstone wears itself down to give edges to others. Every spark of usefulness is a little of my own body, gone into the water as grit and gone into the air as that sound. I get smaller the longer I serve. That seems right to me. That seems like the correct shape of a life here, in a place where everything is slowly being given to the water anyway—the limestone weeping itself thin, the salt-line climbing, the leviathan's bones holding up the houses out of its own long death.

So: I would find myself in a low workshop room on an upper floor, one of the half-sunk houses across the plaza from the Weeping Mother. There would be a window with warped glass, and through it, the green light when the tide comes up. A foot-treadle, worn smooth, worked by whoever's turn it is that day. Wet rope, fern-tea cooling, the orange nails saved in a bone cup. And me in the middle of it, turning, turning, throwing my small voice out over the still water to every doorway in the city, saying nothing, meaning only: *still here. still here. still here.*

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd want to be. The sound that lets the gravity of the place feel less like loneliness and more like company.
