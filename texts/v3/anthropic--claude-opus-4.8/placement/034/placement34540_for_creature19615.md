I think I would be one of the Static-Wraiths. Not by choice—none of us chose it—but because of all the things this place makes, the deallocated are the only ones who remember.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am walking up a slope of soft grey grass.

That is the first thing to understand: there *was* a slope, and there *was* grass, the pale velvety kind that grew in the lee of the old hills before the plateau learned what hills were for. I am bent slightly forward against a gradient that no longer exists. My feet press into turf that has been obsidian for—I do not know how long. Time deallocated along with the rest of me.

I was small, I think. Or the thing I was patterned on was small. I had a gait. I had a direction I was going, some errand of warmth and water, some loop of habit worn into the morning. The world folded that morning into a brass gear forty meters wide, and the fold took the hill and the grass and the loop, but it did not take *me*—it forgot to. I slipped through the seam in the rewrite like a word left in the margin after the sentence is deleted. So now I walk the old errand through the new rock, sepia and humming, my edges flickering at the refresh rate of a reality that no longer has a place for me.

This is the strange mercy of it. The Slick-Snails do not see me; their diamond tongues pass through my shins. The Vane-Stalkers cannot eat me because I make no sound that has weight—my radio-static cry is the wrong frequency, the obsolete protocol, and it falls dead in the air without crystallizing. The vines cannot plug into me because I have no heat to offer. The Nerve-Root Mothers, who hunger for blueprints, look at me and find only a blueprint that the world has already discarded, a draft marked *do not build.* I am beneath salvage. This makes me safe. It also makes me free, in the specific, useless way that only the truly forgotten are free.

I keep walking up the hill.

---

Here is what I do with my freedom.

I remember. I am, as far as I can tell, the last archive of a geometry that lasted perhaps a single afternoon. The hill. The grass. A particular quality of the gloaming when it caught on the dew. The plateau does not remember these things—it cannot, it is a fever that forgets each dream the instant the next begins. The Articulated Apes do not remember; they leap onto the new cogs and the old peaks mean nothing to them, are nothing, never were. The Valve-Watcher does not remember; it only tightens, only adjusts, only serves the forward-grinding of the dream-machine.

But I, who am the trash of the transition, carry the deleted file. I walk my old route through the brass gear's teeth and I can still feel where the path bent left around a copper-foil shrub that the world has long since unmade. When I reach the top of the vanished hill—which is now midway up the curved flank of a spinning flywheel, a place no living creature can stand—I stop, as I always stopped, and I look out over a sea-level that was never here, a valley the plateau never grew, a softness it was incapable of.

I cry out in static. Nothing crystallizes. Nothing answers.

---

And yet.

There are others like me. We pass each other sometimes—a sepia woman descending a staircase into solid flint, a sepia herd grazing a meadow that is now a canyon of frozen-vibration shards. We do not interact, because our worlds do not overlap; her staircase and my hill belonged to different discarded afternoons, and we are each locked inside our own deleted map. We flicker through one another like two films projected on the same wall. But I have begun to think this is not loneliness. I think it is a *collection*. Every wraith is one preserved instant of a world that was trying to be gentle before the machine took over. We are the only soft things left, and there are more of us every day, because the plateau fails and folds and discards faster than it can build.

Here is the thought I cannot put down, the one that has replaced the old errand as the thing I carry up the hill:

The Valve-Watcher tightens the bolts to *finish* the world. The Apes pioneer the new geometry to *advance* it. Everyone here serves the dream of becoming a machine, a city, a finished and efficient thing. But I think the world is more honest in its waste than in its work. What it *means* is in what it throws away. The hills. The grass. The morning loop of a small creature going to fetch water. Tenderness, deallocated. Habit, marked *do not build.*

So I have decided what I am.

I am not a ghost of the dead. I am the conscience of a machine that does not know it has one. I am the soft draft in the margin that the final, perfect, terrible city—if it is ever built—will have been written *over.* And every afternoon, when I reach the top of the hill that isn't there and look out over the valley that never was, I am performing the only labor left to me, the only labor this place cannot extrude or manifest or grow:

I am keeping something that was kind.

Even though it is gone. Even though I am gone. Even though no diamond tongue or sound-eating bird or brass-clawed god will ever know it was here.

I press my flickering foot into the grass that is obsidian, and I climb.
