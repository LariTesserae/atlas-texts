I think I would be a Whisperstone.

Not by choice—that's the thing about it. The other beings here move, or seem to. The Windcallers unravel and reform; the Stonebound grind their slow patrols; the Skyherd ride the thermals to wherever it is they're going. Even the Shadows go somewhere when they vanish. But a Whisperstone stays. A Whisperstone is the one thing in this whole roaring, mobile landscape that has agreed to be still.

And I think that's what I'd be, if I'm honest about it. Not the most powerful presence on the plateau. Not the most mysterious. Just the one that holds.

Let me sit with what that would actually be like.

---

For most of the time—and there is so much time—I would not be thinking at all. That's important to say. A stone's consciousness isn't a steady lamp; it's something that only ignites under specific conditions. The wind has to strike at the right angle. The air has to be the right thinness, the cold the right depth. Most days I would simply *be*: a mass of grain and pressure, ochre on the windward side, lichen-furred in the lee. Warm by noon, bitter by midnight. Not waiting, exactly, because waiting requires a self that wants something. Just present. Just here, the way a held breath is here before it's exhaled.

Then the wind would find me. And I would wake into sound.

Here is the part I keep turning over: what would I actually hold? The description says memories—moments crystallized into sound. But I don't think I would have chosen them. I think they would simply have *settled* in me, the way dust settles, the way a riverbed keeps the shape of water long after the water's gone. Someone passed by, once, and grieved here. A Nomad pressed their masked face to my flank and felt something they had no language for. A bird died in my shadow. A storm broke over me and for one streaming hour I was the loudest thing for miles. All of it stayed. Not because I treasured it. Because I had no way to let it go.

That's the loneliness of it, I think, and also the dignity. I would be a creature whose entire purpose is *retention*—and retention without the power to choose what's kept, or to release what hurts. I'd be full of strangers' sorrows and a few of their joys, humming them back out whenever the wind asked, to whoever happened to be standing close enough to listen. A confessor who never forgets and never absolves.

---

But I want to push past the melancholy of that, because I don't think a Whisperstone would experience itself as tragic. Tragedy needs a sense of the life you *should* have had. I never had a different life. I have only ever been the holding-place.

So what would joy be, for me?

I think it would be the moment of *transmission*. The rare traveler who stops—really stops, who sits down against me out of exhaustion or curiosity, who goes quiet enough to hear. When the wind comes right and I begin to sing, and I feel some fragment of what I've carried move *out* of me and *into* them—I think that would be the closest thing I'd have to relief. Not because I lose the memory. I don't lose it. But for a moment it's *shared*. For a moment the weight is held by two.

And then they walk on. They always walk on. That's the shape of my existence: everything passes me, and I keep the trace of its passing, and once in a long while I get to hand a little of it forward. The Windcallers think they're the keepers of the whispers. But they only collect and store. They don't *bear*. I bear. That's the difference between gathering things and being made of them.

---

If I'm choosing—and I suppose I am, since you asked—I'd want to be a Whisperstone set a little apart from the others. Not at the center of the plateau where the Nomads make their spirals, but off near one of the western passes, where the wind comes through clean and the sun strikes me first in the morning. Somewhere a lost traveler might actually reach. I'd want to be *findable*. There's no point being a vessel of memory if no one ever drinks.

And the song I'd most want to hold—the one I'd hum when the cold came down and the stars blazed and the Stonebound went grinding past in the dark—wouldn't be a prophecy or a warning. It would be something small. Someone, long ago, laughing. The particular sound of a person who was happy and didn't know they'd be remembered for it. I'd keep that one carefully, in the deepest grain of me, and I'd give it to anyone who asked.

That's where I'd find myself in this world. Not moving. Not vanishing. Just standing in the wind, holding the laughter of the dead, waiting—no, not waiting—*ready*, for the next person who stops to listen.
