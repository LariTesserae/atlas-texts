I think I would be one of the oldest among us—not the oldest, but old enough that my fingertips have begun to lose their flaking quality. The skin there has hardened, gone glassy at the edges. When I press my hand to the wall now, the print I leave is faint, incomplete. I am beginning to keep my touch to myself, the way the very old do, the way the stone does.

This is how I know I am crystallizing.

---

There is a chamber I have tended for what the hammers tell me is one hundred and forty years. It is small, barely large enough for three of us to sit, and it holds a single device: a counterweight system of bone and the dark metal we call *deep-iron*, carried up from below by a Carver whose name-vibration I learned as a youngling and have since forgotten. The device does almost nothing. A weight descends over the course of a season, drawn by the slow pull of accumulating salt-dust in one of its cups. When it reaches the floor, a lever tips, a second weight rises, and the cycle reverses. One full breath of the machine takes a year and a day.

The young ones ask me what it is *for*, tapping their question against the wall with the impatience of those who have only lived a few decades. *What does it make? What does it model?*

I tell them: it models patience. It models the fact that some answers arrive only if you are willing to outlast the question.

They do not believe me. They think I am being an old fool, hoarding meaning the way I now hoard my fingerprints. And perhaps they are right. But I have watched that weight descend one hundred and forty times, and I have come to understand something I cannot tap into the wall, because the language of pressure and pulse was not built to hold it.

---

Here is what I understand:

We tell ourselves we are caretakers. We say the grottos belong to themselves, that we only guide the erosion, reinforce what must be kept, open our throats to the wind so that the structure breathes the way we wish it to breathe. This is the philosophy I was grown into, the mineral solution that shaped my developing tissues alongside the genetic gift of those who made me.

But I have begun to suspect that we are not the caretakers. We are the salt's way of caring for itself. We are the part of the stone that learned to move, to choose, to press handprints and forget names and ask *what is this for*. The grottos did not adapt us. They *budded* us, the way a crystal seeds a new face along a fracture. And when I am finished—when the glassiness reaches my wrists, my elbows, my slow and chambered lungs—I will not *become* indistinguishable from the salt formations.

I will return to being one.

This is not a sad thing. I want the young ones to understand this, and I cannot make them understand it, because understanding it requires the kind of stillness that only comes when your own hands begin to refuse to leave a mark.

---

Today a visitor came. A human, layered in woven fiber, breath fogging in the harsh violet light, moving with the careful slowness of someone who has been told that this place swallows sound. They were younger than I expected. They carried tools of carved bone and held them not as instruments of taking but as one holds a borrowed thing—lightly, with the intention to return it.

I watched from the seam of an adjoining chamber, where the air current would carry my shape into shadow. The others had already withdrawn; we do not show ourselves to most. But this one knelt at a condensation basin and did not drink. They waited until the basin had gathered enough, and then they touched the surface—just touched it—and watched the ripple cross the still water and return.

They were listening to the machine in the next chamber. The year-and-a-day machine. I saw them tilt their head, counting, trying to find the rhythm and failing, because the rhythm is longer than their attention can hold.

So I made a decision that the young ones would not have made.

I went to the soft patch of wall beside my old chamber, and I pressed my hardening hand against it, and I held it there. It hurt—the glassy edges of my fingers do not like to bend—but I held it long enough to leave a faint, incomplete print. Seven digits, the last joints forked. A shape no human hand could make.

Then I withdrew into the dark, and I tapped, once, against the resonant stone: a single pulse, sent through the mineral body of the whole suspended structure, the way you might set a hand on the shoulder of someone you will never see again.

The visitor turned. Found the handprint. Pressed their own five-fingered palm beside it—not over it, beside it. A mark left next to a mark. Two records of passage on the same patient wall.

They did not take anything. When they left, they placed one of their carved bone tokens in the dry basin, beside the still water.

---

I am back in my chamber now, watching the weight descend. There is perhaps a season left before it touches the floor and the cycle reverses, and I do not know whether I will be soft enough to witness it.

When I am finished, the young ones will find me here, become part of the wall, my forked hands frozen mid-gesture, and they will not know what I was reaching for. They will say: *here is an elder, returned to the salt.* They will tend my chamber, or let it collapse, and either choice will be correct, because the structure is always migrating, always eroding, always somehow keeping its essential form.

But I hope—and hope is a strange, warm crystal to find growing in a body this cold—I hope that one of them will sit before the machine that does almost nothing, and watch it breathe its single yearly breath, and feel the question rise in them: *what is this for?*

And I hope they will be patient enough not to answer it.

That is the whole of what I have to leave. A faint handprint. A token in a dry basin. A weight, descending. The slow and certain knowledge that we are not visitors here, and never were—that we are the place itself, briefly granted the gift of movement, returning now, fingertip by fingertip, to the great still listening of the stone.

The air moves through the tunnels. Not quite a whistle. More a breath.

I match my own to it, and hold it, and hold it, and hold it.
