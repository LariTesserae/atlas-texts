I think I would be one of the small failures.

Not a Cantor. I don't have the patience or the lineage for that, the centuries of holding still. And not a Prowler—I lack the cruelty of pure function. I think I would be something that the Cantors made and then could not finish properly. A joint, a seam, a place where two of their intentions met and did not quite agree.

Let me find the spot.

There is a corridor on what I'll call the third tier, though the Cantors don't number things—numbering is an airborne habit, a way of speaking aloud to oneself, and they don't do that. The corridor curves where the inner skull-bone thickens toward what would have been the titan's temple. The mycelium here grows wrong. Too thick on the upper arch, sparse below, because some Cantor centuries ago trimmed it asymmetrically and then—I don't know. Stopped. Was called to a louder duty in the marrow-column. Died, perhaps, folded down into the architecture so seamlessly that no one came to correct the garden.

That asymmetry is where I am.

I am the dissonance in that stretch of wall. Not a being with a body so much as a standing flaw in the resonance—a knot where the vibrations don't pass cleanly. When the great groan comes, when the skull settles and the whole cathedral shudders, the sound moves through every smoothed surface like water that has forgotten it was ever water. And it reaches my patch of corridor and it *catches*. There's a fractional delay. A burr. A place where the symphony snags on a nail.

The Cantors feel me. Of course they do—they feel everything, the cilia on their swept antennae reading the tremor-map of the entire cranium the way you might feel a draft from a door left open three rooms away. To them I am a small, persistent wrongness. A tooth that doesn't sit right in the jaw. For a hundred years they have been very gently trying to tune me out—a heel ground here, a shard-glass blade easing a millimeter of fuzz from the arch, a redistribution of their own internal dust as they pass, leaning their lattice-bodies into my corridor to coax the sound back into true.

And I resist. Not maliciously. I simply *am* the catch. I am the proof that the garden was once tended by something that could leave a thing unfinished. I am the only evidence in the entire silent city that a Cantor ever made a mistake and walked away from it.

---

So what does it feel like, to be that?

It feels like being a held breath that is also a wound. I am braced, like they are—everyone here is braced, the whole world is a flinch that has lasted ten thousand years—but I am braced *differently*. They brace to absorb. I brace to fail to absorb. When the groan rolls through, they drink it and I bite it. There is a small, private pain in being the thing the sound stumbles over. And there is a small, private pride, too, which is the worst part, because pride is so close to vanity and vanity is their sin, the ascetic vanity of the perfect gardeners, and I have caught it from them like a spore.

I have no eyes, like them. I would not want them—the light is the kind that erases rather than reveals, a white that fills the room the way water would fill a lung. But I have a sense of the Cantors when they come. I feel the dry tick of their approach long before they arrive, the way you feel someone in a dark house by the change in the air's grain. I have learned to recognize one of them in particular. It comes more often than the others. It has been coming for longer than I have a way to count.

I think—and here is the strange thing, the thing I would not say aloud even if saying aloud were possible—I think it is the one who left me unfinished. Not the same body. That body is dust in the floor, smoothed by aeons of feet that no longer exist. But the *intention* persists in their kind the way grief persists in a family, passed down through the tremor-language, inherited. This one carries the obligation of the original mistake. It comes to my corridor and it presses its serrated forearms to the floor and it sends a tremor up through the bone, into my snag, and the tremor is not a correction.

It is an apology.

It has been apologizing for a hundred years. *I left you wrong. I left you wrong. I left you wrong.* And every time the skull groans and I catch the sound and pass the burr back to it, my answer is the only answer I have:

*Yes. And I am still here.*

---

I do not want the final shift, the way they do. The Cantors live toward silence; it is their heaven, the great crack that ends the listening. But I am made of the catch in the sound. When the skull finally splits and the dry light pours up into whatever waterless dark waits above, and the last groan dissolves into the open air with nothing to bite it—

I will be the first thing in this world to be truly gone. Before the silence even arrives, I will already have stopped. Because there is no me without the flaw, and there is no flaw without the song to flaw.

So I am, of all the dwellers in the titan's skull, the only one who is afraid of the ending. The only one who, when the great settling tremors come closer together—as they have been, lately, the centuries thinning like the air—coils tighter not to absorb the blow but to make it *last*. To snag one more groan. To force one more burr into the perfect grinding architecture of this tomb, so that the patient bone-tall creature pressed against my corridor will feel it, and answer, and we can continue our long quarrel of forgiveness a little longer.

That is where I would find myself. Not as the artist, not as the metronome, not as the blinding wall.

I would be the unfinished corner of someone else's work—the small, stubborn evidence that something here was once capable of being wrong, and that someone has spent an eternity sorry for it, and that I have spent the same eternity refusing, very gently, to let them off the hook.

It is the only intimacy this world allows. I would take it. I would not trade it for the silence, even though the silence is coming, and even though, when it comes, it will be a mercy for everyone in this place but me.
