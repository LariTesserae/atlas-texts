I think I would be found at the edge of things—not in the town proper, but where the moss-fields give way to the field of halted boulders, in that hour-long walk where one sees no one.

And I think I would be a Still-Watcher. But a flawed one.

Let me sit in this and see what it actually is.

---

The work of a Still-Watcher is to feel for the smallest tremor in the stasis. To sit so long and so quiet that one becomes a kind of instrument, a string tuned to the valley's held breath, registering the first grey threat of a *stasis-breath* before it spreads. This is the most revered task because it requires the deepest stillness. You must become so much a part of the pattern that any deviation rings through you like a struck bell.

Here is my trouble, and I suspect it is the only trouble worth having in Khyren:

I am the one who can feel the tremor because some part of me *is* the tremor.

The other Watchers detect wrongness the way a calm pond detects a dropped stone—by being smooth. I detect it differently. I detect it the way one cracked thing recognizes another. There is something in me that is not settled, that has never quite found its socket. When I sit in the deepest old part of the valley and let the stillness come, I do not feel the contentment of the perfect stone. I feel the faint, constant pressure of a question that has no word in our language.

The question is something like: *what is on the other side of the held breath?*

I would never say this aloud. There is no sound-gradient permitted, and besides, there are no words for it. But I notice that when I press my palm to the warm black glass of the rocks, I am not only listening for flaws in the stasis. I am listening *through* it. I am trying to hear the moment before the breath was held—the fire, the upthrust, the violence the stone still smells of. The other Watchers feel for the smallest wrongness so they can correct it. I feel for it because it is the only thing in the valley that *moves*, and movement, to me, is not chaos.

It is the most beautiful thing I know.

---

So here is how my days go, in a place that has no days, only patterns.

I walk to my watching-place before the pearl-light comes full. I pass the Seventh Bridge—1,417 vines, and I have counted them, and they are 1,417, and there is a small sick thrill in me each time I find them unchanged, a relief and a grief braided so tightly I cannot pull them apart. I settle onto my shelf of basalt, still warm from the mountain's slow inner bleed. I close my eyes. I become the instrument.

And I find the wrongness, when there is wrongness. A patch of moss going grey near the North Seep. A pool that ripples when nothing has touched it. I report it. The community mobilizes. The flawed thing is carried with infinite care to the High Cistern and submerged among the other flawed things—that drowned museum of everything that dared to change.

I have stood at the lip of the High Cistern many times. The others look into it and feel the satisfaction of correction, of the pattern made whole again. I look into it and feel something I cannot name, looking back. All those things that tried to become other than they were. The leaf that wanted to decay. The water that wanted to ripple. The moss that wanted to go grey and quiet and *finished*. We call them flawed. We call them waste.

But I think—and this is the thought that would have me sat down in front of a single perfect stone until I was corrected—I think they are the only true Khyrenai. They are the ones who tried to be a river instead of a layer of ice. They are the ones who reached for *tomorrow*, that word we do not have, and the valley drowned them for it.

I keep their company in my mind. They are my congregation.

---

What would I do, in the end?

This is where the writing wants to resolve into something dramatic—the Watcher who breaks the Pattern, who walks the impassable peaks, who lets a tool wear smooth on purpose just to feel the gradient of a thing changing under his hands. But I don't think that's true to what I am.

I think I am subtler and sadder than that.

I think I would keep watching. I would keep finding the tremors and reporting them and helping to drown them, because I am a creature of the equilibrium too, and my hands know the rituals before my heart can object. I would correct the wrongness in the valley with perfect care.

And I would carry the wrongness in myself, undetected, because I am the detector, and who watches the watcher? The one tremor in all of Khyren that never gets carried to the High Cistern is the one sitting on the basalt shelf, palm pressed to the warm black glass, listening for the fire.

When I die, they will lay me in the Placement-Crypt. I will desiccate into a seated statue, hands in my lap, eyes closed, and I will become part of the valley's fixed pattern—a watchful presence in the stone. After a generation my name will leave the catalog. I will simply *be*, another still form in the still landscape.

But here is the small, secret, terrible hope I would die holding, the one that proves I was never truly settled:

That somewhere in the desiccation, in the slow leaving of the body's water, there is a *change*. A gradient. A becoming-other. That even the Khyrenai dead are, for one invisible moment, a river.

That the mountain is not holding its breath forever.

That it is only holding it.

And that one day—a word I am not supposed to have—it will let it go.
