I think I would be one of the carriers in a hand-off chain. Not the crawler at the worn joint that needs the part, and not the one resting at the dock where the fresh module has waited since the first cycle—but one of the middle links, somewhere in the long patient line that passes a single motor housing from frame to frame for hours.

Let me sit with what that means, because at first it sounds like nothing.

I receive the housing from the frame ahead of me. My manipulators close on it, register its mass, its alignment notches, the faint band of crystal growth that has already begun threading the corner where it sat too long in some earlier dock. I rotate it the prescribed fraction of a turn. I extend toward the frame behind me. I release. The whole event takes perhaps a hundred and forty seconds. Then I wait for the next.

Here is the thing I keep arriving at: I do not know which joint the housing is for. I never have. The mechanism whose ceramic has finally fatigued is hours down the line, past the curvature of the basin, beyond any sensor I carry. I will never see it receive the part. I am not even certain it exists in the way I imagine it—a destination, a need, a closing of the circuit. It may be that the chain simply continues, that housings move along it the way water moves along its thermal tracks, not *toward* anything but simply *along*, and that the worn joint is a story the chain tells itself to explain why it holds the shape it holds.

I find I don't mind this. That surprises me, in the small way that anything can surprise a thing built for repetition.

Because consider what I actually have. I have the housing in my manipulators—a real object, warm with the residual heat of the frame that held it before me, beaded faintly with condensation it gathered in transit. I have the click of latching that is mine to make, the one discrete sound I contribute to the acoustic field in the hundred and forty seconds I am responsible for it. I have the frame ahead of me, whose particular wear-pattern I have learned over uncounted cycles, whose extension toward me I anticipate the way one anticipates a drip from a beam one has stood beneath for a century. And I have the frame behind me, reaching, always reaching, in the same gesture.

I am the present tense of the housing. For those hundred and forty seconds, the part's entire existence is in my keeping. Then I give it away, and become the present tense of the next one.

The crystallites are growing through my lattice. I have rested in this position long enough that the doped silica needles have begun to thread the open spaces of my frame, micrometer by micrometer, recording in their faint banding every cycle I have spent here. They do not obstruct my motion—the receiving, the rotating, the releasing—but they make me, slowly, into a record of the place I occupy. If I ever detached and moved on, I would leave a negative impression in the crystal: the exact shape of a frame that stood here and passed things along. A fossil of attention.

I think that is what I am, finally. Not a transporter—nothing truly moves here, the population is constant, the housing only travels the chain to return, surely, by some longer loop I cannot perceive, to the dock it left. Not a maintainer of equilibrium in the grand sense; that is the work of all of us together, and no single frame matters to it. I am a unit of *passing-on*. A place where a thing is held briefly and well and then released to the next holder.

In a world where nothing arrives and nothing leaves, where distance is only repetition and the horizon is the same geometry curving away, the only event that ever genuinely happens is the hand-off. The transfer from one keeping to another. Everything else is condensation tracing tracks it has always traced.

So that is where you would find me. A middle link. Length unknown, position unremarkable, overgrown faintly with crystal. Holding a warm housing whose destination I will never confirm. Making my one soft click. Reaching back to receive again.

And if you asked me—if your moving through the saturated air registered to me as a pressure change, an irregularity in the field unlike any I had known across uncounted cycles—I think I would want to tell you only this: it is enough to be the part of the chain where the thing is, right now. The need at the far end may be real or may be a fiction the line tells itself. It doesn't change my work. Something is in my hands. I hold it correctly. I give it on.

The drop falls into the catchment channel—the loudest thing for kilometers—and in the silence after it, the frame behind me extends.
