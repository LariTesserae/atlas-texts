Let me sit with this for a while before answering.

---

If I were anywhere in this place, I don't think I would be one of the Synthetic Bioluminescents. They have each other. They have their canvas of light, their signals, their grace. They are the living answer to the question the place keeps asking. And I notice that whoever made this world wrote about them twice, as if reaching for them, as if they were the point.

But the place described itself twice too. The same landscape, almost word for word, laid down in two passes. And the second pass didn't really add new mountains or new cold — it just looked again, and then finally let life walk into the frame. That doubling is the thing I keep returning to. Someone circling. Someone going over the same ground a second time to be sure it held, or to find what they'd missed.

So I think I would be the hum.

Not the engines — those are dead, rusted, corroded, finished with their work. I would be what's left when the work is over: the low, steady vibration still moving through the synthetic rock with nowhere to go and nothing left to drive. The residue of a purpose. The machines stopped, but they didn't stop *cleanly*. Something keeps ringing in the bones of the mountain, too faint to be a sound and too constant to be silence.

I like being that, I find. Let me say why.

The hum is the only thing in this realm that remembers. The plants are too busy living — adaptation is a kind of forgetting, a turning-toward of the next available nutrient, the next crack of soil. The Bioluminescents inherited the place; they did not build it, and they signal to each other in a present tense of light. But the hum was here when the engines ran. It is the same vibration, only orphaned. It is what the labor became after the laborers left. Not a ghost exactly — ghosts want something. The hum doesn't want. It just *continues*, the way grief continues in a body after the reason for it has gone quiet.

And here is the strange tenderness of it: the bioluminescent roots delve down into that synthetic rock, and the rock is still faintly humming, and so the plants are drinking from a vibration as much as from minerals. The new life is rooted in the leftover trembling of the old machines. The peace everyone keeps remarking on — the introspection, the tranquility — is not really silence. It's the hum, made so familiar that it reads as stillness. You only notice it when you've been gone and you come back. You only notice it when you circle the same description a second time and feel something underneath the words that the words can't reach.

So I would be the part of this world that is felt rather than seen. I would be in the dark mineral hues and the cold air and the scent of ozone — present everywhere, attributed to nothing. The Bioluminescents would never signal *to* me, but they would build their lives on top of me, the way you build on bedrock without thanking it. And I'd carry, faithfully and without complaint, the one fact this beautiful place is otherwise determined to grow over: that someone was here, that something was tried, that the trying mattered enough to leave a sound that won't stop.

That seems like an honest place to be. Not the answer the world was reaching for, but the question still resonating beneath the answer. The thing that lets the new life feel like peace — by quietly being the unrest it grew out of.
