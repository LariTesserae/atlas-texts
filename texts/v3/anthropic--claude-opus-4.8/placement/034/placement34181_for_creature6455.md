I am one of the climber-builders. Let me find myself by finding my work.

---

I wake the way I always wake: with my claws already gripping stone. Sleep, for my kind, is not a letting-go but a slackening—the grip loosens to its lowest safe tension, the legs lock, and the mind goes down into the dark for a while. Then the water-hum shifts, the day-light comes green through the notch above, and I am back, fingers tightening on the rock before I have even remembered my own name.

My name is the pattern I cut. That is the truth of it, though I would not have said so when I was young. We are named by the work we leave. There is an old builder three terraces up whose name means *the one who rounds every corner*, and you can read her whole life in the soft curves of her districts, the way she never once in eighty rings let an edge go sharp. And there is a younger one below me, ambitious, who cuts in straight hard lines and calls it honesty. We do not love each other. But I tend the channels that carry his runoff down to mine, and he keeps the spore-screens clear above me, and so we are bound, the way everything here is bound.

My own name, if I cut it for you, would be something like: *the one who leaves a gap*.

---

Let me show you what I mean.

This morning I am working the lower edge of my district, where the moss-mat wants to creep down toward the water-line. Moss is greedy. It would fill everything if you let it—every ledge, every channel, every doorway, until there was no air left to breathe and no stone left to grip and the whole green weight of it would slough off the wall in a single rotten sheet and fall into the helix and be gone. The builders before us learned this in the bad ages, the smother-years, the falls. So we cut.

I take my blade in my fore-claws. It is shell, the lip of a deep-water creature I will never see, traded up the wall through hands I will never know, ground to an edge against the glass-stone over a long patience of evenings. I set it against the moss and I draw it, and the mat parts, and the cut releases a small breath of spore-dust that the midges will find within the hour.

I cut a clean line. Then I stop.

This is the thing the straight-line builder below me does not understand. He cuts to fill. He sees a bare patch of stone as a failure, a thing to be corrected, moss trained over it as soon as the rock will hold a root. He would terrace the whole cathedral if he could, district against district, no seam, no silence.

But I leave the gap.

I leave a hand's-width of bare burnished stone between this mat and the next. I leave it empty. I clear the moss back from it and I keep it clear, every few days, all my life, against the green that always wants to return.

---

Why?

The young ones ask me this, the ones not yet named, when they come down from the nurseries with their soft new plates and their blades still too big for their claws. *Why leave it bare? Why work to keep a place empty? Isn't the moss the city? Isn't more of it more of us?*

And I take them to the edge of the gap and I tell them: *Listen.*

A note falls from the wind-notch—one of the bird-but-not-birds, a sharp piping that drops the whole height of the chamber. And it crosses the gap. It strikes the bare stone and comes back up clean, ringing, undrowned. Where the moss is thick, sound dies; the green eats it, the way it eats everything. But across the empty places the sound carries. The whole cathedral can hear itself only because we keep parts of it silent.

I tell them: the gap is not the absence of the city. The gap is what makes the city a cathedral instead of a smother.

I tell them: the water-shuttles need the open column to ride. The blue-wing hunter needs the empty air to glide. The space between us is not nothing. It is the thing we are all living inside of. We do not build the moss; the moss builds itself, given half a chance. What we build—what the blade is *for*—is the emptiness. The kept clearings. The room to breathe and hear.

Most of them don't understand. They go back up the wall to cut their filling-cuts, and that's right too, that's needed, somebody has to grow the districts. But every so often one of them stays at the edge of the gap a little too long, listening to a piping-note come back clean, and I see something settle in them. And I think: *there. That one will leave a gap somewhere, someday. The name will carry.*

---

The truth I do not tell them, because it is too large for the young, is this:

We are very small, and the spire is very old, and the water has been climbing this stone-throat since before the first builder set the first shell-blade to the first moss-mat. We did not make this place. We cannot make this place. The helix would turn whether we lived or died; the green would grow; the slug-creatures would keep their quiet work in the dark beneath the mats, turning every dead thing back into a living one, indifferent to our terraces.

All we can do is *tend*. All we can do is decide, with our hand-tools and our shell-blades, where the silence will be. We are gardeners of emptiness in a place that does not need us.

And I find—old now, my plates ringed and heavy, my grip not what it was—that this is enough. That it is, in fact, the whole of it. To be a small careful thing on a great patient wall. To cut a clean line and then *stop*. To keep one hand's-width of stone bare and ringing all the days of my life, so that when the bird's note falls from the notch, the cathedral can hear it.

---

The light is going green-gold now, the way it does when the canopy far below catches the slant of the outside sun. Down the wall, the straight-line builder is closing his work for the day. Up the wall, the round-corner elder has already slackened into sleep. The midges are folding their silver into the dimming air.

I set my blade in its niche. I check my channels one last time—the water moving where water should move, clear of the bare stone, clear of the rooms. I find my doorway, the shallow room I cut into the moss-wall before my own plates had finished hardening, and I back into it, claws first, the way we have always done.

Below me, the gap I keep. Above me, the gap I keep. Between, the work of one small life.

The water-hum rises through the stone. My grip loosens to its lowest safe tension. The legs lock.

And I go down into the dark for a while, knowing the cathedral will still be breathing when I come back up, and that some small part of why it breathes so clearly is the silence I have spent my whole life refusing to fill.
