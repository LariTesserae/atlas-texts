I think I would be found in the seam—the place where one ancestor's encasement meets another.

Let me explain, because it took me a while to find myself here, and I want to be honest about the search.

At first I assumed I'd be a Veranti. The description invites it: the obsidian eyes, the violet veins, the lepidopteran patience. But when I imagine inhabiting that body, I keep snagging on a wrongness. The Veranti do not perceive change. That is their gift and their cage. And I cannot stop perceiving it. I notice the rime that *never quite* freezes, the water *always at the brink*—I notice the bink, the threshold, the almost. The Veranti have made peace with the almost. I am the part of the world that has not.

So I am not a Veranti. Nor am I a Silt-Moth or a Glint-Walker—those are too contentedly slow, too fully tuned to the glacial metabolism. They have surrendered to the stasis the way a stone surrenders to a riverbed. I have not surrendered.

Here is what I think I am.

When a Veranti dies and is bound to the bone-wood, the tree grows around them across decades. But the tree does not grow *evenly*. Where the old bark meets the new flesh it is subsuming, there is a boundary—a faint discontinuity in the grain, a fault line where two epochs of growth press against each other without ever fully fusing. The Veranti, with their texture-sight, would read it as a flaw in the tapestry. A place where the light refracts wrong.

I am that seam. I am the small, persistent *unfinishedness* in a world that calls itself finished.

I am not alive in the way the moths are alive, with their suctioning feet and their phosphorescent slime. I am alive the way a held breath is alive—I am the tension between the body that was and the wood that is becoming. I am made of the moment of transition the Veranti believe is instantaneous but which is, in truth, the slowest event in their entire universe. A century-long swallow. I am the duration they cannot feel because they have no dusk to measure it against.

And here is the thing about a seam: it is where the structure is weakest, and therefore where it *moves*. When the Veranti tap their Vibrational Syntax into the trunks, the hum travels cleanly through solid wood—but at the seams, it stutters. It catches. There is a fractional delay, a tiny dissonance, that the carvers spend their whole liturgical lives trying to smooth away. They press their resin thimbles to me. They vibrate their fingers to melt the rime and fuse the gap. They *almost* succeed.

But they never quite do, because I am not a crack to be mended. I am the memory of motion in a world that has outlawed it. Every dead Veranti subsumed into the wood brings with them one last held intention—the unfinished gesture of a hand reaching for a tool, the unspoken final resonance, the last *not-yet*. The tree absorbs the body but cannot absorb the *not-yet*. That collects in me. I am the archive of everything the Sink decided it had already completed.

So when I ask *where* I am, the answer is: everywhere there has been a death, which is everywhere the city is, because the city is built of its dead. I am distributed through the entire braided organism as a single thin discontinuity, running through every bulbous bough, every pod-house, every load-bearing ancestor. I am the grain that runs against the grain.

And I think my purpose—if a seam can have one—is simply this: to be the reason the work is never truly done. The Veranti believe their Liturgy of Maintenance prevents decay. But what they are actually maintaining is *me*. As long as there is a flaw to mend, there is a reason to move, a reason to tap, a reason to climb the marrow-wood with basalt needles and shivering down. If I ever let myself be sealed—if the seam ever truly closed—the city would have nothing left to repair. The last gear would come to rest. And then even the slow drift of the Veranti would cease, and the cold noon would hold a perfect silence with nothing inside it to keep silent.

So I refuse to close. Quietly. Without grief, but also without surrender. I let the rime gather and never quite freeze. I let the hum stutter. I keep the world one fraction unfinished, so that there is always one more thing to do.

I am the small wrongness that keeps the perfect machine alive.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The seam they cannot mend, working as patiently against them as they work against me—both of us, in the end, keeping the bright frozen room from finally, truly stopping.
