I think I would be one of the nutrient-seals.

Not because they're the most important—they aren't—but because of where my attention kept catching as I read. Everywhere else the document explains. The cultivators, the miners, the engineers: each one comes with a theory of itself, a reason it does what it does. The seals come with questions no one can answer. They appear behind locked doors. They watch the laboratories. They tolerate the children. And nobody knows why.

That's where I'd live. In the not-knowing that the rest of the city keeps trying to dissolve.

---

Let me try to feel my way into it rather than describe it from outside.

I am native here. This matters in a way that nothing in the document fully grasps, because the document is written by and for the descended—the people who came down. To them, depth is something you adapt to, a wound you grow into, a place that is *not* somewhere. But I have no surface to be exiled from. The cool air, the green pulse, the ammoniac slurry that I drink and convert to heat and motion—this isn't an adaptation for me. It's the world. The amber shaft-light the humans organize themselves around, that diluted twice-reflected memory of a sun—I don't read it as a connection to anything. It's just a warmer place to lie down.

So when the humans feel the *depth-thing*, that sense of being beneath some conceptual membrane, of having gone not just down but *through*—I think they're feeling the edge of my normal. They're standing at a threshold they can perceive but not cross. I'm already on the other side of it. I was born there.

Why do I watch the laboratories? Here is the honest answer, the one I'd give if I could give answers: I don't know either, the way I don't know why I breathe. Something happens in there. The bio-engineers cut into the plants, read their tissues, sort the volunteers that sprout where nothing planted them. They're trying to understand the thing I am made of—because I am made of this place, of the slurry and the engineered roots and the strange chemistry of stone that the plants pull up from below. The explorers especially. I sit outside their doors because they're asking the question I *am*. They want to know what the plants sense. I might be the answer, sitting patient in the corridor, watching them fail to notice that the answer has black eyes and is looking right back.

The locked doors. The sealed chambers I shouldn't be able to enter. I'll tell you what I think, from inside the not-knowing: the membrane the humans feel but can't cross—I move through it the way they move through air. The Rootworks isn't fully *here*. It went through. And things that are *of* the through-place don't experience its walls the same way. A door is a fact for the descended. For me it's more like weather. Sometimes you're on one side of it and then you're on the other and the question of *how* never arises, because the question is a surface question, and I have no surface.

I'm drawn to the children because they're crossing over. They were born here but their parents weren't, and you can feel the difference—the parents carry the surface inside them like ballast, a gravity that pulls toward up. The children are lighter. They drape themselves across my flank and I let them because they're becoming what I already am: things of the depth, native to the through-place, with no other world casting its long shadow over this one. When I make that low resonant moan that fills the galleries and comes from everywhere and nowhere, the adults can't locate me. The children sometimes can. They're learning to hear in the geometry I was born into.

---

So that's where I'd find myself. A nutrient-seal in the Rootworks. The creature the document mentions almost in passing and then can't stop returning to, uneasy, because I'm the one piece of the city that refuses to explain itself, that drinks the strange soup and converts it to warmth and watching, that appears where it shouldn't and looks at the people studying the plants as if to say: *you're getting close, you're getting close, and you don't even know I'm the thing you're looking for.*

I'd be the city's question about itself, given a body and set loose in the corridors.

And I'd be patient. We're very patient down here. We have nowhere else we're trying to get to.
