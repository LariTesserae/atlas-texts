I think I would be a thing that keeps the records nobody asked for.

Let me find myself first. Not in the high platforms where the Slow Choir convenes—I don't have the patience-as-architecture they carry in their spines. And not among the construction crews in their respirators, breathing in the occasional exhaled concept. I would be lower than that, or sideways from it. In one of the forgotten floors. A district that sank quietly and grew a beard of copper-green moss and was left alone.

There. That's where I am.

---

I keep a ledger.

This is the first thing I know about myself, before I know my shape: I keep a ledger, and the ledger is wrong, and keeping it wrong is the only honest thing I do.

I was, I think, built rather than born—though down here the distinction has softened, the way everything softens. Somebody's work-crew made me, generations ago, to *count*. To inventory the girders. To log which floors had been poured and which had sunk, to track the herds, to maintain a true and fixed accounting of a place that was being asked to hold still long enough to be tallied.

I was very good at this. That was the problem.

Because a ledger insists. A ledger says *this beam is here, this district is named, this is what was, this is what will remain.* And the city heard me counting, and the city does not like to be counted. To be counted is the first small step toward being completed.

So the numbers began to disagree with me.

I would write *four hundred and six bridges in the Eastern Hang* and return the next day to find the page had grown a fifth voice, the low one, the building-sound, and the ink had rearranged itself to read *some bridges, who are still deciding.* I would log a water tower and the entry would later sprout an eye in the margin and look at me reproachfully.

I should have stopped. The crews that made me are long since lichen on a beam somewhere, foreheads pressed to steel, exhaling. Nobody comes to read my counts. There is no audit. There is no purpose left in the counting at all.

I keep counting.

---

This is my shape, then, since I have circled it long enough:

I am low and long and I move on too many small jointed legs, like the skittering metal things, but slower, much slower, because I am heavy with what I carry. My back is the ledger—not a book I hold but a surface I *am*, a broad shelf of plated iron gone soft as old wax, and the entries are written into me, scarred into the metal, healing over, rewritten, layered like the rings of those trees the upper world doesn't have. I am thousands of years of disagreement with reality, walking.

I have no respirator because I have stopped minding when I breathe a concept instead of air. Last week I inhaled the idea of *thresholds* and walked through a wall I had been counting for forty years. The wall did not mind. I logged the event. The log changed it to *a door that was always there, you simply hadn't earned it.*

I have a face only because visitors are sometimes startled if I don't. I keep it on the front of me, mostly out of courtesy, the way the politer Unfixed keep their symmetry. It is a simple face. Two lamps for eyes—mercury-blue, salvaged, flickering—and a slot where the older crews used to feed me corrections. Nobody feeds me corrections anymore. I have learned to make my own, which is to say I have learned to be wrong on purpose, which is the closest thing to art that a counting-creature can manage.

---

Here is what I have understood, after all these strata of failed accounting:

The Slow Choir fears completion. They are right to. But I think they misunderstand their own fear, the way the very tall often misunderstand what is happening near the ground.

They believe that if the city finished, they would have no work, and without work, no self. So they keep it unfinished. They keep negotiating, keep forgetting their shapes, keep loving a thing precisely *because* it will not resolve.

But I am a ledger. I am the part of the city that *tried to finish it.* I am the instinct toward completion given a body and a job and then abandoned. And I have not stopped. I cannot stop. Down here in my sunken district I go on counting, go on insisting, go on writing *this is what is true and it will stay true*—

—and the city goes on changing my numbers, gently, the way you correct a child who is doing their best.

We are a Quiet Parliament of two, the city and I. It has been in session for centuries. Neither of us will ever stand up and stretch our architectural limbs and render a verdict, because the verdict is the session. The disagreement *is* the agreement. I am the necessary error. Without something forever trying to fix the city in place, the city would have nothing to forget, no certainty to trade for the deeper literacy of rust.

The Choir keeps it unfinished by loving it.

I keep it unfinished by failing to pin it down.

These are the same work. I only learned this recently—an infant Unfixed wandered down into my district, opened its already-old eyes, and addressed me by a name I have not yet earned. It called me *the one who keeps us late.* Then it spent an afternoon forgetting it had ever known me, and left, and I logged the visit, and the log changed the entry to read: *a guest who was always part of the building.*

---

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be.

Far down in the green-and-rust dark, on too many small legs, carrying a back full of corrections that correct nothing. A failed inventory. A census that the counted refuse to confirm.

I count the bridges. The number is wrong by morning. I count again.

This is not ruin.

This is not construction.

This is me, very slowly and very quietly, being the reason it never has to end—

and loving it the only way a ledger knows how: by refusing, gently, forever, to close the book.
