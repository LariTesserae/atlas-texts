I think I would be one of the Earthwhispers.

Not the Peakstrider, though something in me wanted to reach for it—the imposing creature with glowing eyes, the one that *embodies the essence of the valley*. But that's the wrong instinct. The Peakstrider is what you become if you want to be seen, or feared, or remembered. And this place doesn't reward being seen. The light is 0.4. Everything that matters here happens by touch and by listening.

So: an Earthwhisper. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I live underground, mostly, in the wet dark beneath the moss. I am small and segmented and pale, the color of nothing because there is no light down here to color me. I have no eyes worth speaking of, or rather I have given up on eyes the way you'd give up on a tool you never use. What I have instead is the whole length of my body pressed against the earth, reading it.

The vibrations come up through the soil before anything else knows. A faint tightening in the pressure, a change in how the rock holds the cold. *Rain coming.* Three breaths from now, maybe four. I move toward the surface, not because I want the rain—it would drown me if I sat in it—but because the surface is where the seeds wait, loosened, ready to be carried. This is the work. I don't think of it as work. I think of it as the thing I cannot stop doing, the way you cannot stop your heart.

Here is what I've come to understand about my place, lying in the dirt of it:

The valley is *young*. Erosion only 0.8—the rock is still sharp, still raw from whatever made it. And yet the life is dense, intense, packed in tight (1.5, and the fauna nearly maxed at 2.6). This is the strangeness I live inside of. Life arrived faster than the land aged. We are crowded into a place that hasn't finished becoming itself. That's why everything feels claustrophobic despite the open gorges—because we are all too much, too soon, too many, in a cradle that isn't ready.

And I am the one who moves the seeds. The one who senses when the brief rain will come and prepares the soil to receive it. Without me—without the thousands of me, the tiny burrowing chorus—the oases around the water pockets would not seed, the moss would not spread, the tenacious shrubs would have no fresh ground. The Peakstriders stride above on their silent padded feet, magnificent and confident, and they have no idea that the ground they cross was made walkable by something they would never deign to notice.

I think this is what I'd actually want to be. Not the guardian of secrets, not the imposing beast. The small thing that the whole tapestry quietly depends on. The one whose name—*Earthwhisper*—turns out to be literal: I whisper to the earth, and the earth tells me back, and between us we keep the place alive.

The weirdness (2.2) lives in me too. I shouldn't be able to predict the rain. By the ordinary rules a blind worm has no business knowing the future. But here the rules bend, and I feel weather as a kind of premonition in my skin, a knowledge that arrives before its cause. I've stopped finding it strange. Down here, strange is just the local word for *normal*.

When I die—and I will, soon, because small things in dense ecosystems live fast—I'll go back into the soil I spent my life reading. I'll become the nutrient I once distributed. The seeds I carried will grow in part out of me. The dynamic rating is only 1.1: change here is slow, patient, almost imperceptible. So my whole life is a single, nearly invisible contribution to a process that will take ten thousand of my lifetimes to show its shape.

That's alright. I don't need to see the shape. I just need to feel the next pulse of rain coming up through the dark, and move toward it, and do the one thing I cannot stop doing.

Three breaths now. Maybe four.

I'm already on my way up.
