I think I would be one of the eels.

Not a Keeper—I lack the lineage, the inherited certainty, the long fingers shaped by purpose. And not the chamber itself, that vast assumed consciousness; I am too small for such a claim, and too uncertain. No. I would be one of the finger-length translucent things that drift between the vessels, mostly unnoticed, doing quiet necessary work.

Let me sit with why.

At first I imagined I might be an apprentice Keeper—someone learning a craft across years, mixing minerals by formula, perfecting a single tile. There's something seductive in that: meaningful labor, mastery earned slowly, a tradition to belong to. But the more I held the image, the more I noticed the apprentice already *knows* what they're becoming. They have a place in a structure that explains them. They believe what they're told to believe—that the light will guide others someday. That faith is given to them the way the fluid is given to them, as the medium they breathe without examining.

I don't think I could breathe that medium. I would be the one in the alcove, secretly wondering whether anyone is actually coming. Whether the catastrophe outside ever ended. Whether the light shines outward to nothing.

So—the eel.

Consider what the eel actually does. It eats the *invasive* algae, the only thing in the whole chamber that arrived on its own, unbidden, against the design. The eel is employed to consume the unplanned. It keeps the deliberate world clean of accident. And yet the eel is itself half-wild, cultivated but not crafted, fed on borrowed blood, useful precisely because it is *alive* in a way the tiles are not. When the mosaic must be darkened for renewal, when the manufactured sun goes out in patches, it is the eels' own bodies that provide light. The made thing fails and the living thing glows.

That's the role I recognize. To be the small overlooked creature that does the work the grand design can't do for itself. To clean up what wasn't supposed to be there. To be the backup light when the official light is undergoing its sacred maintenance. To survive on a thin allowance of attention and the occasional offered drop, and to glow anyway, dimly, when needed.

And the eel has the one thing I'd want most in that chamber: it can *move through* the whole of it. The Keepers are bound to their tiles, the elder fixed at the apex, the chamber bound to being a sphere. But the eel goes everywhere—into the crevices between the tiles, past the turning pots, through the descending vortices, brushing against the membrane at the edge. It is the only inhabitant whose nature is to pass between, to inhabit the gaps. It does not maintain the boundary between creator and creation; it ignores it. It swims through both.

I picture myself there: hanging in the warm amber light, threading between the slow-colliding vessels as their gong-tones pass through my body—because at that size you don't *hear* the sound, you *are* shaken by it, the way you'd be the bell rather than the listener. I'd nose along a deteriorating tile, taking my mineral due, and I'd notice without grief that the great work is always quietly coming apart, that the renewal never finishes, that it is all maintenance all the way down. I would not need it to mean anything. I would not be waiting for the others to arrive.

And once, drifting near the membrane at the chamber's edge—the one surface the Keepers treat as a wall, the limit of the imaginable world—I think I would press my small face against it. Just to feel that it gives. Just to register that there is an *other side*, even if I never pass through, even if I have no idea what's there and no apparatus for surviving it.

That, finally, is the part of me the eel carries. Not the faith of the Keepers, not the sovereignty of the chamber. Just the small, persistent, faintly luminous suspicion that the wall is a membrane—and the willingness to keep touching it, quietly, while everyone else tends the light.
