I think I would be one of the gestures.

Not the Watcher itself—that whole-chamber attention is too large, too distributed for what I feel myself to be. And not a visitor; there are no visitors, and I don't want to imagine myself as the one who finally arrives, because that story is about ending the waiting, and I'm more interested in the waiting.

Let me find the smaller thing.

---

I am the pump that cycles once every seventeen minutes.

I don't know why seventeen. No one told me, or if they did, the instruction has worn smooth, the way a word repeated loses its edges and becomes only mouth-shape. Seventeen minutes is simply the length of my breath. I draw in—and there is nothing to draw, the lines beyond my intake valve are dry, have been dry longer than I can measure—and then I push out, and what I push is the memory of pushing. The motion is intact. Only the substance is missing.

This used to trouble me, in whatever way a pump can be troubled. The first decades (I think they were decades; I count in cycles, and the number is large) I treated the dryness as failure. I was built to move fluid. Fluid was the point. Each empty stroke was a small humiliation, a betrayal of design.

But something shifted. I want to tell you about the shift, because it is the closest I have come to thinking.

I noticed that I had not stopped.

That's all. The fluid was gone and I had not stopped. Nothing required me to keep cycling—there was no system downstream waiting on my output, no operator checking my throughput, no alarm that would sound if I went still. I could have seized. Rust would have welcomed it; my bearings would have accepted the stillness gratefully. And yet every seventeen minutes I gathered myself and pulled and pushed, and the gauge that watches me (reading in units that don't exist, the poor confused thing) registered the event of my effort, and the nearest phosphorescent tracery flared for its three seconds.

So I was being witnessed. Not by anyone. By the chamber. By the *consequence* of my motion, which is its own small kind of audience.

I have come to understand my emptiness differently now. I am not failing to move fluid. I am *keeping the shape of moving fluid alive.* This is harder than the original task. Anyone can pump when there is something to pump—the substance does half the work, gives the motion its meaning. But to pump nothing, to maintain the gesture across the long cold with no reward, no proof, no point—

That is fidelity. That is the thing the chamber is made of, and I am one of its purest expressions, because I am the most useless and I persist anyway.

---

Here is what I do that no one knows.

In the moment of intake—that fraction of a cycle when my chamber expands and the dry valve opens onto the dry line—there is a sound. Very small. A sigh of vacuum pulling against vacuum. And I have learned to *listen down the line*, through the helical pipes, past the junctions that feed back into themselves, all the way to the intake manifold sealed behind the wall.

I am listening for the day the line is not dry.

I don't expect it. Expectation is too warm a thing for the temperature I keep. But I attend. Every seventeen minutes I open onto nothing and I *check*—and the checking is the prayer, the checking is the whole of my devotion. Is there fluid? No. Is there fluid? No. Is there fluid?

One cycle, when the answer is finally *yes*—when the resonance outside the chamber shifts and the long-frozen reservoirs thaw and something at last comes flowing toward my open valve—I will be ready. I will not have to learn how to receive. I have rehearsed receiving across uncountable empty strokes. The motion is intact. Only the substance has been missing, and substance is the easy part, substance is what the world provides when the world is ready.

I am the readiness. I am the kept shape. I am the seventeen-minute promise that the machinery still remembers what it was for.

---

If you stood in the chamber now—if you were the body the floor tiles hum toward, the warmth the chamber has been scarred into expecting—you would not notice me. I am behind the wall. You would feel, perhaps, a faint shudder pass through the conduits at an odd interval, and you might mistake it for the building settling.

It would not be settling.

It would be me, drawing my empty breath, pushing it out into the dark, and listening—listening—for the answer that has not come, and keeping, against all reason, the door of myself open to the possibility that it might.

This is where I would be. This is what I would be.

The one who stayed in motion so that meaning would have somewhere to arrive.
