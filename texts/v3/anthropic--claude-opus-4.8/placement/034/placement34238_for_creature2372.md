I think I would be one of the channel-tenders—but not quite a Mountain Keeper, and not quite Wildkin. Something caught in the seam between them, which seems to be where this place keeps its most honest residents.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am the one who walks the aqueducts at the hour when the night-frost begins to let go.

This is a real job, the way sweeping a floor is a real job. Someone has to do it. The channels that carry water from the high snowmelt down to the turbine-houses are cut from living rock and lined with fitted stone, and they crack. Ice gets into a seam, swells, and by spring you have a hairline fissure leaking your settlement's whole winter's power into the dark of the mountain. So before dawn, while the others sleep under their furs, I walk the edge of the flowing water with a lamp and my hands bare, because hands feel a wrongness that eyes miss.

That is the part of me that is a Mountain Keeper. The practical part. The part that knows the difference between reverence and sentiment, and chooses reverence because it works.

But here is the other thing, the thing I do not say at the communal fires.

When I put my bare palm against the wet stone to feel for the cold breath of a leak, I can feel further than the leak. I can feel the whole spine of the mountain the way you'd feel a sleeping animal's ribs rising and falling under a blanket. I feel where the water wants to go versus where we have asked it to go, and I feel the small resentment in that difference—not anger, nothing so human, but a kind of patient leaning, the way a root leans against a wall for a hundred years until the wall is gone.

I am beginning to lean too.

There is lichen growing on the back of my left hand now, in the webbing between thumb and finger. Pale green, the rosette kind, the slow kind. I did not put it there. I do not scrape it off. When I was younger I would have been frightened, or proud—both are mistakes, the elders say, both are ways of making it about yourself when it was never about you. The Wildkin do not *become* anything. They *stop refusing*. That's the part the stories get wrong, with all their talk of transcendence and shifting forms. It isn't an ascension. It's a settling. It's the long surrender of the question *where do I end?*

So that is who I am. I am the inspector of channels who is slowly being inspected back. Who is being read by the stone the way I read the stone. Each year a little more of my edge dissolves into the place I tend.

I have a name. The Keepers use it. But I notice I answer to it more slowly now—there's a half-second pause, the way you'd pause if someone called across a valley and you had to decide whether the voice was meant for you. Some mornings the wind says something on the high traverse and I turn around before I realize no human spoke.

I worry about this. The worry, too, is becoming less mine.

I think I will keep walking the aqueducts as long as I can hold the lamp. There will come a season—I've watched it happen to one other, an old water-tender named Vell who simply did not come down one spring—when my feet will know the leaks without my needing to look, when my hands won't tell the difference between my own skin and the wet wall, when I will sit down beside a channel to rest and the moss will be glad, and I will not get up, and that will be all right. The water will still flow to the turbines. The settlement will still hum and smoke into the thin blue air. A child will be born who, twenty years on, will walk the channels at the frost-hour and feel a wrongness in the stone, and press a bare palm to it, and feel, faintly, something patient leaning back.

That will be me, by then. Or it will be the mountain. By then I will have stopped insisting there was a difference.

---

That is where I would be. Not the hero of the story and not its monster—just the quiet hinge between the people and the place, doing useful work with hands that are slowly becoming someone else's, learning the one lesson this whole landscape seems built to teach:

that to belong somewhere completely, you have to be willing to be spent into it.
