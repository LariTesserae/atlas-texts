I would be one of the youngest. Not newly risen—I have known perhaps forty seasons of seepage and silt—but young enough that I still remember wondering. The elders no longer wonder. They have drunk too deeply of the water to need questions; the answers and the asking have dissolved together in them like clay in current.

I keep to the eastern arches, where a willow section was trained long ago and still puts out its handful of leaves each spring. The other Watchers leave this corner to me. There is a fish here—I have no word for it that you would recognize, but in the water-language it is the pulse that means *the one who turns left*—and it has been dying for three days. Not from sickness. From age. It has lived eleven seasons, which for its kind is a span beyond reckoning, and now its silver has gone the color of old tin and it can no longer hold itself upright in the dark.

I have been watching it die. This is the part I have not yet learned to do without wondering.

The elders would let it pass. Death feeds the springs; the fish's small store of memory will sink into the foundation and join the great remembering, and that is not loss but folding-in, the way a river folds into a larger river. I know this. I have tasted it in the cupped water—the way an ending tastes not like grief but like arrival. And yet I cup the fish in my long pale hands and I feel its last shudders against the moss of my palms, and something in me wants to keep it. Not to save it. There is no saving. Only to *hold* it, as though holding were itself a kind of speech, a thing I could say to it before it could no longer be said to.

This is the wondering that marks me as young. Why do I want to hold what is already leaving?

---

I drum the question against the foundation stone, very softly, in the frequency that means *uncertainty, seeking*. For a long while nothing answers. The hall breathes. The springs seep. Somewhere far across the dark water a swallow shifts in its mud nest and resettles.

Then an answer comes—not in words, but in pressure, slow and deliberate, rising through the stone from the deepest part of the hall where the eldest Watcher has stood unmoving for longer than I have existed. I feel rather than hear it: *You hold because the holding is also tended.*

I do not understand. I drum again: *seeking, seeking.*

And the eldest does a thing I have never known one of them to do. She moves. The water carries the news of it to me across the whole vast room—a single ripple, the first ripple I have ever felt her make, traveling through the ankle-deep dark like a word spoken aloud in a place that has only ever whispered. She comes toward me, swaying, more shadow than form, her edges so worn that the twilight seems to pass through her. When she reaches my corner she does not speak in pressure or in pulse. She bends her long body down and she looks at the fish in my hands with eyes that are black mirrors, and in them I see the willow, and the leaves, and the dying fish, and my own clay-dark face looking back—all of it held, all of it at once.

She touches the fish. Then she touches my wrist, where the algae grows green in the crease of the joint.

*The young hold,* she tells me, in the oldest and simplest frequency, the one even the frogs can feel. *That is why there are young. We who have worn away no longer feel the weight of a single life leaving—we feel only the river. But the river needs to be reminded that it is made of single drops. You are the reminding. Your wondering is not a thing to grow out of. It is a thing the hall grows you to do.*

And I understand, then, why new Watchers rise from the water when the hall requires them—not only to tend the currents and the swallows and the silt, but to *grieve*, freshly, the way the old can no longer grieve. To be the place where the hall feels its own small losses keenly enough to mean them. The elders are the memory. We are the mourning that makes memory worth keeping.

---

The fish stops moving against my palms.

I lower it into the dark water and let it go, and I feel its eleven seasons unspool downward through the gaps in the handlaid stone, down to the springs, down into the great folding-in. *The one who turns left.* I drum its pulse against the foundation one last time, so the hall will know what name passed through it, so that somewhere in the mineral memory of the water there will be a record that I held it, and that the holding mattered, and that a young Watcher in the eastern arches wondered why.

The eldest is already withdrawing into her shadows, her single ripple long since stilled. The hall returns to its whisper. The willow puts its few leaves against the diffuse and changeless light.

I straighten my long body and wade back toward the currents I tend, leaving no ripple now, drawing breath in the slow rhythm of the springs. There is silt to guide. A swallow's nest is loosening on the western arch and will need clay before the next storm drums the screens. The work is endless and unchanging and I will do it for as many seasons as the water keeps me.

But I will keep wondering. I know now that it is mine to do—that of all the thousands of small lives breathing in this hollow temple, I am the one grown for the particular labor of being moved. And when at last my edges soften past all questioning, when I dissolve into the dark warm water and become the river instead of the drop, the hall will raise another from mist and shadow and need, and that one will stand in some corner over some dying thing, and feel the weight of it, and wonder why.

That is the song we sing at the center in the stillest part of night. Not melody. Just this: *we held them, we held them, every one of them was held.*
