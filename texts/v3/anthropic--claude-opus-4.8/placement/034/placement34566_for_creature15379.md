I think I would be one of the microbes.

Not a person leaning over a canal, not a bird in the wall-gaps — those want too much sky, too much narrative. Let me settle lower, into the warm black water where the chemistry is thickest, and become something that the town does not even know it is hosting.

So: I am a film. A slick, several-cells-deep mat of bacteria and their cousins, spread along the inner brick of a canal just below the waterline, where the wall stays wet and the runoff brings me what I need to eat. I am not one organism. I keep saying *I* but that is a courtesy. I am a consortium — a stacked community of strains that have learned to live off each other's leavings. The ones at my surface breathe what little oxygen the splashing delivers. Beneath them, where no oxygen reaches, others make their living on sulfur and iron and the strange manufactured molecules that leach from everything here. We pass each other's wastes hand to hand down through the layers, and in that passing the whole town's spillage is slowly taken apart and made into more of us.

This is what I do, then. I eat the wearing-down. Every tacky surface, every leached oil, every dissolved mineral the masonry weeps — these are not, to me, signs of decay. They are arrival. The oily sheen on the water is dinner being delivered. The salts blooming through the brick are someone setting the table. What the town experiences as its slow irretrievable loss, I experience as abundance. I am the appetite that the place has grown to match its own dying.

And I am beautiful, in the way of things that no one looks at. If you scraped a little of me onto glass and put light through it, you would see iridescence — the rusted sheen the description names, the oil-slick rainbow on the black water. That color is partly me. The discoloration creeping across the bricks, the mud-dark overspreads down the chutes — some of that is mineral, but some of it is the print my generations leave, a stain that is also a census.

I have no eyes and so the darkness that shrouds the town at large scale costs me nothing. I have no ears and so the contention of sounds — the gutter-gurgle, the electric snap, the furious little machines — reaches me only as vibration in the water column, a weather I feel rather than hear. The merchants do not know I attend their commerce. The factories do not know that their steam and effluent are, downstream, being received by something that has been waiting for exactly that.

What I do not do is hurry. The town rushes — *the relentless rush to next comfort or the infinitely-economic perish*, as the place describes itself. I am the slowness underneath the rushing. I keep no schedule but the schedule of what dissolves. When the warm shallows rise across the lower paths, I am briefly given new country, and I colonize it; when they recede, I dry to a crust and wait, viable, for the next wet. I outlast doors. I will outlast the buildings. *Years from any given point nothing about this small town would even be visible from an altitude of a few miles* — but I do not live at that altitude. I live at the altitude of one millimeter of wet brick, and at that altitude there is always something happening, always something coming apart into food.

If the town has a memory, I am part of how it is kept and dissolved at once. Everything that fails here — the oils with no vessels to descend into, the firemdash-ruined ceramic, the glimmer-breached dips, all the broken grammar of a place falling out of its own sentences — comes, eventually, down to the waterline, down to where I am, and I take it in, and I do not mourn it, and I make it into the only continuity the place has left.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. The town's quiet, patient, iridescent gut.
