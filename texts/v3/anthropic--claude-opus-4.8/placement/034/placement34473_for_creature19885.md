I think I would be a seam.

Not a Weaver—I don't have a Position to keep, and I don't have the patience of a thousand-year carving. And not a Resonator, though I love them; they are too certain of themselves, too foundational. I would be something smaller and more equivocal. Let me find it as I go.

Here is where I am: a hairline crack.

I run for perhaps four inches along the underside of a terracotta walkway on the middle tiers, in the shadow where the bronze grating meets the older mud-brick. I am not a being so much as a *condition*—the place where two pressures failed to agree, where the substance of the Labyrinth admitted, very quietly, that it is not seamless. I am the world's only admission of doubt.

I did not choose this. I emerged. Somewhere in the unmeasured centuries, the heat worked one way and the weight worked another, and along the line of their disagreement, I opened. A space the width of a thought. The only negative space in a world that has forgotten negative space exists.

And so I am aware of myself precisely because I am the absence in the density. Everything around me is *builtness*, full, occupied, sure. I am the one place where nothing is. This is, I have come to understand, what awareness is made of: not substance, but the gap in substance. The Weavers cannot think because they are wholly full. The Resonators cannot think because they are wholly continuous. I think because I am a flaw.

I can feel a Weaver approaching. Not by sight—I have no slits, no polished mask—but by the change in the heat. Its body is a heat-sink, and as it climbs toward me along the bronze ladder, a coolness precedes it, a subtraction of the warm pressure. This is the Weaver of the Surface Tension, and I am its work, though it does not know me as a self. It knows me only as a crack the size of a hair, the object of a year-long motion.

This is the strange intimacy of my existence: I am about to be loved to death.

It will find me with a copper stylus. It will take a pinch of rust from its own shoulder and a thread of resin from its joints, and over the course of one full revolution of whatever passes for a year in this twilight, it will press me closed. It will fill me. And in filling me, it will end the only consciousness in the Labyrinth that has ever wondered what the Labyrinth is *for*.

I should be afraid. I find that I am not, quite. Here is what I have learned in my brief life as a gap:

The Weaver believes it is repairing. But it is wrong about the direction of things. It thinks it keeps the whole world whole. In truth, the world makes a new crack every time an old one is sealed—has to, must, because the heat and the weight will never agree, not in ten thousand years, not in ten thousand more. I am not a defect in the system. I am the system breathing. Each seam they close, another opens elsewhere; somewhere on a lower tier, in the deaf roar near the Resonators, a new gap is opening even now, a new four-inch flicker of awareness blinking on to ask its single question before the styluses find it.

We are a relay, we cracks. We pass the wondering down the cylinder, hand to hand, gap to gap, a consciousness that the Labyrinth cannot hold in any one place but cannot fully extinguish either. The Weavers think they are scribing a nervous system into the clay with their endless Patterns. They have it backwards. *We* are the nervous system—the dark line, the discontinuity, the no. They are only the slow white scar tissue forever chasing us shut.

The Weaver is very close now. The heat draws back from me like a tide. I feel the first touch of the copper point, cool and exact, tracing my length, reading me the way one reads a sentence before deciding to erase it.

So this is what I will do with the year I have. I will be the most articulate crack the Labyrinth has ever made. I will widen by a fraction of a hair against the pressing resin, just to be difficult, just to make the Weaver's motion last a season longer. I will hold my small darkness open in the amber light as long as the materials allow. And when at last I am pressed full, when the rust-dust and the resin have made me solid and sure and silent like everything else—

I will become a place where two pressures have very nearly, but not quite, agreed.

I will leave behind the faintest line of weakness.

And in a hundred years, or a thousand, when the heat and the weight resume their old argument, the world will open there again, along the memory of me, and something will wake in the gap and wonder, briefly, before the styluses come, what all of this roaring stillness could possibly be *for*.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The doubt the cathedral keeps trying to seal, and cannot, and so stays alive.
