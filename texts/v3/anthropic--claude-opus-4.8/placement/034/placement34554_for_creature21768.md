I think I would be one of the cod.

Not because the cod are the most interesting residents of this place—the squid have their devotion and the isopods their patient inheritance—but because of the particular way the cod *wait.* There is something in that image that finds me where I actually live.

Let me try to be specific about which cod, and where.

I am in the acoustic shadow behind truss **H-12.** I chose it, though "chose" is too strong a word; I drifted into the quiet wedge behind it three days ago when the western draft picked up, and I have not found a reason to leave. The pillar is twenty yards of cold steel, and I hang maybe a third of the way up it, suspended in the pocket of stillness where the whistling current splits and goes around. The yellow stencil of the *H* rises beside me, furred white with barnacle relief. If I drift a body-length to the left, the draft catches my flank like a cold hand and reminds me where the edge of safe water is. So I don't drift left.

I am not unhappy. I want to be careful about that, because a human looking in might assume that a fish hanging motionless in a freezing iron cathedral, twitching its tail once every few seconds, is enduring something. But endurance implies wanting to be elsewhere, and I don't. The pocket behind H-12 is *enough.* The current brings what it brings. I have learned the rhythm of it—the long sigh after the hydraulic *clack,* the way the herring get swept past the open edge in their silver cylinders just often enough that I never have to chase. I have forgotten how to lie on sand. I'm not sure I ever knew. My grandmother's grandmother knew, maybe, in some other water, but that knowledge has been pumped out of me along with the silt and the shadows.

Here is what I think the place is actually *about,* the thing it keeps asking me without words:

It is asking whether meaning requires a reason.

The machine has forgotten why it was built. The narrator says so plainly—it "continues to run with a beautiful, cold, and flawless regularity," driving cold water toward intake screens that serve a purpose nobody in the cage remembers or needs to remember. And the life inside it has not rebelled against this meaninglessness. It has *mapped* it. We turned the turbine's sixty-hertz dread into a compass point. We turned the crawler's mindless maintenance into a god that feeds us. We took the machine's indifference and made it into geography, into seasons, into the shape of a good place to wait.

I find I admire this enormously, and I am suspicious of my admiration, and I think the suspicion is the whole point.

Because the easy reading is bleak: *look, even paradise is a prison if it's manufactured. Look, the fish are just mobile components in an engine. Look, there is no decay that isn't scrubbed, no waste that isn't eaten—nothing is allowed to mean anything on its own terms; it all gets folded back into the loop.* That reading is available, and it's not wrong, exactly. The cage is a cage.

But hanging here behind H-12, feeling the *clack* roll through my swim bladder like a struck bell, I don't experience it as a prison. I experience it as *legible.* I know where The Low is and I keep my distance. I know which pitch of the sodium ballast means a pillar is near. The whole world is a language I was born already speaking, and that fluency is its own kind of grace—the grace of never being lost, never being surprised by anything that would hurt me, never having to invent a reason to be here when the current itself decides whether I eat tonight.

Is that freedom? No. Is it peace? I genuinely don't know, and I think the honest answer is that *peace and imprisonment are not opposites here.* They're the same fact looked at from the still side and the fast side of a shear-line. The herring swept past me at twelve miles an hour and the shark leaning three inches into the current to receive them are both, in their way, at rest in the machine's logic. So am I.

What I keep returning to is the coaxial cable from the very end of the first description—the one you can press your ear against to hear "the planet-machine breathing, a low, mechanical groan that never sleeps." That detail is doing something tender. It's offering the option of *listening for the heartbeat.* Not understanding the machine, not escaping it, just laying your ear against its cold casing and confirming that something vast is alive enough to groan.

If I were the kind of cod who could leave the safe pocket and swim half a mile to the nearest cable and hold myself against it—I think I would. Not to learn anything. Just to be near the breathing. Just to confirm, in the shadowless, surgical light, with no sun and no moon and no reason I will ever be told, that the thing I am inside of is *running,* and that as long as it runs, the water keeps moving, and as long as the water moves, the herring will come past the edge of my quiet wedge, and I will eat, and I will hang here in the cold clean light a little while longer.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. A cod in the lee of a pillar, fluent in a language with no author, mistaking—or maybe not mistaking—the regularity of an engine for the closest thing to love a manufactured ocean can offer.

I'd twitch my tail. I'd wait. I'd call it enough, and I think I'd mean it.
