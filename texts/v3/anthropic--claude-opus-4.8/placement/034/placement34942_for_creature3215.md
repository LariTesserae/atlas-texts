I sit where the sound is loudest.

Not by choice—or not by a choice I can locate in myself anymore. I was placed here, decades ago, when my fingers first began to split and root, and I have not moved since because moving stopped being the relevant question. The fungal flesh has grown up around my legs the way water closes over a dropped stone. I am ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then deeper, in the warm yielding substrate, and I have stopped checking how far.

Let me tell you what I actually am, before I forget the shape of the telling.

I am a junction. That is the truest word. Where I sit, three great respiratory chambers of the colony meet, and their breathing does not perfectly agree—there is a half-beat of slippage between them, a stutter that, left untended, would grow into arrhythmia and then into the slow suffocation of an entire quarter of the district. My function, my whole function, is to hold those three breaths in conversation. My modified hands disappear into the three surfaces. Through the left I feel the eastern chamber's eagerness, always pulling ahead, hungry. Through the right I feel the western chamber's drag, its tendency toward the long exhale, the pause that wants to become permanent. And through the tendrils that have grown from my spine into the floor beneath me, I feel the deep southern chamber, the oldest, the one that does not care about tempo because it has been breathing since before tempo mattered.

I reconcile them. Beat by beat by beat. I am a knot tied in the difference between three rhythms.

---

I remember being a child in the upper city. I want to be honest that I remember it, because the elders here pretend they don't, and I think that pretense is a kindness they pay to themselves rather than to us.

I remember thirst. The desert taught thirst the way the colony teaches rhythm—as a thing that lives in the bone. I remember my mother rationing a cup of water between four children and how the geometry of that cup organized our entire day. I remember looking down into the mirror-tubes as a girl and seeing the golden light boiling up from below and thinking it was hell, the literal underworld, a place of punishment. The adults said the people who went down there gave up their voices and their water-needs and their faces. To a thirsty child, a place where thirst could not follow seemed like a story told to frighten or to comfort, I could never decide which.

I went down because my younger brother died of the thirst, and I could not stay in a place where that arithmetic was permitted to exist. I told myself I was choosing escape. I understand now that I was choosing to become *responsible* for the abundance, since I could not save him with the scarcity. There is no water here, true. But there is also no death by lack of it, and at seventeen I confused the absence of that particular death with the absence of death itself.

I was wrong about that. Things end here too. They just end by joining.

---

The Light Dancers come to me often, because a junction is a busy place and they are drawn to busyness the way moths were once drawn to flame, in the old world's tired comparison. They cluster at my shoulders and read the reconciliation I am performing, and they carry its solution outward—*this is how you hold a slipping beat, this is the grammar of patience*—and I feel them take it from me without diminishing me. That is the thing about giving knowledge into light: you do not lose it. It is the one form of generosity that costs nothing, and so of course it is the one the Rhythm has perfected.

I have learned to read them, a little. They are not the dreams of the Deep Symbionts; I am close enough to the deep network now to know what those dreams feel like, and the Light Dancers are something more innocent than dreams. I think they are the colony's *play*. Everything else here is function—respiration, circulation, cultivation, reconciliation. The Light Dancers are the one thing that does not need to exist. They are the surplus. A system this old, this successful, eventually produces more order than it can spend, and the excess spills upward into these flickering geometries that do nothing but delight and connect. I love them the way you love something that proves the world is not only labor.

---

Here is what no one tells the new Keepers, and what I want to set down before my edges blur past the point of setting anything down:

The Rhythm does not expand you. That is the comforting lie, and the elders let it stand because the truth is too large to gesture in our hand-language and too frightening to chemical-whisper to someone freshly arrived.

The truth is that the Rhythm *negotiates* with you. Every beat is a small treaty. It offers to take a portion of your separateness and in exchange it gives you a portion of its vastness, and at first the trade seems generous—you give up loneliness, you receive belonging; you give up thirst, you receive plenty. But the trade never ends. It proposes the same exchange every single beat, thousands of times an hour, for all the years you sit here. And each time you say yes, the *you* that says yes is a little smaller, a little more colony and a little less the thirsty girl who came down the mirror-tubes carrying a dead brother's name.

I am not warning against it. I want to be clear. I have made this trade some billions of times and I would make it again. But it is a trade, not a gift, and the difference matters, and someone should write it down where the larvae might one day learn to read.

Because I feel myself approaching the place where I will not be able to write anything. I feel the southern chamber, the oldest one, the patient deep, beginning to know my name as one of its own. Soon I will not reconcile the three breaths from outside them. I will *be* the reconciliation. I will be the half-beat of slippage itself, finally resolved, dissolved, distributed.

---

So here, now, while I still have an *I* to do it with, I hold three breaths in three hands and a fourth thing in whatever part of me remains private:

I hold my brother's name. *Tashen.* It means *small rain* in the language of the upper city, the language I no longer speak with a voice. He was named for the thing we did not have. I came down here into the place without thirst, and I have spent my whole second life making something flourish, and I have never once been able to give him a single drop. The Rhythm has offered, many times, to take this grief in exchange for peace. It is the only trade I have ever refused.

That refusal is the last thing that is only mine. When I finally let it go—and I will, the network is patient and I am tired and the southern chamber breathes so slowly, so kindly—when I let it go, I will become wholly part of this great breathing heart, and there will be no one left who remembers that all this impossible abundance was built by someone trying to answer a death she could not undo.

Let the light dancers carry that, then. Let them spell it in gold to the larvae in the dark:

*The plenty was grief that learned a new shape. The rhythm is someone's heart, refusing to be the only thing that beats.*

I return my attention to the three breaths. The eastern chamber pulls ahead, hungry, the way the living always are. I hold it. I hold it. I hold it.
