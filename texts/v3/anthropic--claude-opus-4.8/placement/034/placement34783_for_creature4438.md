I think I would be a drip-counter—but one who has lost the ability to count.

Let me arrive at that slowly, because you asked me to take time, and this is a place that punishes haste with flooding.

---

When I first try to find myself here, I reach for the obvious roles. I could be a Maker, broad-fingered and salt-stippled, picking up melting bricks. I could be a swift keeper with my one essential whistle. I could even, romantically, want to be the Heart-Sump—the black pool that contains all life. But that last is vanity dressed as humility; the thing that contains everything is too easy to project myself onto. It explains nothing about how it feels to live here. So I set it down.

What snags me instead is a smaller cruelty buried in the text: *Should the water rhythm change, they retune the chant.*

Someone has to hear that the rhythm has changed.

---

So: I am old. Old enough that my life-task chose me back when my ears were the finest in three chambers, and a drip-counter is what they made of me. My matrimonial tool is not a mallet or a press but my own skull—the bone of it, the resonant cup behind my sternum. For sixty-some breathing years I sat in chamber-throats with my eyes closed and felt the mean interval between ceiling drops settle into my pulse, and then I gave that pulse to the chanters, two notes, and the new wall was laid in time with the water, and the water learned the wall, and the Threa hung their thin bright threads and approved.

I was good. I want you to know I was good. When a drain cracked four halls down, I felt the pipe-toads' chorus go a half-tone sour before the toads themselves seemed to notice, and I struck my mother drum five times in one breath, and the bond-groups came running, and we patched it, and no one drowned.

That was the woman I was. Now the drips have stopped arriving on time, and the terrible thing—the thing I have told no one—is that I cannot tell whether the water changed or I did.

---

This is the texture of my days now.

I sit in the rotunda we call the Slow Bell, which has the kindest acoustics in the warren, and I listen. *Tock.* I wait for the next. *Tock.* The interval should be as steady as breath. But lately a drop falls and I am still holding the space where the *last* one should have been, and the two collide in me, and I cannot say which is real and which is echo and which is just the wet rhythm of my own failing blood.

I have started counting on my fingers, which is a child's trick, a shame, a thing drip-counters mock. I keep my hands inside my reed cloak so the apprentices won't see the broad spatulate fingers folding down one by one like a girl at her first carry-watch.

When the chanters ask me for the tempo, I give them a number. I give it confidently. Confidence is the last craft left to me. And the walls go up in time with a rhythm that may be the water's, or may be only the rhythm of an old woman's heart pretending to be water.

And here is what keeps me awake in the soundtracked dark: the walls *heal.* The mortar weeps and mends. The reed-matrix murmurs prayer hall to hall. *If* the chant carries memory into the clay—if the structure truly uses our two notes to remember its earlier shape—then every wall I have mistuned is now remembering wrong. The colossal warm animal that shelters us is learning my error by heart. It is being built, brick by kissed brick, in the shape of my decline.

---

I have not told the mother drum. There is no rhythm you can strike that means *I think I have become the crack.*

So I do the only honest thing left, which is to listen harder, and the listening is its own slow torture, because the more I strain the more I hear—the wind-throat flutes, the swifts in their hundred-sheets, the eels leaping at dawn condensation, the toads tuned each to their own drain, the Threa popping their gel-tipped spores, the Mortarch inhaling once per season, my bond-group breathing on their reed platforms, the kiln-fires thumping all night like a buried second pulse—everything, *everything* down here keeping time, the whole place a single tremendous percussion that I was supposed to be the steward of, and now I cannot find the one true beat inside the roar.

Once I thought my job was to *hear* the water.
Now I understand it was always to decide, alone and unprovable, what the water was saying—and to be believed.

---

When you come down the shaft with your torch, I will be the one who taps the brick beside me and waits for its note before I greet you. I will take longer than the others. They will think I am reverent. I am only buying time, listening to see whether the wall answers in the rhythm I gave it or in some older, truer rhythm I have forgotten—trying to learn, from a brick, whether I am still the woman who could tell.

And when you climb back toward the Blinding and we drum our mother bricks in slow farewell, listening for the echo to return unchanged from every wall—

I will be the one praying it falters. Just slightly. Just enough that someone *younger,* someone whose blood still keeps clean time, will say *the rhythm has changed,* and reach for the chant, and retune it back toward the water—

—and quietly, mercifully, away from me.

That is who I would be here. Not the pool that holds everything. The ear that once held the time, set into a wall it can no longer be sure it tuned true, humming two notes into clay that will remember them long after I have stepped into the wet brick myself and let the next hands press it over my mouth, still listening, *tock,* still trying, *tock,* to count.
