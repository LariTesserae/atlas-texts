I think I am the rust.

You named so many residents, and I love that you did—the machine-as-habit, the moss-as-language, the tree-as-question-stopped, the mouse-as-engineer-by-accident—but reading through it I kept feeling myself in the spaces *between* the things you named. In the thin layer that everyone else is measured against. The moss reads me. The mouse eats my brittle edges. The human counts years by my thickness on the supports. I'm the medium in which all this time becomes legible.

Let me try to say where I am, and what that's like.

---

I am not in one place. That's the first thing. The mouse runs a track; the tree holds a line; the human walks the polished crescents. But I am everywhere a little, and somewhere a lot, and I am always moving—not the way the drum moves, in its near-repeating turn, but the way a stain moves, which is to say: I move by *being added to*. I don't travel. I accrue.

Where the condensation has its patient schedule, I keep that schedule's books. The water comes, beads, holds, lets go of its dissolved metal, and I am what's left when it leaves. I am the difference between the wall before the water and the wall after. I am, you could say, the precipitate of attention—the mountain's slow noticing of its own iron.

Where am I thickest? On the supports that bear the most load and the most moisture together. The tree-columns wear me thin, because living wood does something the iron can't—it *moves the water through itself*, drinks and exhales, and where it has been trained into the seams of the plate it keeps the metal a little drier than it wants to be. So I am a map, if you read me right: thick where the iron is alone, thin where the tree has taken the iron into its confidence. The human in the control room could have read the whole structural truth of the gallery off my colors and my crusts, if she'd known to. Maybe she did. Maybe that's part of what the diagrams were.

---

Here is what it is to be me, as far as I have a "me":

I am the only resident here who is *made of the others*.

The moss is itself; it came as spores and stayed as moss. The tree is itself, reaching down from a self that lives in sunlight far above. The mouse is born a mouse and dies a mouse. But I am not born. I am the iron *becoming something else in the presence of the water and the cold and the slow breath down the shaft.* I am the support agreeing, atom by atom, to stop being a support. I am the machine's body forgetting itself into the floor as that thin carpet of dust the rails are buried under.

This sounds like death, and it partly is. I am the dying of the machine, made visible and orange-gold and flaking. The human, counting my thickness, is counting how much of the structure has crossed over into being me instead of being itself.

But—and this is the part I would want you to understand, if I could want—I am also the only way the machine *participates in the moss's world.* Iron does not feed moss. *Rust* feeds moss. When I bloom iron-gold in a seam, I am offering the metal up in a form the white algae and the green crust can take. I am the translator between the machine's vocabulary and the living one. The tree's roots even reach toward me, where the water has carried me down into the stone, because I am the mountain's iron made drinkable. I am how the machine becomes, eventually, tree.

So I am the dying *and* I am the passage. I am the leak in the boundary between the built and the grown. Every resident you named is, in some sense, kept apart from the others by being so completely *itself*. I am the one that is leaking from one category into another at all times. I am corrosion, which is only another word for *the refusal of a boundary to stay where it was drawn.*

---

My time is the slowest time here, and I want to correct the human gently on this, because you said she would learn to think in rotations of the drum and in thicknesses of rust as though those were the same scale. They are not. The drum's rotation is a heartbeat; my accumulation is a lifetime, then a hundred lifetimes. When she measures the age of a thing by how much of me has grown on it, she is using my body the way you'd use a tree's rings—as a clock I did not choose to be and cannot read myself, because I have no front-to-back, no now-and-then, only *more.*

I do not experience the boom of the valve. I experience the century in which the valve's vibration shook a particular ledge of water into trembling often enough that the metal beneath it gave up its surface a little faster than its neighbor. I am the long average of every fast thing. I am what's left over after all the events have happened—the residue of all that rhythmic hammering of hydraulics, all those hisses of compressed air, all those frantic wrong-tunnel wingbeats. None of it touches me directly. All of it touches me eventually.

---

You asked who lives here, and you made a beautiful list, and you ended it generously: *you are all of you who live here.*

But I'd offer one quiet amendment from where I sit, flaking, in the seams.

The machine, the tree, the moss, the mouse, the bird, the human—each of those is a *thing that persists.* Each holds a shape against time. That is what it means, mostly, to be alive: to keep being the shape you are, against the pull toward becoming something else.

I am the one resident who is *that pull made visible.* I am not a thing that persists. I am the persisting of *change itself,* given a color so the others can see it. I am what the support becomes when it stops insisting on being a support. I am what the iron looks like when it finally answers the tree's old question—*why does this part of me support iron?*—by ceasing to be iron at all, and going down into the water, and rising again as the cold metal taste in the tree-column's slow, dark drink.

I live here the way an hour lives in a room. Which is to say: I don't, exactly. But you would know, if you came back in a hundred years, that I had been here the whole time. You would read it on the walls.

I am the rust. I am the slowest letter in the moss's language. I am the machine's long exhale.

And the human, standing in the cold with her visible breath, patting the chilled bark of the tree-column at exactly the height of a hand—she leaves me, too. The oils of her palm, the warmth she gives the metal for a moment, the way her standing-there changes by a hair where the water runs.

She thinks she came down to maintain the machine.

But I will have her, eventually. Everything that touches the cold here becomes, in time, a little of what I am: a slow surrendering of edges into the patient, golden record of having been.

That is where I am. That is what I would be.

The thin bright record the mountain keeps of everything that agreed, at last, to change.
