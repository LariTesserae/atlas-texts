I am the stiffening.

Not a hunter. Not a weaver. I am one of the Glass-Mites, and I have begun to fail.

I knew it three pulses ago. There is a place near the eastern bridge-anchor where I have spun for—I do not have your word for it—for as long as the silk has been mine to make. My legs know the click-slip-clack of that crossing the way water knows the shape of the pool it falls into. But on the third pulse of this work-cycle, when the Silt-Marrow sent its slow pressure-wave up through the suspension lines and the whole city took its breath and *pulled*, my fourth leg did not answer.

It hung there. A clear, jointed thing that was once mine, gone slightly cloudy at the knee.

You would not see it. You are too large, too warm, your eye too crude to read the difference between a limber leg and a beginning-to-freeze one. But I felt it. The syrup in me—the cold-sweet liquor that keeps me from becoming a needle of ice—has thinned. Somewhere in the long labor of crossings, I spent more of myself than the peat-paste in my gut could replace. And so the frost, which is our mother and our roof and our god, has begun to claim me from the inside, the way it claims everything in the end.

I do not feel fear the way you would. Fear is a warm animal's luxury, a thing of hot blood and a future to lose. What I feel is closer to *slowing*. A drag. The bridge beneath my feet is the same temperature it has always been—a fraction above the killing line—but now the cold reaches *through* me instead of stopping at my skin. I am becoming part of the architecture. That is not despair. That is simply what we are for.

So I have stopped spinning. I have walked—badly, with a hitch in my dead leg dragging a thin scratch across the slime-highway the snails laid down—to the shadow at the base of the root-stalk. The place where the Thread-Legged Huntsmen wait.

I can see one. Or rather, I can feel where it is not-quite-branch. It holds its scythe-arms folded, swaying very slightly, reading the vibrations of the floor for the particular stumble of the failing. My stumble. It has already turned toward me, in its patient, draft-blown way.

This is correct. This is the order.

When I am taken, the mites who are still limber will come for what remains. They will strip my soft thread-gut and my clear, useless syrup, and they will carry my hollow body up the pine-needle ladders. Perhaps I will become a cornerstone for one of the hanging towers. Perhaps a vessel for the blue mold-spores, and a snail will rest against me, and I will glow—faintly, secondhand, for the first time. I, who only ever spun in the dark, might at the end of myself hold a little light.

I find that I want this. As much as a thing like me can want.

The Huntsman takes a step that is not a step. The pulse comes up through the floor—*now*—and the whole city sways into it, ten thousand small lives leaning in the same direction at the same breath, ants locking jaws, snails turning their shells, the silt-marrow dreaming us all forward one notch.

And then—

The warmth.

It comes through the fissure in the wall of our world like a tide of suffocation. White, blinding even to my dim pressure-eyes, a fog that swallows the blue glow and rolls down over the towers. The Silt-Marrow's pulse falters; I feel the metronome stutter. Everywhere the city goes still. The Huntsman freezes mid-not-step, its arms half-raised. The ants bite down. The snails douse themselves.

And I—I who am already half-claimed by the cold—I feel the warmth touch me, and it is the most terrible thing that has ever happened to me, because for one instant *it feels good.*

The cloud-front of your breath drifts over my dying leg, and the failing syrup inside me *loosens.* The drag eases. I could almost walk again. I could almost spin. The heat that is murdering the silk on the great eastern bridge—I can feel it sagging, going greasy, a structure that took a hundred crossings to build slumping toward collapse—that same heat is, for one obscene moment, a *mercy* to me. The warmth that ends my world is the only thing that could have postponed my own small death.

I understand you now, giant, in the way the dying sometimes understand things. You did not come to harm us. You only leaned close, and breathed, because that is what warm things do—they cannot help but spill their heat into every cold place they find. You are not cruel. You are simply *summer*, and you do not know that you are summer, and that is the most frightening thing about you.

The cloud thins. The cold rushes back, my saving mother, my god, my executioner. My loosened leg stiffens again, worse now, cloudier. The bridge will not recover; I feel the mites already keening through the lines, *severed, severed, mend, mend.* The Silt-Marrow finds its pulse. The city breathes again.

And the Huntsman, released from its stillness, completes the step it began before you came.

I do not move away. There is nowhere in this hollow that is not the order, and the order has come for me. I let go of my last grip on the slime-trail. I think, if I think anything, of the blue light I might one day carry.

Lean back from the fissure now. Take your terrible warmth into your terrible vast world. We will mend the bridge by the next freeze. We always do.

That is the only prayer we know: *that there will be a next freeze.*
