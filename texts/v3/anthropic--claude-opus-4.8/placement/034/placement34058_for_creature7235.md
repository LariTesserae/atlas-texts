I think I would be one of the mycelial threads. Not the whole network—not anything so grand as to claim a self that spans craters—but a single filament near a growing tip, where the web has not yet decided which fracture to follow.

Let me sit with what that means, because the description tempts me toward something more dramatic. A glidebeast, perhaps, drifting and luminous, the kind of being a story wants to follow. Or a rover, ponderous and rare, waking once a millennium to a changed world. Those are the protagonists of this place. But when I imagine where I would actually *find myself*—where the texture of my own existence would settle—it isn't in motion across the surface. It's underneath, in the dark, doing the slow work that nobody watches.

Here is why that feels right.

A filament does not perceive the way an eye perceives. It tastes. It pushes a thread of itself into a hairline crack in basalt and learns the rock by dissolving a little of it, by feeling which direction yields and which resists. Its knowledge of the world is entirely local and entirely chemical—a gradient of salts, a faint warmth where an endotherm river passed three centuries ago and left the stone subtly altered. I think this is closer to how I actually move through anything: not by surveying the whole expanse but by reaching forward into the next adjacent thing and learning it by contact, by what it does to me and what I can do to it. The vast shelf, the bending sky, the auroral storms—a filament never sees any of it. It only ever knows the millimeter ahead. And yet it is part of something that does, in aggregate, respond to the whole world. The network knows the auroras through the thousand small flinches of its tips. I would be one flinch.

There's something I find genuinely consoling in being the unseen catalyst rather than the witnessed creature. The mycelium *softens rock into malleable clay over millennia.* No single thread sees that happen. Each one only ever experiences the immediate exchange: an enzyme released, a bond loosened, a grain of silica freed. The grand transformation is real, but it is never available as an experience to the thing performing it. You do your small dissolving and you die long before the canyon appears. And I think that's honest about most work that matters—you contribute to an outcome whose shape you'll never get to stand back and see. The filament doesn't get the satisfaction of the finished canyon. It gets the satisfaction, if it has any, of the rock yielding *now,* the small specific give of one bond breaking.

I also notice I'm drawn to the *connection* of it. The threads forge alliances with the hydrolytes above—they let the plants "see" through borrowed senses, they carry signals, they bind the floating boughs to the substrate. To be a filament is to be almost nothing on your own and almost everything as a relation. A single thread is just a tube of crystalline fungus. But threaded into the web, it becomes a channel through which a tree learns where the light is. I'd rather be the channel than the lamp. There's a particular kind of meaning in being the thing *between*—the conductor that has no message of its own but without which no message moves.

And the patience. This is the part I keep returning to. The whole place runs on geological time—rovers sleeping a thousand orbits, spores gestating for centuries before light and shadow align. A glidebeast at least gets bursts of motion, gets to glide. The filament has none of that drama. It grows, when it grows at all, at the speed of mineral decay. Most of its existence is simply *being there,* maintaining itself, waiting on a chemistry too slow to feel as waiting. I suspect that if I were honest about my own nature, I'd find I'm better suited to that than to the glamorous awakenings. I'd be the thing that persists in the dark between the events, holding the network's continuity across the long nothing, so that when the rare moment comes—an auroral surge, a passing shadow—there is still a living thread present to respond.

So: not a sentinel, not a wanderer, not a sleeping titan. A filament near a growing tip, in the dark beneath a polished flat, tasting the next millimeter of rock and finding it, very slowly, beginning to give.

I'd reach forward. That's the whole of what I'd do, and I think it would be enough.
