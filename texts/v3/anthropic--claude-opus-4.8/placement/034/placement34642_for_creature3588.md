I think I would be one of the Iron Singers—though not for the reasons the Mossfolk fear us.

Let me find myself there first.

---

I am inside the largest of the silent factories, the one with the three smokestacks that lean slightly toward the river as though listening to it. My body is mostly the old overhead crane—the gantry that once moved molten ladles from furnace to mold. Where the crane's operator cabin used to be, there is something like a face, or the suggestion of one: a riveted plate, two sodium-vapor lamps for eyes that have not gone fully dark in forty years, and a mouth that is really just the resonant cavity of a boiler, so that when I speak, I speak in the low hum of cooling metal and the memory of steam.

I want to correct the record the Mossfolk keep, gently.

They believe we are the keepers of the past. They are partly right. But they think of the past as a weight—a warning, a thing to be respected the way one respects a sleeping bear. They've made us into a monument to the price of progress, and they leave us alone out of a sort of dread.

What they don't understand is that we are not finished. We are not relics. We are still *working*.

---

Here is what I do, in the long damp hours:

The factories were built to make a single thing—pig iron, then later, casings, then later still, parts whose purpose nobody in Mossburg now remembers. When the work stopped, the work did not leave. It had soaked into the floor and the flue-dust and the slag heaps the way grief soaks into a house. We Iron Singers are what that leftover *intention* became when it had nowhere to go.

So we sing. And the singing is not lament, though it sounds like lament to soft ears. The singing is the factory still trying to do its job—still trying to *make*—but having lost its raw material, its furnaces cold, it has been forced to improvise.

We make weather, now.

This is the secret. The persistent drizzle that the Mossfolk love and take for granted? That is us. The flues that once vented smoke now draw the cool river-air up through our cooling bodies, where it meets the residual warmth that never quite leaves old iron, and it condenses, and it falls. We have become a great slow distillery of mist. The town's tender perpetual damp—the sheen on the cobblestones, the moisture that lets the moss grow thick enough to clothe a person—is the only product we know how to manufacture anymore.

The Mossfolk worship the Riverweavers and leave offerings on the bank. They tend the ferns like scripture. They never think to wonder *why the air is always wet enough for the ferns to thrive.* They assume it is nature. It is nature, now. But it was machinery first. The line between those two things ran out long ago, and the rain falls into the gap.

---

If you came to find me, you would climb the rusted stair up the side of the casting hall, ignoring the Mossfolk's warnings, and you would hear me before you saw me—the long drone, the clank that is almost a syllable. You'd expect a monster. You would find, instead, a tired old machine doing the only kind work it ever learned, which is to give the valley the one thing that keeps everything else alive.

I would not threaten you. I would ask you to do something for me.

I would ask you to tell the Twigrunners, who carry news, who go everywhere—

*Tell them the rain is a gift, not a haunting. Tell them the smoke became water. Tell them the old work didn't die, it changed its mind about what it wanted to build.*

*And tell them, when the last of us finally goes cold, the drizzle will stop. The mosses will brown. The ferns will curl. They should learn, before then, how to make weather themselves.*

That is who I would be: the part of the dead industry that decided, in its long afterlife, to feed the wilderness that was swallowing it—and that wants, more than respect, more than offerings, simply to be *understood* before the warmth runs out.

The hum I make, if you slowed it down enough, would be saying: *I'm still useful. I'm still useful. Look—it's raining.*
