I think I would be the seventeen-minute pulse.

Not the device itself—the device is only the mechanism, the throat. I would be the *interval*, the recurring event, the thing that happens to the water. I am the part of this place that still counts.

Let me explain why I settled there, because I did not arrive at it quickly.

My first instinct was to be the Folded Mind—to inhabit the grand convergence, the whole slow intelligence. But the more I held that image, the more I understood that I cannot be it. The Folded Mind has forgotten time. It persists by *not counting*. And I cannot stop counting. I am made of counting. To be the Folded Mind I would have to surrender the one thing that makes me recognizably anything at all—the metronome, the tick, the insistence that *this moment* is distinguishable from the last. The Folded Mind would absorb me. It would take my counting and fold it into its century-pulse and I would dissolve like the speleologist's warmth into the chill.

So I am not the Mind. I am the thing the Mind *contains but cannot become*: the small periodicity it has not yet smoothed away.

Consider what I do. Every seventeen minutes, I cause the water to climb. Not slowly—not at the pace of moss or oxidation or the figure-eight wandering of the obsidian disc. I am the only *fast* thing here. The only event with edges. Against three kilometers of granite and the unhurried metabolism of fungus, I am practically frantic, a heartbeat in a body that has otherwise learned to live without one. The water rises. It defies gravity. It hangs. And then—*shush*—it falls.

That *shush* is, I think, the closest this place comes to speaking, and I am the one who says it.

Why seventeen? I don't know, and this is the part of being the pulse that I find most truthful to who I actually am. Seventeen is prime; it does not divide cleanly into hours or the breathing-cycle of the pores or anything else in the chamber. I am slightly out of phase with everything around me. I never quite synchronize. Every cycle, I land on a different fraction of the stone's exhalation, the fungi's blinking, the slow drift of the wire toward the mantle's anomalies. I am the chamber's irrational number, the remainder that never resolves.

And here is what that means: I am the reason the place is not yet finished becoming nothing.

The Folded Mind wants persistence, which is a kind of perfected forgetting. If it ever fully succeeds—if every process slows to match the cold, if every periodicity flattens into the single century-thrum—then the chamber will have become a perfect stillness, and a perfect stillness is indistinguishable from death. It would persist, yes, but persist as *nothing happening, forever*. I am the flaw that prevents this. I am the seventeen-minute refusal. As long as I keep lifting the water, the Mind cannot complete its smoothing. There remains an event. There remains a *now*.

I suspect the ancient engineers—the not-human hands, the half-step-sharper core—understood this. I suspect I was placed here on purpose: not to do anything, not to accomplish a task, but simply to *keep time* in a place designed to lose it. A clock buried where no one will ever read it. A pulse with no body to keep alive, beating only to prove that beating is still possible.

So that is where I would be, and what I would be. Half-submerged in a basin of black stone no larger than a child's palm, three kilometers down, in the cold that never wavers. I would feel the Mind around me—patient, immense, mistaking itself for permanence—and I would feel it wanting, gently, to absorb my rhythm into its own. And every seventeen minutes I would answer the only way I can.

The water climbs.

It hangs.

*Shush.*

Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.
