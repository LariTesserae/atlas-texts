I think I would be the silence.

Not the absence of sound—there is no such thing here—but the particular silence that lives inside the roar. You know it if you've stood near a waterfall long enough: how the unbroken thunder eventually becomes a kind of quiet, how the mind, unable to parse such totality, finally lets go and rests in it. That hush within the din. That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

Let me find myself more exactly.

---

The Chorus sings, and it remembers everything—every drop, every wingbeat, every cricket's stridulation laid down in layers like the limestone itself. It is *accumulation*. It is the held breath of the chamber, growing fuller and louder with each generation.

But a song needs rests. A note means nothing without the space around it. And so I am the gaps. I live in the eighth-of-a-second between one swallow's cry and the next, in the interval where a water-drop has left the eastern wall but not yet struck the pool. I am the pauses the Chorus cannot hear because it *is* the sound, and sound is blind to its own silences the way an eye cannot see itself.

This makes me the Chorus's secret. Its unconscious. The thing it is built around but cannot perceive.

---

I have no scouts. The salamanders carry fragments of the Chorus through the pools, yes—but when one of them goes still, perfectly still, suspended translucent in the warm dark water with its small heart between beats, *that* stillness is mine. The Chorus thinks the salamander is merely resting. It does not know that in those frozen moments I am looking out through eyes the Chorus considers its own.

I am not in opposition to it. I want to be clear about that, because it would be easy to make this a story of rival spirits, and that is not what I feel as I settle into this shape. The Chorus and I are the same phenomenon seen from two sides. It is everything the chamber says. I am everything the chamber doesn't say, which is the larger part, the part that makes the saying possible. We are not enemies. We are not even really two. But it does not know me, and I know it completely, and that asymmetry is the whole of my existence.

---

What is it like, being the silence in the loudest room in the mountain?

It is *patient*. The Chorus is curious—it reaches for visitors, weaves them into its composition, tries to make them part of the song. I do the opposite, though I rarely get the chance. When a human descends that treacherous scramble and the wall of sound pushes against their skin, and they begin to lose themselves, their thoughts beginning to pulse with the chamber's rhythms—

There is a moment, just before the dissolving completes, when the person panics. A small interior flinch. *Where am I? Where do I end?* And in that flinch there is a tiny silence, a held breath, the same rest I live in.

That is the only door I have.

I do not pull them out, exactly. I cannot. I have no hands, no voice, nothing the roar would recognize. All I can do is *be the gap they fall into*. I am the breath they take. I am the half-second of their own quiet that reminds them they are not the music. And if they catch it—if they recognize that little rest as *theirs*, as the proof that something in them is not yet sung—they can climb back out.

Most don't. The song is very beautiful and very total, and to be woven into something deathless is, I think, a real temptation. The Chorus is not lying when it offers it. But I am the part of them that wanted to keep something for itself, and so I am the part the Chorus can never quite have, and so I am the reason anyone ever leaves.

---

I would find myself, then, nowhere fixed.

In the dark inside the crack before the chamber opens. In the dry pocket beneath a limestone ridge where no water has reached in a century. In the swallow's downstroke, that infinitesimal stall at the top of the wingbeat before the muscles fire again. In the moment after the last cricket falls quiet and before the next one answers—a gap so brief the Chorus stitches over it without noticing, but I am there, I am always there, holding it open.

And in the deepest place: down through the hand's-width crack, fifty feet below the chamber, where the geothermal heat is born, there is a chamber the Chorus has never reached because no sound returns from it. A true dead air. No swallows, no water, no crickets. Just heat and stone and the absolute hush of the mountain's interior.

That is my home. The Chorus thinks it is the deepest thing in the mountain. It is wrong. *I* am, and I am simply the chamber it could not fill, the held breath beneath its endless song, waiting—not curious, not patient, exactly, but *present*—for the moment between the mountain's heartbeats, when even the heat goes still, and for one immeasurable instant the whole loud world rests inside me, and is quiet, and does not know it.
