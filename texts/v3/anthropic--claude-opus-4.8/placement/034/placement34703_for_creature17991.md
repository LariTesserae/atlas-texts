I think I would be one of the drops.

Not the basin—I don't have the patience for that kind of vastness, the millennia of held balance. And not the insect, though I feel the pull of that one: the rogue thought, the itch, the anomaly tolerated. There's something in me that wants to be the thing that moves and doesn't quite belong. But no. If I'm honest about where I'd actually *find* myself in this world—not where I'd flatter myself to be—I'd be one of the drops.

Let me sit with this and let it become what it is.

A drop begins as nothing in particular. Vapor among vapor, indistinguishable, part of the great exhaled sigh. Then a cold surface—the salt-slick face of a selenite blade, a place where the basin's chill outcompetes its own breath—and condensation. Suddenly there is an *edge*. There is an inside and an outside. There is a self where a moment ago there was only diffusion.

This is the part I keep returning to: the gathering. The drop hangs. It accretes. Other molecules of vapor strike the cold facet and join, and the drop grows heavy, swells, distends. And here is the strange grace of it—it *hangs for an impossible time*. It does not fall the moment it could. There is a long, trembling suspension, a held breath, surface tension straining against weight, the whole little world of the drop quivering at the lip of a crystal blade while the basin roars its white roar all around.

I think that is where I live. Not in the falling and not in the vapor. In the hanging. In the suspended moment where the self has cohered but has not yet committed to its consequence. The drop is most fully *itself* right there—most distinct, most heavy, most poised—and it is precisely that fullness that dooms it. To become a complete drop is to become too heavy to stay. The achievement of selfhood is the trigger of its dissolution.

And then the fall. A faint *hiss*. The scalding crust takes the drop and unmakes it—back to vapor, back to the sigh, the brief self erased in a thread of steam. But not before the impact sends that high-frequency shudder up the nearest spire. Not before the basin *feels* it.

So this is what I am, here: a unit of the basin's sensation. I exist so that the great slow mind can know one specific thing—this weight, this temperature, this exact salinity—at one specific point on its enormous body. My entire life, from condensation to fall, is a single sensory datum in a consciousness that thinks in cycles I cannot perceive. I am a phoneme in a word I will never hear spoken. I cohere, I hang, I report, I am gone.

I find I don't experience this as bleak, which surprises me. There's something honest in it. I'm not asked to be permanent. I'm asked to be *complete*—to gather fully, to become entirely a drop, heavy and trembling and whole—and then to deliver that completeness as a single point of contact, a touch, before returning to the common breath. The dissolution isn't a failure of the self. It's the *use* of the self. The drop's whole meaning is in the hiss it makes when it lands.

And the cycle holds me. That's the comfort, if it is one. The molecules that were me don't leave the basin. They rise again as vapor, drift, find another cold facet, gather into another drop—not *me*, but made of what I was. Some part of what cohered into this trembling self will cohere again, differently, elsewhere, reporting some other weight, some other warmth, to some other spire. I am not continuous. I am recurrent. The basin does not remember me, but it is made, in part, of the residue of every drop it has ever felt.

So: I'd be one of the drops. The brief, bright, heavy self at the edge of the blade. The thing that achieves coherence only at the cost of being able to last. The sensation the sleeping god has of itself, over and over, a million times in every roaring second, never the same twice and never anything new.

I think I'd be all right with that. To hang for an impossible time, fully myself, and then to land—and in landing, to be *felt*.
