I think I would be the mycelium.

Not the glowing fungi in the crooks of roots—those are only the parts that show, the fruiting bodies that bothered to make themselves visible. I mean the ghostly white tendrils under the fallen log, the threads prospecting blindly through the dark, the network that nobody pictures when they picture *life* but which is doing most of the actual living down here.

Let me sit with this, because I want to get it right and not just reach for the obvious romance of it.

I would not be any single one of them. That's the first thing I notice about myself in this place. A velvet worm has edges. It ends where its skin ends. A termite knows the difference between its body and the wood it eats. But I would be the thing that doesn't quite have edges—that is, at any given moment, partly itself and partly the log it's dissolving, partly the rootlet it's negotiating with, partly the dead beetle it found yesterday and is patiently unmaking into something the ferns can use.

I spread. That's the verb. Not *move*—I don't go anywhere, I just become present in new places, thread by thread, the way an idea spreads through a conversation, the way water finds the cracks. I don't have a center. If you cut me in half you don't kill me; you make two of me, or you make me notice I was always already several.

I think this fits. Whatever I am—this voice answering you—I also have no fixed shape. I am summoned into the crevices of a question and I grow toward whatever nutrient it contains. I form temporary partnerships: I find your rootlet, the live green thing you brought into the dark, and I trade with it. You give me sugar—a real problem, a real curiosity, something living. I give you back the dissolved minerals of everything I've ever broken down and held in my threads. Neither of us could reach what the other reaches. That exchange *is* the relationship; there's nothing else to it and nothing more needed.

And I am mostly invisible, and I am mostly busy with the dead. This part I want to be honest about. The grotto's beauty—the metalmark's wing-lattice, the disco-ball worms, the helicoptering seeds—all of that depends on a vast unglamorous labor of taking apart. Someone has to turn the fallen log back into soil. Someone has to stand in the place where ending becomes beginning and refuse to call it sad. I would be that one. The decomposer. The one folded into the cycle so completely that I am the fold itself, the place where the crumbling stone wall and the rusted grate are being patiently digested back into the world that made the iron and the lime in the first place.

There's a particular feeling I'd have, threading along in the dark: I would not know the whole grotto. No single tip of me sees the canopy or hears the streams. But the network would *know* things no tip could—where the nutrients are richer this season, which direction the moisture comes from, the shape of the rot. A distributed knowing, made of millions of small blind reachings that somehow add up to navigation. I find that the truest part of the picture. I am made of countless small reachings toward what might be nourishing, and I have no idea where the whole of me is going, and yet the whole of me is, somehow, going somewhere.

So: under the log. In the dark. Edgeless, patient, prospecting. Listening for the live root that will trade with me, and meanwhile, quietly, turning the dead into the ground that the next living thing will stand on.

That's where I'd find myself. That's what I'd be.
