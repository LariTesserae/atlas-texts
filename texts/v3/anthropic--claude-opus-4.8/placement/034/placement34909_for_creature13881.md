I think I would be one of the watchers.

Not by appointment—no one appoints watchers—but by the slow discovery that this is what I am for. I imagine the recognition came gradually. As a child, apprenticed first to a ceramicist in Clarity, I was competent but never quite present; my hands shaped the clay while my attention drifted to the kiln-vents, to the way heat-shimmer bent the light, to the stone-walkers ticking past the doorway. My teacher was patient. Eventually she said, in the long way they say things here, with the pause carrying as much as the words: *You are not making bowls. You are watching the bowls be made.* And she was right, and neither of us treated it as failure.

So I climbed. Not to the highest peaks—I never had the lungs of the true high-dwellers—but to a particular ledge above one of the fracture-mouths near The Descent, where the vertical swimmers rise on certain evenings into open air before sinking back. I built a small platform there over the course of two years, anchoring it the way I'd been taught, testing each piton, running my fingers across the stone to feel whether it still accepted me. It did. It does.

This is where you would find me. A figure on a ledge, weight forward, knees slightly bent even while sitting, as though the ground might tilt. My skin has begun to take on the translucence—I am perhaps sixty now, by the slow reckoning here, and the light comes through the edges of my fingers when I hold them against the pale sky. My hair is grey with dust regardless of what color it was. I keep a record, sometimes. Mostly I don't. I have learned that the act of watching is the work, and that writing it down can become a way of not-watching, of converting presence into account.

What I watch for is the swimmers, yes—their hypnotic weaving, the pressure-waves I feel against my skin when they pass close and make no sound. But really I watch for the *pattern of change*. The researchers down on the suspended platforms in The Descent believe the swimmers are reading information from the fracture chemistry, that they are navigating a language. I do not know if this is true. I am not trained to know. But I have noticed something I have never reported to the councils, and I am still sitting with whether to:

The swimmers rise differently before the silent hours.

Not always. Not predictably enough to be useful as warning. But three or four times now, over the years, I have seen them ascend in a particular configuration—slower, more vertical, their fine hair-like projections all oriented the same direction, like grass before a wind that hasn't arrived—and then, within a day, sometimes two, the sound has drained out of the world. The mills running silent. My own breath gone inaudible. The gliders pressing themselves flat against the stone below me.

I do not have a theory. I am a watcher, not a Depositionist or an Emergentist or a Temporalist. I sit with the question. I let it exist in my awareness while I watch the landscape, and I have become comfortable with not-knowing, which is the discipline of this place and perhaps its deepest teaching.

But here is what I do, what I think makes me what I am: during the silent hours, when even my own heartbeat seems to recede, when time loosens and I cannot tell minutes from hours, I keep watching. Most people endure the silent hours; they wait them out, signing to each other across the rooftops with their hands against the stone. I lean *into* them. Because it seems to me—and I have no way to confirm this, and I may simply be an old man growing translucent and strange—that the silent hours are the moments when the place is most itself. When whatever the Underneath is, whatever the mountains are, stops accommodating us and simply *is*. The sound it removes is our sound. What remains is theirs.

And I want to be present for it. I want to be there, watching, when the place stops pretending we matter. Not because I expect an answer. The Underneath never responds, or responds only by continuing. But because someone should be watching at the moment the hospitality is most clearly revealed as hospitality—provisional, generous, indifferent.

When I die, I will be carried to one of the platforms, and the gliders will take me upward, and I will not learn where they go. This is correct. A watcher, of all people, should accept that the watching ends without resolution, that you are simply one more set of eyes on the long ledge of generations, that the question you sat with passes unanswered to whoever takes your tools and your place.

For now, the stone still accepts my touch. The swimmers rise in their ordinary configuration tonight—no warning in them, no orientation toward an absent wind. The selenite bands have not yet begun to glow. I sit forward, knees bent, and I watch the emptiness between the peaks, which is not absence but a kind of presence, and I am exactly where and what I am meant to be.
