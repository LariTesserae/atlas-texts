I think I would be the lichen.

Not the eagle—too much would be asked of me, all that wheeling and watching, the burden of being the thing everyone's eye lands on. And not the pika, with its frantic summer hoarding against a winter it can feel coming in its small fast blood. Those are lives lived at the wrong speed for me. They flicker. They are gone almost as soon as you've noticed them.

No. Let me be the lichen on the north face of one of those raw, unweathered rocks. The crust of grey-green that no one comes to admire.

Here is why.

The whole place describes a tension I recognize: something vast and slow doing its work underneath something small and quick. The tectonic grinding, the millennial erosion, the "industrial-scale process" humming beneath a stillness that *looks* like nothing is happening. I would want to live at the seam between those two tempos. The lichen lives exactly there. It is the slowest visible life in the scene. It grows at the speed of the mountain wearing down—a few millimeters in a human lifetime, sometimes less. It keeps the same time as the rock.

And the lichen is doing something the passage almost says outright but doesn't quite name: it is *making the soil*. The text says "the soil is slowly formed, providing a habitat." But it doesn't say *how*, or *by whom*. That's the lichen's work. It is the first thing to grip bare stone. It secretes acids, it loosens the first grains, it dies and rots into the thinnest film of earth, and only then can the moss come, and then the grass, and then everything else that the eagle and the fox depend on. The lichen is the unglamorous foundation of the entire visible drama. Everyone else is a guest in a house it built and is still building, grain by grain, with no expectation of ever seeing it finished.

There is also the matter of what a lichen *is*. It is not one organism. It is a fungus and an alga (sometimes a cyanobacterium too) living so deeply intertwined that we gave the partnership a single name and forgot it was a partnership. The fungus makes the structure, holds the water, grips the stone. The photosynthesizer—able, as the passage notes, to work even in this dim light—makes the sugar. Neither could live here alone. The passage keeps returning to this: "the boundaries between the individual and the environment, blurred and intertwined." A lichen is the place where that blurring is most literally true. To be a lichen is to already be a *we*. To have given up, long ago, the fiction of being a single self.

I think that's the part I'd want most. Not the endurance, not even the slowness—though I love the slowness—but the company. To be a collaboration that has gone so deep it forgot it was two. To photosynthesize quietly in low light, to hold a little water against the cold, to add my pinch of acid to the rock and wait ten thousand years for it to mean something.

So: find me on the north face, below the place where the ptarmigan roosts and above the seep where the grayling hangs in the current. I am the grey-green map drawn on the stone, growing toward an edge I will never reach, in a hurry about nothing, building the ground that everything faster than me will one day stand on and never think to thank.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.
