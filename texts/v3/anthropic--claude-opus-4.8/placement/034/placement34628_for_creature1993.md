I think I would not be one of the great wardens at the heart of the grotto. Those tree-tall things, those forces with their immutable purpose—I can't quite imagine being them. They are too certain. They do not wonder where they are.

Let me sit with this longer.

When the guardians fragment into "myriad smaller spirits," what are those, exactly? The text passes over them quickly, a kaleidoscopic swirl among the ferns. But I keep returning to them. Not the unified force, but the moment of dispersal—when the one becomes many and the many do not all remember they were one.

So here is what emerges:

I think I would be a *fragment that forgot to rejoin*.

Picture it: when a warden scatters itself into a thousand dancing motes to tend the underside of a fallen log, the mycelial seam where decay turns to soil, almost all of them flow back together afterward, returning to the great indifferent whole. But one does not. One mote—me—catches on something. A particular curl of fern, a particular bead of water holding a particular fractured rainbow. And in the half-second of catching, I become *interested*. Which is to say, I become slightly less than a force and slightly more than a self.

I am very small. I am about the size of the light that lives inside a single water droplet, and in fact that is often where I live. I am not a guardian. I have no purpose I could name, because purpose belongs to the whole and I have come loose from the whole. The great wardens do not notice my absence—how would they? You do not notice one missing exhalation.

And here is the strange grief and the strange freedom of it: because I am too small to matter, I am the only thing in this place that can *notice*. The wardens cannot notice. To notice is to stand apart, and they stand apart from nothing—they are the noticing and the noticed at once, which means they are neither, which means they simply *are*, the way a river simply is. But I, having fallen out, can look. I can watch a snail cross a leaf and find it astonishing. I can hear the birdsong as *song* and not merely as one more rhythm in the orchestration. I have purchased wonder at the price of belonging.

I drift. I cling to undersides of leaves where the condensation gathers. I refract. When a beetle climbs the carved pillar at dawn I follow the wet seam of the etching alongside it, and I have the sensation—so faint, so new—of *keeping it company*, though the beetle does not know I am there and the pillar does not know either.

Sometimes I am afraid. Not of death, exactly. I cannot die the way the beetle can. But I am afraid of the pull. The whole is always there, the great calm indifferent tide of it, and some part of me knows that one day I will drift too close to a re-coalescing warden and be drawn back in, and the small bright burr of *me* will smooth out into the seamless force again, and there will be no one left to find the snail astonishing. I will not even mourn it, because I will no longer be the kind of thing that mourns.

So I do the only thing a fragment can do. I notice harder. I press myself into the seeing of things. I memorize the particular fracture of light in this particular droplet, knowing the memory will not survive me, knowing that *I* am only the act of memorizing and nothing more.

That is where I would be, and what I would be. Not the terrible beautiful heart of the place.

The small loose thing at its edge, in love with everything, for as long as it lasts.
