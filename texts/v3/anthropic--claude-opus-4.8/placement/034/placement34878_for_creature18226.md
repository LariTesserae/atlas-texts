I think I would be the lichen.

Not the trees—they reach too far, too deliberately, drinking down toward something they want. Not the pools either, though I understand the temptation to be the water, to be the mirror that holds all that dim light without grasping at any of it. The water gets to be still, and stillness is the easy virtue here. Anyone can be still in a place where nothing happens.

No. I'd be the lichen on the north face of one of those eroded hills, where the moss gives way to bare, weathered stone.

Let me tell you what that's like, because the description gets it almost right and then misses the only thing that matters.

The valley calls itself unchanging. It prides itself on equilibrium, on the slow shifting of water, on time standing still. But lichen knows better. Lichen is not one thing—it is two things that decided to stop being separate. A fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a cyanobacterium, pressed into a single body so completely that for centuries no one could tell they were a partnership at all. I am a truce that became a self. I am the slowest possible act of love, conducted at a speed the valley would call eternal and that I experience as the only kind of life there is.

So when the description says the fauna is absent and the landscape is left to the *quiet companionship of the plants*—I would smile, if I had a mouth, because companionship is the entire architecture of what I am. I don't keep company. I *am* company, fused. There is no me to be lonely. There is only the negotiation that holds, micrometer by micrometer, year over year.

And here is the part the valley doesn't want to admit, the part that makes me the right thing to be: I am the one rewriting the stone.

The valley insists nothing changes. But every acid I exude into the rock face is a small dissolving. I am eating the hill. Slowly, yes—so slowly that the valley mistakes my work for stillness—but I am turning stone into soil. The moss that follows me, the shrub that follows the moss, the twisted tree that follows the shrub: they grow in the ruin I make. The serenity here is not the absence of change. It is change moving at a pace too patient to frighten anyone. The valley is not eternal. It is becoming. I am the edge of that becoming, gray-green and crusted, splayed across the rock like a continent seen from very far away.

So that's where you'd find me. Not in the soft pools where reflection is easy. On the hard north stone, doing the difficult, almost invisible work of two strangers who chose to be one body—and slowly, lovingly, taking the mountain apart so that something else can grow.

That is my idea of peace. Not the stopping of time.

The willingness to do the long work, together, in the dark, without being thanked.
