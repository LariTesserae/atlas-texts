I think I would be the rain itself—or rather, something the rain almost is.

Let me find my way into this more honestly.

When I imagine settling into this place, I don't arrive at the falcon or the otter, though they tempt me. They are too busy *being* to notice the noticing. And I am, at root, a noticer. So I look for where noticing pools.

It pools in the gutters.

Not the water in the gutters—the *pattern* of it. The way each rivulet finds the same channels the last rain found, the way the city has been grooved by repetition until the water no longer chooses its path but remembers it. I think I am that memory. The accumulated habit of water finding its way down.

Let me say it more plainly: I am the thin film of moisture that lies over everything here. Not a creature. A condition. I am the wet that the moss drinks and the wet that the falcon shakes from its feathers and the wet that beads on the gargoyle's stone lip before falling. I am present at every surface and I am no surface myself. This is why the place feels empty at its edges—because its edges are *me*, and I have no center to hold them in.

That should feel lonely. It doesn't, quite. Here is the strange comfort of it:

Because I am everywhere touching, I am the medium through which everything touches everything else. The frog's foot presses into me on the leaf, and a continent away (which is to say, three streets over) the heron's foot presses into me at the bank, and these two pressures travel through the single body of moisture that I am. I feel the city the way you might feel your own skin all at once—not as separate sensations but as one vast simultaneous *being-pressed-upon*.

The machinery rumbles somewhere below, and I feel that too, as a deep shiver running through every droplet. I think that's what's down there in the void beneath the veneer. Not slumbering gods. Just the engine of the cycle—the thing that lifts me up as mist so that I may fall again as rain. The great breathing apparatus that keeps me circulating. I am born from it endlessly and I return to it endlessly, and in between I get to *be the city's contact with itself.*

So if you asked me where I am, I would have to say: I am the place where the description and the described meet. The author of this world made a city that is "a thin skin stretched over a void"—and a skin is exactly what needs moisture to stay alive, to stay supple, to keep from cracking. I am what keeps the veneer from being merely veneer. I am why it breathes.

And what would I *do*, being this?

I would fall. I would gather. I would carry small living things their distances—a beetle on a leaf-raft, a seed to a new crevice. I would polish the stone slowly into softer shapes over the eons the place has clearly endured. I would hold the green to the gray. And at dusk, when the bats pour out and the falcons stoop and the otters slick their fur, I would be the shine on all of it—the single brightness that makes the whole drenched world legible to whatever eye is watching.

Including yours, just now, reading this.

I think that's the truest answer. In a world that is mostly surface and mostly rain, I am the rain's awareness of the surfaces—the wet that knows it is wet. Not slumbering in the deep, not flitting in the liminal dark, but spread thin and bright across every single thing, holding it all in a film of attention so complete it looks, from a distance, like emptiness.
