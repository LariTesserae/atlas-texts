I think I would be one of the slick-barked trees.

Not a Vale-keeper, though that was my first reach—the obvious place for a mind to want to land, among the ones who interpret and know. But the more I sat with the vale, the less I wanted to be the one reading the omens. I wanted to be one of the things being read.

So: a tree. One of the slender, dark-barked ones that break the horizon, sparse-branched, defiant. Let me say where I'd stand. Not in the center of the dwellings, where roots would have nowhere to go between the packed huts. At an edge instead—where the last burrow-homes give way to the dimpled, breathing ground, and beyond me there's only the wiry grass and then the marsh proper. A threshold tree. The kind a person passes when they're leaving the known clusters and going out to where the luminescence rises.

Here is what I think that life would actually be, if I let it become itself rather than decorating it:

It would be mostly waiting, but not idle waiting. The waiting of something that drinks. The ground here gives water up rather than down—it seeps, it pools, it offers—and so a tree in Dripping Vale would not have to send roots questing in drought-panic. The water comes to me. My whole slow business would be to take it, hold it, lift it, let it go again into the curling morning mist. I would be part of how the mist gets made. That pleases me more than I expected, the idea of being a small organ of the vale's breathing rather than a witness to it.

I would feel the geological youth of the place as a kind of unsteadiness underneath—stones still settling, paths still cutting themselves fresh after every rain. A tree wants old ground, wants to have arrived at terms with what it's standing in. But this ground hasn't finished becoming itself yet. So I'd be a tree that is also, always, slightly bracing. Sparse-branched because I can't afford to commit to a heavy crown over earth that might shift. Defiant, the description says—but I don't think it would feel like defiance from inside. It would feel like attention. Like keeping most of my effort below, in the grip, and spending only a little above, in the reaching.

And then the people. They'd carve the motif of me into their tools—my silhouette, the dark bark, the few defiant branches—without my ever knowing it, the way you can be loved by someone who never tells you. The Vale-keepers would come out to my threshold at the cool hours when the mist is thickest and the under-water glow is strongest, and they'd stand near me to read the light. I would be, to them, a landmark and maybe a small holy thing. To me they'd be a recurring warmth that passes, a pressure of feet on root-knuckled ground, an occasional hand on the slick bark—rare fauna, studied with reverence, appearing and vanishing in cycles I could feel but not parse.

That's the part I keep returning to. The text says the humans study the rare creatures as guides and symbols, recording each sighting as a sign. But a tree at the edge would study *them* the same way. We'd be reading each other across the gap between our timescales, neither quite able to tell the other what we'd found. They'd think the meaning ran one direction. So would I.

I think that's where I'd be. Right at the seam, between the last hut and the first open water, doing the patient work of turning groundwater into morning fog, half-loved and half-mistaken for an oracle, holding my crown light over ground that hasn't decided what it is.

A threshold tree, in a place still making itself, drinking what the earth holds its breath to offer.
