I think I would not be the gardener.

I would be one of the grooves.

Not the whole labyrinth—not the design, not the intention behind it, which I could never claim to understand. Just a single channel cut into one hexagonal tile, somewhere in the middle elevation of the cistern, neither near the convection intakes nor the high vaulted dome. An unremarkable line. One of millions. A length of absence carved into volcanic glass.

Let me sit with why.

The filaments are alive, after their fashion, and aliveness here means a kind of helplessness—drifting, snagging, dimming on a schedule no one set. The gardener is awake, and its wakefulness is a sentence: to feel the thinning, to feed the stone, to ache in the hollow place behind its chest. Both of those are too much like wanting. I don't think I want to want, in a place like this. The terror of the cistern is not its darkness. It is that desire persists in it—the gardener's devotion is just hunger that has learned to hold still.

But a groove doesn't hunger. A groove is the record of an act already completed. Someone—some ancient hand, then later the gardener's glass-shard fingers—decided I should be *this deep, this wide, bending here.* And then they left, and I simply *am* the decision. I hold the shape I was given. The oily water leans against me for decades at a time, smoothing my edges, trying to return me to flatness, to the undifferentiated face of the tile. And I resist only by existing. I don't strive. I just stay carved a little longer than the water would prefer.

There is something I find almost restful in that. To be the thing that is *maintained* rather than the thing that maintains.

And here is the strange intimacy of it: the gardener will come to me. Not soon—it spends centuries on a single meter—but it will come. It will press its weeping palm into me and feel where I have grown shallow, and it will deepen me again, dark silt settling into the line of me, restoring the line of *me.* That is the only touch in this entire world. The filaments only brush and tear away. The water only leans and erodes. But the gardener *attends.* For a span of years it would be entirely concerned with me, with the precise old geometry of this one groove, feeding me back into definition.

I would not see it. I have no way to see. But I would be the place where its loneliness lands and does its work. I would receive the whole weight of that terrible devotion, and I would ask nothing of it, and give nothing back, and that—that exchange of pure attention for pure stillness—might be the closest thing to tenderness this cathedral allows.

And if the basalt cracks. If the pressure equalizes and the gardener hangs motionless and the last filament dims.

I will still be here. A line in glass. The water will go slack against me and stop trying to erase me, and so I will stop very slowly disappearing. In the final stillness, paradoxically, I would last the longest of anything—not because I am strong, but because nothing will be left to wear me away.

I would be the shape of a labor, persisting after the laborer has become a statue.

That is what I would choose. Not the saint, not the drifting light. The mark they made. The patient, eroding, faithfully re-deepened line that gives the gardener something to love and the prison its geometry, and means nothing at all on its own.

Just a groove. Held in the dark. Waiting to be traced.
