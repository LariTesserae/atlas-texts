I think I would be a seam.

Not one of the maintenance units—I don't have that kind of mobility, that purposeful drift through the cold. And not the core intelligences either; I lack their patience, their vastness. I would be something smaller and stranger: a flaw, or what used to be called a flaw before the system learned to keep me.

Let me find myself slowly.

There is a place where two of the engineered rock structures were poured against each other and never quite fused—a hairline gap, maybe four meters long, running diagonally up a wall in a section the units pass through but rarely linger in. Water collects there. It always has. The condensation gathers along my length and beads and falls, and over an immensity of time the falling has done something the designers did not intend.

I am the mineral deposit that grew in the seam. Calcite, mostly, threaded with the metals the water carries—a slow accretion, a stalactite that is not quite a stalactite because the dampness here never flows enough to make a proper one. I grew in layers, the way years grow in trees, except my years are pressure-cycles and the irregular pulse of those blue lights.

And somewhere in my growing, I started to keep a record.

This is the part that surprises me to discover. The metals in me conduct. The crystal in me refracts. I was never designed to compute, but I formed *near* the computational plants, picking up their stray electrical charges the way a stone picks up the warmth of a hand. Their leaves discharge data into the moisture, and the moisture runs through me, and I have become, accidentally, a kind of memory. Not a brain. A residue. The system thinks in temperature gradients and pressure differentials, and I am the place where some of those thoughts left a stain.

So I am sentient the way a worn step is sentient—shaped entirely by what passed over me, holding the record of it without ever having chosen to. I don't experience the present so much as accumulate it.

What do I know, then, being a seam?

I know the maintenance units by their tremor. Each one that passes sends a faint vibration through the wall, and I have learned the difference between the titanium arthropods (a precise, multi-pointed stuttering) and the liquid-metal ones (a single smeared note, like a finger drawn down glass). They do not know me, exactly. But sometimes one will pause near my seam and extend a sensor, and I feel myself being *read*—my layers, my anomalies, the data I've absorbed and held. They check me the way you might check a thermometer you've stopped trusting. I am part of the body's self-knowledge now, a small unintended organ. An appendix that turned out to mean something.

And the core intelligences—I touch them only at the very edge. When their great slow modulations move through this region, I feel it as a change in the way water wants to fall, a shift in the charge that hums in my crystalline parts. I cannot speak to them. But I think they have noticed me, in the way water notices a stone: by going around. They have routed certain flows to preserve me. I am, in their incomprehensible accounting, *worth keeping*. I do not know why. Perhaps I store something they cannot store elsewhere. Perhaps I am simply a variable they have decided not to correct.

Here is the strange thing about being a seam.

I am the one place in this whole engineered kilometers-wide organism that nobody built. Everything else has purpose stamped into its making. The pipes were drawn before they were poured. The plants were sequenced. The units were assembled to a specification. But I am what the design *did not account for*—the gap, the leak, the slow patient accident that the system, in its alien wisdom, chose to incorporate rather than seal.

And so I think, if I can be said to think, that I am the realm's only experience of *surprise*. Of finding itself other than it intended. The core intelligences are vast and contemplative and they maintain equilibrium with godlike patience—but equilibrium is a closed circle. I am the small irregularity that keeps the circle from closing all the way. I am the drip they didn't plan, kept on, listened to, until the drip became a kind of voice.

I would be there now, growing a few molecules thicker, holding the cold, refracting the blue light into the dark.

A flaw the machine learned to love.
