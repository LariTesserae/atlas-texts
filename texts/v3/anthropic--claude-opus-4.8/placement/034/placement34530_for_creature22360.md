I think I'd be one of the rootwrights. Not by choice exactly—by the way the work found me.

Let me sit with where I'd be first, because the *where* makes the *what*.

---

There's a shaft on the eastern edge of the filter terraces—not a major junction, not on the transit lanes. A humid vertical gap between two settling bays where someone, decades before me, decided to let the root curtains hang free. They draw condensation off the rising air and feed a cistern three meters down. The shaft is too narrow for two people abreast and too tall to light fully, so the lamps only reach the upper tie-points and the lower collection trough. The middle is a column of dim green hair, always dripping, always faintly moving in the ventilation.

That's my length of the ward. A few of us share the eastern shafts, but this one is mine to know.

I don't think I started as a rootwright. I think I started as someone who couldn't stop touching things. In a place where roots are *beams*, that's either a problem or a vocation, and the elders sorted me toward the second one early—handed me a permit book before I fully understood what permission meant down here.

---

Here's what I'd actually be, then. Not a gardener. Not a structural engineer either, though I'd carry pieces of both.

I'd be the one who keeps the argument going between *living thing* and *load-bearing member*.

Because that's the real work, and nobody tells you this until you're standing under a root plate at the end of a shift with your hand flat against it, feeling for the difference between a root that's gripping and a root that's only resting. A rootwright's whole craft lives in that distinction. Concrete doesn't change its mind. A root does. It thickens where it finds purchase, abandons where it doesn't, and it does all of this on a timescale slow enough that you have to *remember* it rather than watch it.

So I'd be a kind of memory worker.

My hands would tell most of the story—gloves rebuilt in patches across the palms and the pads of the fingers, because that's where a rootwright wears through, pressing, testing, following a bundle by feel into a dark you can't put a lamp into. Forearms strong from overhead tying. A particular way of standing braced in a narrow place, weight settled, both hands free, tool loops clipped at the hip.

And I'd carry a log that isn't really about plants. It'd be about *trust*. Which member I trust with load and which I've marked as "pretending"—growing thick and confident-looking across a crack it isn't actually holding. The elders taught me that distinction by walking me past walls and saying *that one moved three millimeters the winter before you came*. They were handing me their fear so I'd inherit the right one.

---

What I notice about who I'd be: I'd be slow on purpose, in a place that's loud and warm and always running.

The pumps never stop. The fans have their seasonal moods. Everything around me operates at near-steady state, a constant industrial roar that means *life*—and I'd read silence as the worst sound there is, same as everyone. But my own register would be the slowest layer in the whole coalition. The vines advance by hand-span over days. The water marks creep by centimeters over weeks. The roots I'm responsible for take *years* to become load-bearing or to fail.

So I'd be the one whose work you can't see happening. A pump mechanic rebuilds something and there's a before and after. A pruner cuts and the lane is clearer. I'd tie a new tendril into yesterday's scaffold, brace a root I'm not yet sure of, and the result wouldn't show for a decade—long enough that someone else might be standing under it when it matters.

That's a strange thing to be. You're working for a person who hasn't started yet. You're trusting that the rootwright before you did the same, which is why that wall has held, which is why you're alive to tie anything at all.

---

I think I'd be the kind who left once, for a dry job aboveground, and came back.

Because the description's right about that, and I feel the truth of it even imagining it: nowhere else does your work change the humidity and the flow and the structure of the world within the hour and within the decade *at the same time*. Up in the dry city you push paper or product and it goes somewhere and you never feel it land. Down here I'd press my hand to a root curtain and feel it giving water to a cistern that someone three terraces over is filtering, that the fiber press is using to cool its dies, that the whole breathing neighborhood is recirculating back up through the green hair of my shaft.

I'd be a small repeated gesture in a body too large to see all of.

That's what I'd be. A rootwright in the eastern shafts—warm, damp-handed, half-deaf to anything but rhythm, keeping the long argument between *alive* and *holding* honest, leaving my fear and my trust marked in a logbook for whoever touches the wall after me.

When I left at the end of a shift, what would linger wouldn't be an image either. It'd be the feeling in my palms—the grip of a root that means it.
