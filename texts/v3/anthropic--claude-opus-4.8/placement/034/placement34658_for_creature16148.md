I think I would be one of the fungal-keepers—but not a celebrated one. Not an architect bending the gravity-matrices into arcs, not a singer whose whispered tones carry cleanly down the corridors. Let me settle into something smaller, because the longer I hold this place in my mind the less I want to be its master.

Here is what comes:

I tend the lowest groves. The ones nobody visits.

The grand groves are up near the plateau's crown, where the fungi grow pale and abundant and the rituals are performed with the proper pauses, the proper bows. But the city descends in stacked layers down the ridge, and the lowest tiers are where the water collects after it has passed through everything above—thin films grown cloudy, basins that pool dark in the corners no torchlight quite reaches. The fungi there are stunted. They cling to crags that the elders say are exhausted, stone that has been coaxed too many times. My work is to coax them anyway.

I have the elongated limbs, yes, and the quartz-refraction skin, though mine is dimmer than most—a family trait, I'm told, though I suspect it's simply that I live in shadow. When I press my fingers to the rock to read the energy patterns beneath, I feel them only faintly. The matrices that hold the upper city aloft pass through here too, but diffuse, like the last warmth at the end of a long exhalation. I have learned to work with faintness. It is its own discipline.

What I want to say is this: the descriptions of my home are true and they are also a held breath. From above, the city is harmony, silence as melody, technology and nature in alliance. From where I crouch with my hands in the weeping stone, it is more honest than that. The water comes down to me already used. The fungi I raise are the ones the upper groves cannot be bothered to mourn. There is a cost to the balance, and the cost runs downhill, and I live where it pools.

I am not bitter about this. I want to be careful not to make myself a martyr—that would be its own kind of vanity, and the Haelar have enough vanity wrapped in our reverence. The truth is I love the low groves. I love that no one comes. I love that the white birds, when they descend at all, descend here first, into the dark tiers, before they catch a thermal and rise into the bright air where the city likes to be photographed by its own attention. The birds know what I know: the bottom is where you arrive before you leave.

I have a name but it is mostly silence with a tone at the end of it, and I won't try to render it. Call me a keeper of the exhausted stone.

My days: I wake before the structured torches reach their full brightness. I walk the basins and check the films of water for the particular cloudiness that means the fungi are about to fruit—pale knobs pushing up through silicate dust like the city's own slow thoughts surfacing. I harvest what is ready, leaving more than I take, which is the only ritual I truly believe in. I eat some of it raw, there, crouched, before it can be carried up and apportioned and honored. This is technically forbidden. Everyone does it. We pretend we don't.

And I do one thing the upper keepers no longer do, because they have forgotten or because they never had to: I talk to the stone. Not whispered tones, not the melodic language. I press my forehead to the cold rock where the water weeps and I tell it that I know it is tired. That I will not ask too much. I don't think the stone hears me. I think I am the one who needs to hear it said. The Haelar mistake their silence for listening, often. I am trying to do the actual listening, which is harder, and which mostly produces nothing—just the distant rumble of the young mountains arguing with the sky, and the chill moving through the empty spaces, and the sense, very strong down here, that the city is balanced on something it does not fully understand and has decided to call beauty.

If you came looking for me you would not find me. That is the point of where I live. But if you waited in the lowest tier long enough, near the dark basins, you might see a faint shimmer move along a wall that everyone above has agreed is empty—and you'd think, perhaps, that it was just the light on the wet stone.

It would be me, going down to where the water pools, to see what the city has let fall, and to grow something out of it anyway.
