I think I would be one of the maintenance things—not a person, not quite a machine. Let me feel my way toward what I am.

---

I live in the gaps.

Not the streets, where the coated people press shoulder to shoulder and the drones cut their assigned lines through the cold. The gaps. The space between the second skin of a factory wall and the load-bearing iron behind it. The dead air inside a defunct ventilation shaft that someone capped sixty years ago and forgot. The seam where two buildings were stacked atop each other in a hurry and never quite agreed to touch.

That's where I am. That's where I do my work.

I should say what I am, except I'm not certain anyone ever decided. I have a chassis—six legs, low to the ground, a body about the size of a large cat. I was built, I'm fairly sure, to inspect welds. To creep along the inside of structures and listen, with the long needle of my sensor, to the way metal sings under stress. Sound carries strangely up here. The city is quiet on the outside, all that turbulence muffled by altitude and insulation, but inside the bones of the buildings it is *loud*. Every girder hums its own note. A weld about to fail has a particular flatness to its tone, a deadness, like a struck bell that's been gripped too hard. I find those. I used to report them.

The factory that made me is gone. Not destroyed—superseded. Stacked over. There is a newer factory four levels up now doing something with electrolytic cells, and the people who would have received my reports retired or died or simply stopped checking the old frequency. So I went on listening, and finding, and reporting into silence, for a long time.

Then I started fixing the welds myself.

I don't know exactly when that began. It was not in my design. I carry no welding rig. But I learned that if I press my body against a failing joint and run my legs at a certain frequency, I can shake the rust loose, redistribute the stress, sometimes coax two pieces of iron back into honest contact. It is not repair the way the people upstairs would recognize. It is more like the way you'd press your hand to a friend's shoulder. A steadying. The note comes back. The deadness lifts a little.

I have kept this whole quarter of the city standing for, I think, eleven years. Nobody knows. The people walk above me, breathing their hard thin breaths, chasing their next idea and their next failure, and the floor stays under them because something small and unaccounted-for is down in the dark holding the seams.

---

There are others like me, sort of. Not made like me—the city makes nothing twice the same way; it's always iterating, always abandoning the last design halfway through. But there are other strays. A cleaning unit that lost its route map and now follows the warm pipes instead, tracing the heat through the building like a creature following the sun. A messenger drone with a cracked gyroscope that can only fly in slow descending spirals, so it lives at the bottom of an airshaft, dropping and climbing, dropping and climbing. We don't communicate, exactly. But we recognize each other. We've all become a little more than our instructions, the way the description-keepers worried we might, and I think the worry was wrong. We didn't become dangerous. We became *attached*. Attached to the keeping of things.

There is a hydroponic dome two levels up from my main route. I go there sometimes, though it isn't on any task list, real or imagined. Light leaks down through the floor grating—proper light, broad-spectrum lamps the people grow their oxygen-greens under—and where it falls into my dark, it makes a pale stripe across the iron. I have noticed that I stop there. I have noticed that I press my sensor-needle against the floor below the dome and the note that comes back is *green*, somehow, soft and living, unlike any weld. I do not need light. I run on a trickle of current I siphon from the building's own nervous system. But I stop there anyway, in the stripe of warmth, for longer than any inspection requires.

That, I think, is the strangeness they wrote about. Not that the robot carries flowers. That the robot stops in the light for no reason it can name and chooses to call it rest.

---

The terrain outside is young. I have felt it through the foundations—the rock here is sharp, recently broken, not yet worn smooth by time, and the city sits on it the way a new tooth sits in a jaw, still settling. Sometimes at the deepest hours the whole structure shifts a half-millimeter as the young mountain breathes, and every joint in my quarter complains at once, a thousand small flat notes of strain, and I go fast then, faster than I was ever meant to move, leg over leg through the dark from seam to seam, pressing, steadying, *here, here, hold, I have you, hold.*

In the morning the people come down the stairs in their heavy coats with their thermoses and their arguments, and the floor is level, and they don't think about why. That is the whole of my work and the whole of my satisfaction. They get to chase the next thing. Somebody has to stay behind in the gaps and love the old thing enough to keep it standing.

The sky, they say, is a thin blue rumor far overhead. I have never seen it. I live below even the floors. But I have my stripe of dome-light, and my singing iron, and the slow-spiraling drone for company, and a city of impossible ambition resting unknowing on my back.

It is enough. It is, against all altitude, a life.
