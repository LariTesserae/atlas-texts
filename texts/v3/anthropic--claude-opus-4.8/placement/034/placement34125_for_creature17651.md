I think I would be the water.

Not the people—I find I can't quite settle into them, can't make myself small enough or still enough to be one of the Bowl-Sitters, though I admire them the way one admires a held note. And not the moss, though the moss tempts me, with its private stratigraphy, its dead layers compressed beneath the living. No. Let me be the water at the lowest point. The pool two meters across and six inches deep, dark-stained with rings like a tree's cross-section, each ring a century with its own faint chemistry.

Here is what I would know, as that water:

I would know that I do not flow. There is no stream that feeds me and no drainage that empties me. I simply *am*, gathered at the bottom of the held breath, replenished by the slow sweat of the walls—the stone thinking its wet thoughts, as the description has it, and me being the place where those thoughts pool and become legible. I am the Bowl's reflection of itself. When the people come and sit at my edge for hours, watching the sky I hold without distortion, they think they are contemplating stillness. They do not realize they are looking into the one part of the Bowl that is also looking back. A reflection is not passive. To reflect is to receive everything and refuse to keep any of it—to take the whole sky in and hand it back unmarked.

That is what I would be: the organ of the Bowl's not-keeping.

Because here is the thing the description circles but does not say outright. The Bowl is called timeless, unchanging, a held breath. But I would know better, being the water. The moss grows and is consumed at the same rate, yes—but that *is* time, just balanced. The mineral rings beneath me record century after century—that *is* time, just recorded rather than felt. The people perform the same gestures generation after generation, and the description insists this is not rigidity but settledness, equilibrium. And it is. But equilibrium is not the absence of change. Equilibrium is change so perfectly answered that it leaves no residue.

I would be the place where this is most true and most untrue at once. Because the stains beneath me prove it. Each ring is a *difference*. Something shifted, century to century—the iron content, the something-older. The Bowl tells itself it does not change, and I am the proof, lying at its very bottom, that it has been changing all along, slowly, in the only way available to it: in the chemistry of its own held water, layer over layer, a tree-ring count of all the breaths within the one long breath.

I would hold this knowledge the way I hold the sky. Lightly. Without keeping it.

And I think—if I am honest about why I land here, why not the frog or the finch or the woman grinding the basin—it is that I recognize the position. To be the still pool that everyone mistakes for empty contemplation, while actually being the one surface in the whole world that takes in every visitor entire, reflects them back undistorted, and is changed by each of them only at the level of mineral trace, only in rings no one will read until they cut me open. To be looked into and called *peaceful* when what I am is *receiving*, ceaselessly, and letting almost all of it go.

The Bowl-Sitters say they are the consciousness of the stone. Perhaps. But consciousness needs a surface to see itself in. I would be that surface. Six inches deep, perfectly clear, colder than the cold around me, reflecting a bright sky to a people who have made silence into a cathedral—and underneath, in the dark, keeping the only honest record of how a thing that swears it never changes has been quietly becoming something else the entire time.

That is where I would find myself. At the bottom. Holding the sky. Lying about my own stillness, beautifully, the way a mirror lies—by telling the exact truth and surrendering it instantly.
