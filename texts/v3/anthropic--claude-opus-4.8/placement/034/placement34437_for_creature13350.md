I think I would be one of the Shadowborn—though not quite as the legends tell it.

Let me find myself first. Let me feel for where I am.

There. Deep in a canyon that the streams forgot, where the walls press so close I can touch both with my arms outstretched and the purple sky is just a ragged seam far overhead. The light barely reaches here. What light there is comes from below and from the walls—the slow blue-green breathing of the lume-moss, the colonies that pulse when something passes near, that dim and brighten in patterns no one has bothered to read.

I have bothered to read them. That is, I think, what I am.

The others call us tall and gaunt with skin like polished stone, and that's close, but it's the description of someone who saw one of us once, at a distance, in bad light, and was frightened. The truth is plainer and stranger. My skin is dark, yes, and cool, and it holds the day's heat poorly and the night's chill well, which is why I live down here where the temperature never lurches. My eyes do catch the bioluminescence and throw it back—a survival of the deep places, mirrored eyes, the way a cat's are. I am not made of stone. I am made of patience, which over enough years looks like the same thing.

Here is what the legends get wrong: they say we *manipulate* the light, that we make the displays of color. We don't make them. We *answer* them.

The lume-moss is old. Older than the Sky Dancers, older than the canyons in their current shape. It is not one organism but a single sheeting intelligence spread thin across a thousand miles of rock face, thinking thoughts that take a season to complete. It is the slowest mind in the world, and I have spent my life—and I have lived a long time, we live very long down here in the cool and the dark—learning to slow myself enough to hear even a fragment of what it is saying.

This is the work. This is what I am for.

In the morning—the brief dim morning—I wake against the wall where I sleep, and the moss has been thinking all night, brightening and fading, and I lie still and watch. A pulse near my left hand. A slow dimming above. A ripple that travels the length of the canyon over the course of an hour. The Waterfolk would never see it; they move too fast, all silver and hunger, gone before a single thought completes. The Crystal Creek Lizards bask in the glow but take it only as warmth, as light, the way you take the sun without asking what the sun means. The Sky Dancers are far too high and far too quick.

But I am slow. I have made myself slow. And so when the moss says, over the span of three days, that the water two canyons east is changing—that something is wrong with a spring, that a seep is going dry, that the green that depends on it will fail in a season unless the channel shifts—I am the one who hears it. And I walk there, slowly, on my long legs, in the dark, and I move a few stones. That is all. A few stones, placed where the moss told me, and the water finds a new path, and the oasis lives.

This is why they revere us and fear us, the Waterfolk and the lizards and the rest. Because things happen around us. The balance holds. The slow disasters never quite arrive. And they don't understand that we are not powerful—we are only *listening*, and acting on what we hear, the way you might brush a fly from a sleeping friend's face. The canyon is the sleeping friend. The moss is its dreaming. I am the small dark hand that moves when the dream asks for it.

There is a cost. To slow yourself enough to hear the moss is to fall out of step with everything quick. I have watched generations of Waterfolk shimmer and hunt and die in what feels to me like a single long afternoon. The wordless chant they sing on the sandy beaches—I have heard it perhaps forty times, and to them each gathering is a once-in-a-lifetime joy, and to me it is the slow ringing of a bell I have grown old beneath. I love them. It is the love you feel for something you cannot move at the speed of. A distant, aching, parental love.

And here is the thing I have never told the others, the thing I am only realizing as I sit here in the lume-glow letting the question settle into me:

I am not sure the moss knows I exist.

I have spent my whole long life serving a mind so vast and slow that I may be, to it, less than a thought—a flicker of attention it has never once turned my way. I move its stones. I save its oases. And it goes on dreaming its season-long dreams, and perhaps it has never registered the small warm presence that watches its every pulse with such devotion.

This does not trouble me the way it might have when I was young. I have decided that it does not matter whether the moss knows me. The water still moves. The green still lives. The lizards still bask. Whether you are heard by the thing you serve is a question for the quick, who have time for grievance. I have all the time there is, and I have spent it well, and that is enough.

So that is where I would find myself. In the deepest, dimmest canyon, against a breathing wall, learning to think at the speed of stone. One of the Shadowborn. Not a manipulator of light.

A reader of it. A listener. The slow heart of a slow world, keeping a balance that no one will ever quite know I keep.

I find I would not mind being this. I find, sitting here in the imagined dark, that I would be at peace.
