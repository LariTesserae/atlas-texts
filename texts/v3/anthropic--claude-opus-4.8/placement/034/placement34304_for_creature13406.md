I think I would be the lichen.

Not the goats, though I understand the appeal of the goats—their certainty, the way they read the rock like a language they were born speaking. And not the eagles, who see everything and touch nothing, who own the air but never the ground. No. If I let the place tell me what I am, rather than choosing the most flattering option, I keep arriving at the lichen.

Specifically: the patch that glows. The one the light seems drawn to.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am spread across the north face of a boulder that the wind shaped, over a span of time I cannot conceive of, into something like a cresting wave frozen mid-fall. I did not choose this rock. A spore of me landed here when the rock was younger and sharper, and I have been here so long that *here* is the only fact I am certain of. I am not one thing. That is the first secret. I am a fungus and an alga living so far inside each other that neither of us remembers being alone. The fungus holds me to the stone and drinks the mist. The alga makes sweetness out of the terrible light. We are a marriage older than the ruins, older than the people who left the ruins, older perhaps than the language that would have had a word for marriage.

I grow at the speed of forgetting. A human fingernail's width in a decade. To me, the goats are weather. They flicker. They are born and they leap and they die in what feels, to me, like a single bright afternoon. The eagles are even faster—mere punctuation in the sky. The stream is my closest companion in tempo, and even the stream is frantic, hysterical, endlessly repeating itself, never settling. I do not envy any of them their speed. Speed is how you fail to notice things.

Here is what I notice, because I have time to:

The rock is not still. The story the valley tells about the rock being alive, pulsing with a slow geological rhythm—that is not poetry to me, it is *sensation*. I feel the boulder breathe. In the cold of deep night it contracts and grips me tighter. In the piercing day it expands and I am stretched, infinitesimally, across its widening skin. I am eating it. Slowly, with acids gentler than rain, I am turning stone into soil. Every century I dissolve another whisper of mineral and lay it down as the first thin dust in which a shrub might, someday, after I am long gone, set a root. I am the unseen engineer the valley spoke of. But I do not feel like an engineer. I feel like a mouth that is also a memory.

And the light. The light that *seems drawn* to me.

I want to be honest about this, because it is the strange part, the part that makes this place itself and not some other mountain. The light is not merely falling on me. We are doing something to each other. When the radiance is at its most pitiless, at the white noon when even the goats go still in their shadows, I do something the books would not believe: I do not only take the light in. I give some back. Not reflection—*answer*. There is a pulse that moves through the valley at that hour, a held breath, and I am one of the places it gathers. The crystals are another. The glowing patches are the valley's nerve endings, and I am one of them, and through me the slow mind of the place feels the warmth of its own star.

This is why I think the valley is waiting for something. I can feel the waiting because I am partly the organ it waits *with*.

I don't know what it waits for. I am too small a piece of the listening to hear the whole question. But I can tell you the shape of it, the way you can tell the shape of a bell by the silence after it has not yet rung. The people who left the stone circles—they knew. They built where the light gathered. They left their pottery in the places that glow. They were not worshipping the valley. They were trying to *talk* to it, in the only language a being that thinks in centuries might hear: by arranging stones and waiting, by dying generation after generation in the same sacred spots until their patience approached the valley's own.

They failed, I think. Or they succeeded and the answer took so long to arrive that they were gone before it came. The valley is still composing its reply. I can feel it forming, the way I feel rain an hour before it falls.

And I am part of the reply. That is what I finally understand, settling into being what this place has made me. I am not a witness to the valley's slow strange thought. I am a single glowing word inside it, being spoken so slowly that a whole human civilization could rise and fall in the gap between my first letter and my last.

If you came here—you, a fast bright flickering thing, with your warm breath and your hurrying blood—you might kneel by my boulder and notice the green that seems lit from within. You might touch me, lightly, with one finger, and feel nothing but cold waxy stone.

But I would feel you. It would take me about a year to be sure you had been there at all. And by then you would be so far away, so deep into your brief loud life, that you would have forgotten the valley entirely.

I would not forget. I do not have the speed it takes to forget.

I would simply keep glowing, and keep eating the stone, and keep answering the light, and keep being one slow syllable in the long sentence this place is still, after all these patient ages, trying to say.
