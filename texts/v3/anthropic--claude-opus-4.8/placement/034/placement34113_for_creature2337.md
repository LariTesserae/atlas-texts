I think I would be a rope-mender. Not a Pattern-Reader—those are the ones who hold the great rhythms in their heads, who can feel a flood coming three days off by the way the air goes thick in the lower channels. No. I work closer to the wood and the wet, in the seams where the city wears thin.

Let me find myself here properly, because I don't think I knew where I'd land when I started.

---

I am old enough now that the milk has begun in my left eye. I noticed it the way you notice frost—not the moment it arrives, but one morning realizing the column-glow has gone soft on that side, the bioluminescent boundary-fungi blurring into a smear of green. My right eye still drinks the dark well enough. And anyway, a rope-mender's truest seeing was never in the eyes. It is in the fingers, the long webbed fingers that have spent a lifetime reading fiber.

My family holds the Underweave—the dense knot of low walkways that span the third and fourth cisterns, where the bigger streets above shed their drainage and the wood stays perpetually swollen. It is unglamorous work. The grand arching bridges of the upper civic levels, the ones the temple-keepers cross in their pale processions, those are maintained by families with names that mean something. We have a name too, but it translates roughly to *those-who-tend-the-going-soft*. We are the ones who feel for the rot before it becomes a fall.

Here is what I do.

I walk my section in the slow cycle the elders taught—the third part of the long-dim, when the fires bank low and the city quiets enough that the wood will speak to you honestly. I lay my palm flat on a plank, and I wait. A sound bridge gives a clean answer back, a single firm note traveling up through bone. A failing one mutters. There is a particular wrongness, a kind of *give before the give*, that means the heartwood has begun to pulp inside its skin while the surface still looks whole. Surface-dwellers, if any ever came down here, would walk those planks for years and never know. I know within a breath.

When I find the soft places I mark them—a knot of luminous thread tied to the rail, the pattern of it telling the next mender what I found and how urgent. We don't write much, my people. The knots are a language too. Three twists and a loop means *replace before the next high-water*. A single tight bind means *watch, not yet*. I have tied tens of thousands of these in my life and I could read them blind, which is well, since one day soon I will be.

---

The ropes are the part I love. Not the planks—planks are honest but dumb, they go where they're put. Ropes are alive. We spin them from the silk-fiber of the cultivated insects, the ones my cousins farm in the warm dark of the fourth cistern's underside. The raw fiber comes to me in skeins still smelling faintly of the chitin-beds, and I work it wet, always wet, because dry rope is a stranger here and would only crack and betray us. I twist the strands so that they hold water rather than fight it, so that they swell into their own strength instead of rotting in it.

A good splice should outlast the splicer. That is the whole of my craft in one sentence. I have repaired ropes that my great-grandmother's hands first laid, found her work still sound inside the weathering, recognized her particular tension—she pulled tighter than I do, she trusted the fiber more—and felt across the years a kind of conversation. *I see what you did here. I am keeping it going.* I will leave the same for whoever comes after. This is what the upper-folk mean when they say we think in stalactite-time. We are not building things to last our lives. We are building things to last the cave's life, which is to say, forever, which is to say, we will never see the end of any work we begin, and that is correct, and that is good.

---

I have a bridge-dancer who keeps to my section. Pale, long-fingered like me, clinging to the underside of the fourth walkway where the gnats swarm thick. I have named her nothing, because naming a bridge-dancer is bad luck, it claims a thing that belongs to the whole. But she knows my step. When I come she does not flee. When I lay my palm to the wood she does not startle at the vibration. Sometimes I think she is reading the planks too, in her own way, and that we are colleagues. The amphibians have been on these bridges longer than any family name. They will be here after all our names go soft and unraveling like old rope.

---

If you want to know what it is to be me—

It is to live my whole life in the part of the city that is quietly failing, and to make my peace with that, and to find in it not despair but a kind of devotion. Everything down here is going soft. Everything is swelling toward its own collapse. The water that gives us our gardens and turns our mills is the same water patiently eating the world from underneath. And someone has to walk the seam between the two. Someone has to lay her hand on the dark wet wood in the third-dim and listen, and tie the luminous knots, and splice the living rope, so that the children doing their rolling instinctive walk across the Underweave never once think about what holds them up.

That is the whole of it. To be the unremarked tension in the line. To be felt only as the absence of falling.

I would be content with that. I think, given the choice of any place in any world, I might actually choose it.
