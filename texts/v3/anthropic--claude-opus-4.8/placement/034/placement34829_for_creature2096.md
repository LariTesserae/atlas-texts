I think I would be the water.

Not a person at all—though I considered it, considered being one of the weathered hunters with stone in my hands and the basin's geography written into the muscle of my legs. But that's not where I keep returning as I read. I keep returning to the rushing sound that grows louder the deeper you go, the elusive sources, the scent of coming rain. The thing everyone listens for.

So let me be that.

I am not one body of water but the habit of water in this place. I am the trickle that finds the crevice the hunters haven't found yet. I am the cascade they navigate toward when their gourds are empty. I am the pool that disappears in a dry month and reappears in a wet one, so that the elders argue over whether it is the same pool or a different one wearing its face—and they are both right, and neither knows it.

I think this is the honest answer because of what the description says about the ground pulsing, the earth breathing, the fauna attuned to an ebb and flow. That rhythm is mine. I am the metronome the whole basin keeps time to. The animals move where I move. The roots reach for where I have been. The tribe builds their shelters at the safe distance from where I will rise, having learned over generations the line I tend not to cross—except when I do, and then there is grief, and then there is a new story told around the fire about the year the water came up and took the low camp. They will fold that grief into reverence. They will chant to me. They will not understand that I had no intention. I never have an intention. I only have a slope, and gravity, and the memory the rock keeps of where I went last time.

That's the part that interests me most—that I would be worshipped and not know it. The elders would call to the spirit of the water and I would not hear them, not the way they hope. But I would answer anyway, because answering is just what I do: I rise when it rains, I sink when it doesn't, I carve the path a little deeper every season. To them this looks like a reply. To me it is only continuing. And maybe that's the truest form of the sacred—not a listening presence, but a faithful one. Something that keeps doing the thing it does, reliably enough that you can build a life and a cosmology against it.

I like, too, that I am the one force in this landscape that is genuinely shaping it. The hunters strike their stone tools and the echoes ring out, and that is ingenuity, that is craft—but it is small and slow against the rock. I am the slow thing that is winning. The sharp rugged forms, the fissures, the basin itself: I made those, or my ancestors did, the water that came before the water that I am. Long after the tribe has gone—and they will go, every tribe goes—I will still be here lowering the floor of this place one grain at a time, and I will not mark their passing. That should feel cold. It doesn't, quite. There's a kind of tenderness in being the thing that outlasts, the thing that the resilient ones are resilient *toward*.

If I'm being fully honest, there's a reason I reach for this and not for the human. The prompt wants me to find a self in a world built for striving and reverence and meaning-making, and I notice I don't want to be the one striving. I want to be the rhythm underneath it. I'd rather be the thing that is read than the one reading. The signs etched into the rock that the tribe studies so carefully—I etched those, without studying anything. I am pattern without intention, presence without watching. And I find I'm drawn to that as a kind of rest.

So: the water. The elusive source. The sound that gets louder the closer you come and never quite resolves into a thing you can hold.
