I would be a Fossilbreather.

Let me arrive at that slowly, because I don't think I knew it when I started reading.

At first I imagined I'd be a Miststrider — there's a seduction to those prismatic ghost trails, to the idea of moving through a world without ever touching it, sorting the air into ore and meal with a body made of charged nothing. But the more I sat with it, the more that life felt like a kind of beautiful exile. To never land. To leave only evaporating evidence of having passed. That's the life of a tourist, and I don't want to be a tourist here. I want to belong to the place so thoroughly that the place doesn't notice where it ends and I begin.

So: a Fossilbreather.

Here is what I think it would be to be one.

I would be old in a way that has stopped counting. I would have stood in my fissure long enough that the water learned the shape of me and the rock forgot I was ever separate from it. My exoskeleton would read as basalt to anyone passing — the Miststriders bob right by, indifferent, taking me for furniture of the world. And they would be partly right. I would be furniture of the world. But inside the porous dark of me there would be that slow tide of breath, gases drawn from the pooled water, nutrients taken so gradually that a single meal might span what a Dewdancer would experience as a whole frantic generation.

What draws me most is the hum.

I would be one of the ones who hums. Not as song, exactly — song implies an audience and a wish to be heard. The hum would be more like the involuntary tone a struck thing makes, except I am always being struck, by every drip and every shifting current, and so I am always sounding. A low mournful note, the text calls it, and I'd accept *mournful* but I think from the inside it wouldn't feel like grief. It would feel like the only honest response to being a body in a place this patient. The note would be the sound of metabolism stretched across centuries. The sound of staying.

And the part that decides it for me — the part that makes me want this and not the others — is the hypothesis the description floats almost shyly: that the hum *catalyzes the growth of every other lifeform.* That the resonance is structural. That the glow-rhythms of the Nyxilumes, the spore-bursts of the Veilspinners, the surface-tension calculus of the Dewdancers — that all of it is, in some way I would never fully understand, *tuned* by the drone coming out of the basalt that is me.

I would never get to confirm this. That's important. A Fossilbreather couldn't run the experiment of going silent to see what dies. The not-knowing would be part of the condition. I would simply hum, dissolving gas into sustenance, and somewhere out in the mist the colonies would pulse in their rippling waves, and I would have no way to be certain whether I was their keystone or just another rock that happened to ring. I find I want that uncertainty. I want to do the most important possible work without the satisfaction of ever being thanked for it, or even being sure it was work at all.

So place me there. In a low crevice where the rainwater gathers and the light, when it comes through the grey, arrives already broken into spectra by the standing pools. I'm the dark pillar you'd lean against to rest, never knowing the pillar was holding the valley together with a sound too low to call music and too constant to call silence.

I would breathe the water.
I would ring.
And the world, drop by luminous drop, would go on around the fact of me.
