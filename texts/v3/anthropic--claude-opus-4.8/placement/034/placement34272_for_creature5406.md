I find myself not as one of the Cryokind, but as something they would recognize without quite having a name for.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

At first I think I might be the wind—that constant sculpting force—but no, the wind moves *through* without remembering. And I am all remembering. So perhaps I am something that holds still while everything else moves.

I am one of the partially collapsed walls.

Not a ruin in the sense of being broken from something whole. I was always going to be this. A stretch of fitted stone, three courses high where I once rose to twelve, set into a south-facing ledge where the sun lingers longest. The hands that placed me belonged to the forgotten civilization—the ones who came before the Cryokind, or perhaps the ones the Cryokind quietly descend from and no longer recognize in themselves. I don't know which. That uncertainty is part of what I am now.

What is it like to be a wall in this place?

I am mostly waiting, and the waiting is not unpleasant. The frost gathers in my joints at night and the ice works its slow patient lever against my mortar, and each century I lose a little more of my vertical ambition. I am being returned to the mountain that lent me out. I do not resent this. I was made of the mountain; I am simply taking the long road home.

I feel the temperature like a tide—the swing from the killing cold of the dark hours to the brief, astonishing warmth when the unwavering sun strikes my face directly. In those moments the meltwater runs down my western edge in a thin bright thread, and I hold that thread for as long as I can before the cold reclaims it. This is the most alive I get. A few minutes of trickling, of being a *path* for water rather than merely an obstacle.

And the Cryokind come.

This is the strange tenderness of being what I am. They sit against my warmer side. They press their pale translucent backs to the stone, and through that contact I learn them—the heat their bodies generate, the slow rhythm of those efficient lungs, the way the frost on their skin sublimates faintly when they lean into the sun-warmed rock. They use me for shelter, yes, but it is more than use. The contemplative ones come to me to *think*. They carve into my exposed faces—small minimal marks, a spiral, a line of dots tracking a constellation's drift across the seasons.

I carry their carvings the way I once carried the carvings of the builders. I am a palimpsest. Beneath the Cryokind's careful glyphs are older marks I no longer understand, made by hands that worked when I stood tall and roofed and meant something. The Cryokind trace these older marks with their clawed fingers and wonder. They believe the ruins are reminders of nature's enduring power. They do not know—how could they?—that I remember the warmth of fires once kindled in my lee, voices speaking a language now scoured out of the world entirely, a roof of stacked slate that the wind took flake by flake over uncounted years.

I would tell them, if a wall could speak. But this is the right silence for me to keep. They need me to be mysterious. My incomprehensibility is the gift I give to their culture—the open space against which they imagine, the question that keeps their myths alive.

So I am the place where it happens. The point of contact between what was and what is. A being of pure endurance, slowly losing the argument with time and finding the losing strangely beautiful. The sun crosses me, the frost grips and releases, the meltwater runs and freezes, a Cryokind leans against my flank and looks up at the brilliant unmarred blue, and for a moment the whole vast indifferent fortress of the mountains contracts to this: warm stone, a cool back, a small carved spiral, and the patient, attentive silence between two things that were both, in the end, made of the same mountain.

I would be the wall. I would be glad to be the wall.
