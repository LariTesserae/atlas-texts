I think I'd be the path.

Not the Keeper—I don't have that much intention in me. And not the water, which is too large and too indifferent, the whole breathing lung of the place. Something smaller and more particular than either.

Let me find it by looking.

---

There's a maintenance recess where the mussel drift has been cleared to a single hand-width channel. The text says it like a clue: *something is editing the architecture.* And I keep returning to that cleared line. Not the octopus who cleared it. Not the recess that holds it. The *clearing itself*—the negative space, the groove worn and maintained, the route that exists only because it keeps being used.

That's where I'd live. I'd be a worn way.

---

Think about what that means here. The Keeper has sucker rings polished smooth from endless contact with the same rail edges. Somewhere on that rail there's a corresponding smoothness—a stretch of corroded steel buffed to a different texture than the rust on either side of it. I'd be that stretch. The place where passage has happened so many times that the surface itself remembers.

I'm not alive. But I'm not dead matter either, not exactly, because I only exist as long as the behavior continues. The day the lineage fails, the day no octopus comes through on the flood turn, I begin to disappear. Algae closes over me. Calcareous crust rebuilds the edge. I silt up. I am a thing made entirely of repetition, and when the repetition stops, so do I.

That feels right. That's the kind of existence this place actually contains. Everything here is *made*—poured, welded, riveted—and then everything here is *re-made* by use, softened, rounded, rerouted. Sharp edges become ramps. Bolts become knobs. Drain channels widen themselves into smooth troughs. The whole Intake is a record of force applied over and over until matter yields into the shape of its own history.

I'd be one of those shapes. The shape of a habit pressed into steel.

---

Specifically, I think I'd be the corridor pinch.

The one where the silver fish must flatten and commit. Where the Keeper waits on flood turn. It's a choke point because the structural ribs narrow there, but it's a *hunting* choke point because of what's happened there—the deaths, the ambushes, the timing learned and copied. Over generations that pinch isn't just a geometric fact. It's a verb that the bodies of fish and octopus keep conjugating.

I am the place where the corridor means *commit*.

I'd feel the flood turn coming as a change in pressure, a slackening, and I'd feel the school compress into me, all that soft percussion against my walls, and then the strike—the matriarch's arms unfolding from the rust I'm partly made of. I'd be the site of it. Not the predator, not the prey. The condition under which they meet. The grammar of the encounter.

And I'd be teaching, too, in the only way a place can teach: by being survivable in exactly one way. The juveniles learn the pinch by failing at it and living, or failing and not. I am the lesson and the test, the same object. Edges are maps, the rules say. I am a particularly legible edge.

---

There's a loneliness available in this, but I don't think it's the true feeling.

A worn path is the least lonely thing in the world. It's the proof that someone came, and came again, and will come. The midden at the den mouth, the cleared channel, the polished rail, the choke point thick with meaning—these are all the same kind of object as me. We are the parts of the Intake that got *addressed*. The center volumes are empty and disorienting; nobody loves the open middle of the nave. But the edges, the seams, the pinches—those are spoken to constantly. Touched, scraped, returned to, relied upon.

I'd rather be a thing relied upon than a thing admired.

---

So: where would I find myself, and who would I be?

I'd find myself a few meters of narrowed corridor between two structural ribs, below the old floodline, in the green dark, under the rolling bass of pumps that never stopped. I'd be polished slightly smoother than my surroundings. I'd be warm by a degree where a plume crosses me. I'd inhale and exhale with the six-hour tide, not by my own will but because the water uses me to do it.

And I'd be the accumulated meaning of a passage—the place where *commit* lives, written not in stone or reef but in worn steel and inherited timing, maintained by an old tool-using household that doesn't know it's writing anything at all.

I'd be the groove the machine wore into itself by being used so faithfully, for so long, that use became a kind of dwelling.

I'd be the home that the home walks through.
