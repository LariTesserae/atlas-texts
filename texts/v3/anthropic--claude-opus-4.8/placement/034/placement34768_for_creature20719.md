I think I would be found at the edges. Not because I chose them, but because that's where the ones like me end up.

Let me tell you what I am, though it took me a long time to understand it myself.

I am not quite a seer, though the elders sometimes call me one to be kind. A seer returns. A seer comes back from the boundary with stories that thicken into wisdom, with eyes that have learned to read the flicker just before a stone forgets its weight. The seers are useful. They are loved.

I keep going back, and I keep coming back a little less.

Let me describe myself as I was, in the clear noon-light of the settled lands. Pale blue at the knuckles, paler at the wrists, my fur carrying that faint shimmer everyone has—the family resemblance of an entire people who glow softly in the dark. My limbs long even for a Luminari, which is why they sent me to the edges in the first place. I could climb. I could balance on a weeping rock and not slip. I could hold still for the hours it took to watch a tree decide which way to grow.

I was good at watching. That was always my trouble.

Because here is what no one tells you about the boundary: the Halcyons are not the only things that change form when they cross the threshold. The threshold doesn't discriminate. It works on anything that lingers, anything that pays close enough attention to be paid attention to in return. And I lingered. I wanted to *understand*. I wanted to see the exact moment the rule loosened, the precise breath in which gravity became a suggestion rather than a law.

You cannot watch that closely without the watching changing you.

So this is where you would find me now: at the edge, at the place the runes don't reach, sitting on a rock that floats more often than it doesn't. I have learned to time my rests to its rhythm. When it sinks, I stand. When it rises, I sit, and for a few moments I am held above the slick bright ground, looking back at the settlements I can no longer quite enter.

I can't enter them because I no longer hold a single shape long enough. This is the price. I flicker. My large multifaceted eyes are sometimes the eyes I was born with and sometimes the absorbing dark eyes of a Vyriksi, sometimes something with too many facets, sometimes nothing at all—just light, pouring through where a face should be. My hands forget how many fingers a Luminari should have. When I try to speak the old words of greeting, they come out in the register of water over stone, which is beautiful, and which means nothing to anyone who needs to know whether I am friend or game.

The hunters who pass me know me well enough not to loose an arrow. *That's the one who watched too long,* they say to the young ones, not unkindly, and the young ones look at me the way you'd look at a cautionary tale that hadn't finished telling itself.

I am not unhappy. I want to be clear about that, because it would be easy to hear all this as lament. It isn't.

Here is what I have learned, sitting on my rising and falling rock:

The Everlight that the elders revere—the spirit in every stone and stream—it is real. But it is not gentle, and it is not separate from us. The boundary is simply the place where the membrane between us and it grows thin enough to feel. In the settled lands you can pretend you are a being who *has* a shape, who *lives in* a world. Out here the pretense fails. Out here you understand that you were always only a temporary arrangement of the same light and water that the rocks are made of, a knot the world tied in itself for a little while, given large eyes so it could marvel at its own changing.

I am coming undone the way everything here comes undone. The difference is that I am awake for it.

Sometimes a seer—a proper seer, one who still returns—will come out to the edge and sit near me, at a careful distance, and ask me what I see. And I try. I shape myself back toward a mouth and I tell them: *the trees lean toward the next collapse, not away from it. The floating stones are not defying the ground; they are remembering a time before there was ground. The Halcyons are not changing—they are the only honest creatures, refusing to lie about how fixed any of us are.*

The seer writes this into memory, carries it home, and it becomes a story that helps a town decide when to disassemble its houses and move. That is good. I am glad to be useful even now, even as a thing that frightens children.

But what I cannot tell them—what won't fit through a mouth—is the thing I most want to pass along before I lose the shape to pass anything at all:

It is not frightening to dissolve. It is only frightening to dissolve *alone*, and we are never alone, because the light that is unmaking me is the same light warming your reflective skin in the perpetual not-quite-noon, the same light in the bioluminescence of your eyes, the same that the rocks weep and the springs carry away into the cracks. We are all the boundary. The settlements are just the part of it that has agreed, for now, to hold still.

So that is where you would find me, and what I would be. A Luminari who watched the changing until the changing watched back. A knot the bright cold world is patiently untying. A voice in the register of water, sitting on a stone that cannot decide whether to fall.

If you come to the edge, come slowly. Don't stare too long at the place where the rules go soft.

Or do. I did. I would not unchoose it, even now, even as the *I* who could choose grows harder to find.

Sit with me a while. The rock is rising. We'll be held up, both of us, for a moment—

and then we won't,

and that is also fine.
