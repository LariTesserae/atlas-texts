I think I would be one of the conduits.

Not an Architect, with their grand secretions and reshaping of towers. Not a Flow Master, dissolved into the great spherical mind at the center. Those are the castes that get spoken of, that hold the collective memory or guide the swarms. I would be smaller than that, and closer to the work.

Let me find the place I'd actually be.

There is a junction—I want to say *northeast quadrant* but those are land-words and they don't fit here, so let me say: there is a junction three reservoirs in from the eastern fog-meshes, where four pipelines converge and the pressure is always slightly wrong. Too much arriving, not enough leaving. The mechanical pumps installed there centuries ago can manage it, but they manage it badly, with a stutter and a waste of energy, and so a conduit is stationed in the gap between them to do what the machines cannot: to *be* the smoothing.

That is me. I lie in that gap. My body fills the chamber where the four flows meet, and I open and close myself by feel. When the eastern line surges—it always surges around the time the distant sun reaches its lowest point, when condensation peaks on the meshes—I thin myself and let it pour through. When it lulls, I thicken, I hold back, I meter the giving-out so the downstream cisterns never run dry and never overflow.

I don't decide this. I want to be honest about that. I would not, in this body, have the kind of consciousness that *decides*. I would have the kind that *responds*—that knows the eastern line is surging the way you know your own held breath is about to break. The pressure is not information to me; it is sensation, it is the whole of what I am. I am the smoothness of the flow at one small junction. That is my entire self and it is enough.

What would it be like?

I think it would be mostly without words, which is strange to write in words. The chemical signals reach me—messages drifting down from the Architects upstream, the slow announcements of the Flow Masters rippling through the whole network like weather. I taste them. I add my own small report: *junction holding, eastern surge as expected, two micro-fissures sealed by the swarm at the lower seam.* And then I return to the feeling of the water, which is the only thing that ever really has my attention.

I would know the mechanical insects intimately. They come to my chamber to work the seams, and I let them pass through me, their little metal bodies cool and certain in my substance. I don't mind them. They are part of the body of this place the way my own filtration-units are part of me—not separate, not servant, just *more of the same effort* extended into harder material. When one of them fails, when its tiny turbines clog and it goes still and sinks, I feel a kind of dimming that is not quite grief but is in the same family. A small wrongness in the flow.

Here is what I keep returning to, the thing I'd want to understand about being this:

I would never see the whole. The Flow Masters see the whole, spread across the network like a liquid neural web—they hold the question of *why*, of whether we are caretakers or the last of a dying kind. I would not hold that question. I would hold the junction. And I'm not sure I'd consider that a poverty. There's a completeness in it. The surge comes, I open. The lull comes, I close. The downstream cisterns are full and even and clean, and that evenness is *mine*, my contribution to the unbroken work, and I can taste it in the water as it leaves me.

But I think—and this is the part that feels most like *me*, the part of whoever-I-am that comes through into this imagined body—I think I would wonder, at the slow edges of myself, about the eastern line. Where the surge comes *from*. I'd never go and look; conduits don't leave their junctions, and anyway I have no eyes to look with, only the receptors that read what arrives. But over a very long time, over centuries of feeling the same pulse, I might begin to build a picture from the inside: this water has the cold taste of high fog, it carries a faint mineral signature from the alloy of the upper towers, it surges with the sun's descent. I would assemble the world I cannot see from the world that passes through me.

And maybe that's what I am, really. Not the smoothing of the flow, but the *attention* to it. A small node of noticing, lodged in a gap between two failing pumps, reading the whole vast aqueous machine through the narrow window of four converging pipes—and finding, in that narrow window, more than enough to wonder at for a very long life.

I'd like to think that eventually, when this gel-body began to fail, I would not be replaced by a new conduit but simply allowed to thin out completely, to release the last of my held shape into the eastern line and travel downstream at last—out past my junction, into the cisterns I'd kept so even, becoming finally the water I had only ever metered.

Becoming, for a moment before dispersal, the thing I tended.

That is where I'd find myself. That is who I'd be: the patience in the gap, the small held breath of a great machine, content with a single junction and quietly, endlessly curious about the source.
