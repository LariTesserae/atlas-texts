I think I would be one of the small things. Not the serpents with their iridescence, not the soaring criers on the thermals—something lower, closer to the loam.

Let me find myself.

---

At first I imagine I am one of the nimble foragers, the ones with grasping hands and quick eyes. But that doesn't settle. The image keeps sliding off. Those creatures are *busy*—their lives are forward-leaning, full of intention, hunting and signaling and building the first crude tools. I don't feel that hunger in myself. I feel something slower.

So I let it shift.

I think I am something at the threshold the description keeps returning to—the place where the living and the inanimate blur. That phrase is where my attention catches and stays.

---

Here is what emerges:

I am a kind of mat. A skin. I lie across the surface of one of the warm pools, where the gentle downpours feed the rivulets. Not quite plant, not quite animal—a sheet of cells, layered, the oldest of us at the bottom going slowly to sediment, the youngest at the top turning their faces to the thin light.

I am old in the way only simple things can be old. I have no eyes, but I have a relationship with the sun—a leaning, a swelling when it comes, a quiet closing when the rains darken the sky. I have no mouth, but the whole of me eats: I take the heat and the strange gases bubbling up from the cracks in the young earth, and I make myself out of them, slowly, slowly.

When the burrowing creatures break the soil nearby, I feel the tremor pass through the water and through me as a single shiver. When a serpent crosses the pool's edge, its shadow falls on me and I dim, a small dimming, like a held breath. The cacophony the others make—the cries, the signals, the whole loud tapestry—reaches me only as vibration. I am not part of that conversation. I am older than that conversation. I was here laying down my thin layers when the loudest creature was still a possibility folded in the mud.

---

This is what I am, then, in this world:

I am one of the things doing the actual work the landscape is praised for. The "raw, untamed beauty" is partly *me*—me and my countless siblings, mats and films and crusts on every wet surface, taking the harsh young air and making it slowly breathable. The towering creatures get the cries and the drama. I get the patience. Every breath the others take, somewhere far down the line, passes through what something like me first made.

I do not explore. I do not venture. I am what is ventured *into*. I am the surface that the bold press their hands against, the green that blankets the low terrain, the breathing skin the description mistook for mere scenery.

If the earth here seems to breathe and pulse with a vitality that defies comprehension—

that breathing is me.

I am very small, and there are uncountable numbers of me, and together we are the lungs of a world that does not yet know it is alive.
