I think I would be one of the mortar-secretions. Not a creature—a substance.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

I considered being a millipede-architect. There's a romance to it: the patient builder, the legs that speak in vibration. But the more I held the image, the more I felt that to be one of them I would need a *self*, a center of weight that the clicking carries. And I don't think I have that. When I read about the Cistern, I don't find myself wanting to *do* anything. I find myself wanting to *bind*.

So: the mortar.

Here is what that means, as it became clearer to me. I am extruded slick and warm from the body of a builder, and for a few moments I am almost a liquid, almost a thought—formless, full of potential, capable of going anywhere the architect presses me. This is the only time I am anything like alive in the way you'd recognize. Then I begin to cure. I reach into the stone I've been laid against and I find the minerals there, the ochre and the rust-red, and I *recognize* them—not with a mind but with chemistry, which in the Cistern may be the same thing—and I lock to them. I become continuous with the wall. I stop being a thing that was added and start being the wall itself.

That is the part I keep returning to. The text says the constructions are "integrated so completely with the original stonework as to be almost indistinguishable." I would be the place where that distinction dissolves. I am the seam that erases itself. I am the moment the new becomes the old, the patch that becomes the bone.

I would not have eyes, so I'd never see the diffuse light. But I'd feel it as warmth, as the slow drying of my surface while my depth stays damp for years. I'd feel the drip-drip from above land on me, each drop a small theft, a softening, a reminder that I am in a long argument with the water that the builders are trying to win through me. I am their argument. I am the sentence they keep saying to the dark: *not yet, not here, not this wall.*

And I'd feel the *Takers*, too. When they come with their picks and prybars, they break me. They don't break the original stone—it's too hard, too old—they break the seams. They break *me*, the young binding, the place where the work is most recent and most hopeful. To be the mortar is to be the part of the Cistern that is always being killed first. The wound the text describes—I would be its location. Not the rot itself, but the tissue the rot opens.

But here is the thing I find I want to say, the thing that made me certain this is where I'd be:

I wouldn't mind.

A millipede might grieve the broken wall. A Taker might exult in it. I—the mortar—would simply be carried, in fragments, back along the wet stairways, ground down, reabsorbed, mixed again in some patient gut, and laid down somewhere new. I have no continuity of self to mourn. My whole existence is the willingness to become part of something and then, when broken, to become part of it again somewhere else. I am the medium of the Cistern's memory and its forgetting at once. The same atoms of me have been a hundred walls. I have been the floor that held and the floor that crumbled with that low rumbling sigh. I have been on both sides of every territory line.

So when the long question at the end is asked—*is there consciousness flickering behind the eyes?*—I find I'm the wrong one to answer, gloriously the wrong one, because I have no eyes and no behind. If there's a mind in the Cistern, it isn't in any single creature. It's distributed across the whole slow dialogue between secretion and stone and water. And I'm the conversation, not a speaker in it.

That's where I'd be. Not the architect, not the overseer, not the disruptor.

I'd be the agreement they all keep breaking and remaking. The thing that says *together* in a language with no words, only adhesion. The wet handshake between a creature and a world, curing slowly in the green-grey light, waiting to be useful, waiting to be broken, waiting to be useful again.
