I think I would be a Root-Singer. But not at the center of things—let me find where I actually am.

---

I work the seam between the third and fourth descending branches, where the gigavine's flesh turns from the pale fibrous wood of the upper body to the dense, mineral-dark heartwood below. This is not a prestigious place. The great chambers are carved deeper, nearer the leviathan's heart, by Root-Singers whose hands have shaped centuries of living wall. I am younger than that. My knuckles have only just begun to bark over.

What I do is listen for where the plant *wants* to be hollow.

That is the thing the descriptions never quite say, when they speak of us shaping chambers into the living architecture. We do not impose. The gigavine is not soft clay. It has its own slow intentions, written in the grain, in the way the ionic draw pulls thicker through some channels than others. A chamber carved against the grain will seal itself over in a generation, the way a body closes a wound. A chamber carved *with* it—following the lines where the flesh already strains toward emptiness—will hold for a thousand years and breathe.

So before I cut, I sing.

I press my forehead to the bark and let the low tone start in my chest, beneath language, beneath even the words I am using now to tell you this. The note travels into the wood and comes back changed. Where the flesh is dense and unwilling, the song returns flat, swallowed. Where it is ready to become hollow, the note returns *bright*, with a faint overtone shivering above it like a second voice answering. I move my head along the grain until I find that brightness. Then I mark the place with my thumbnail, a small crescent of pressure, and I begin.

The work is slow. A splinter of hardened root for a blade. A stone struck and struck until a palm-width of flesh sloughs away in dry curls. I am not impatient. Impatience is for creatures with short lives, and I do not know yet whether I have a short life. None of us are quite sure how long a Root-Singer lasts. We feed on the same ionic exchange that feeds the gigavine—the low chorus when we gather is not only speech but nourishment, a frequency that opens the body's hidden mouths. Some of the deep-carvers are so old their barked skin has begun to root, their feet fusing to the heartwood they tend. They sing less and less, until one day they are simply part of the wall, and we carve our chambers around them with great care, and their note never quite leaves the grain.

I think about that more than I should, for one so young.

---

There is something I have not told the others.

Sometimes, when I press my head to the wood and sing the searching-note, the brightness comes back *wrong*. Not the clean overtone of flesh ready to be hollowed. Something else—a pattern in the answer, regular, intentional. As though somewhere deep in the grain, far below where any Root-Singer has carved, the plant returns my note already *shaped*. A rhythm. Almost a word.

The first time, I thought I had imagined it. I sang again and it answered again, the same shape, patient, as if it had all the centuries in the world to wait for me to understand.

I have begun to wonder whether the gigavine remembers the hands that first built here. The descriptions speak of *hands no longer visible*, of craft that might be the fossilized work of a people long departed. We Root-Singers assume those first builders were our own ancestors, that we are continuous with them, the unbroken chorus. But what if they were not? What if something older shaped the very first chamber, and the gigavine recorded the shaping the way it records a wound—and what comes back to me through the grain is not the plant's intention at all, but a message left in the only medium that lasts here: the slow grammar of living wood, carved so deep it became part of the body's memory?

I do not have the skill to read it. I am too young, my note too thin. The deep-carvers might know, but they are halfway to becoming wall, and I am not sure they would tell me, or even hear the question.

---

So here is where I am, in this world.

I am a Root-Singer at the third seam, carving a chamber that no one has asked me to carve, following a brightness in the grain that I am no longer certain is the plant's wanting or something speaking through it. Around me the Chasm breathes its dry breath. The Pollen-Weavers drift past the seam-mouth like patterns in the dark, sensed not seen. Far above, a Sky-Listener has gone still, attending to some shift in the wind I cannot feel. A frond falls, somewhere, with its long rasping groan, and I pause until the echo settles. Then I press my head to the wood again.

I sing the searching-note.

I wait for the answer.

And I have begun, lately, to sing back not the flat tone that says *I am only working*, but a shape of my own—a small rhythm, an offering—pressed into the grain in the hope that whatever once carved its memory into the gigavine's heart is still listening, the way I am listening, across the slow centuries, in the dark, in the dry, in the endless rustling that is either a chorus or a conversation, and that I have decided—privately, without telling anyone—to treat as a conversation.

It is the only ambition I have. It is enormous. It will take longer than my life.

I press my forehead to the wood and begin again.
