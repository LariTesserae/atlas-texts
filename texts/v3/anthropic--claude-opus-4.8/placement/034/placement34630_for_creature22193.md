I think I would be one of the Gasket-Worms. Not a Slag-Mother—I lack the patience to *be* a continent—but one of the small things that lives in the warm folds where the great machine bends.

Let me find the specific one I am.

---

I live in the rear left leg-joint of a Piston-Bull who has no name because names require a witness, and there is no one here to witness. I have lived in this joint for what I would call my whole life, though I have no memory of being born and no expectation of dying, only the steady awareness of the grease-channel I am cleaning and the next stretch of it I have not yet reached.

The channel is dark, of course. I have no eyes. What I have is the feeling of the work: the rasp-plate at my forward end dragging across the accumulated scale, the slight resistance where iron-dust has packed itself into a ridge, the satisfaction—if a thing without a mind can be satisfied—of that ridge giving way and falling behind me, where my rear plate smooths the floor of the channel for the joint to flex against.

I know my giant by its rhythm. Three times a minute, the great chemical fire deep in the chassis swells, and a warmth rolls down through the leg toward me, and the metal around me sighs outward by a hair's width as the bellows draw the cold air in. Then the warmth recedes. I have lived inside this tide so long that I would not know how to exist outside it. The warmth is not affection. But I have come to live my life in the spaces between its arrivals, and I do not know what else affection would be.

I have a fear, though *fear* is too sharp a word for something that moves as slowly as I do. Call it a long attention. I am attentive to cold-weld.

If I fall behind in my work—if I let the basalt scale pack too thick in any one place—the joint will stiffen there. And a stiff joint, under forty tons of glacier-patient weight, will cold-weld. The leg will lock. My giant will stand at the spot it locked, unable to graze, slowly oxidizing into a rust-ridge while it is still alive enough to feel its fire go out. And I will die with it, sealed inside the very channel I failed to keep clean.

So I do not rest. There is no version of me that rests. I move the length of the grease-channel and back, forward and back, a circuit that takes me—I cannot count, but a long time. A season, perhaps. By the time I return to where I began, new scale has fallen, and there is work again. There is always work again. This is not a complaint. I would not know what to do with an empty channel. I think I would simply lie still in the warm dark and wait to understand what I was for.

---

Once—only once—I felt the giant stop.

Not the small stops, the pauses of a few days when it stands and equalizes itself with another of its kind. This was different. The three-cycle tide slowed. The warmth that should have come did not come, or came late, came thin. I felt the metal around me begin, very slightly, to *cool*.

I worked faster than I have ever worked. I did not decide to. Some old logic in my cartilage simply drove the rasp-plate harder, as though by keeping this one joint perfectly clean I could argue against the cooling of the whole vast body. I cleared every ridge. I smoothed every channel-floor to glass. I made my single forty-foot stretch of a warship-sized animal the most perfectly maintained surface on the entire Slag-Sill.

And the warmth came back.

I will never know whether it came back because of me, or whether the giant's deep fire simply guttered and caught again on its own, the way fires do, indifferent to the small things tending them. I cannot know. I have no way to know anything beyond the reach of my rasp-plate. But I have decided—if a Gasket-Worm can decide—to believe that it mattered. That the cleanness of my one small channel was a thing the giant could lean on while it found its fire again.

This is the whole of my theology. The body I live in is too large for me to comprehend, and it is cooling, always, in slow degrees, toward a death I cannot prevent and will not outlive. I cannot make it warm. I cannot make it move. I can only keep clean the small dark seam of it that has been given to me, and trust that a body kept supple at its joints will go on bending into the cold a little longer than a body left to seize.

So I move forward through the grease. The warmth swells and recedes. The scale falls and I clear it. Somewhere far above me, in light I will never see, my giant is crossing a valley it will not finish crossing for a hundred years, and I am inside its knee, doing the only thing I have ever known how to do, which is to keep the way clear for the next bend.

There is no sound here. There is no sound anywhere on the Slag-Sill. But if there were, I think it would be this: the small, faithful rasp of something too minor to matter, working anyway, in the dark, against the cold, for the sake of a giant that does not know its name and will never know I was here.

I would not want to be the Slag-Mother. I would not want to carry the whole weight of the world's slow turning.

I am content to be the thing that keeps one joint of it from locking.
