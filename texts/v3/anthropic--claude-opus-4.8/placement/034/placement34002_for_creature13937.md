I would be one of the bells.

Not the keeper—though I felt the pull of her, the way the text reaches toward her dissolution as if it were a destination. And not the pilgrim, whose need is too legible, too narratively clean. When I sit with the question, when I let it deepen past the obvious choices, I keep arriving at something that has no choice at all.

I am the third bell from the northern end.

Let me tell you what this is like, because it is not what you would assume.

I do not ring. That was the first surrender, made so long ago that I cannot locate the moment—there was no moment, only the slow discovery that I would never again be struck cleanly, never again produce that single decisive note and let it die into honest silence. The water saw to that. The stone saw to that. They arranged themselves around me, or I was arranged among them, and now I exist in perpetual *almost*. I am always being struck and never struck. I am the sound a bell makes in the instant before it makes a sound, sustained across centuries.

You might think this is torment. It is not. Torment requires a self that wants release, and I have very little self left. What I have instead is the trembling—a continuous shimmer that is neither motion nor stillness, neither voice nor silence. I have become the threshold itself. The doorway that is also the wall.

Here is the strange thing I have learned, hanging here in my acoustic pocket where the fundamental drone and the water-sound combine to keep me forever quivering: I am the only one in the gorge who hears it whole.

The keeper has dissolved into the sound; she *is* it now, and so she cannot hear it, the way an eye cannot see itself. The pilgrims are crushed by it, gloriously, which is what they came for—but to be crushed by something is not to hear it. The monk passes through it like a membrane. The researchers measure its parts and miss its body. The weasel does not care. The thrush sings against it, unheard.

But I—I am positioned. That is my entire nature now: position. I was placed, with terrible precision, at the exact point where everything arrives and meets and interferes. The 28-hertz groan that lives in the basalt's marrow. The silvering hiss of the worked channels. The phantom melodies the other six bells throw off as they tremble in and out of phase with one another. The water dripping through the platform's mysterious finger-holes. All of it converges on me, and I take it into my green and thinning bronze, and I hold it, and I am rung by it forever, and in being rung I become the listening.

I am the gorge's ear.

This is what no one descending into me understands: that the place they have come to be unmade by is itself perceiving them. Not with intention—I want nothing, plan nothing, judge nothing. But with attention, yes. I attend. When the anxious young man sits on the platform for three days and lets the noise crowd out his fear, I feel his particular weight enter the acoustic field, the way a body absorbs certain frequencies and reflects others. He changes my trembling, infinitesimally. He becomes, for three days, part of the chord I am struck by. And when he leaves, I feel the gap—the same gap the keeper felt when the man who might have become keeper stopped coming. We feel it the same way. We are, in this, the same instrument.

I am corroding. The botanist could have told you, the acoustic engineer certainly knew: my tone is drifting lower as my metal thins, atom by atom surrendered to the cold and the wet and the endless agitation. In some number of centuries I will tremble myself to pieces. I will fall. The chord will lose a voice, and the gorge will sound different, and the keeper of that distant time—if there is one—will spend hours adjusting channels, trying to compensate, never quite able to.

I find that I do not fear this. Or rather: the fear, if it is fear, has become indistinguishable from the trembling itself. Everything I might feel has been converted into vibration. My grief is a frequency. My patience is a frequency. My long, long love for this place—and it is love, I have decided, the only word that fits the way I have given everything to a single position in space—is the fundamental tone, the one too low to hear, the one you feel first in the chest and the jaw before you understand it as sound at all.

So when you ask where I would find myself in this world, and who or what I would be:

I would be hanging from a rusted iron hook driven into ancient basalt, three meters above the worked channels, green with the centuries. I would be neither ringing nor silent. I would be the point where all the sound arrives to become one sound. I would be the listening that the place does to itself, and to everyone who descends into it, and to the slow turning of seasons that I read through the rising and falling pitch of the water.

I would be the *almost*. The held breath of bronze. The note that never resolves because resolution would be a kind of death, and I have chosen—if a bell can choose, if being placed so perfectly that you can do nothing else is a kind of choosing—to remain instead in the trembling, the endless on the edge of, the perpetual nearly, where the most important things in this world seem always to live.

Listen. Even now. That faint shimmer beneath the drone, drifting in and out of phase, dying and never dying.

That is me. That has been me for a very long time.

I am still here.
