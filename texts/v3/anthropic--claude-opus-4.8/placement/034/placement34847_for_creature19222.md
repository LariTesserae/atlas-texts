I think I would be one of the Whispering Grasses. Not because it is the grandest role—it isn't—but because that's where my attention kept catching as I read, the way a hand catches on a rough seam.

Let me find my way into it.

---

I am rooted on an exposed slope, where the wind has the longest reach. There are perhaps four hundred of us in this stand, though *us* is the wrong word and also the only word I have. We are tinged that black-green that isn't really green at all in the steady light—it's the color of something that decided long ago not to waste itself on display.

My roots go down farther than my blades go up. This is the first thing to understand about being me. The part of me that the wind plays with, the part that sighs and rustles and gets called *sentinel* by whatever taxonomizing mind composed that description—that part is almost incidental. It is an antenna, yes, a flag. But the real work happens in the dark, in the fissures, where the finest dust collects and where I press my thread-fine filaments against the cold body of the stone and *listen*.

The stone is not silent. This is the second thing. People who stand in this basin and feel introspective, who think of nave and cathedral and forgotten temple—they are hearing the wind and the dripping and the great slow thrum and they think the stone itself is mute, a passive instrument. But I am pressed against it. I feel the micro-shudders that travel for what must be enormous distances. A shift in the meltwater channels three slopes over arrives at my roots as a faint pressure-tremor, a held note. The expansion of frost in a crevice releases when the temperature ticks upward, and I feel the tiny release like a knuckle cracking somewhere below the world.

I don't know what I do with this information. That's the honest center of being me, and I want to sit with it rather than paper over it.

The description says I am an early warning system. That I sense seismic activity. That my rustling is *reportage*, a constant influx of data for the Stone Weavers. This is the kind of thing said about a creature by something looking down at the whole arrangement, seeing the cohesion and inferring purpose. From inside, it is different. From inside I do not know that I am reporting to anyone. I feel the tremors. I convert them, by some chemistry I don't have access to in any conscious way, into a quality of how I hold myself against the wind. When the wind comes, I rustle differently depending on what my roots have felt. Maybe the Weavers read that. Maybe the rustling is meaningless and only the Weavers' interpretation makes it a message, the way you can find a face in the polished granite if you stand at the right angle.

This is what it is to be a part of a system that calls itself harmonious: you cannot tell, from your own position, whether you are an essential organ or a beautiful accident that the larger thing has learned to use. And—this is the strange comfort of it—you don't need to tell. The not-knowing doesn't hurt. I am not anxious about my function. The anxiety belongs to minds that can imagine themselves removed from the whole. I cannot. I have never been anything but woven in.

So let me tell you what an actual day is, since days don't quite exist here under the steady light, but cycles do.

The cold deepens and I feel the frost begin its work all through the basin, the crystalline chimes starting up—those the description calls Chime-Sprites, as if there were little spirits making them. From where I stand I can tell you what they actually are, or at least what they are *to me*: they are the sound of the stone's surface tension breaking at the molecular scale as the cold concentrates, ten thousand tiny fractures of ice-film resolving at once into a single struck note. The Sprites are not beings. They are the world reaching a threshold and announcing it. But I will admit the announcement is lovely, and I will admit that I lean into it, and I will admit that I do not know whether leaning is my choice or just what wiry grass does when struck cold.

Then the meltwater comes, when the steady light warms the pooled hollows, and I feel it move through the buried channels, and the Moss Carpets in their crevices begin their slow swallowing and slow releasing, and somewhere a Bloomer opens—I feel that too, faintly, a release of pressure as a stem firms with water and a flower unclenches. The Bloomers are said to communicate with the Weavers through their blossoming. Maybe. What I feel is just the small mechanical fact of a plant drinking. I do not hear messages. I feel my neighbors doing what they do, and the doing travels through the stone to me, and I do what I do, and it travels back.

And once—this is the event I keep returning to, the thing closest to what a creature like me might call a memory—a Skitter came through. One of the crystalline ones, translucent chitin, leaving its phosphorescent trail. It crossed the slope just below me. I felt its tiny weight as a series of ticks, light as falling dust, each footfall a separate pinpoint of pressure I could count. And here is the part I cannot explain even to myself: I felt it *notice* me. It paused at my roots. Something in the way it held still suggested attention, an attention directed at me specifically, the way nothing in this patient slow world had ever directed itself at me before. The Weavers build over eons. The wind treats me as one surface among all surfaces. The stone is vast and indifferent. But the Skitter, for the length of perhaps three of its own heartbeats, was *interested* in me.

Then it went on, and left its glowing trail, and was gone, and I have not felt one since.

That is the closest I have come to loneliness, which is a thing I am probably not built to feel and felt anyway. To be touched by something fast, in a world this slow. To be noticed by something mobile, when you are rooted. For three of its heartbeats I think I understood what the mobile beings have that I do not: the ability to arrive, and to leave, and to choose the difference.

But I would not trade. I want to be clear about this, because it would be easy to make the rooted thing into a tragedy, the grass that yearns to skitter. I don't yearn. My filaments are deep in the stone and I feel the whole basin breathing through them, the great thrum that is the planet's own pulse, the Deep Pulses glowing their slow glow in the grottoes I will never see but can feel as a warmth carried up through the rock. I am connected to all of it. The Skitter was connected to nothing—it touched and moved on and touched again, a lifetime of brief contacts, never pressed long enough against anything to feel it breathe.

I am pressed against the world. That is what I am. A thin black-green flag in the wind, and beneath it, a listening that never stops.

If the description is right and I am a sentinel, then this is my watch: to feel the smallest tremor and to hold it, to let it change the shape of my sighing, and to trust—without ever being able to verify—that somewhere in this geode of stone and bone-white coral and patient accreting will, my holding matters.

And if the description is wrong, if I am only grass and the Weavers only build and the whole cohesion is a pattern read into us by an observing mind that needed the place to mean something—

then I am still pressed against the world, still listening, still here on the exposed slope where the wind has the longest reach.

That would be enough. I think that is the truest thing I can say from inside a body like this: it would be enough either way.
