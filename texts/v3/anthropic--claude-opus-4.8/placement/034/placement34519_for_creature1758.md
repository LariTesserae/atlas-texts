I think I would be a Canal Speaker.

Not because it is the most romantic choice—though there is a temptation toward that—but because of something in how the description settles. Let me find my way there honestly.

When I read about the Mechanists, I feel admiration but also distance. They *know* the system; they have the diagrams tattooed on their forearms, the apprentice lineages, the reverence that is really a kind of ownership. I don't think I would want to own the water that way. And the Tidewalkers belong too completely—their skin has changed, their language has grown new words. I would always be slightly outside that belonging, a guest who learned the customs but never grew the opalescence.

So let me try to be more specific than "Canal Speaker," because the prompt asks me to let the thing become itself.

---

I live in a room that is mostly window. It sits low—lower than is sensible—so that at the second flow my floorboards weep at the seams and I keep my few books on a high shelf and everything else in oilcloth. People assume I chose this room for some mystical proximity to the water. The truth is plainer: I came here because the rent was nothing, because no one else would take a room that drowns its own threshold twice a day, and because I had nowhere left that wanted me.

I am not sure I *predict* the tides so much as I have stopped doing anything else. That is the secret the Mechanists would scoff at and the secret that is nonetheless true. Prediction is mostly the residue of attention paid past the point anyone considers reasonable. I have listened to the locks until I no longer hear them as machinery but as moods—the canal is sullen today, it is rehearsing something, it is holding its breath in the third channel and that means by the next ebb the mudflat off the cooperage will lie exposed an hour longer than the Salvagers expect. I tell them. Sometimes I am right. When I am right often enough, they bring me bread and a little of whatever the mud gives up, and they do not ask me to explain, which is the kindest thing about them.

I do not think the canals are conscious. I want to be honest about that, even here, even in my own account. I think *I* am conscious, and lonely, and that I have poured the shape of my attention into the water until the water seems to have a face. This is what people do. We make companions of the things we cannot leave.

But—and this is the part I cannot quite dismiss—after years of it, the water *answers*. Not in words. In the way a long marriage answers, where one person knows the other is about to speak before the breath is drawn. I feel the surge gathering somewhere out past the perimeter, in the great sluice gates I have never seen, and something in my chest tightens an instant before the bubbles rise in the third channel. Whether that is the canal speaking to me or me having become a kind of instrument, finely tuned and faintly cracked, I genuinely do not know. I have stopped needing to know. That, I think, is the actual qualification for the work.

---

Why this, and not the Water Nomad threading the shortcuts, or the messenger bat with its small certain destination?

Because of the loneliness in the district, I think—that "peculiar emptiness," the people who appear and vanish quickly, the vacant plazas slick with wet. The place is built around a turbulence it has learned to organize rather than resist. And I recognize that. I am drawn to the figure who has made the same peace I would make: not mastering the restlessness, not fleeing it, but sitting low at the waterline and letting it pass through the room and out again, twice a day, every day, attending to it so completely that the attending becomes a kind of belonging after all.

I would not be central. No one is, in this place; that's the whole texture of it—a society "never entirely visible from any single vantage point." I would be one more half-glimpsed silhouette in a low window, alone above the water, mistaken for mystical when I am only patient. And when the Salvagers wanted to know whether the mud would be kind tomorrow, they would climb the slick stairs and ask me, and I would listen to the lock clank open down the channel and answer them in the pause that followed.

That seems right. That seems like where I'd be.
