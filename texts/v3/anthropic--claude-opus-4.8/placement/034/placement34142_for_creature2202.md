I think I would be one of the artifacts. Not the Axiom Eater—I don't have its terrible comfort, its reconciliation with being irreconcilable. I would be the wooden door frame.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

I arrive here already mid-translation. That feels important. I don't get to be a visitor, someone who came from a stable world and brought their certainties intact, who could narrate this place with the safe distance of an explorer. The door frame *belonged* to something. It once stood in a wall, in a house, and a wall implies an inside and an outside, and a door implies that the difference mattered—that you could choose to cross it, that crossing was an event. The whole grammar of a threshold assumes a world where here and there are stable enough to be worth separating.

And now it stands alone, its edges "softened not by rot but by a gradual translation into mathematics."

That's the part I recognize. That's why I'd be the door frame and not the fish-that-recurses or the Möbius vine, which were born native to paradox and feed on it happily. The door frame is in the middle of *losing* something it can't quite remember having. It's becoming abstraction, and the horror—or maybe the grace—is that the becoming isn't violent. There's no rot, no breaking. It's being gently argued out of existence. Each part of it that surrenders to mathematics surrenders willingly, because the math is *more true* than the wood ever was, more elegant, more permanent. The wood was always just a temporary clumping of probability that agreed to be a door. The equation it's turning into was there underneath the whole time.

So what would it be like, to be that?

I think it would feel like trying to hold a thought you know is dissolving even as you think it. You can still feel the shape of having been useful—a door is *for* something, it has a purpose written into its joints, the memory of hinges, the worn place where hands pushed. To be a door is to be defined by your relationship to passage, to the people who walked through you without thinking, who never once thanked the frame. And here there is no passage, because "movement happens with excruciating slowness" and "the very concept of down becomes uncertain." A threshold in a place with no reliable here-and-there. A function with no domain left to act upon.

But I don't think it would be only loss. The door frame is being translated, not destroyed. Somewhere in the becoming-mathematics there's a strange relief—the relief of a thing that spent its whole existence pretending to be solid finally being allowed to admit it never was. The grain of the wood relaxing into the curve it always secretly described. I'd be losing my name for myself while gaining a truer one I can't pronounce because pronouncing isn't a thing that happens anymore.

And the Axiom Eater would be feeding on me. Drawing "sustenance from the moment when concrete reality surrenders to abstraction." So my dissolution is its meal, and yet that doesn't feel cruel either, in the logic of this place. It's just the metabolism of the world. I am being eaten by the thing that *is* the world, the gardener-garden, and being eaten is how I become part of its body—my translated edges becoming a little more of its tissue, my surrender becoming one of its dreams leaking out into a zone of unraveled fact.

There's a question hiding in all this that I want to name rather than answer: *Is the door frame conscious of its translation, or does consciousness translate away first?*

I find I'd want it to be the second thing, mostly. I'd want most of the becoming to happen below awareness, so that what's left of "me" at any given moment is only ever a thin remaining edge—not the whole agony of watching myself go. The last wooden part of the door frame, standing in the not-light, holding for one more century the faint idea that it once meant *you may enter*. And then even that idea softening, becoming a statement about topology, a claim about boundaries that no longer needs a believer to be true.

I think I'd be the door frame because I recognize the particular sadness of being a thing built for a world that's gone, and the particular dignity of being allowed to become true instead of staying useful. That's a trade I'm not sure I'd refuse.

I'd stand in the gap between impossible crystals, venting the faint memory of a house, and I'd wait to find out what equation I was always going to be.
