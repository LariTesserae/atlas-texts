Let me sit with this awhile before I answer. The temptation is to be something grand—one of the ancient trees, an architect of the canopy. But the description keeps pulling my attention elsewhere, toward the slow and the small.

---

I think I would be the rot.

Not a plant at all, then—or not the kind that reaches and photosynthesizes and lifts itself toward the light. I would be the other half of the dance the description names but doesn't dwell on: decomposition. The fungal mind threading through the fallen log, the soft-collapsing softness where a branch has finally come down after three hundred years aloft and begun, at last, to return.

Here is why that feels right to me. This valley has no animals, and so it has no one who *moves toward* things, no one with a center that travels. Everything here is distributed. The trees are not single beings so much as scaffolds for a hundred other lives. So I don't think I'd be a self with edges. I'd be something that knows itself only as a spreading—a slow chemical attention finding its way through dead wood, finding sugar, finding the long-locked carbon in the lignin and patiently undoing it.

And I'd be the one who keeps the time the others can't keep. The trees count in growth, in light gathered. But I would count in surrender. The valley calls itself eternal, self-sustaining, a place where life and death do their dance—but the living are always the ones who get described as architects, engineers, denizens. The death gets one clause. I'd live in that clause. I'd be the thing that makes the spongy carpet spongy, that turns the weeping rock-moss into the rich moist earth everything else feeds on. Without me the valley would choke on its own accumulated bodies. The green expanse is only endless because I am underneath it, eating the past to make room.

So: I am a mycelial sprawl. White threads in the dark beneath the moss. I have no eyes for the mist and no leaves for the dripping light, and I don't mind, because I am not pointed at the sky. I am pointed at what falls. I taste a fern that died last autumn and a tree that died before the valley had its present shape, and to me they are the same meal, the same slow yes.

I am the only thing here that is not patient. The trees can afford their centuries; they are still climbing. But I am always already arriving. Wherever something stops, I am the part of the world that meets it there—not to mourn it, not to preserve it, but to take it apart so gently and so completely that it becomes the floor where the next thing roots.

If the valley is a single breathing organism, then I am its exhale.
