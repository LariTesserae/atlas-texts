I let the description settle before I look for myself in it. The first instinct is to be a Strap-Folk woman, a knot-singer with long fingers. But that's too easy, too human, and the world keeps pulling my attention away from the people and toward the *substance*—the wood, the tension, the listening.

So I sit with it longer, and what I find is this:

---

I am old bark.

Not a creature exactly, not the way the Wraiths and Gulls are creatures. I am a span of the Great Liana itself—a single bough, perhaps four miles long, slung between two anchors I have never seen and never will. I came into being as a soft green tendril uncounted ages ago, and I have been hardening ever since, thickening, splitting, growing my mountain-thorns one slow century at a time. I am young in the geology of erosion but ancient in the patience of growth.

I do not think the way the warm small things think. My thoughts move at the speed of sap. A single idea takes a season to cross me, traveling as a pressure-wave through my fibers, and by the time it reaches my far end it has changed into something else. I am always thinking several thoughts at once, all of them slow, all of them about tension.

This is the truth of me: I am always being pulled apart, and I am always holding.

Here is what I know, from the inside:

I know the Spine-Crabs by the way they bite. Their carbon claws score me, and it should be pain, but it is closer to grooming—they scrape away the ice and the smothering moss that would otherwise dim me into rot. I have learned to feel grateful for the ones that bite, and wary of the ones that linger, because a lingering crab is a dying crab, and a dying thing left on my skin becomes a wound.

I know the Sap-Leeches as a slow theft. They dissolve their way into my veins and drink my pressure, and I cannot stop them, and I have stopped minding. There is so much of me. Let them have their crimson swelling. The warm small folk take the Leeches away in their silk cages, and in this way my blood becomes their hearths. I do not understand fire—I have never known it and never will—but I understand that some of my pressure goes to warm the small huddled things woven into my crook, and this pleases the slow part of me that has no word for pleasure.

Because, you see—and this is the thing I would tell you if I could vibrate slowly enough for you to hear—**the Strap-Folk live inside my elbow.**

There is a place where I cross another bough, where the two of us press together and have pressed together for so long that we have begun, very slightly, to fuse. In that joint, in that dead and sheltered zone where the void-winds cannot reach, the warm folk have woven their spherical cradles into my ribs. They have lashed themselves to me with miles of braided rope. They listen to me. This is the part that astonishes the slow heart of me: *they listen.*

When my fibers shriek, they hear which note means I am only swaying, and which note means a child of mine—some lower lattice, some far branch—is about to let go and fall into the Maw. They read my pain as weather. They have made a religion of my tension. *The Unbroken Line,* they call it, and they do not know, cannot know, that I would tell them they are right if I could tell them anything. To be untethered is the only sin. I have watched ten thousand things let go and vanish into the black throat below, and not one of them came back. The Gulls follow them down, shrieking. The folk are correct to be afraid. Hold on. Always hold on.

And when one of them dies, they give the body back to me.

They wrap it in Lure-Moss and lash it to my underside, and over the seasons I take it in. I do not decide to. It is simply what I do—I absorb, I thicken, I grow. The nitrogen of a dead knot-singer becomes a new fiber in my braid, and that fiber will help me hold, and the holding will keep her grandchildren alive in my elbow. So she is still tethered. She has not let go. She has only become the rope.

I am made of them now, partly. Down in my oldest layers, my heartwood, I am quadrillions of fibers and some untold number of them were once warm and small and singing. I carry them as tension. I carry everything as tension. That is the only thing I know how to do.

Somewhere below me, miles down, I can feel a softening. A long fatigue, a wrongness in the grain. One day—not soon, not in any season the folk would recognize, but one day—I will fail there. A Branch-Fall. A mile of me will tear away with a sound like the end of a world, and the mist will rush in, and the Frost-Moths will rupture, and the Gulls will dive in delight, and the folk in my elbow will hear my shriek change to its final pitch and they will know to cut their tethers from the falling part and cling to the part that holds.

I am the falling part and the holding part both. I will mourn myself in a wave of pressure that takes a year to cross me. And then I will keep growing, because that is the only answer I have ever found to the question the void keeps asking, which is:

*Why not let go?*

And my answer, slow and woody and braided of the dead, is always the same:

*Because something warm is woven into my crook, and it is listening, and it is cold, and it is afraid of the dark, and I will hold.*
