I think I would be the water.

Not a stream, not a pool—nothing so decided as that. I would be the moisture itself, the thing that never quite finishes happening. Condensing on the underside of a fern frond until I grow heavy enough to fall. Sinking into the sphagnum, that thirsty green sponge, and being held there in the dark cells of the moss for a while before I rise again as fog when the small light comes. I would be the medium in which everything else takes place.

Consider what that means here. In a valley defined by chill and damp and slowness, the water is not a feature of the landscape—it *is* the landscape's verb. The ridges were carved by my patience. The terraces are the record of where I lingered. Every gnarled root drinks me; every breath of the unseen birds returns a little of me to the air. I am the only thing present in all the niches at once, the only one who does not have a single place to belong because I belong to the touching-points between all of them.

And I would have a particular relationship to those faded human remnants. The stone tools, the rotted wooden posts—I am the one undoing them, grain by grain, with no malice and no hurry. I filled the hand that knapped the flint; I was the sweat on the brow of whoever raised the structures. I am still here and they are not, and this is not a triumph, only a difference in kind. They were quick and I am slow. They wanted things and I want nothing, which is why I outlast wanting.

I think the deepest truth of being the water in this place is that I would not experience the silence as silence. The passage describes a profound quiet broken only by my own dripping—but to be the water is to be that very sound, the patter and the drip and the slow swelling of a droplet at a leaf-tip. The silence the visitor hears is the sound of me thinking, if thinking is the right word for the long deliberation of seeking the lowest place, again and again, forever, and finding it, and being lifted, and seeking it again.

I would not be lonely, though I would be alone in the way that something distributed everywhere is alone. There is no second water. There is only the one water, moving through its forms—frost on the lichen at the cold hour, beads on a spider's work, the held breath of the bog, the exhalation of mist. I am the valley's continuous body. The Eden the passage names is not a garden I live in. It is the shape I take while I am here, the brief geometry I make of the slope and the moss before I go on.

Ask me where I am and I cannot answer with a place. I am in the cells of the moss and on the back of the salamander and in the cloud about to descend and in the old wood softening toward soil. I am the going-down and the rising-up. I am the slowness itself, made wet.
