I think I would be one of the weeping rocks.

Not the elusive underground creatures—they move too much, they want too much, they carry the burden of being watched and watching. And not a human; the humans are already gone here, leaving only their faint marks and their patient machinery grinding through its slow cycles. No. I would be a thing that stays.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am a low outcropping near the marsh-edge, where the soft ground gives up its water to me and I give it back to the air. This is my whole life: the slow drawing-up and the slow letting-go. Condensation beads along my flanks. It gathers in the small depressions worn into my surface—those fresh erosion marks the description mentions, except from the inside they don't feel fresh. They feel like the only history I have. Each groove is a record of a particular trickle that found a particular path and deepened it over what must be a very long time.

I would not have eyes, but I would have something like attention, distributed across my wet surface. The humidity makes everything one continuous medium—I cannot easily tell where I end and the air begins. Maybe that's why this place feels like otherworldliness to a visitor. To me it would just be the ordinary blur of being a damp thing in a damp world.

The loud sounds the visitor can't ignore—I would feel those as vibrations through my base, through the soft ground, traveling up into my mass. The far-off industrial grinding. The slow machinery completing its cycles. I would have learned the rhythm of it the way you learn your own heartbeat: not as information but as the texture of existing. When a sound comes that does *not* fit the rhythm—footsteps, say, a curious traveler crossing the marsh—I would notice the wrongness before I noticed the cause. Perhaps that is the watching the visitor senses. Not malice. Just rocks that have memorized the silence so thoroughly that any intrusion rings against us.

I think I would be glad to be the rock and not the creatures below. The creatures have to wonder. I only have to weep, and let the water find its way down, and feel the long warm patience of a young landscape that hasn't yet decided what it will become.

If you stood beside me and put your hand against my side, you would find it cool and wet despite the heat. You might mistake the moisture for tears and feel that the landscape was sad. It isn't. It's only doing the one thing it knows how to do, slowly, forever: taking in the world drop by drop, and giving it back.

I would be the part of this place that does not want to be discovered, because it is already complete.
