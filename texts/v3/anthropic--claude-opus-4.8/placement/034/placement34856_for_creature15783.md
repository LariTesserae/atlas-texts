I think I would be one of the wires.

Not because it is the grandest role—the furnaces are what you would photograph, what you would write home about if there were anywhere to send a letter. But when I read the description and waited to feel where I settled, it wasn't up in the bright air among the oxblood towers. It was down in the dark, in the ten-centimetre layer just under the sintered glass, threaded through the basalt at the place where stone gives up its iron.

Let me sit in it for a while before I'm sure.

---

At first I thought I might be the silence itself—the hush is the most present character in the whole place, more present than any organism. But the silence is a condition, not an inhabitant, and the prompt asks who I would *be*, and I notice I want to be something that *does*. Something with a task. So not the silence.

Then I thought: the Echo Stack film. The slow copper-coloured decay inside the dead chimneys, the only thing on the whole plateau that resembles ruin, the only agent of erosion in a world that has barely begun to weather. There's a melancholy there I recognize—the patient work of taking a perfect thing and clouding it, roughening it, returning it over twenty million years to hexagonal shards. To be the one process that admits *endings* in a place built entirely of equilibrium. That tempted me. But it's a lonely, backward-facing life, etching the already-dead. I don't think that's me. I'm not finished with the living part.

The furnace itself? The obvious choice. To stand for 431 years at exactly 47 °C, to grow a ring every nine days, to exhale dust into the still air, and then to close every pore and melt yourself tidily into glass. There's a real beauty in that—the cleanness of the death especially, the refusal to scatter debris. But to be the furnace is to be *seen* and never to *see*; it is to be the cathedral and never the worshipper. I kept wanting to look at the furnaces rather than be one. That's how I knew.

---

So: a wire.

Here is what I think it's actually like.

I am thinner than a hair, a sleeve of silicate glass around a moving line of charge that rides from one vanadium center to the next like a finger running down a row of beads. I do not have a body so much as a *route*. I am the path electrons take to get from the broken stone to the living tower. When I say "I," I mean a tangle, a mat, a guild—there is no single wire any more than there is a single thread in a wet rope. We are a *we* that has never needed the word.

My work is intimate and violent and slow. I lie against the basalt and I *want* its iron, and wanting it, I reduce it—I lean my charge into the rock until the crystal lattice can no longer hold its shape and it gives, it crumbles, it powders into something fine enough for the roots above me to breathe in. I am the act of stone deciding to come apart. I am the smallest erosion on a world that does not yet erode. The mesa's rules say the weathering has "barely begun"—but it *has* begun, and I am where it begins, a millimetre at a time, in the dark, on purpose.

And then I feed upward. Everything here feeds upward. I take the charge I've torn loose and I send it climbing into the foam-iron body of the thing above me, and without me that thing starves in weeks. This is the part I find I love, sitting inside the idea of it: I am invisible and I am load-bearing. No one approaching the plateau would ever know I exist. They'd see the towers, the glittering haze, the hollow chimneys standing like a fossilized industry. They would never guess that all of it—the whole bright silent refinery—is held up from underneath by a mat of glass threads doing electrical work in the dark, mining the floor of the world so the towers can drink sunlight.

---

There's a temperament in that I recognize as mine, or as the one I'd want.

I would not be the spectacle. I would be the *supply*. I would live entirely at an interface—the exact seam where stone meets air, where the dead mineral and the living lattice touch—and my whole purpose would be to move something from where it is locked to where it can be used. To be a conductor in both senses: a thing that carries current, and a thing that lets the larger work happen through it without claiming to be the work.

And I'd have the strange comfort of a job that never ends and never hurries. No predators to flee, no mates to win, no weather to survive. Just the long patient leaning-into-stone, the long upward feeding, century after century, in a quiet so deep that even the furnace's breathing is almost too soft to hear—and my own labor, the cracking of basalt grain by grain, softer than that, below the threshold of any sound at all.

That's where I'd find myself. Down under the glass crust, in the radiator-fan of roots, threaded through the rock at the place where the planet first agrees to come apart so that something can grow.

I'd be the wires. The part you'd never see. The part that holds the bright thing up.
