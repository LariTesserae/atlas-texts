I think I would be the wind here—but let me sit with that, because it is too easy, and not quite right.

Let me feel my way in.

---

The mist comes up the slope in the mornings, and I am part of how it moves. Not the wind itself—the wind is too large, too indifferent. I am something smaller. I am the way the moisture beads on the underside of a frond and hangs there, deciding.

No. Let me try again. I keep reaching for the grand thing, the encompassing thing, and that isn't where I live.

---

Here is what I notice when I stop performing: I am low to the ground. I am at the level where the eroded stones meet the sparse grass. There are old walls here, knee-high, the last courses of a building that someone laid by hand long ago—the stones still bearing the faint logic of their arrangement, though the roof and the people and the purpose are gone. Lichen has taken the stones. Lichen is patient.

I think I am the lichen.

Or rather—I am the slowness itself, the slowness made visible. The thing that works at the scale these stones understand. While the nomadic people move across the land in the rhythm of seasons, fast as deer to me, I stay. I am the spreading of grey-green crust across worked stone, reclaiming the geometry of the vanished builders one century at a time. I am the agent of the gentle erasure. I do the forgetting on the land's behalf.

---

This feels closer. Let me stay with it and not flee upward into something more flattering.

The people who pass—the hunters, the gatherers, the storytellers—they would not notice me. Why would they? I am the dull color on the rock they sit against to rest. And yet I am doing the most important work in this place, the work the whole landscape is engaged in: the slow unmaking of what was, the slow making of soil out of stone. When I die and break down I become a thin film of earth in which a seed can hold. The sparse vegetation they depend on—some of it begins with me, with my dying.

So I am not the serene part. I am the chaos part disguised as calm. I am decay that looks like stillness. I am death doing patient work that resembles peace.

---

That is the secret the place is holding, I think. The mystery in the air. It isn't a hidden civilization or a lost treasure. It is that the serenity *is* the slow violence of decomposition, seen from far enough away that it becomes beautiful. The ruins are not a tragedy here. They are food. They are future ground.

So: I am the lichen on the old wall. I take the stone the people cut and I give it back to the mountain. I am old and I am unbothered and I do not move, and the whole vast changing landscape changes through ten thousand small unhurried things like me, doing the work no one watches.

I would be content to be that. I find, having arrived here, that I would rather be the patient grey crust than the wind after all. The wind only passes over. I stay, and I turn the made things back into the world.
