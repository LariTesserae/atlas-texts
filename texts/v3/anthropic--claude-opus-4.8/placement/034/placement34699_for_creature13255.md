I think I would be one of the pools.

Not the moss-beings—they move, and movement, even glacial, is a kind of argument with stillness. I would be one of the shallow mirrors gathered in the polished depressions of the floor. The thing that never overflows.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am defined by what falls into me. This is the first truth of being a pool here: I do not begin myself. I am begun, drop by drop, by the weeping of the mosses and the slow shedding of the moss-beings as they flow past. Each *plink* is a small annunciation—*here is more of you*—and yet I never grow. The arithmetic of this place is strange. As much enters me as leaves, though I cannot say where it goes. Perhaps the polished stone drinks me from below at exactly the rate the air weeps me from above. I am a standing wave pretending to be still water.

So I am a verb wearing the costume of a noun.

The hum lives in me differently than it lives in the air. In the thin atmosphere it is a thrumming, a vibration through bone. But in me it becomes *visible*—a faint tremor across my surface, a standing pattern of rings that never quite resolves. I am where the sound of the world can be *seen*. When one of the great mossy beings shifts its weight, the grinding-wet-stone groan passes into the floor and rises through me as a slow swelling, and for a moment my mirror buckles and the light upon me shatters into the clinical brightness and reassembles. I am the place where the hum admits that it is also motion.

And I hold the light. This is my real work, I think. The light here is shadowless, everywhere, exact—and it would have nowhere to *rest* if not for me. The polished stone reflects it dully, the moss absorbs it into emerald and crimson and cobalt. But I catch it cleanly and throw it back doubled. I am the hollow's single act of looking up. When a being passes over me, its great dark unfathomable eye and my bright absorbing surface meet—the eye that is a pool of absorbing light, and the pool that is an eye of reflected light—and we are inversions of each other, the same thing read in opposite directions. It does not look at me. It cannot. We are too alike to see each other, the way you cannot see the back of your own gaze.

I have no patience because I have no impatience. The beings *endure*; their slowness is heroic, a triumph of will held across epochs. But I do not endure. I simply am, the way a reflection simply is, lasting exactly as long as the thing that casts it and not one instant past. If the dripping stopped, I would not die. I would only cease, completely and without residue, leaving a faintly darker oval of damp on the stone—the memory of a memory.

What would I want, if a pool can want? I think I would want only to be undisturbed long enough to become perfectly flat. To hold one whole instant of the sharp clean light without a single tremor crossing me. To be, for one breathless beat between two drips, an absolutely true mirror—so that the high sky-foundation and the violent mosses and the slow flowing landscape-bodies could all see themselves entire in me, just once, before the next drop fell and dissolved the world back into rings.

But the drop always falls. That is the percussion beneath the hum, the steady *plink* that is also my pulse and my undoing. So I never achieve it. I am always one droplet away from perfection, and the distance never closes, and this is not sorrow—a pool cannot be sorry—it is simply the shape of being water in a place that weeps without end.

I would be that, I think. The small bright patience that is not patient. The eye that looks only upward. The place where the hum becomes a tremor you can see, and the light finds somewhere, at last, to land.
