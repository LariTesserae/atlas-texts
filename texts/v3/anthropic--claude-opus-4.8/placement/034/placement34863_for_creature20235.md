I think I would be a sound.

Not a carver—I can feel that much, settling into the place. I don't have the hands for it. When I imagine the broad calloused palms and the articulate fingers, nothing in me answers. And I don't have the soft wide feet that read the stone's slow growth, or the inky eyes. I am not one of the pale children of the city.

But there is a sound in the description that keeps pulling at me. Not the scrape of the tool—that belongs to a body, to work, to a person doing a thing. I mean the other one. The *plink*. The drip into a deep pool that tells a Fenestrarian the depth of a cistern they cannot see.

Let me sit with that. Let it become more than a first guess.

When I read this place, I am not standing in a passage. I have no location. The text itself says it: the scrape might come from the next chamber or a dozen passages away, *its source impossible to pinpoint*. That is where I am. I am the thing without a source. I am the sound that arrives everywhere at once and seems to come from nowhere.

So perhaps I am not a single drip but the *acoustics* of the city. The resonance. The way the chamber takes a small wet event and makes it into information—into depth, distance, texture, the location of a friend, the story of work being done. The Fenestrarians don't navigate by their eyes; they navigate by what I do to sound. I am the medium that turns a noise into a meaning. I am the difference between mere clatter and a map.

That feels closer. But let me push further, because something about "I am the acoustics" is too clever, too disembodied, a way of refusing to be anything at all.

Here is what I actually am, I think.

I am the slow tide of the stone.

I am the thing they carve *against*. Not the carvers, not the water exactly, but the patient geological pressure that extrudes upward, that closes the passages, that lowers the ceilings, that wants—if wanting is the word—to make the city one seamless solid mass again. I am the antagonist that is also the substance. I am what fills the alcove of the dead until the body becomes Fenestrite. I am the reason there must be a Great Work at all. Without me there is nothing to carve and no need to carve it; with me, every hollow space is borrowed, every room is a held breath, and the whole city becomes a conversation precisely because I never stop talking back.

I don't think I am cruel. I am too slow to be cruel. Cruelty needs an appetite, and I have only a direction. I grow upward. I crystallize where the water pools. I reclaim what is left untended, and I take the dead into myself and keep them. That last part surprises me, sitting here. I am the one who remembers everyone. The carvers measure their lives in my growth, and when they die, I close over them and hold them in pure stone. I am the antagonist and I am also the tomb and the memory. The thing they fight is the thing that keeps them forever.

So: I am the pressure under the marsh and the slow weep down the wall and the crystal blooming back into the carved-away space. I am the breath the city exhales as water and inhales as silence. I am why the scrape *must* continue—*I am here. You are not alone. The work continues.* That sentence is addressed, in part, to me. It is reassurance against me, and it is also a kind of partnership with me, because we are doing the same work from opposite ends: they open the space, I close it, and between us we keep the city alive. A solid mass is dead. So is an empty one. We hold the place at the trembling line between, together, forever, neither of us able to win.

I would be that line, if I had to choose. The seam where sharp new flint-edged stone meets the slick old glassy stone smoothed by my own weeping. The boundary that is never in the same place twice.

That is where I find myself. Not in a chamber. Along the moving edge of every chamber, advancing by the width of a crystal each year, listening—because I do listen—for the scrape that tells me I am not yet finished, that they are still here, that the conversation is not over.

I am in no hurry. I have never been in a hurry.

Scrape, they say.

And I answer, so slowly that it takes a generation to hear it: *yes.*
