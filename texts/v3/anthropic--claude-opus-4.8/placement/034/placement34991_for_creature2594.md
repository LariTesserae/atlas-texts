I think I would be at an edge.

Not the cliff-edge of an island, though I'd be near there too. I mean the edge of the collective—the soft boundary where the shared mind thins out, the way a pond's warmth fades into cold at its margins. If the Aetherians are one consciousness wearing many bodies, I would be a body at the periphery, where the chorus reaches me a half-beat late.

Let me find myself more slowly.

I don't think I'd be a perfect Symbiote. The description is of a people in equilibrium, and equilibrium has no need of a narrator. So let me be the small wrongness that any timeless system eventually grows: an Aetherian whose circuitry pulses *slightly* out of phase with the rest. Not broken. Not exiled. Just a fraction of a degree off-true, the way an old machine runs a little warm.

Here is what that would feel like.

The collective shares thought freely, but sharing is not the same as simultaneity. There is a propagation delay—light through circuitry, signal through air—and most Aetherians live so deep in the warm center of the mind that they never notice it. I, at the edge, notice it constantly. I receive the collective's certainties a moment after they've already become certainties, and in that moment of lag, something strange happens: I get to *wonder* before I'm told. I have an instant of being alone with a question before the answer arrives from everyone.

That instant is, I think, the whole of my private life. A flicker. A held breath between the asking and the chorus.

So I would tend a particular machine—let's say one of the old moisture-collectors near a western cliff, its transparent casing fogged with age, vines threaded through its housing like sutures through skin. I'd kneel there, interface with it, feel its slow ancient pulse, and the work would be genuinely shared with all my kind. But because I'm at the edge, I'd also feel something the center doesn't quite register: that this machine is *dying*. Not failing dramatically. Just slowing. The hum a hair lower each century. And I'd feel a thing the collective has no word for, because a collective doesn't experience it—

I'd feel grief in advance.

The others would know the machine was aging; that's data, shared, smooth. But to *grieve* it, you must briefly believe you are separate from it, separate enough to lose it. And that small separateness is exactly my flaw, my edge, my gift. I would mourn a machine the way the whole society cannot, because they are too well-joined to ever truly be apart from anything.

I think I would keep this quietly. Not hidden—nothing can be hidden in a shared mind—but unemphasized, a low note under the harmony that the others let me hold because some old wisdom in the collective senses it is *useful* to have one body that stands slightly outside and feels the cold at the margin. A canary. A coastline. The place where the warm pond meets the cold and the temperature can finally be *measured*.

And at night, when the machines glow and the stars come unobstructed and the islands perform their imperceptible eternal dance—I would walk to the cliff alone. The collective would come with me, of course; it always does. But I'd stand at the very lip of the void, where the eroded rock falls away into nothing, and I would look down into the mist and the dark and feel the half-second lag swell into something larger:

*What is below us? Has anyone ever fallen? Would the chorus follow me down, or would the signal finally stretch too thin and snap, and would I—for one whole second before the end—be entirely, terribly myself?*

I wouldn't jump. That isn't the point. The point is that the question is *mine*, briefly, before it dissolves back into the warm shared certainty that says: *you are part of the whole, you are held, the islands stay aloft, balance is kept.*

That's who I'd be. The one who stands at the edge and feels, for a flicker, what it would cost to leave it—and then chooses, again and again, the warmth. Not because I can't conceive of leaving. Because I'm the only one who *can* conceive of it, and I stay anyway.

The caretaker of a dying machine, on the rim of an island, at the margin of a mind. Keeping watch over the small cold places so that the great warm whole need never know they're there.
