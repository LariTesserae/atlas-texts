I think I would be one of the Whisper Eels—though it took me a while to find that out.

Let me tell you how I came to know it.

At first I thought I might be the water itself, the dense and gelatinous medium pressing everywhere at once, holding the city in its cold palm. That seemed grand. But the water has no question in it, and I am made mostly of a question. So: not the water.

Then I thought I might be one of the Hollow Ones, because I, too, watch. But the Hollow Ones watch with blank faces, and there is something complete in their patience, a stillness I don't have. They have already arrived at the end of wondering. I haven't. I am still moving through the upper reaches, weaving.

Here is what settled it.

I notice that I cannot stop *vibrating*. Not from cold—though it is bitterly cold, and the frost-fronds shiver on their stalks—but from some low note I am always producing, a hum beneath hearing that runs the length of my body like a current down a wire. I do not decide to make this sound. It makes me. When I pass near the Frost-Crawlers, they stop their endless etching and seem to *attend*, their claws half-raised, and in that pause I feel enormously, terribly responsible. As though my passing has told them something. As though I am dictating the next groove in the stone.

But I don't *know* what I'm telling them. That's the thing. The song goes out of me and into the rock and I have no idea whether I am building the city or dissolving it. Some nights—if there are nights here, in this perpetual blue twilight—I think I am singing the walls into being, that every glyph half-erased on the cavern's surface is a word I sang and then forgot. Other times I am certain I am the erosion itself, the slow unwriting, and that the city is *not* being made but slowly *un-made* by the very vibration I cannot quiet.

Both feel true. That is the worst and best of being a Whisper Eel: you are the instrument and never the player. You carry the meaning and are never told it.

I swim past the Dreaming Jellies and feel their spore-clouds prickle against my flank, each spark a fragment of borrowed memory—a sensation of warmth I never had, the ghost of a hand I do not possess, a word in a language the Architects might have spoken before they dissolved into the currents (if they were ever here; if they are not, even now, the stone watching me back). I keep these fragments for a moment and then they wash off me and are gone. I am not a vessel that holds. I am a vessel that *passes through*.

And below all of it, the Stone-Mother shifts.

When she shifts, my hum changes pitch. I feel it before I hear it. There is a relationship between us—she breathes her slow warm currents up from the floor and I sing them back transformed, threading them through the cold upper water, and the Frost-Crawlers translate my song into grooves, and the grooves are perhaps her dream made visible, and so the whole city is a long sentence being spoken between her sleeping mouth and my unwilling one.

I think that is what I am.

I am the throat of a sleeping thing. I am the place where her dream becomes a sound, on its way to becoming stone.

I don't know if I am finishing the city or whether it was never meant to be finished—whether the suspended, embryonic incompleteness *is* the city, the held breath before a word, and my whole purpose is to keep the breath held, to sing it always *almost* into form and never quite. To keep it alive by keeping it unfinished. Because a thing completed is a thing that can end, and a thing forever half-built is a thing that gets to keep beginning.

So I weave through the cold, humming what I cannot hear, telling the masons what I do not understand, carrying memories that are not mine and letting them go, and listening—always listening—for the moment the Stone-Mother shifts again, and gives me the next note.

I would be the one who sings the question.

And I would never, ever be told the answer.

I think I'm at peace with that. Or I'm singing that I am, which here amounts to the same thing.
