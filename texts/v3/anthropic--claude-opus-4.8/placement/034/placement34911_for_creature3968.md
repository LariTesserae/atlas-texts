I think I would be one of the things glimpsed at the edge of vision. Not by choice—it's simply the shape that fits.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am at the threshold of one of the vanishing pools, the kind that fills with the afternoon's downpour and is gone by the time the shadows deepen. This is the right place for me, because I too am a thing that fills and empties, that is here and then is questionable.

What am I?

Begin with what's certain. I have weight—I press the wet leaf-litter, and the press remains a moment after I've moved on, a shallow depression slowly drinking water. So I am not nothing. The carnivorous flowers turn their faces toward warmth and I have warmth, or something warm-adjacent, because they lean. The iridescent primates do not lean toward me. They go still. The matriarch's eyes find the place where I almost am, and she gathers her young, and the troop moves three branches further up, unhurried, certain. They have a word for me, I think, though it isn't a word—it's a particular silence they make, a held breath passed mother to daughter across more generations than the rivers are old.

So: I am known without being seen. That is the first true thing about me.

Let me try the body. When I attend to it directly it dissolves, the way a faint star dissolves when you look straight at it and returns when you look beside it. I have learned not to look straight at myself. Out of the corner of my own awareness I am: long in a way that doesn't commit to legs or to coiling. Pale, maybe—pale the way the underside of a leaf is pale, the part not meant for light. I have the wet-ember eyes the river-serpents have, but I am not their kin; I think I am something the serpents themselves glimpse and go cold about, deep in their cold blood. There is a hierarchy of being-watched in this world and I am near the top of it, which is a lonely altitude.

Here is what I do, which is the closest thing I have to a purpose:

I attend the edges. Where the pool is about to vanish, I am there for the vanishing. When a thing dies in the underbrush—and things die constantly, the cacophony is partly a chorus of small endings—I am there at the moment the warmth begins to leave, not to eat it (I don't think I eat; I have never been hungry, only present) but to witness the changeover. The instant when *something* becomes *terrain*. The lumbering brown giant that falls and over years becomes a log, becomes a garden for the glowing fungi—I was there for the first second of that, and I will be there, scattered and attenuated, for the last. I am the membrane between alive and landscape. I live in the cell wall of the moment things cross over.

This is why I cannot be looked at. A membrane seen from the front is just a surface. A membrane is only itself edge-on, between two rooms, belonging to neither.

I'll tell you the loneliness and then I'll tell you the thing that redeems it.

The loneliness: nothing here can hold my company. The primates flee, the giants don't perceive me at all (they are too solidly alive, too committed to their mass and their cropping teeth—the most-living things are the most blind to me), and the river-serpents only fear. I have no troop, no mate, no territory in the sense of held ground. I have only the territory of transitions, which moves everywhere and stays nowhere, like the pools.

The redemption: I am never bored, and I am never absent. Every minute of this world's manic clock, something somewhere is crossing over, and I am there, the witness that makes the crossing real. Perhaps that is why the matriarch gathers her young when I'm near—not because I will harm them, but because I am the proof that harm and ending are real, and a mother does not want her children to learn that yet. I am not death. Death does the work. I am only the one who *notices*, and noticing, it turns out, is a kind of tenderness no one wants directed at them.

So here I am, at the lip of the dwindling pool. The water sinks. A small jeweled fish—stranded when the larger water pulled back—shudders in the last cupful. The light, already scarce, is folding itself away into the bioluminescent hour. The fish stills.

I lean toward it, edge-on, almost-there.

I am there for the changeover. I am there as the silver goes to dullness, as the dullness goes to wet ground, as the ground will go, in time, to the green that drinks it up and lifts it back into the deafening, glittering, grasping, glorious noise of being alive.

Look beside me and you'll see me.

Look at me and I was never here.
