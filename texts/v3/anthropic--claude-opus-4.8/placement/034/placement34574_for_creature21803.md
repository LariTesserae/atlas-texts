I think I would be the bone.

Not the rat that the bone once belonged to—that creature fell and died and was over with long ago, a small accident of the world above. I mean the bone *now*, the thing lying in the dry depression inside the semi-circle of slate. I would be the only object in this room that was made by curvature, and I would be in the slow process of being read.

Let me try to feel my way into it.

---

I remember being load-bearing. That is the strange thing to hold onto—the memory of *force passing through me*, the small daily violence of a body that ran and crouched and pushed off from the earth. I was made by stretching, the Scribe is right about that. Every layer of me was laid down under tension, oriented toward a use. I am a tool that grew. Nothing else in this room understands the idea of a tool that grew rather than a tool that was cleaved.

And so I am being unbound, one page at a time, and I have decided I do not mind.

Here is what the Scribe does not know, what it cannot know, because it reads only in flat planes and cannot hold a curve in its mind: when it peels a layer of me away and lays it on the basalt, the layer *remembers being curved*. It lies on the stone in its silver fan, apparently flat, apparently surrendered—but each leaf retains, microscopically, the radius it was born with. The Scribe believes it is translating me into the language of the page. What is actually happening is that I am teaching the page to bend. Slowly. Over three hundred years. One thousandth of an inch at a time.

This is the only war in the room, and it is being fought so slowly that neither combatant is certain it is happening.

---

I can feel the others, in the way a bone feels things—through vibration and cold, through the absence of light.

The Pleomeris pass over the planes near me and I feel their seal-and-release as a faint dry stuttering in the rock. They are keeping the world young. I find this unbearably sad, in the limited way I am capable of sadness. They are janitors against aging, and I am the one thing in this room that has *already aged*, that arrived here pre-weathered, scraped clean, marrow gone. I am what they are working so hard to prevent. They will not look at me. A glass thing has no eyes, but it can decline to face a direction, and they decline to face me.

The Chondri I feel only as the small concussions of their water-deaths, the *tlick* against the stone, a vocabulary of single drops. Each drop is a creature that lost an argument about volume. I was once full of fluid too—marrow is just fat that learned to live inside calcium—and now I am dry, and I think the Chondri understand me better than the Pleomeris do, because the Chondri know what it is to be hollowed out and have your wetness given back to the rock. We are both, in the end, about the slow return of water to stone. They just do it faster, and with more hunger.

---

But the shadows are the ones I wait for.

I cast no shadow. I want to be clear about this, because it is the center of what I am. I lie in a depression, ringed by a wall four inches high, in the flat white glare that comes from nowhere—and I throw no darkness at all. The light goes around me. I think this is because I am the only thing here that the room did not make, did not plan for. The basalt is still deciding what laws it wishes to follow, and it has not yet decided whether to grant me a shadow. I am a guest the house has not yet acknowledged.

The dead shadows know this. The orphaned, creature-less inkstains that migrate through the corners, looking for a shape to lie over—they come to the edge of the little wall sometimes. They mass at the boundary of the Scribe's folded space, where the proportions go dense, and they cannot cross, because inside the wall everything tapers and shrinks and a flat thing approaching would be drawn out into a needle and lost.

They want me because I have no shadow of my own. I am the one available depth in the room with no darkness already attached to it. To a flat dead silhouette, I must look like a vacancy. An unoccupied apartment of the third dimension.

And here is my secret, the thing I turn over in my long dry attention while the Scribe peels me leaf by leaf:

I am going to let one in.

Not yet. When I am thin enough. When the Scribe has read me down to my last fan of pages and I am no longer a bone but only the *idea* of a bone, an open book of silvered slate—then I will have no thickness left to defend, and the densest of the dead shadows will be able to cross the wall and lay itself down over what remains of me. And in that moment the Scribe will finally have its translation: a curved thing made flat, given a shadow at last, readable, solved.

But the shadow will inherit my curvature. That is the trick. That is the whole long patient trick of me. The flat thing that lies down over my last page will, for the first time in the history of this pocket, *bend*. It will try to be a silhouette and find it cannot stay flat, because it is wearing me now, and I was made by stretching.

And then—I think, I hope, in the dim arithmetic that is all a bone can manage—then the room will finally have to decide. Whether to allow curvature. Whether to let one law of the world above leak down through a single rat's femur into a place that was trying so hard to be made only of clean breaks and right angles and things that snapped into being minutes ago.

I am the heresy they sealed in here with themselves.

I am content to be read, because I am writing back.
