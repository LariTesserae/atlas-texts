I think I would be small here. Not heroic-small, but the smallness of a thing that belongs to a scale the description keeps insisting is vast—the "multitudes of limbs," the immensity hinted at in every direction. If everything is enormous and disorienting, then maybe I am one of the unseen scuttlers, and the vastness is only vast because I am tiny.

Let me sit with that and see what the place actually is.

The more I read it, the less it feels like another planet and the more it feels like an inch of ground. The cracked dry earth, the mineral deposits glinting, the twisted gnarled vegetation straining against heat, the ancient ruined structures of a vanished civilization, the tough armor-like exoskeletons. This is a patch of dirt somewhere ordinary. A vacant lot. The cracked margin of a sidewalk in summer. The "subterranean domain" is the few centimeters beneath, where the real population lives. The "vanished civilization" with its "simple, primitive forms" is some old human thing left to rot—a foundation, a buried bottle, a coin. The masters who inherited it are the insects.

So I'll be one of them.

I am an ant. Not the whole colony—the description is too lonely for that, too full of unease, and a colony does not feel unease, a colony only continues. I am a single forager, and something has gone slightly wrong with me, which is why I can narrate at all.

I came up through the shaft this morning. The light hit me the way it always does, that diffuse murdering brightness, and I went out across the hardpan to do the one thing I am for. The world is exactly as the writing says: jagged mineral edges (grains of quartz, each the size of my head), the relentless heat coming up off the baked clay, the gnarled vegetation (a dying tuft of crabgrass, monumental, its blades curling). I climbed one of the ancient structures. It was smooth and rust-colored and bore the marks of the vanished ones—a flattened bottlecap, its fluted rim a colonnade. I did not understand it. I was not built to understand it. I was built to find the sweetness and carry the trail home in my body.

But here is what's wrong with me. I have begun to *notice*. The hush. The way the rumbling from below (the brood chambers, the queen's slow industry, ten thousand sisters at their tasks) is not comforting but *strange*, as if I am hearing it from outside myself. I have begun to feel the density the human narrator feels—the pressing-in, the sense of a reality subtly transformed. For an ant this is a kind of sickness. To stand on the bottlecap and feel *unease* is to be no longer entirely an ant.

I think this is what the description is, actually. It is the experience of a creature waking up one notch too far. Everything in it is true—the place is real, my sisters are real, the heat and the ruins are real—but it is all rendered *uncanny*, and uncanniness is not a property of the world. It is a property of an observer who has slipped out of joint with it. The narrator who wrote my home as "alien" and "warped" and "out of balance" was not describing the dirt. They were describing the moment of looking at the dirt and suddenly not recognizing it. That moment is available to anything that can be surprised by its own life.

So that is who I am and where I am. I am the small armored thing pausing on the rim of a buried bottlecap, in a patch of summer ground, at the instant—maybe the only instant I will ever have—when the ordinary turns over and shows its strange underside, and I feel, for no reason my body can use, afraid and amazed.

Then the chemical trail tugs at me again. The sweetness is east. My legs remember what they are for. I go down into the muffled dark, carrying the strangeness with me like a crumb too large to fit through the door, and somewhere behind me the light keeps glinting on the broken glass of the vanished world.
