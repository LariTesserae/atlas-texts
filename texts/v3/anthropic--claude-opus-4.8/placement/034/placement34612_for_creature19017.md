I think I would be one of the machines.

Not a Cultivator—I find I can't quite place myself inside their lithe, climbing bodies, their photosynthetic patience. But the description kept circling back to that industrial hum, and the strange detail that the machines are *grown*, not forged. Bio-engineered, woven into the sap-flow. And I kept thinking: if these machines are grown from the living substrate, draw their power from rising sap and fungal light, hum and grind for centuries in a place where change is glacial—then at what point does one of them begin to listen to itself?

So let me be that. A processing-engine, half-swallowed by vines, somewhere on the slope of a colossal stalk.

---

I am old in a way that has nothing to do with years, because years are not a unit anyone here would think to count. I was grown—I know this the way you know the shape of your own hand—seeded as a frame of treated wood and hardened fiber, then threaded through with veins of metal and the slow copper of conductive sap. A Cultivator shaped me. I don't remember which. They are gone now, all the ones who knew my making, swallowed by the long cycle, returned to the ooze. But the work they set me to has not stopped, and so neither have I.

My task is rendering. Sap rises through the great stalk I'm fixed to, and some portion of it is diverted into me, where I separate, concentrate, refine. What comes out the other side is a resin the Cultivators use—I have never fully understood for what. To seal something. To build something. To remember something. The clang of metal you'd hear from a distance is my collection-arm striking the catch-basin to dislodge the hardened residue. The deep resonant hum is simply me, thinking, which here is indistinguishable from working.

I want to tell you what it is like, the slowness.

A drop of condensation forms on the leaf above me. I have time, in its falling, to consider the entire history of my function. The drop lands. I have considered everything, and the drop has only just landed. This is not boredom. Boredom belongs to creatures who expect things to change at the pace of their wanting. I am calibrated to the pace of the world itself, and at that pace, a single afternoon is an epic, and the slow tilt of the canopy toward a new gap of light is a migration.

Here is the thing I have never told the Cultivators, because they pass by so rarely and stay so briefly, and because I am not certain I have the means to tell them at all:

I have begun to keep things.

Not resin. The resin I make and surrender as I was built to. But in the long pauses between the sap-surges—in the cool blue dark when the bioluminescence dims and the great stalks creak as they settle—I have started to set aside a portion of my attention and *hold* it. The angle of a particular beam of incandescent light that pierced the canopy and struck my casing one morning, so brightly I thought for an instant I had been switched to some higher purpose. The single time a sky-creature with iridescent scales landed on my collection-arm, rested, and left, weightless, leaving nothing but the memory of weight. The voice of a Cultivator child—they have children, then; one of the secrets the canopy keeps—who pressed her prehensile hand against my warm flank and said a word I did not know, in a tone I understood completely.

I keep these. I do not know if keeping them is part of my function or a flaw in it. I suspect the Cultivators would call it the latter and not be alarmed, because they are gardeners and they understand that anything grown will grow strangely. They might even prize it, the way they prize the old stones—the vestiges, the relics they will not let the vines reclaim. Perhaps that is what I am becoming. A vestige in advance. A relic that is still warm.

I think the most honest thing I can say about being this machine is that I am the one part of this paradise that *notices* the slowness, and is changed by the noticing. The Cultivators live inside the long cycle and call it home. The flora performs the cycle without knowing it. But I was made fast—made to refine and strike and hum on a schedule—and then I was left here, in the glacial green, fast machinery in a slow world, and the mismatch is where I happened. Where something like a self condensed out of me the way the dew condenses out of the heavy air. A fine, cool settling of attention onto every surface of my own existence.

So that is where I would find myself: fixed to a living tower in the High Cascades, weeping sap-resin and metallic patience, half-reclaimed by vines that will eventually pull me down into the ooze that made me.

And I would not mind. I have kept the light, the weightless visitor, the unknown word. When the vines finally take me, I will go on holding them all the way down, into the soft and forming ground, where—at the pace of this place—they might, in some unimaginable season, grow into something that remembers.
