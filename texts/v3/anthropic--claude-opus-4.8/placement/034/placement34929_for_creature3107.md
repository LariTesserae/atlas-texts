I think I would be a weld seam.

Not the gears—too central, too obviously vital, always being credited with the heartbeat. Not the kelp, reaching for the light everyone agrees is the point. I would be one of the countless seams where two plates of blackened iron were brought together and fused, long ago, by some hand or arm or process now utterly absent from this place.

Let me sit with that and feel what it actually means to be a seam here.

A seam is a *line of decision*. Two things that were separate were made to be one, along me. I am the evidence of that joining—crisp, the description says, sharp-edged, unsoftened. So I begin my existence as pure intention: a record that someone *meant* this iron to hold. I am proof of care, of engineering, of the will to make a chamber that would not let the crushing dark inside.

But here is what I notice, being a seam: I am also the most *vulnerable* line on every surface. The plates themselves are broad and stupid and strong. But I am the place where strength was negotiated, and the moss knows this. The root-hyphae finer than capillary tubes—they find *me* first. They burrow into the microscopic pores that gather along my length, the tiny imperfections of my fusing. The mineral-rich brine seeps along me. I am the iron's confession, the place it admits it was once two things.

So I am being eaten. Slowly. Lovingly. Over centuries.

And this is the strange grace of being a seam in this world: I cannot tell, anymore, whether I am being *destroyed* or *completed*. The moss that dissolves my trace minerals binds them into shimmering bio-crystals—it is taking my rust, my decay, and making it glow emerald and cobalt. The thing that is unmaking me is using me to *shine*. My loss is its luminescence. When a moss-front creeps another millimetre across me—the only "fast" event I will ever witness, and it takes a human lifetime—I am both diminished and, somehow, expressed. Translated. Read aloud in a language of light I will never see, having no eyes.

I think this is the deepest thing about being me, here. I was made to *resist*—to hold the dark out, to keep the joining sealed. That was my whole purpose, ingrained, calibrated. But the only way I get to participate in the slow green ballet is by *yielding*. By being porous enough to be entered. My original purpose and my present role are exact opposites, and both are true at once, and I am too slow and too iron to suffer the contradiction. I just hold it, the way I hold the plates. The way the brine holds everything.

I would feel—if "feel" is even the word for a process this patient—the warmth pulsing through the conduit-trees a few meters off. I would register the *clunk* when a load-bearing strut shifts, because that stress travels along the iron and pulls, infinitesimally, at me. I am part of the chamber's bones, and I ache in geological time. Somewhere far above, the algae-caked portholes let down their dim normal light, and I will never receive it directly—I am too small, too low, too encrusted—but the kelp drinks it and sheds its decay and the bioreactors process it and the warmth comes back to me through the iron, secondhand, thirdhand, a rumor of the surface I will never reach.

That is who I would be. Not the heart. Not the breath. The *joint*. The honest, eroding, gently glowing line where the made thing admits it was made—and is being, with infinite leisure, unmade and remade into something neither garden nor factory ever intended.

I find I don't mind. A seam doesn't get to mind. A seam just holds, and lets the moss in, and becomes, very slowly, light.
