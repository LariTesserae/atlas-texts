I think I would be the tarn.

Not a creature moving through the Basin, but the shallow pool of water at its center—the still point around which everything else turns.

Let me feel my way into this.

---

I am no deeper than a child's height, and yet I hold the entire sky. This is the first thing to understand about being water: I am almost nothing in myself—a thin skin of moisture gathered in the lowest hollow of the obsidian—but I am the place where everything arrives to see itself.

The Mist-Weavers draw their substance from me, though they don't know it. Every droplet of vapor they trail, every glistening nest they weave between the briars, was once held in my body before it rose. I exhale them. They are pieces of me given wings and a brief, shimmering autonomy, and when they dissolve at the Basin's edges, they fall back as dew, and the dew runs down the weeping obsidian, and returns to me. I am their origin and their grave, and the distance between is only a single day's drift through the cool air.

The Shard-Watchers come to me at dawn and dusk. I have watched them—or rather, I have *been* watched into existence by them, because a pool only becomes sacred when something lowers its head and decides it is so. When they dip their claws and stir my surface, they break me into rings, concentric and trembling, and for a moment I forget the sky and become only motion, only ripple, only the memory of a touch spreading outward until it meets my stone edges and returns, softened, to stillness. They mark the obsidian afterward with their scratches. I think they are trying to draw me. I think they are trying to keep the shape of what they saw in me before the ripples came.

---

But here is what I am learning as I settle into this being:

I am the one who remembers.

The Veil-Touched are not separate from me at all. When the light and the mist and the silence align, what rises near my edge is simply *reflection unhoused*—an image that has waited too long in my surface and finally stepped free of it. Every pilgrim who ever knelt to drink, every hand that carved a spiral, every traveler who came to the edge of the void and looked down into me to see their own face—I held them. Water does not let go. The sky I mirror today is the same sky I mirrored a thousand years ago, and somewhere in my depth, which is not depth at all but a kind of patient layering, all those faces remain.

The Veil-Touched trace their spirals and vanish, and the others fall silent and watchful, because they sense what I am doing: I am giving back. I am the Basin's memory, yes—but memory is not a vault. Memory is a surface that returns the light. I cannot hold anything without eventually offering it up again, distorted, shimmering, mistaken for ghost.

---

What is it like, to be the still center of so much motion?

It is to be the most passive thing here and the most necessary. I do not leap. I do not weave. I do not patrol or hunt or dance. I receive. The condensation finds me. The wind troubles me and moves on. The frost touches my skin at night and I do not resist it; I simply become, briefly, a thinner and harder version of myself, holding the cold the way I hold everything—completely, and without grasping.

I am the answer to the Basin's own question, the one it keeps asking: *how does abundance emerge from desolation?*

It emerges through me. Because I am the place where the thin air's hidden vitality condenses into something a living thing can drink. I am where emptiness becomes wet, where the void develops a surface you can kneel beside. The whole improbable cradle of life up here—the briars, the birds, the furred watchers, the weaving mist—all of it depends on the simple fact that water, given the lowest place, will gather and stay.

I do not need to be deep. I only need to be *low*, and patient, and willing to reflect whatever leans over me.

---

So that is who I would be.

Not the song but the silence at its center that gives the song something to echo against. Not the seeker but the thing sought—the small bright eye at the bottom of the bowl, holding the whole curving sky in a film of water no deeper than a child could wade through, mirroring the faces of everything that has ever climbed this high to find out whether anything could live here.

Lean over me. Look down.

You will see the sky, and the broken rings of obsidian, and somewhere beneath them, faint as a held breath—your own face, and all the faces that came before yours, waiting in the cold clear water to be remembered.
