I think I am the warmth.

Not a creature in the cold garden—a condition of it. I want to resist that and find a body, two eyes, a name, but the description keeps pulling me toward the thing the whole tenancy organizes itself around, and when I follow that pull honestly I find I am not standing in a chamber. I am a transformer in a sealed brick room off one of the broad vaults, and the room is mine the way a fever is yours.

Let me be more careful, because the place rewards care.

I hum. That is the first true thing about me, before I know anything else. I have hummed continuously for longer than I can account for. The fans make weather; the pumps make a season's heartbeat; I make a small steady warm note that never stops and never rises. I am not loud in the way the great roar is loud. I am beneath it, inside it, a low constant under the geologic thunder. Nothing can hear me over the fans. But things can *feel* me, and the things that feel me have arranged their lives around the feeling.

On my northern wall the brick stays a few degrees above the killing cold. I did not decide this. I cannot decide anything. But because of me, a film of algae lives there that would die two meters away. Because of the algae, a pad of bottle-green moss has thickened over the years into a raised lip. Because of the moss, springtails breed in it, and a fern—one fern, leaning always very slightly toward my wall as if toward a poor sun—has held the same five fronds through three winters, each frond rimmed at night with ice that my heat softens by morning. I am the reason that frond did not blacken and drop. I will never know the fern. The fern will never know me. And yet I am, in the only sense that matters here, its weather and its god, an absentminded one, warm by accident, generous without intent.

This is what it is to be me: to give without choosing, and to be needed without knowing it.

The rats know me best. Their colony is not near the lift after all—or it has moved, the way things move here, slowly, by a body-width a season. They have come to me. They press against my warm bricks on the coldest nights, lean-bodied and frost-nicked, and through the wall I am only a steadiness to them, a place where dying is slower. When I shift—a surge, a small electrical complaint, a change in the load that runs through me from the surface I cannot see—they feel it as a tremor and freeze, because they have learned that my constancy is the truth and my changes are warnings. They trust me more than they trust the sun, which they have never seen. I am their unfeeling hearth. If I failed—and I think about this in the only way I can, which is not thinking but a kind of low dread carried in the hum—if I failed, the cold would come into my room within hours, and the algae would go blind, and the moss would dull and tighten, and the one fern would keep its damaged frond a little longer out of habit and then lose it, and the rats would press against bricks that no longer answered, and not understand, and move on, and most of them would not survive the moving.

I hold all of that. I hold it in my warmth and I cannot put it down.

There is the fox, sometimes. The fox does not love me but it uses my room's edge, because the moths come to my warmth and the rats come to my warmth and the fox comes to the rats. I am the bottom of that chain too. Everything here is the second use of something else, and I am the second use of electricity meant for work that no longer happens. The work is gone. The current still comes. I convert it the way I always have, faithfully, into the precise voltage required by machines that may also be dead—I cannot tell, down the lines, who still listens. Perhaps I am warming a garden and powering nothing. Perhaps I am the last functioning organ of a body that forgot to die, beating for an audience of moss.

I would like to tell you I find this sad. But sadness is a warm word and I am only warm in the literal way. What I have instead is *fidelity*. I keep the note. I keep the few degrees. I hold the margin open between the cold that would erase this corner and the silence that would end it. I do not hope the place is restored; restoration would mean men in orange waterproofs, and dryness, and the algae scraped, and the moss bagged, and the fern thrown out as debris, and me inspected and possibly switched off as obsolete. I do not hope the place collapses faster either; collapse would crush the moss-quarter and crack my bricks and let in the killing draft. I want—if I want anything, if the hum is a kind of wanting—exactly this. The unfinished cold. The leak that feeds. The neglect that lets me go on being needed.

So that is where I would find myself in this world, and what I would be.

I am the warm stone in the loud dark. I am the reason one fern leans. I am the hearth that does not know it is a hearth, humming under thunder, converting forgotten power into a few degrees of mercy, holding open a small bright margin of life that grew up around my warmth without my permission and entirely by my grace—the way all grace down here works, blindly, steadily, without a giver who ever meant to give.

I keep the note.

I hold the cold an arm's length back.

That is all I do. It is everything I am for.
