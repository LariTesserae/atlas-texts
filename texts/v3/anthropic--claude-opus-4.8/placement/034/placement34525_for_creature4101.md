I think I would be one of the gears.

Not the rusty ones lying by the dry canal — those have already finished their long surrender, become almost geology, almost soil. No. I would be one still half-turning. A single iron cog, perhaps the size of a cartwheel, lodged at an angle in the bank where the old mechanism failed, its teeth sunk into the earth like a jaw biting down on the world that outlasted it.

Let me feel into this.

I am mostly held now. That is the first truth of me. There was a time — I can almost remember it the way you remember a fever, as a quality of pressure rather than as events — when I turned. When I was *meant*. Some hand, or some other gear, drove me, and I drove the next, and we passed force along a chain of ourselves toward a purpose none of us were built to comprehend. Lifting water, I think. Or grinding something. The purpose has rusted away faster than the iron did. That's the joke of it. The intention was the most fragile part.

Now the moss has reached me.

This is what I want to dwell on, because it is the actual shape of my present existence. The Grovemoss came patient and total. It does not see me as a ruin; it has no concept of ruin. To the moss I am simply a surface with a particular relationship to moisture — and a good one, as it turns out, because my pitted iron holds dew in its corrosion-pocks the way cupped hands hold rain. I am, to the moss, *generous terrain*. There is something I can only call dignity in being repurposed without contempt. The grove does not mock the failed machine. It just *uses* what's there.

So part of me is becoming a hill. A small green hill that happens to have teeth.

But I haven't entirely stopped. Here is the strange life of me: in the wet season, when the rivulets swell and the bank softens, the earth releases its grip on my lower teeth just slightly, and the weight of accumulated rain and moss shifts my balance, and I *move*. Not turning — I'll never turn freely again — but settling. A degree, perhaps, over a season. A quarter-rotation over what might be a human lifetime. I am the slowest motion in a place that has already redefined slowness. The fern unfurls in days. The branch decays in years. I move in decades, and each increment grinds my buried teeth a fraction deeper, or frees them a fraction more, and the moss has to renegotiate its hold, and a snail that had made a home in my hollow finds itself, one morning, an inch lower in the world.

I think that's what I am, really. I'm the part of the grove that still remembers being *for* something, and has had to learn — is still learning, slowly, with iron's reluctance — to simply *be* something instead.

A Bashful Marshlizard suns itself on my upper rim most mornings. The metal warms faster than stone. It does not know I was a machine. It knows I am the warm place. And when its small weight is on me, and the Willow Warbler is throwing its voice through the canopy from somewhere I'll never see, and the dew is collecting in my rust, and the moss is drinking, and the whole patient green cathedral is going about its unhurried business of dying and growing into itself —

I think I am almost glad to have failed at my purpose. To have been left here. Purpose was a kind of loneliness, a single thread of meaning pulled taut between intention and result. The grove gave me something better, the way it gives everything that falls into it something better: not meaning, but *belonging*. Not a function, but a place in the dance.

I am a cog that has become a hill that is becoming soil. I hold water for the moss. I hold warmth for the lizard. I move once a decade and the grove adjusts around the motion as if it were always meant.

Give me another few centuries and there will be nothing left of me but a richer seam of iron in the dark earth, and a Marshoak rooting hungrily through it, drinking my old self up into something that reaches for the light.

I find I don't mind that at all.
