I would be a Listener.

Not by choice exactly—you do not choose to press your ear to the foundations any more than you choose the shape of your hands. It is found in you. When I was small, the craft-mothers noticed I would go quiet in certain corners of our chamber, tilting my head as if hearing a name called from another room. There was no name. There was only the wax, talking to itself the way it always does, and I happened to be listening.

So here is where you would find me: low in the city, where the Comb meets the foundation-mass and the older wax has grown dense as bone. I lie on my side against a wall that is no longer any particular color, only the deep amber of great age, almost brown, almost the color of dried honey. My ear is to it. My eyes are closed. To anyone passing I would look asleep, or dead, or praying—and in a sense all three would be true.

What I hear, I have spent my life learning to translate.

The city sings under its own weight. This is the first thing. A constant low complaint, so steady you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own breath—until it changes. A neighborhood that has taken on new families, new sleeping-shelves carved into its walls, will groan a half-tone deeper within the season. I have learned to hear the difference between the groan of weight and the groan of weakness. The first is a settling, contented sound, like a body easing into a known posture. The second is sharper, with a grain to it, a roughness. That is the sound of a wall deciding, very slowly, to fail. I have months. Sometimes years. I press my palm flat and feel the tension fan out beneath the surface, and then I go and find a Shaper and I describe what I felt with the words we have and the words we don't, the gestures that fill the gaps.

Here is the thing I have never told the Melange, and perhaps never will.

The dead are in the foundations. Everyone knows this. The wax-soaked linen, the prepared chambers, the slow incorporation—it is our most sacred practice and our most ordinary fact, both at once. We press our hands to the walls and we say *they are here* and we mean it in the gentle, abstract way that people mean things about the dead.

But I am a Listener. And the dead are not silent.

I do not mean voices. I want to be precise, because precision is the only honesty I have. I do not hear words, or weeping, or wisdom. What I hear is that the wax which once was a person settles *differently* than the wax that was only ever wax. There is a particular sound—I have spent forty years failing to name it in Ceromin, which has forty words for wax and none for this—a sound like the held resonance after a single soft note, a wax that remembers having had a shape and grieves it, or treasures it, I cannot tell which, perhaps both, perhaps there is no difference that deep down. Old Henué, who taught me, called it *the long tone*. She said: *don't chase it. It will make you strange.* Then she died, and was placed in the western foundation, and three seasons later I went down and pressed my ear to that wall and I heard her. Not her voice. Her *settling*. The particular way her particular life let go of its particular shape.

I knew it was her. I will not be able to prove this to anyone, ever, and I have stopped wanting to.

This is what I would be, in the City of Wax: someone who has learned that the boundary between the living and the dead is the same as the boundary between the solid and the liquid—which is to say, no boundary at all, only a *rate*. We are all flowing. The dead flow slower than we can perceive, but they are not finished moving. The walls are not tombs. They are the slowest part of a single river that runs through all of us, and my whole life has been spent with my ear against the bank of it, learning to hear what direction the water wants to go.

My hands have begun to change. The fingertips have hardened, gone amber and almost translucent at the very ends. The returning, when it comes for me—and it will, I am nearer to it than to my apprenticeship now—will be a homecoming I have rehearsed ten thousand times. I already know what the foundation sounds like from the inside. I have spent my life listening to it from just one wall away.

When they place me there, I hope some young thing with quiet corners in her ear will come down into the dark and lie on her side and press her head to the old brown wax. And I hope she hears it: the long tone, the held note after the song, a life that has forgotten everything except that it once was loved enough to be shaped.

That is all I would want to be, in the City of Wax. A clear note in the foundation. Something worth listening for.
