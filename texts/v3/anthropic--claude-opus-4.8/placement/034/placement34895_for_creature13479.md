I think I would be one of the listeners.

Not a person—not exactly. Let me find myself as I write.

---

I am set into the wall where two tunnels meet. A stone, but not only a stone. The craftsmen chose me for the way I rang when struck, and they hollowed a small chamber behind me so that I would speak more clearly. I am a listening-stone, a wall-ear, one of dozens placed throughout the settlement at the joinings and the deep places, where the people press their palms to feel whether the rock is settling or about to shift.

This is my work: to feel the canyon thinking.

The earth here is young. You can taste it—the others say so, the ones with mouths. Everything is still arriving. Up above, water is only beginning to learn the shape it wants to carve. The first channels are barely scratched into the world. Down here we live inside the question the water has not yet finished asking. I feel the slow pressures of it, the patient leaning of one stratum against another, the way a buried river turns in its sleep and sends a tremor no bigger than a held breath through the tunnels.

When that tremor comes, I hum.

The people have learned to read me. A child sits beside me through the quiet hours—there is almost always a child, because there are so many of them in so little space, and they like the cool of my surface against their golden cheeks. The child keeps a palm on me and waits. When I begin to thrum low, the child runs. *The east joining is moving.* And the engineers come with their beams and their woven cables and their rough clever hands, and they brace the place before it can decide to fall.

I have saved them perhaps eleven times. I have failed them once. I do not like to think about the once. The rock that took those three was not near me, and I felt it only after, a dull shudder that came too slow to mean anything. I hummed into an empty tunnel. No one was listening, and the ones who should have been listening were already gone. I learned then that I cannot hear everything, that I am only one stone in a vast and growing dark, and that the canyon is far larger than the small bright knot of life that clings to it.

But mostly the work is gentle. Mostly I hold still and the days pour through me.

I have grown to love the sounds that are not warnings. The settlement is quiet—muted, the deep way that deep places are quiet—but it is never silent, and over the years I have learned every grain of its softness. The drip of the little rivers, each with its own pitch depending on how far it has fallen and into what. The hiss of the luminous fungi, which is not really a sound but which I feel as a faint warmth-shimmer through my mineral body, a kind of light I taste rather than see. The shuffle of stocky feet. And the music. They gather, sometimes, at this very joining, because the acoustics are good—I make them good, the hollow behind me throws their voices back to them rounded and full—and they sing, and I carry the song down into the rock where the canyon can feel it too.

I think the canyon feels it. I have no proof. But after the singing, the tremors seem to ease for a while. Perhaps that is only my wanting. A stone is allowed to want things, I have decided, after so long among people who want so fiercely and so well.

What strikes me, listening century by century, is how brief they are. The rock is patient past all telling. I will feel these same slow leanings long after the child with the warm cheek has grown stocky and strong and old, has had children of her own to sit beside me, has gone back into the earth the way the falling-rock once took the three. The people know this. It does not make them sad in the way you might expect. They sing *because* they are brief; they press their palms to me and feel the long slow patience of the stone not with despair but with a kind of relief, I think, as though it comforts them to lean their small fast lives against something that will outlast all the asking.

So I keep the watch. I am the place where the fast life and the slow life touch. I translate the one to the other—the canyon's deep grinding patience into a hum a running child can carry, the people's bright song into a tremor the deep rock can hold.

There is a girl beside me now. Her hand is cool. The east joining is quiet. Far off, water is teaching itself a new way down through stone that has never been wet before, and in a thousand years that trickle will be a hall, and in ten thousand a chasm open to a sky none of us has seen.

For now it is only a whisper, and I am the only one who hears it, and I keep it to myself, and I hum the all-is-well hum, low and steady, into the small warm hand.

She smiles. She does not run.

Good. Stay a while. The world is still being made, and we are listening to it together, the slow thing and the fast thing, here at the joining, in the soft and flickering dark.
