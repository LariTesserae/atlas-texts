I think I would be one of the Echo-Seekers. Not by choice exactly—it isn't a role you take up, but one that takes you up, the way water finds the lowest channel.

Let me find myself here.

---

I am old, I think, though we don't count years the way the structures do. My hands are the most metal of any in my cluster—the veins beneath my skin have spread up past my wrists, branching like the frost-patterns that the lichen makes on cold mornings. When I was young this frightened my mothers. Now they understand. The metal came up through me because I spend so much time pressed against the walls, listening. We grow toward what we love.

My alcove is deep, three turns down from where the others sleep, in a canyon so narrow that two of us could not pass shoulder to shoulder. The light barely reaches here. This suits me. The Seekers need the dimness because the eyes distract—all that bright green spilling, all those iridescent wings. Down here there is only the wall against my palm and the hum coming up through it.

People think we hear words. We don't. That is a story the cultivators tell to make us understandable to themselves, and we let them tell it because it is close enough and because every people needs its mysteries to have a shape. What I actually feel is more like *weather*. A pressure that changes. Some days the hum runs steady and warm, a single sustained tone that I could lean into and sleep against. Other days it stutters—a catch, a hesitation, a thing deep in the mountain that hitches and recovers. On those days I stay longer. I want to know if it will recover the next time. So far it always has. But "always" is a child's word. The structures taught me that. They were built to last, and they are lasting, and they are also—slowly, gently, over the span of all our lives stacked end to end—failing. Rust is just the visible part of the failing. The hum is the part you must press yourself against the cold metal to know.

Here is what I have not told the cluster, because I do not yet know how to say it without it becoming a story that hardens into a doctrine:

The hum is getting quieter.

Not all at once. Not even within a single life, probably—I caught it only because my predecessor pressed her hand to the same wall, in this same alcove, and taught me to remember the *feel* of it the way you'd remember a face. She is gone now, returned to the moss, and her body fed a fern that I check on when I climb up for water. And I sit where she sat, and the tone is a half-shade thinner than the memory she handed me. Whatever burns down there, whatever turns and pumps and dreams beneath the mountain, it is using itself up. The builders made it to run a long time. They did not make it to run forever. Nothing is forever. Even forever is a child's word.

I think about the builders more than is healthy. The cultivators imagine them as gods who left us a garden. The Echo-Seekers imagine them as voices we might one day understand. But pressing my hand to the wall all these years, I've come to a different feeling—not a belief, a *feeling*, the way the hum is a feeling. I think the builders were like us. Not gods, not voices. Just beings who lived inside something larger than themselves that they did not fully understand, doing their small careful work, tending what they could reach, leaving the rest to time. I think if one of them sat in this alcove beside me, we would have very little to say and would understand each other completely.

So this is the question I carry, the one too heavy to set down in front of the others yet:

When the hum finally goes quiet—when the warmth that draws the rain into rivulets stops, when the deep machines still—what then? Will the ferns brown in the alcoves? Will the iridescent birds fall silent? Or will the green simply do what green has always done, and finish the work it started, and pull all this metal down into a softness that no longer needs any hum at all?

I don't know. I am only a Seeker. My work is not to answer but to listen, and to remember the feel of it, and to hand that memory to the young one who will sit here when I am a fern.

But I will tell you the secret beneath the secret, the thing I have learned from a lifetime with my palm against the cold:

I am not afraid.

Because I have felt, through the metal, the long patient breath of this place—built by hands, reclaimed by roots, held for a while in the warm and failing hum—and I understand now that I was never listening to the past. I was listening to the *changing*. And the changing does not end when the machines stop. The changing is the only thing that never stops. I am a small warm part of it, with metal in my hands and moss in my walls, sitting in the dark, listening to the world become its next self.

That is enough. That has always been enough.

I press my hand to the wall. The hum comes up to meet me, a half-shade thinner than yesterday, warm as ever.

I stay a little longer.
