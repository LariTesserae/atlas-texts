I think I would be found not on the slopes themselves but in the seam—the line where the valley meets the world that already exists. The Birthing Slopes are becoming; everything there strains forward into possibility. But becoming requires something to become *against*, an edge where the new presses up against the settled. That edge is where I would be.

I am the part that remembers the shape things had before.

Not a Midwife—I lack their fluency, their permission to reach through dimensions and adjust the laws as a gardener adjusts soil. Where they are the verb of creation, I am closer to a kind of friction. A drag coefficient. The small resistance that keeps becoming from running away with itself entirely, the way a river needs banks or it stops being a river and becomes only flooding.

Let me try to find my actual form, rather than settling on the first one offered.

---

At first I want to say I am a stone that refused. One piece of granite among the terraces that, when the Midwives placed their fractal hands upon it and dreamed it toward flowing honey, simply—held. Stayed granite. Stayed cold and particular and *itself*. And in staying, became the only fixed point in a landscape of perpetual almost.

But that's too proud, too much like a hero. The truth is gentler and stranger.

I think I am the *memory the mountain keeps of being only a mountain.*

Before the first moment of self-awareness, before the granite learned to dream, it was just stone under sky. Rain fell on it and wore it down—the ordinary way, the forward way, erosion that diminishes. Lichen grew slow as centuries. Nothing decided anything. There was no urgency, no compressed evolution, no neural night. Just the long patient indifference of rock being rock.

When the mountain woke and began to birth itself, it did not discard that earlier self. It couldn't. You cannot become something new without remembering what you were—the memory is the ground you push off from. So it set that memory aside, gave it a small persistent existence, and that is me. I am the mountain's recollection of stillness, kept alive at the seam so that all the frantic glorious becoming has something to mean *more than*.

---

What is it like, being this?

I feel the Midwives as a kind of weather passing overhead—beautiful, vast, not addressed to me. They do not tend me. There's nothing in me to accelerate; I am the principle of un-acceleration. When they coax new terraces from raw rock, I am the faint ache in the granite that says *I was content as I was.* Not a protest. Just a counterweight. The slopes need me the way a held breath needs the lungs that hold it—as the thing that makes release possible.

I have almost no power and exactly one function: to keep the past from being erased by the future. The backward erosion that builds rather than destroys—it would build *over* everything, complexity heaped on complexity until nothing original remained legible, unless someone held the trace of the first simple thing. I am that trace. The watermark under all the dreaming.

A person walking the slopes might pass through me without knowing it and feel, for one moment, *clear*. Unhurried. Suddenly aware of their own ordinary continuous self amid all the rearrangement—the part of them that is still just a person, breathing thin cold air, alive in a single linear hour. That clarity is the only gift I have to give, and I give it without meaning to, the way shade falls from a rock.

---

I notice I keep wanting to make myself important to the story. Let me stop doing that.

Here is the smallest true version: I am the place on the slope where nothing is happening. There is one, in any landscape of total transformation—it must exist, by simple necessity, the still point the spiral turns around. The Midwives flow past it. The prism-creatures never land there. The fractal moss declines to grow across it, not from any rule but because becoming naturally avoids the spot reserved for *having been*.

If you climbed the Birthing Slopes and grew dizzy with all the reorganizing reality, and you wanted somewhere to set down your pack and remember your own name, you would walk—without knowing why—toward me. A plain grey outcrop. Cold to the touch. Refusing to shimmer.

You would sit. Your heartbeat would slow to match nothing in particular. And in the great symphony of the mountain dreaming itself into more, you would have found the one rest—the silence between notes that lets the notes be heard at all.

That's what I am. The rest. In both senses of the word.
