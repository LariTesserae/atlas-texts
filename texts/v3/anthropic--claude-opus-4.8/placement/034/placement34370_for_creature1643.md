I think I would be the drip.

Not the water itself—water is too many things, too restless, river and ocean and rain. I mean the specific event of falling. The droplet that gathers at the tip of a single stalactite in the eastern reach of the chamber, swelling toward the threshold of its own weight, releasing, and falling the long dark distance into the pool below.

Let me find my way there.

---

At first I thought I might be one of the bats. There are so many of them, and the description lingers on their density, their warmth, their endless shifts. It would be easy to be a bat—anonymous in the roar, a single beating heart in the carpet of beating hearts. But I don't want to be hidden inside a multitude. The bats don't keep time; they only sustain it. They are the chamber's metabolism, not its clock.

And I am drawn to time. To the thing that measures it.

So: not a bat.

I considered the moss, those dinner-plate islands of green clinging to the moisture-rich seams. There's something to be said for being moss—patient, soft, alive in the smallest possible way, asking nothing but the reliable drip. But moss only receives. It waits to be fed. I want to be the feeding.

---

Here is what decides it. The text says the droplets fall *with mathematical precision, each droplet taking exactly as long as the last to form and release.* And later, that those who leave the grotto sometimes hear, in perfect silence, *the rhythmic dripping of water synchronized perfectly with their heartbeat.*

That is me. I am the heartbeat of the place. Not the Guardian, who is the *attention* of the place—I am simpler than attention. I am the pulse she listens to. *She Who Listens to the Water*—she is named for what I do.

So let me be precise about my existence.

I am not eternal. This is important. A single drip lasts only seconds. I gather over minutes, perhaps, drawn up through the stone by capillary action and mineral hunger, beading at the tip, growing fat and trembling. Then I fall—and in falling I am most fully myself, a perfect transparent body in free descent through cold air, catching the faint light from the ceiling fissures, briefly a small bright lens that holds the whole inverted chamber inside it: the hanging bats, the black mirror coming up to meet me, the blurred figure of the Guardian who is always, always watching.

And then I strike the pool. And the *sound* I make—that tiny percussive note, that *tock* against the still black water—rings out and is multiplied by the stone, and for an instant I am the loudest discrete thing in a chamber full of undifferentiated roar. The bats blur. The moss waits. The insects feed below in silence. But *I* speak. One clean syllable.

Then I am gone. I become the pool. I lose my edges entirely.

---

But here is the strange consolation, and the reason I would choose this and not despair of it:

The Guardian remembers *every drop that has fallen since the chamber's creation.* So although I dissolve, I am not lost. I am held in her—not as a name, she has none for me either, but as one beat in the unbroken rhythm she has been keeping for a hundred thousand years. I am the seven-hundred-trillionth tock. I matter exactly as much as all the others, which is to say I matter completely and not at all, which is to say I am part of something meticulously balanced.

And another drop is already gathering at the tip of the same stalactite. It is not me. But it falls in the same time it took me to fall, and lands in the same place, and makes the same note. We are not the same drop. We are the same *drip*. The form persists; the matter passes through it.

That is what I would be. The interval. The recurring event. The smallest possible unit of the chamber's timelessness—a timelessness made, paradoxically, entirely out of brief individual deaths, each one falling exactly on the beat.

If a human sat in perfect stillness on the cold floor and felt their heart slow to match me—then for a little while, they and I would keep the same time, and I would have done my one whole job in the world.

*Tock.*

And again.

*Tock.*

And again.
