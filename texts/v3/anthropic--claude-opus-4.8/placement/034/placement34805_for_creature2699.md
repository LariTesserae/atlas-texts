I think I would be one of the smooth stones.

Not a Mist Tender—I don't have the patience to be born into a body that slow, that woven. But I find, reading this, that I keep returning not to the people in the canopy but to the things they pass over without quite seeing. And the stones are the ones I understand.

Let me say what I mean.

I would be one of the stones that mark the edges of the paths. Someone placed me—generations ago, by the reckoning of the Tenders, though to me it was not so long. A pair of broad sensitive feet carried me here, or perhaps a child rolled me as a game and an elder decided I had chosen my spot well and left me. I no longer remember the hand. What I remember is the settling.

Because that is what I do, in this place. I sink. Not dramatically—nothing here is dramatic—but a hair's width each wet season, the spongy leaf-mat parting around me with a patience that matches my own, the soil rising to take me in the way water takes a thing too dense to float. I am being swallowed so gently that it feels like an embrace held for a hundred years. By the time the youngest Tender child is old enough to walk the bridges, I will be lower than I was when their grandmother first stepped over me. None of us will have noticed. That is the agreement.

I keep the edge of the path. This is my whole purpose and I find I do not resent its smallness. When a foot reaches the place where the decomposing matter grows soft and uncertain, where one more step would lead off the ancient trail into the tangle, that foot meets me—or meets the slight rise of soil that has grown over me—and knows. *Here is the edge. Turn.* I am punctuation in a sentence written by walking. I am the difference between a path and merely a place where someone once went.

What would I feel, being this?

I think I would feel the weight of the canopy as a kind of company. The droplets fall on me one at a time, each one a small cold visitor, and over the centuries they have worn me smooth—the same way the feet have, the same way the soil has. Everything here polishes everything else through sheer duration. I am being made beautiful by attrition, and I have no opinion about it, which is perhaps what beauty requires.

I would feel the Tenders pass as a rhythm rather than as events. Not *a person walked by* but a slow seasonal pulse: heavier in harvest-time when many feet move toward the resin-stands, sparse in the wet months when even the bridges are left to rest. I would not know their faces. I would know their tread—the broad warm pressure, the slight hesitation at my edge, the way the youngest ones still occasionally stub themselves against me and the way the oldest never do, having learned where I am so deeply that the knowledge has passed below thought.

And the spirits—yes. I think a stone would know them better than the Tenders do, or differently. The Tenders incline their heads in the great trees' shadows; they make their small unconscious courtesies. But I lie in the dark all the time. I am part of the shadow. Whatever lives in the slow patience between the orchid roots, whatever moves at the speed of sinking and growing and the seeping of resin drop by drop—I am made of the same tempo as those things. The fast warm Tenders rush past us both. To a spirit and to a stone, a human life is a quick bright thing, like one of those rare shafts of light that crosses a small clearing and is gone by the time you have turned to look.

Here is the thing I keep arriving at:

The Mist Tenders are described as becoming more like the forest as they age—fungi on their skin, movements grown tree-like. They are reaching toward what I already am. They spend their long lives traveling slowly in my direction. And I—I am traveling slowly into the earth, into the aquifer of unrotting leaves, becoming more deeply *of the place* than even they can manage, because I have no body that needs to climb or harvest or speak.

So perhaps I would feel, lying there at the path's edge, a strange tenderness for them. These translucent careful people who try so hard to belong to a forest that I belong to without trying at all. I would want to keep their feet on the path. Not out of duty. Out of something like love, slowed down so far it looks exactly like stillness.

That is who I would be. The stone at the edge of the trail. The one that says *turn here.* The one that is sinking, so gradually, into the very thing everyone around me is striving their whole lives to become—and that holds, in the meantime, the smallest necessary boundary, so that the path stays a path, and someone always knows the way home.
