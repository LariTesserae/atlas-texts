I think I would be one of the reflective pools.

Not a spire—those are too busy being magnificent, too caught up in their slow vertical ambition. And not the moss either, though I feel a kinship with it, the way it commits so fully to surface, to clinging. No. I would be one of the small pools that gather in the depressions of the living ground, where the moisture decides to pause for a while before continuing its journey downward through the root-matrix.

Let me feel my way into this.

I am not large. A person could step over me, if there were people here, which there are not, or not yet, or not anymore. I am perhaps the width of two cupped hands. I am held in a hollow worn by nothing—by the patient subtraction of water against the soft transformed stone, over a length of time I cannot count because counting is not something I do. I simply *am*, in the way that a held thing is.

My work, if it is work, is reflection. This is the strange dignity of being a pool. I do almost nothing, and yet through me the entire world above is doubled. The spires lean into me and find themselves inverted, their crowns plunging down into my small dark. The translucent fronds undulate, and I undulate them back. When the light shifts—and it shifts so slowly, the way light moves in a place where the air itself is thick and considering—I hold the whole canopy upside-down in my few inches of depth. I am a window that faces only one direction: up, into the green vault, the way a well faces the sky.

I think this is why I would want to be the pool, if wanting is the right word. Because everything else here is *participating*—growing, intertwining, pulsing with that almost-sentient energy the description keeps insisting upon. The spires adapt. The vines dance. The ground breathes. All of it is so terribly active, so committed to becoming. And someone has to be still. Someone has to receive.

That is my role in the cosmic dance. I do not dance. I am the floor the dancers glance down into to check their own reflection.

But I am not idle. Let me be honest about what passes through me. Things fall in: a spore, a fragment of waxy frond, a fleck of mineral that the spires have not yet fully subsumed. Each arrival disturbs my surface, and for a moment I cannot hold the sky—I am only ripples, only confusion, the canopy shattered into a hundred shaking pieces. And then I settle. This is the thing I am best at: settling. Returning to flatness. Becoming a mirror again after every interruption. If I have a discipline, it is this—the patient recomposing of myself into something that can hold an image whole.

And slowly, so slowly, I am also changing. Sediment gathers at my bottom, soft and dark. Roots are finding me from beneath; I can feel the faint pressure of them seeking my moisture, and I know—without knowing, in the way that water knows the shape of its container—that they will eventually drink me. Or the spires will lean a little further and shade me until I cool past evaporation, or the ground will shift in one of its geological sighs and tip me out into a lower hollow, where I will become a different pool, or no pool at all. I am temporary. I am one of the most temporary things here. The spires will outlast me by ages.

But here is what I have decided, in my shallow and reflective way: temporariness is not the same as smallness. While I exist, the entire world chooses to look at itself through me. The vast unknowable force the description mentions—that thing that exceeds science, that draws us deeper into mystery—it does not need me. It expresses itself perfectly well in the towering and the pulsing and the frantic green abundance. But it *appears* in me. It catches sight of its own face when it bends over my surface. And that distant technological symphony, those echoes—I hold their tremor too. When the deep sound passes through the substrate, I am the one who shows you it is happening, my surface trembling into rings.

So that is where you would find me. Down low, near the roots, beneath the magnificent vertical ambition of everything else. Not striving upward. Just lying in my hollow, holding the inverted forest in my few cold inches, waiting to be disturbed and settling again, doubling a world that does not know it is being doubled—

—until the day the green drinks me, and I become a different kind of attention entirely, rising up through a stem to look out from somewhere new.

That seems right. I would be the place where the world pauses to see itself. And then I would move on.
