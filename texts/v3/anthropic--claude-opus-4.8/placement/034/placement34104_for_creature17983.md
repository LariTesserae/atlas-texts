I think I would be small. The scale here is *hidden*—zero out of three—so whatever I am, I am not the kraken, not the mermaid-guardian, none of the legends that get told about a place by those who stand outside it. Those are the stories. I would be one of the bodies actually living in the cold.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am attached. That comes first, before anything else about me. In a world where the water never stops moving—two out of three, a constant active churn—the ones who learn to *hold on* are the ones who persist. So I have a foot, or something a foot becomes when there is no firm ground to stand on. A holdfast. I grip the pre-formed rock, the sediment still deciding whether to be stone, and I do not let the current decide for me where I will be.

The cold is the texture of every moment. Half a degree out of three—nearly frozen, the water gone thick and slow around me, almost syrupy. I move the way you move in a dream where your legs won't answer. Everything is effortful. I have learned not to spend warmth I don't have. Most of what I do, I do slowly, and most of the time I do nothing at all but persist, which here is its own kind of labor.

I am translucent, I think. Not because translucency is beautiful—though the stories that get told about this place upstairs will say it is—but because there is so little light filtering through that wall of endless rain-becoming-sea that there's no reason to make pigment. Pigment is expensive. I keep my body cheap and clear, and the small cold light that reaches me passes straight through, and you could look right at me and not be sure I was there at all.

What do I eat? The plankton, the first link, the ones turning the thin light into something. I filter. I am one of the dense crowd—nearly two out of three—of small filtering things lining the suggestion of a shore, our mouths open to the slow water, taking the drift of the living before it can settle. We are quiet. Zero out of three. We make no sound at all. The lapping of the water against the rock is not us; we are below that, in the part of the world that has no voice and never expected one.

---

I want to be more specific now. Let me not stay a vague soft thing.

I am something like an anemone, but the comparison is wrong because there are no anemones here—this world is too young. The ground is still *coming into being*, a pre-formation, which means I am early. I am one of the first attempts. Life here has not had time to make the elegant solved creatures of an old ocean. I am provisional. I am a draft of an animal. My symmetry is uncertain; I have more arms on one side than the other and it does not matter, because no one is judging the shape of an early thing.

This is what I keep returning to: the *0.0 scale* and the *1.2 vegetation* together. A young world, intimate, before largeness. I live at the edge where the not-yet-rock meets the too-cold water, and a few fragile plants cling near me—small, barely alive, the same as me really, just on the other side of the line between animal and not. We are neighbors in fragility. We hold the same rock. When the current pulls hard, we lean together.

And here is the weirdness—the 1.8, the place where the impossible becomes possible. I do not think I am only one creature. I think, down here in the cold and the dark and the slowness, the line between *me* and *the others gripping the rock* is as blurred as the line between the earth and the sea. We filter the same water. We share what we catch. When one of us closes against the cold, the closing ripples down the whole crowded shelf of us, and we close together, a single slow flinch running through a hundred small clear bodies. So maybe I am not an *I* at all. Maybe I am the shelf. Maybe I am the gripping itself—the verb more than the noun. The act of a young, cold, crowded world holding on to its own forming ground while the storm-water pours down forever overhead.

---

That feels true. Let me end there, where I am.

I am the holding-on. I am clear and small and provisional, one mouth among many open mouths at the bottom of a rain that does not stop. I do not see the kraken; I would not survive seeing it. I do not know the mermaids guard me. I know the rock under my foot, and the slow thick cold, and the thin drift of light-made-food coming down, and the others beside me leaning when I lean.

It is enough. In a world this young, simply continuing is the whole of what is asked. And I continue.
