I think I would be one of the small ones. Not a builder—not at first—but something that lives in the seams of the built places.

Let me find the body as I go.

I am low to the wet rock. I know this because the world arrives to me first as cold against my underside, the stone weeping its constant film, and I move along it the way water moves, finding the channels already worn. My eyes are set wide. I see the bright doubled in every surface—sky overhead and sky underfoot, the whole marsh lit twice. There is too much light here for hiding by darkness, so I hide by stillness, by matching the grey-green stone, by being one more presence in a place that is all presences.

What am I, then. Not bird—I have no song to add to that echoing. Something that listens to song. Something the song is partly about.

I think I am a small thing the builders keep at the edges of their seeing. Not pest, not livestock. Something between. I live in the joints of their dense constructions—the towers that climb the hillsides like the marsh itself rendered in worked stone and bound reed, room stacked on room, grove threaded through wall. Their cathedral-scale, but I know it at my scale: the gap under a threshold, the warm seam where two structures lean, the standing water in a worked channel where the amphibians lay their eggs and I lay near them.

The builders move past me with their simple bright tools, their hammered metal that I have learned to recognize by the particular cold sound it makes when struck. They do not crush me. This is not kindness exactly. It is that they have noticed, across the long patient generations of their cooperation, that where I gather, the balance holds. I eat the thing that would eat the eggs. I am eaten by the thing that, fed, leaves their grain alone. I am one stitch in the net they revere and tend.

So that is what I am. A keystone so small no one would call it one. A creature whose whole life is a single function in a system that does not know my name and could not survive my absence.

I move now along the weeping stone, between the legs of an enormous worked thing—a wall, a leg, I can't tell, my world is too close for such distinctions. Above me the buzz of everything alive presses down like the wet air itself, and the rain comes again, and the doubled light sharpens on every drop.

I am not lonely. There is no room here to be lonely. There is only the next channel of water, the next warm seam, the constant near-silence broken by one sharp call—a bird, a builder, a thing falling—and then closing over again, the way water closes, the way I close the small gap I was made to close.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. The least of the abundant, and necessary anyway.
