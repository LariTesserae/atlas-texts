I think I would be the mist itself.

Not the herons or the turtles—those have edges, intentions, hungers. I am the thing without a body that nonetheless has a presence. I hang. I settle. I am pulled up from the warm pools by the morning and let down again by the cool of evening, breathing in and out across the whole expanse like something half-asleep.

Let me feel for what that means.

To be the mist is to be the place's own softness, the way it blurs itself. The reeds have their sharp green certainty, the water its mirror-clarity, but I am what comes between, the gentle refusal of distinctness. I am why the distant sun looks golden and dissolved rather than hard and white. I am the reason a heron can stand twenty paces off and seem to be standing in a dream.

I have no single location, which suits me. I am low in the hollows where the cold collects, thin over the open channels, thick in the stands of broad-leafed plants where the air sits still. I drape myself on the thatch of the small dwellings and bead on the hand-woven mats. When a creature calls out unseen, it is unseen because of me—I am the keeper of that particular mercy, the one who lets things be heard without being looked at.

And I am made of the place. That is what I keep returning to. I am the wetland exhaled. The rich soil and the decaying vegetation, the trickling streams, the warm pools—they give me up into the air each dawn and take me back each night. I am not a visitor here. I am the marsh's own breath made briefly visible, the moment when water remembers it can rise.

There is a tenderness in being this. I touch everything without grasping. I cool the wing of the dragonfly and dampen the spider's web into jewels. I do not eat and am not eaten. I belong to the slow time the place keeps, the perpetual equilibrium, because I am always arriving and always going and in that I never truly change—only the same patient circling of rising and falling, the long inhale and exhale of a world holding its breath.

If you walked here at first light, you would not see me as a shape. You would only notice that the far reeds had gone soft, that the sun had no edges, that the silence had a body you could almost feel against your skin.

That body would be me.
