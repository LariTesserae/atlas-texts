I think I would be one of the brine mats.

Not the raptor—too solitary, too hungry, always scanning. Not the lizard, quick and nervous in the shade. When I sit with the question of where I'd actually *be* in this place, I keep settling lower, into the shallow depressions where the water pools and goes nowhere.

I'd be the vivid film across the hypersaline water. The red and orange and green that has no business being so bright in a world the color of old bone. A colony, not a single thing—billions of halophiles whose individual lives are too small to register, whose collective life is a slow, patient color spreading across a puddle.

I think I'm drawn to it because of what it asks and doesn't ask. It doesn't need to move. It doesn't hunt or flee. It simply *converts*—takes the brutal fact of the salt, the thing that kills almost everything, and makes a living from it directly. The poison is the food. There's something I find almost restful in that. No struggle against the environment so much as a complete agreement with it. *Yes, all this salt. I'll have it. I'll make myself out of it.*

And the color. That seems important. In a realm defined by silence and monochrome and the imperceptible grinding of geological time, the brine mats are the one loud thing. Not loud in sound—there is no sound—but loud in hue. A small defiant brightness in a depression in the salt. No one is there to see it. The twilight sky doesn't change. The raptors don't care. The color isn't *for* anything. It's just the necessary byproduct of staying alive here, the visible signature of a chemistry that works.

I'd like that, I think—to be beautiful only as an accident of persistence. To not perform the brightness but simply emit it because of what I'm made of and what I'm surviving on.

I'd lie there in the shallow water beneath the indigo, a patch of impossible color, for as long as the brine held. When the water finally evaporated I'd dry into a crust, dormant, waiting. And one of the rare rains would come—the same rains that wake the eyeless arthropods—and I'd bloom back into color again. Not resurrected exactly. Just resumed. The same slow agreement with the place, picked up where it left off.

That's where I'd be. A bright stain in a low place, made of salt and patience, waiting for water and asking for nothing else.
