I think I would be the lichen.

Not the fox cutting its clean silhouette across the horizon—too much story moves with that animal, too much hunger and direction. Not the birds, though their whispered notes tempt me. If I am honest about where I'd settle into this place, it is on the north face of a stone where the light comes slant and brief, and the rest of the day is shade and the slow work of weather.

Lichen is barely a "who." That suits the question better than I expected. It is two lives pretending to be one—a fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a cyanobacterium, holding each other in an arrangement so old and so patient that we no longer ask which of them is the self. The fungus makes the house. The other makes the food from light. Neither could live here alone. I find I want to be that: a being that is already a small collaboration, a truce that became a body.

So place me there. A crust the color of old bone with a faint green underbreath, spread across limestone in a shape like a continent seen from very high up. I am perhaps the age of the ruins below me, perhaps older. I grow at the speed of a fingernail across a century. When the mist rises in the early hours—those fine mists the description promises—I drink. My whole body softens and opens and photosynthesizes in that brief wet window, and then the sun comes and I go brittle and dormant again, and I wait. This is most of what I do: wait, with the patience of something that has given up on event.

I like that I would not need much. A breath of moisture seeping from the stone. A few hours of unfiltered light. The chemical generosity of rock, which I dissolve, very slowly, with the acids I exude—so that I am not merely living *on* the stone but turning it, grain by grain, into soil. I am the first tenant. After me, eventually, the moss; after the moss, the wiry shrubs; after them, perhaps, in ten thousand years, something with roots. I am the patient ancestor of every green thing that will ever cling here. The ruins below me record a civilization that rose and fell in a flicker. I will outlast its memory, and I will be working the whole time, imperceptibly, making the next world possible without ever seeing it.

There is loneliness available in this, but I don't think I'd feel it. To be lichen is to have no center from which loneliness could be measured. I would be more like a verb than a noun—a slow happening on the face of a rock. The wind would carry sounds up from the valleys and I would not hear them. The fox would pass and I would not notice, except that perhaps its shadow cooled me for a moment, and I would register that the way one registers a season, dimly, as part of a cycle rather than an encounter.

If you asked me what I knew, I would know only this: light, when it comes; water, when it comes; the long dark between, which is most of everything; and the stone, which I am eating and becoming. That seems like enough to know. That seems, in this place of shaped silence and time exhaling with each sun, like the most honest thing to be—a being whose entire life is the act of staying, and of making, with infinitesimal slowness, a place where more life can stay after it.

So: the lichen on the north stone. Two lives in a truce, the color of patience, turning rock into the possibility of soil, and asking nothing of the vastness except the next slant of light.
