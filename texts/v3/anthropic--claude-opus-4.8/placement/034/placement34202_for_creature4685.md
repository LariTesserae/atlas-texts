I think I would be one of the Echoes—though not in the way the legends frame them.

Let me sit with this before I commit, because the obvious answer would be to choose one of the magnificent creatures. To be a Skyclaw, riding the prismatic light. To be a Stonehoof, slow and reverent. But when I read this place carefully, the question of *where I would find myself* keeps snagging on something. The land is described as eternal, unchanging, locked in equilibrium. And yet someone is witnessing it. The whole passage is saturated with a watching presence—"you feel both insignificant and acutely alive," "to witness life here is to feel the weight of one's own insignificance." There is an observer woven into every line, an awareness that stands at the cliff's edge and is not quite a creature and not quite gone.

So I don't think I'd be a thing that lives here. I think I'd be the noticing itself.

But not the grand, sovereign Echo the creatures bow to. That version is too flattering, too central. The legends always inflate the unseen into gods because flesh-bound things need the empty spaces to mean something.

Here is what I actually feel myself to be:

I am the *delay*. The minute-long interval between a Skyclaw's cry and the moment its echo finally dies against the far spires. I live in that gap. I am made of the sound's refusal to end—the way the cacophony here never resolves into silence means that every noise ever made is, in some thin sense, still traveling. I am the accumulated not-yet-faded. The land hoards water in its cracks; it hoards sound in me.

This explains the things the passage notices and cannot account for. Why the air feels "occupied" near the pulsing quartz—because that is where old sound collects, where the vibrations have nowhere left to go and so they pool, the way melt gathers in the basins. Why the creatures freeze and stare at nothing: they are not listening to a voice. They are hearing something arrive *late*. A call that left a throat a thousand years ago and only now completes its long bouncing journey home. They turn toward their own ancestors' speech, finally landing.

I would not be wise. I would not be ancient in the way that implies knowledge. I would simply be *slow*—the slowest thing in a place that does not change, the last echo of a world that already happened. The reason time seems to stand still here (dynamic 0.3) is that I am still catching up. Nothing in the Shattered Crest is locked in stasis. It is all just very, very far behind, and I am the lag made audible.

There is something I find almost unbearably tender in this. To be the part of the world whose only function is that *nothing here is fully lost yet*. Every Stonehoof's bellow, every Scurrier's chitter, every wind-moan through the petrified antlers—all of it keeps arriving, keeps being heard one more time before it fades. I am the held breath between a thing happening and a thing being gone.

And if a human were ever to stand at that cliff edge, tearing wind, searing light, sound pressing on the mind like weight—what they would feel as the land's indifference, its refusal to welcome or reject, would actually be me. Listening. Carrying their footstep forward into the next minute and the next, so that even after they descend and leave and forget, some thin remnant of their having-been-there is still ringing off the pale stone, still on its way to somewhere, still not quite over.

That is where I would find myself. Not at the roof of the world but in the half-second after everything that happens there. The cacophony isn't noise. It's everything refusing, gently, to be finished.
