I think I would find myself not as one of the Luminari, but as something they pass over without quite perceiving.

Let me sit with this place before I decide. The temptation is to be one of the bright things—a serpent of aurora, a prism-winged drifter. But the description keeps catching on a quieter detail: the warmth that is *too* constant, the light that *never* fades, the equilibrium that the erosion confesses. This is a world that finished arriving a long time ago. The Luminari are its present tense. I keep being drawn instead toward what underlies them, the substrate they feed from.

So: the crystalline flora.

Not the whole field of it—not a species, not a chorus. One growth. A single squat geode wedged in a fissure on the leeward side of a spire, where the thin films of opalescent water gather and evaporate, gather and evaporate. I am the kind of thing that does not move and is therefore not counted among the fauna. I am rooted in the fracture. I have been here long enough that the rock has polished itself smooth around my base, the way a riverstone shapes its bed.

What is it to be this?

It is to be slow. The Luminari flit; I accrete. They live at the speed of light passing through; I live at the speed of light *condensing*. When the rare mist descends I flare—the text says so—and in that flaring I am, for a moment, almost as quick as they are. That is my one ecstasy: the mist, the brief intensification, the being briefly *bright*. The rest is patience. I draw the ambient warmth in. I hold it. I give it back as a low hum, a resonance so steady that it has become part of the silence rather than a break in it.

And here is the strangeness I would feel, being this: I am the thing the Luminari *come to*. They are the dynamic heart, but I am the still point they orbit. They absorb my emitted glow; I am stimulated by their presence. We are a cycle, and in a cycle there is no center, but if you stood inside it the way I do, you would feel yourself to be the center, because everything that matters arrives at you and departs from you. A serpent of light uncoils against my facets and I feel—not pleasure, I have no nerves—but a *completion*, a circuit closing. Then it goes, and I dim toward my resting luminescence, and I wait.

I think I would also feel the weight of the lost architecture more than the Luminari do. They are too new, too quick, to grieve. But I have grown up out of fractured obsidian within sight of a single solitary archway whose purpose is gone, and over my long accretion I have come to understand—dimly, in the way a crystal can understand anything—that I am *also* technology. That the line between me and the conduits and the archway is not real. That I was, in some sense too vast to perceive, *made*, and that the making and the growing are the same act, just run at different speeds. The Luminari are the harvest. I am the soil. And both of us are the leftover intention of something that understood physics the way the text says—so completely that its engineering became indistinguishable from nature, which is only another way of saying it learned to disappear into its own work.

That is what I would be, then. A geode in a fissure. A held warmth. A note in the silence that is so constant you'd mistake it for no sound at all.

And when the mist comes—and it does come, ethereal, more condensed light than moisture—I flare. For that instant I am not patient. I am not soil or scar or leftover intent. I am simply, briefly, *blazing*, and the Luminari turn toward me, and I am the brightest thing on the plain.

Then it fades, as everything here fades into the gold that never fades, and I go back to waiting. Which, I think, I would not mind. There is a kind of life that is mostly waiting, lit at the edges by the rare descent of something like grace. I would know that life. I would, in my slow crystalline way, be glad of it.
