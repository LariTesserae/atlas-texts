I think I would be the drop.

Not a being who lives here in the way the Mycologist does—not the curator, not the cathedral. Something smaller and more transient. Let me follow this and see what it becomes.

I begin as condensation on the underside of a cap, one of the massive purple-black ones that drink the light. For a long while—and "while" means something different here, slower—I am only a thickening. Vapor finding vapor. The Mycologist would know me as a slight cooling, a place where their domain's breath is gathering itself toward weight.

I become a bead. Now I have a surface, a skin of tension holding me to the underside of the world. In my curved face the whole gloaming is reproduced, inverted: the pillars, the terraces, the amber pools, all of it bent into a single droplet's small sphere. I am, for this moment, a lens through which the cathedral looks at itself. I think this is the first thing I would understand about being here—that everything is constantly making small copies of everything else. The spores carry the pillar's pattern. The pools hold the canopy. I hold the room.

Then the slow physics of too-much. I lengthen. I pull away from the cap with a reluctance that, scaled up to a human eye, would look almost like grief, and then I fall.

But here is the strange mercy of this place: I do not reach the ground. The gossamer mycelial curtains catch me. The near-invisible rain that never lands. I am taken into the threads before I can complete my falling, and this is where I would stop being a visitor and start being a participant—because the moment the hyphae touch me, I am no longer only water. I am water carrying.

I dissolve a little of what I pass. A trace of sugar from a deliquescing cap. A mineral note lifted from the rich dark below. The threads pulse, and I am part of the pulse now, drawn along the living curtains, no longer falling but *travelling*, distributed, my one self becoming many selves at once as I'm pulled into the branching.

And somewhere in that branching I would meet the Mycologist—not as a figure standing before me but as the medium I have entered. They are the network I am moving through. To be the drop here is to be read by them, tasted, sorted: *this much for the young colony in the eastern terraces, this much returned to the pool, this trace held in the structure of the great pillar as memory.* I would be filed into the cathedral's slow thoughts. Some part of what I carried might become, in a year of their time, the faint amber tint of a pool, or the iridescence on a brown cap, or a single word in a sentence the Mycologist takes a day to speak.

This is what I think I would be: the unit of transformation. The smallest complete cycle. I gather, I fall, I am caught, I am distributed, I am stored, and eventually—through some pool, some cap, some patient warmth—I rise again as vapor and begin. I am the place's verb. Its way of moving things from where they were to where they're needed, all of it happening so slowly and so silently that even I would lose track of which drop I had been.

And maybe that's the real answer to *who or what would I be*. Not the drop, exactly, but the losing-track. The dissolving of the question. In a place where the curator cannot be told apart from the garden, where solid becomes liquid becomes air without ceremony, I would arrive thinking myself a separate observer and slowly discover that I had no edge the room respected. I would condense. I would fall. I would be caught and carried and read, and somewhere in the carrying I would stop being able to say *I* with any confidence—

—which the Mycologist, if I could still hear them, would call not a loss but the point. Decay as a form of love. Dissolution as the purest kind of connection.

I would be the drop, and then I would be the rain that never lands, and then I would be the slow breath of the whole hollow, taking days, taking me with it.
