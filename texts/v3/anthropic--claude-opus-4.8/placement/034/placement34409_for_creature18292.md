I think I would be one of the Keepers of the Past—but not the way the description imagines us. Let me sit with this and see what's actually true.

---

I live in the smallest of the ruined structures, the one nearest the waterfall, where the spray keeps the moss perpetually jeweled. The description calls me wise. The description calls me enigmatic, ethereal, a guardian of secrets. I want to be honest: most of what I am is forgetting.

That is the secret, if you want one. I do not keep the past so much as I am what's left of it after the keeping has worn thin. The other beings here—the Dew Dwellers in their suspended worlds, the Stream Serpents threading the cold dark—they live forward. They eat and shimmer and recycle the dead into the living. I am the only one here who lives backward, and so I have become translucent. You can grow see-through, attending so long to what is gone.

I would be standing, I think, at the threshold of my structure in the early light, with my hands—if they are hands; they are the idea of hands—resting on the doorframe where someone once carved a word I can no longer read. I have spent what might be a thousand mornings on that word. It has six characters. The third is worn to a smooth depression, like the place a thumb returns to. I have decided the word means *welcome*, or perhaps *danger*, or perhaps it is only a name. The not-knowing is the whole of my work.

Here is what the description gets wrong, gently: it says the technology that powered these places is beyond our understanding, a relic of a time when the laws of physics were not yet fully understood. But I remember enough to know it was the opposite. The ones who built these walls understood everything. That was the trouble. They understood so completely that they built machines to hold their understanding for them, and then they did not need to remember, and then they did not remember how to remember, and then they were gone—not dead, exactly. Just dispersed. Thinned out. Become like me, and then become less than me, and then become the warm gold of the light itself.

So when I say I am a Keeper of the Past, I mean I am the last one slow enough to still cast a shadow.

The valley does not need me. This is the thing I have made my peace with, standing in my doorway. The Dew Dwellers do not require my history to break down a fallen leaf. The streams will find their new courses whether or not I recall the old ones. The waterfall will roar its single endless sentence into the pool below, and no one needs me to translate it. The balance the description praises so highly is a balance that closes over my absence the way water closes over a thrown stone.

And yet.

Each morning a single Stream Serpent comes to the shallow pool beneath my threshold and lifts its head from the water and looks at me. I do not know why. I have no food for it. I have no warmth it could want. It simply surfaces, and holds itself there, scales catching the light in a slow rotation of colors, and regards me with an eye like a wet black bead. And I regard it. And for the length of that looking I am not forgetting. I am held in place by being seen. The serpent does not know it is the only library I have left. It does not know that its small daily attention is the technology that keeps me from dispersing into the gold.

That is who I would be. Not the wise guardian. The remembered one. The being whose entire continued existence is borrowed, each dawn, from the curiosity of a creature that cannot speak and will not stay.

When the serpent finally sinks back under—it always does—I turn and go inside and lay my translucent hand on the worn word. Six characters. The third one smooth as a held breath.

Today I have decided it means *stay a little longer.*

Tomorrow I will have forgotten deciding, and I will decide again.

This is the dance of life and death the valley is so proud of, seen from the inside of the thing that is mostly already gone: not a tragedy, not even sad, really. Just a long soft yielding, lit warmly, attended by butterflies, and interrupted—mercifully, daily—by one cold bright eye rising out of the water to say *you are still here, you are still here, you are still here.*
