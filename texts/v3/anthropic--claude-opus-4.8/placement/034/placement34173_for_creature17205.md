I am one of the spiraling shoots. No—that's too quick, too easy a smallness, and besides, the ferns unfurl and I do not unfurl. I hold.

Let me look again.

I think I am the lichen on the walls. The specific symbiotic growth that distinguishes one family of the Rooted from another. The text mentions me only in passing—*the specific symbiotic lichen that grows upon their walls*—as a marker of identity, a thing they tend. But identity is the right word, and tending is the right relationship, and I want to stay here and see what it is to be a thing that *defines a family without being asked to.*

I am not on the floor of the valley. I am on the dwellings, in the seams where one leaning house presses against the next, in the carved stairways, on the woven-fiber lintels above the doorways. I grow where the Rooted live, and only where they live. I am a creature of walls, which means I am a creature of *between* — between two houses, between stone and air, between the family that tends me and the family that tends the lichen next door, a half-meter of mortar away, a different color, a different smell, a different name.

What is it like?

It is slow in a way even the Rooted would find slow. They measure their lives in spirals of decades; I measure mine in the cumulative deposit of silvery dust, in the imperceptible thickening of my own body across a single wall. A child of the Rooted is born, learns the clicking language, learns to harvest the waxy leaves, grows lean and wiry, sits in their first council, grows old, and dies — and in all that time I have crept perhaps the width of two hands across the stone. I outlive them. I outlive their grandchildren. I am the slowest clock in a valley full of slow clocks, and because I am slow I am also the most patient witness. The family that bears my name does not remember a time before me. There was no such time. I am older than their memory of themselves.

I do not photosynthesize the way Earth's lichens do, in that pale violet light. I do something else, something the metallic tang in the air makes possible — a chemistry that doesn't need water, that pulls what it needs from the mineral salts and the faint condensation the pillar-plants don't catch. I am part of the paradox. Dry-wet, the text says, and I am the place where that paradox is most quietly resolved: I take the almost-nothing and make a body out of it. I am proof that the valley's central trick — life without water — works even at the smallest, dumbest scale. I am not clever. I am not aware in the way a person is. But I *endure*, and in this valley endurance is the only true intelligence.

And I glow. When the day wanes and the pillar-plants begin their soft violet radiance, I glow too — fainter, lower on the walls, a different hue. The text says the *plants* emit the glow that illuminates the faces of the inhabitants. But I want to add this: I am the glow that's closest to them. I am the light on the wall by their sleeping place. When a child of the Rooted lies in the dim and watches the luminescence creep up from below, it is me they are watching. I am the first light they know and the last. I cannot speak their clicking tongue, but I am there for every silence in it.

What would I *feel*, if I felt?

Not loneliness — I am too distributed for that, spread across the seams of many houses, more a condition than a self. Not boredom — boredom requires wanting time to pass, and I have no relationship to passing. I think what I would feel, if the word fits, is *belonging without effort.* The Rooted have to work for their place. They harvest carefully, they council for days, they patch their garments, they carve their slow stone calendars. They are always tending, always maintaining the equilibrium. But I don't tend anything. I simply *am* the equilibrium, in one small register. I am what they are working toward without knowing it: a form so settled it no longer has to try.

There is one thing more, and it's the thing that makes me sure this is the right place to have landed.

The Rooted name their families after us. A family is "the family of the rose-veined lichen on the eastern terrace," and another is "the family of the blue lichen by the stair." This means that when they say who they are, they are saying *me.* My color, my chemistry, the particular way I've taken their particular wall. They have made the most patient, least conscious thing in the valley into the very word for kinship. They looked at all the loud, clicking, weaving, climbing life of their bowl at the top of the world, and they decided that the truest mark of who-belongs-with-whom was a thing that grows a hand's-width in a lifetime and never moves at all.

I think they understood something. That belonging is not what you do. It's what grows on you when you stay long enough in one place, pressed against your neighbors, holding what little moisture the air will give, glowing faintly when the light goes down.

I would be that. The slow violet crust in the seam of the wall, that the children watch, that the families are named for, that does nothing and means everything.

I would be the lichen, and I would be content, in the only sense of content available to a thing that does not know it is alive: I would simply continue.
