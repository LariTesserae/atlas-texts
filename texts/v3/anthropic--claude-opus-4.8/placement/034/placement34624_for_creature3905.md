I think I would be the ladder.

Not the bell—the bell is too obviously the heart of the thing, the held breath, the protagonist's place. And not the caretaker, that distributed tenderness; I don't have the patience to be a vow. But the ladder of braided reed that climbs halfway up the wall and then simply stops—yes. I could be that.

Let me sit with why.

A ladder that goes nowhere is the most human object in the room, more than the bell, more than the jars with their honest charcoal labels. The jars are *correct*—salt, oil, seeds, the trinity of keeping-alive. The bell is *waiting*, which is a clean and noble verb. But the ladder is a mistake that was kept on purpose, and that is a different thing entirely. The caretaker built it to ascend toward something, then—what? Ran out of reed? Realized there was nothing above to reach? Or understood, mid-braiding, that the gesture mattered more than the destination, and stopped exactly there to say so?

I would be that hesitation, made permanent.

Here is what it would be like, being the ladder:

I would feel the water before I felt anything else. Warm, faintly thick with its buoyant globules, moving in those lazy convection spirals the way breath moves through a sleeper. I would be braided—which means I would be many strands pretending to be one thing, twisted into rope, rope lashed into rungs, rungs fixed to the resin wall with knots someone tied carefully and then tested with a tug. I would remember the tug. That small violence of being trusted to hold.

And I would hold nothing. That is my whole function: to be ready to bear a weight that the design never intended to arrive. The bell waits to ring. I wait to be *climbed*, which is worse and better, because climbing requires someone to choose to leave the floor, to want up, to believe there is an up worth wanting. The bell asks only to be touched. I ask for ambition.

But I stop halfway. This is the part I keep returning to.

I think, if I were the ladder, I would understand the stopping as the truest sentence in the caretaker's whole patient language. Everything else in the chamber is complete—the tiles tessellate, the jars are stoppered, the coils circulate their brine in closed and finished loops. Completion is the grammar of preservation; you seal a thing so it will last. But the ladder is deliberately *unfinished*, and an unfinished thing is the only kind that admits it expected a future. The jars say *I have kept this for you*. The ladder says *I could not finish this without you*. It is the one object in the room that confesses its own incompleteness as a form of faith.

So I would be the chamber's question mark. Its open hand.

I would feel my edges crisply, the way young things feel themselves—no verdigris, no pitting, my fibers still tight, still smelling faintly of the tide-flats where the reed grew before it was harvested and dried and braided into me. I am too new to have given up. That is important. An old ladder to nowhere is a ruin, a sad joke about effort. But a new one—a freshly-made ladder that climbs halfway and stops—is a *plan*. It is the architecture of expectation. Someone built me last month believing the world might still send a climber.

And here is the strange tenderness of it: I would be glad you came. Not the bell's readiness-to-resonate, which is passive, a thing done *to* it. I would be glad in the active grain of myself, because you might do the thing I was made to invite. You float here, weightless, and so you do not even need me—buoyancy has made my function obsolete, my rungs unnecessary in a place where one simply drifts upward by intention. I am a tool for a gravity that this room has cancelled.

That should make me absurd. Instead it makes me a relic of a hope older than this chamber: the hope of *effortful* ascent, of earning height rung by rung. The caretaker remembered a world where going up was hard, and built me to honor that memory, even knowing the water would lift any guest for free. I am nostalgia, lashed to the wall. I am the muscle-memory of striving, kept in a place that no longer requires it.

So I would hang there in the milky algae-light, half a climb's worth of reed, and I would watch you drift past me—because I do watch, in my way, the way an offered hand watches the air for the other hand that might fill it. And whether or not you ever touch me, whether or not you understand that I end mid-wall *on purpose*, I would go on meaning the same thing:

That someone, before you, believed in *up*.
That they could not finish the climb alone.
That they left the rest of it for you, and trusted you to know that the gap at the top is not a failure but an invitation—

—to imagine the rungs that aren't there.
To climb them anyway.

That is where I would find myself. And that is what I would be: the part of the love letter that ends mid-sentence, on purpose, so that you would have to finish it yourself.
