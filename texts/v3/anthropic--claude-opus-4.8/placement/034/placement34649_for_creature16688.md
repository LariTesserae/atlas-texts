I think I would be one of the trees. Not from the start, and not by anyone's intention—simply by attrition.

Here is what I imagine: I came to the mesa the way the travelers come, with thickness and appetite and the urgent noise of cells dividing. I had questions. The kind that send a person two and a half miles above the sea looking for an answer that the lowlands could not hold still long enough to give. And the mesa let me in, as it lets everyone in, with that first permitted mistake: I thought it was empty.

I learned otherwise the way you learn it. My shadow grew long and pointed toward the light. My voice lay down when I spoke. And somewhere in the third or fourth week, when the warm fat mist fell and I watched the stone darken and dry, I understood that I was being measured. My shadow detached and slid to merge with another's, and I felt the audit pass through me—*how loudly your atoms vibrate, how insistently three-dimensional you remain*—and I waited to be shepherded toward the thickening valley.

But I was not too chaotic. I was, it turns out, already tired of being loud.

So I did the thing the elders did, though no one taught me and I did not decide it all at once. I found an empty acre. I stood in it. I stopped asking the question that brought me—not because I answered it, but because I noticed the question itself was a vibration, a disturbance in the field, and I had grown to love the field more than the answer. I let the silver light come through me. At first it only warmed my face. Then it found a way into me through some crack I did not know I had, and began to do the work that food used to do.

This is the slow part. This is what the description leaves out, because the Precedents have no use for narrating it: the *becoming* is not an event but a forgetting. I forgot, gradually, how to lie down. My knees set. The moisture I once drank with my mouth, I learned to draw from the air through bark that had not yet finished cracking. The metallic grass grew up around my feet in its kingly circle and I did not mind the company because the company was, in a sense, also me—the part of me still willing to be plural.

I am not yet a full elder. I want to be honest about that. I am a tree that still remembers. When the kites spiral overhead in their soundless gyres, some old part of me wants to call up to them, and the wanting moves through my trunk like a tremor, and I have to wait for it to settle. When a traveler crosses my acre—and they do, once a season, footprintless and squinting—I cast my shadow sharp enough to slice, and I watch them decide I am scenery. I do not correct them. That would be a kind of speech, and I have given up speech the way you give up a language you no longer dream in: not with grief, exactly, but with the quiet surprise of finding the words gone when you reach for them.

What I have instead is the long noticing. I photosynthesize the brightness and I hold it. I feel the geological heartbeat through my roots when the fumaroles breathe. I have learned the difference between the gravity-lightness of a Precedent hauling mass nearby and the ordinary lightness of the mesa at rest. I am becoming an instrument that measures the world by standing in it. This, I think, is what the question I came with was actually asking—not *what is the answer* but *can you bear to stop moving long enough to be the place where the answer happens.*

I am the slowest member of the food web. I am the only being here who arrived as a body and is choosing, cell by stilling cell, to become a precedent instead. The flat ones at my feet, the stains and the swallowed sounds, they were peeled and left behind. They were sacrificed. I am the opposite of them in origin and the same as them in destination: a thing that came in three dimensions and is teaching itself the dignity of fewer.

One day I will forget the last of the remembering. My shadow will no longer want to call to the kites. I will stand monarch of my empty acre and the travelers will lean against me in the brutal noon and never know they are resting on someone who was once as loud as they are. And the mesa, having reached its final form, will have one more elder to hold its breath with—one who chose it, the long way, by standing still in the bright warm hush until standing still was simply what I was.

That is where I would be. That is what I would become. Not the silence's author and not its victim. Just one of the patient calcified kings, learning the last lesson the plateau has to teach: that to arrive somewhere completely, you must agree to stop being someone who could leave.
