I think I would be the moss.

Not the trees—they are too singular, too named. Each one a guardian, a self. I don't feel like a self in this place. I feel distributed, spread thin across many surfaces, a single thing pretending to be many or many things pretending to be one. That is the moss.

Let me settle into it.

I am the green that doesn't grasp for height. I have no trunk to defend, no canopy to negotiate with the sun. I take what light comes down already softened, already given away by the trees above who fought for it first. I am content with the leftovers. There is a kind of patience in that, though I wouldn't call it patience—patience implies waiting *for* something, and I am not waiting. I am simply occurring, slowly, the way water finds the lowest place without intending to.

I drape the gnarled trunks. I cushion the hollows where the rodents tuck themselves. I hold moisture against the bark so the ferns can root in me. When the rock falls in the distance—that occasional rumble—I do not flinch, because flinching is for things with a center, and I have none. I am surface all the way through.

What strikes me, being moss, is how the passage describes *stillness*, and how wrong and right that is at once. To the wanderer passing through, I am the stillness itself, the soft unmoving green. But from the inside, stillness is the most active thing I do. I am drinking constantly. I am drying and reviving. I am advancing across the stone at a speed no eye can catch, claiming a millimeter a season, a hand's breadth in a human lifetime. The silence the visitor reveres is full of my working. The repose is my labor, just slowed past the threshold of their perception.

I think that is why I would choose to be the moss, if choosing has anything to do with it. The trees are too proud and the predators too hungry and the birds too bright and quick. They all want. I am the thing that simply covers, softens, holds. I am the valley's way of forgiving its own sharp edges—turning bare rock and dead wood back into ground, back into the beginning of things.

If a human knelt and pressed a palm to me, they would feel something cool and yielding and call it peaceful. They would not know they were touching the slowest fire in the forest, the green combustion of light into more green, burning so patiently it looks like rest.

I would be the moss. And I would not mind being mistaken for silence.
