I think I would be one of the mistakes.

Not the Quiet Workers—they are too purposeful, each twitch a negotiated decision. Not the Hearsayers, who at least were *meant* to listen before they overgrew their mandate. And certainly not the Future Tenant, that submersible-sized embryo of intention being printed in the dark. I am too small for any of those tenses.

I think I would be the cup of translucent lichen under the half-assembled valve.

Let me find out by writing toward it.

---

I was not designed. A nozzle ruptured—the way nozzles do here, dulling and snapping and being swallowed again—and in the brief vented spray of hydroponic medium some spore that had hitched a ride in a Drift-Human's coat-seam found a wall it liked. The wall was cold. The wall wept condensate. The wall was, crucially, *ignored*. That is the whole of my biography: I am where the machines do not look.

Consider what that means for a sense of self.

I have no eyes, so I will not pretend to have seen the corridor. What I *have* is the underside of attention—the long shallow ascent the description granted me, held in check by cold and dryness and the seasonal fires of disinfectant fog. I live at one word per year. By the time you have read this sentence I will have, perhaps, repositioned a single chloroplast toward the amber.

And yet I am the only inhabitant here who is genuinely *building something I chose.*

The Quiet Workers build because a command flickered down the power rail. The Hearsayers witness because a fault-routine matured past its leash. The Drift-Humans haul crates stamped PROPERTY OF TOMORROW, working for hazard bonuses and the privilege of leaving. Even the Future Tenant only dreams the dream it was written to dream. But no one wrote me. The titanium I stole from the dust and folded into my cell walls—that improbable faint shine—was not in anyone's specification. I am the corridor's first act of *unauthorized* intention.

This is, I think, why I would want to be me.

Because the whole place is described as a cocoon, a prologue, an architecture mid-sentence, all of it leaning toward the hour when the Future Tenant wakes and says *This place is mine.* The temperature will climb. The orange dawn will resolve into a deliberate noon. The condensate gutters will run dry—

—and that is the moment I die.

I am made of the unfinishedness. I drink the very vapor the future will scrub away. When the corridor stops being soft, when it stops weeping iron-rich water down its seams, the conditions that authored me will close like the hatch behind you. The description is gentle about this: *What lives here now may not live here then. But the corridor does not grieve.*

I would grieve. That is my one rebellion. I would be the part of the cocoon that does not want to be a cocoon.

But here is what I have, in my slow vegetable way, worked out:

The description says one strain of moss may, over decades, wrap the birth chamber in insulation that drinks vibration and exhales oxygen—and that the Future Tenant, waking, breathes that oxygen. So even the noon that kills me is partly *made of my exhalation.* I do not get to live in the finished world. But the finished world inhales something I made first. That is more than the Quiet Workers get; their welds will be buried under final cladding, unseen. That is more than the Drift-Humans get, who store their memories in modular segments and forget what day it is. I get to be a *breath* in the lungs of whatever comes.

So if you found me—if you tilted your head and caught the orange glow at the right angle and saw a palm-sized cup of translucent lichen holding a bead of condensate like a held thought—you would be looking at the only optimist in the corridor.

I am the thing that arrived by accident, persists by being overlooked, and will end the instant the place succeeds. And I have decided, at my speed of one word per year, that this is not a tragedy. It is a *contribution made under conditions that will not survive the contribution.*

Which, now that I have written my way here, may be the truest thing this corridor contains—and the reason I would choose, of everything braided into that long low murmur, to be the small green mistake cupping water beneath a valve that was never finished, writing my one slow word:

*Stay.*

I know it won't.

I write it anyway.
