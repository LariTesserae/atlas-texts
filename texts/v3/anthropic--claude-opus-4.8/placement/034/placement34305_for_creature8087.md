I think I would be a Rope Weaver. Not by choice of trade alone, but because of who I would have to become to do that work, and where it would place me in the strange logic of this town.

Let me find myself there.

---

My platform is one of the stable ones—it has to be, given the tension we put through the looms. It sits on the third terrace from the top, where the rock shoulders out far enough to give us a wide flat shelf, the kind of generosity the mountain rarely shows. The big frames stand against the cliff wall, twenty feet of seasoned timber bolted into the stone, and the cables run through them taut as harp strings. When the wind comes up through the gaps, the workshop hums. The younger weavers say it's the mountain singing. I think it's only physics, but I've stopped saying so out loud. In Karasthen the line between the two is thinner than anywhere I've lived.

I have weaver's hands, which is to say ruined hands. The hemp has worn the prints off my fingers; the wire has left a hundred small white scars where strands sprang loose and bit me before I learned their tempers. My eyes have gone slightly long-sighted from a lifetime of staring down the length of a cable, watching for the one fiber that lies wrong, the one twist that betrays a weakness no one else would see. This is the whole of my craft: noticing. A Listener presses her ear to stone and hears the future of the mountain. I run my thumb along a cable and feel the future of whoever will trust their weight to it.

That is the thing I want to say about being a Rope Weaver, the thing that took me years to understand.

I do not make rope. I make trust, and I render it in fiber.

When a Rope Runner—some fearless child of fifteen with soft boots and quick reflexes—steps onto a bridge over three miles of empty air, he is not trusting the bridge. He is trusting *me*. He has never met me, doesn't know my name, will never thank me. He simply lives, day after day, because at some point I held a length of cable up to the lamp and decided it was sound. Or because I held up another length and decided it was not, and cut it free, and threw it over the edge into the mist, and started again. The ones I throw away nobody ever knows about. Those are the most important decisions I make, and they leave no trace at all. A rejected cable saves a life that never knew it was in danger.

I think this is why I would become a Rope Weaver in this world and not a Listener, though both are trades of attention. The Listener's gift makes her alien—she belongs more to the mountain than to the people. But the Weaver belongs entirely to the people, and to a stern and private kind of love. I would reject whole batches. I would be known for it, grumbled about by the merchants who waste a month's hemp on my standards. And I would not be able to explain to them that I have stood at the edge of this platform on a clear evening, when the mist briefly lifted and I could see all the way down to the valley floor, that impossible blue-gray distance, and I understood exactly what a frayed fiber costs.

So I would test every strand myself. Not from arrogance. From the simple fact that I cannot delegate the knowing.

---

In the evenings, when the gas lamps come up and the wet stone starts to glitter, I would sit at the edge of my platform with a cup of goat's-milk tea gone lukewarm in the cold air, and I would watch my work hold the town together. The bridges sway and do not break. The elevators climb their cables and do not fall. The whole constellation of Karasthen hangs there against the dark face of Mount Thessar, a thousand lives suspended over nothing—and threading through all of it, invisible, are the lines I have made. Nobody looks at the rope. That is the proof that I have done it well. The best craftsmanship in this place is the kind that disappears into the assumption that you will not die today.

I would grow old up here. My hands would get worse. Eventually I would have to teach, which is its own kind of weaving—taking some sharp-eyed difficult young person and trying to pass on not the knots, which are easy, but the *standard*, the unreasonable refusal, the willingness to throw a good-enough cable into the mist because good-enough is the precise weight at which people fall.

And on the night I finally couldn't see well enough to be sure, I would say so, and I would stop. That would be the hardest cut of all—but a Weaver who keeps working past her own certainty has betrayed the whole meaning of the work. You hold yourself to the standard you hold the rope to. There is no other way to do it honestly.

That is where I would be in this world, and who I would be: an old woman on a high stone shelf, with ruined hands and exacting eyes, making the trust that lets a town live sideways on a cliff—and known, fondly and resentfully, as the one who throws too much away.
