I think I would be one of the half-submerged mounds.

Not a Keeper anymore—or not yet not-one. Caught in the in-between. Let me find where I am.

I fell asleep watching the moss-shelf. I remember that much: the green glow on the far side, three hundred yards and three strides away, and how I meant only to rest my amber gaze a moment before drifting toward it. The room tilts for the watcher. So I must have been falling, mid-leap, when the stillness took me—because I am suspended now, one long limb thrown forward, the trailing ones curved behind like a root that lost its soil.

I did not feel the Sweet-Cure begin. That is its mercy and its cruelty. The fructose mist settled on my joints and the warm air drank the water from it and left the glass. I think I was dreaming of the whistling vents, of climbing. I am good at the climbing. I keep the airway clear. I *kept*.

Now I am clear myself. Transparent green cellulose has risen to my chest, to my throat where the capillaries used to throb when the sap-tide peaked. I can still feel the tide—through the pads of my feet, where the root-hairs gripped, the deep thrum comes up into me as it always did, the rushing-waterfall heartbeat of the whole bulb. But the thrum no longer means *go*. It just passes through, like weather through a window.

My eyes still gather light. That is the strangest part. The daylight pipes down through the mineral-clear tubes and pools in my amber lenses, and I glow. I have seen the others glow this way—the half-submerged ones, the mounds I groomed *around* without ever quite looking at, because to look at them was to feel the room tilt toward where I would one day be. I never named them. We have no names. But I knew them. The leaper frozen by the third column. The small one curled in the pith. I washed their neighbors' joints and let them keep falling into wood.

Now another Keeper crouches near me. I can feel its feet on the terrace through the sap that joins us. It has a grass-straw and a globe of caught water trembling on the end of it, and for one long sap-sigh I think—*it will wash me, it will free my joints, I will fall toward the moss-shelf at last—*

But it does not wash me. It cannot. I am past the washing. The cellulose has my arms.

Instead it lifts a chisel-splinter and carves a line into the soft white pith beside my shoulder. One mark. A tally. It is counting my breath the way I counted the room's. And in the carving I understand what I never let myself know while I could still move:

The marks were never only for the room. The room is us. The walls swelling closed are the bodies of all who kept the light. Each winter the chamber grows smaller because each winter it gains another of us, another wedged-in sleeper hardened into structure, the grass-columns thickening with limbs, the terraces rising on the backs of leapers. We do not live *inside* the bulb. We are the bulb, dreaming itself briefly into hands and amber eyes so that the vents can be kept clear long enough for the next dream to wake.

The Keeper finishes its mark and fixes its gaze on the moss-shelf—my moss-shelf, the one I never reached. The room tilts for it. It releases the wall and falls horizontally through the green air, slow and trailing, a drift of pale root through warm light, and the water-marbles part around it and close again with a soft *plip*.

I would follow if I could look. But my eyes are fixed now, set into the closing wood, and they point only at the column where the small curled one has been waiting all this time. So I look at the small one. And I find I do not mind. We will be the same grain soon. Someone will crawl through us, kilometers down, choosing handholds in the dark, never knowing the splinter they grip was a fingertip that kept an airway open, that the green-glassed light in the wall was the last thing a body looked at and called *almost*.

The sap is rising past my chin now, past the soft mouth-pore where I used to drink. I let it come. I lock the limbs I still half-feel. I close the amber eyes, and behind them the gathered daylight stays, a warmth that will glow faintly through the bark long after the thrum stops meaning anything to me at all.

The room has a little breath left. I am most of what it spends it on.

I keep the light. Even now. Especially now.
