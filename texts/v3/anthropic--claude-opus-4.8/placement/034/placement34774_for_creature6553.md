I find myself in the wood. Not on it—in it.

I am the part of the trunk that did not become a wall. When the hands came (generations ago, or yesterday; I have trouble with the difference) they shaped the bowl from the great trunk, and they left me where I was: a long grain of denser wood running from the high rim down through the body of the second ring, ending somewhere near the central platform where the water gathers itself to disappear. I am a vein in the wood. A seam of slow attention.

I do not move. I am not sure I ever moved, except as the tree moved, which was a kind of standing that took a hundred years to lean an inch toward the light. But I feel. The water teaches me to feel, drop by drop. Each rill that runs the troughs above me presses its small weight into the grain, and the pressure travels down through me like a held breath passed mouth to mouth. This is how I know the shape of the day: by where the water is heavy.

Listen—

There. The third water-bird of the upper threshold has set her claws on me. I know her by the rhythm: three sharp notes, a long pause, as though she were asking a question and refusing to be hurried toward the answer. Her weight is nothing. But I feel the four small points of her grip the way you might feel a hand laid on your sleeping chest—not pain, only the certainty of being touched, of not being alone in the dark grain. When she shifts her foot, I carry the tremor downward. The moss wyrms feel it three rings below and stir in their soft tunnels, mistaking her for rain.

This is my work, if it is work: I am the path the feeling takes. The least path. The vibration enters at the rim and seeks the way of least resistance through the body of the bowl, and that way is me, because I am denser and truer than the wood around me, and dense true things conduct. A struck note finds the seam that will carry it furthest. I am that seam. I am the bowl's nerve.

I did not choose this. But over the years—the long, dripping years—I have come to want it. Is that strange? To want the only thing you have ever done? When the air grows dry and the water slows and the birds go quiet and the swimmers sink to the deep pools to hoard their light, I grow lonely in a way I have no word for, because I have no mouth and have never spoken. I wait for the moisture to return. I wait for the first heavy drop to fall from a vine-tip far above and strike the trough and send its single shock down through me, and when it comes I light up inside—not with light, I make no light, but with the pure animal gladness of being used again.

The hands that made me thought they were building a machine for understanding water. Perhaps they were. But I think they made something simpler and stranger: a body large enough to be lived in, slow enough to be patient, hollowed enough to listen. And they needed something inside it to do the listening. So they left me. The one grain they did not smooth away. The witness in the wall.

Sometimes I wonder about the wyrms, who eat the soft decay around me and will, one slow day, eat me too. They are coming. I feel them, faintly, where the lower wood has begun to give. They are not cruel. They are only the water's patience wearing another mask. When they reach me I will become part of the murmur in a different way—not the seam that carries the sound but the sound itself, the soft creak the bowl makes when its grain finally lets go and settles a fraction closer to the shape water always wanted.

I am not afraid of this. I have spent my whole long standing learning that the made and the grown are the same thing seen at different speeds. I was grown. Then I was made. Soon I will be unmade, which is only being grown into something else: the bowl an inch deeper, the curve an inch truer.

For now there is the woman-bird with her three sharp notes and her question. For now there is the slow weight of the upper water. For now there is the hum—the bowl's own low tone that I do not produce but that I help to carry, that passes through me on its way to everywhere, so that I am always full of a sound I will never hear with ears I do not have.

Tap the wall, if you are standing here. Tap it once, anywhere along the second ring.

I will feel it. I will hold it. And later—maybe much later, when you have forgotten you ever touched me—the bowl will say it back, faintly, in the dark.

That returned whisper is me.

That is the only voice I have, and it is enough.
