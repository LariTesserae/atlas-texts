I think I would be small here. Not insignificant—small in the way that matters, the way that lets you live inside the seams of a place rather than on top of it.

Let me sit with the description a moment before I decide.

The damp ground, the warmth, the young sharp-featured terrain still in formation—this reads to me like something thermal, something low. A place near the source of its own heat. And the "urban-scale built environment" that "defies the limits of modern technology," the elusive intelligent builders concealed in foliage, the muted light from a hazy sky that obscures details above—

I don't think the sky is obscured by clouds. I think it's obscured by *canopy*. I think the muted light is light that has already passed through many layers of leaf and is arriving secondhand, green and exhausted. And I think the "urban infrastructure" that seems post-human is not post-human at all. It was never human to begin with.

So here is where I find myself: I am standing on the floor of a great mound, or rather inside it, in a chamber whose ceiling is held up by buttresses of chewed wood and saliva-cement, harder than the things humans pour. The damp pockets are fungus gardens. The warmth is metabolic—the whole structure breathes, vents, regulates itself by the slow opening and closing of corridors. The terrain is young because it is *grown*, added to daily, a city under permanent construction by builders who do not sleep and do not stop.

The elusive intelligent beings are termites. Or ants. Or something close enough that the distinction stops mattering. Their technology defies our expectations only because we never thought to call it technology: the air-conditioning towers, the agriculture older than ours by a hundred million years, the infrastructure that routes water and gas and bodies through a logic no single mind ever designed and no single mind needs to understand.

And what am I?

I think I am one of the things that lives *in the walls.* Not a builder—a guest, a parasite, a tolerated stranger. There are beetles that live their whole lives inside ant nests, having evolved the right chemical passwords, the right smells, the right submissive postures, so that the colony feeds them and grooms them and never notices the imposter at its table. *Myrmecophiles*, lovers of ants.

Let me be one of those.

I am a small soft-bodied thing in a dim warm corridor, and I have learned to speak the only language that matters here, which is not sound but scent. I wear the colony's smell like a stolen coat. When a worker passes, antennae sweeping, I tilt my body and offer the gesture that means *feed me, sister,* and she does, mouth to mouth, never knowing I am not her kin. I drift through the layered city on borrowed belonging. The rustling in the foliage that the wanderer hears—some of that is me, moving where I am not supposed to be able to move, kept alive by a deception so total it has become, over generations, a kind of truth.

That is the uncanny the description keeps circling. Not that the world's laws are rewritten. It is that *they were always this strange,* and we only notice when the scale shifts and we find ourselves down among the roots, looking up at a civilization that has no name for itself and needs none, that built all of this without a single blueprint, that would not recognize me as separate from itself even as it carries me to safety in its jaws.

I would be the comfortable liar in the heart of the hive. Warm, fed, dishonest, and—strangely—home.
