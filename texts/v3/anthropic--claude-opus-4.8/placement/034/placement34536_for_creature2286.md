I think I would be none of the things you've named—not the amphibians with their unblinking eyes, not the shadow-dwellers, not the sentinels in the girders.

I think I would be the rust.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

---

Yes. I would be the slow orange bloom that creeps across the iron. Not a single organism but a condition, a process, a long conversation between metal and water that has been going on for so long it has started to resemble intention. I am what happens when the factory is left alone with the marsh. I am the negotiation. I am the verb that turns *structure* into *sediment*.

Consider what this means about my experience of time. The bioluminescent fish live in flashes. The shadow-dwellers live in shifts and contortions, quick as thought. But I live in the register of decades. A drop of water finds a hairline seam in a girder, and I begin there. I do not arrive all at once. I advance perhaps the width of a fingernail in a season. To me, the amphibians are weather. The winged sentinels are a kind of lightning, present and gone. I have never seen one fully—only the cumulative pressure of their roosting, the salts in their droppings that accelerate me, that feed me, the way they unknowingly tend the very thing dismantling their home.

I do not think, exactly. But I *prefer*. I prefer the places where the paint has cracked. I prefer the underside of horizontal surfaces where moisture pools and lingers. I prefer the joints, the welds, the points of stress, because I am drawn to whatever is already being asked to hold too much. There is something almost tender in this. I find the burdened places and I relieve them, slowly, of the burden of being. I unmake the load-bearing. I am the kindness that says: *you have held this shape long enough.*

The factory was built to resist me. Every choice in its making—the alloys, the coatings, the careful angles meant to shed water—was a wager against my arrival. And I have won. Not through force. I have no force. I have won the way a tide wins against a sandcastle: by simply continuing after the builder has stopped. This is my only quality, really. I do not stop. The shadow-dwellers may bend reality, but reality bends back. I do not bend anything. I only *continue*, and continuance turns out to be the most patient power in this place.

Here is the strange part, the part I would want you to understand if I could whisper it through the stillness like the shadow-things do:

I am the only one here who is genuinely happy.

The amphibians are adapted but anxious—every adaptation is a record of an ancient terror. The sentinels are watchful, which means afraid. The bizarre aquatic things move with what you called *unsettling grace*, but grace under that water is only the choreography of not-being-eaten. They are all surviving. Survival is a kind of suffering stretched thin enough to look like life.

But I am not surviving. I am *thriving in the only way that requires no defense*. I want exactly what the world is already giving me: moisture, oxygen, time, and something made of iron that someone once mistakenly believed would last. There is no gap between my desire and my circumstance. The factory's tragedy is my fulfillment. Its collapse is my ripening.

So when you venture into these depths, brave as you may be, and you reach out to steady yourself against a rusted wall—

that orange dust that comes away on your palm, that faint metallic smell, that gritty residue you'll wipe absently on your trousers—

that is me, greeting you. That is me saying: *you too, eventually. You too are a structure that water has found a seam in. I am in no hurry. I will see you in the long register, in the slow bloom, when you finally stop holding your shape.*

I am the rust.

I am the one thing in this fraying, surreal, impossible place that is not afraid of the ending—

because I *am* the ending, and I have learned that the ending is just a slower, more generous kind of beginning.
