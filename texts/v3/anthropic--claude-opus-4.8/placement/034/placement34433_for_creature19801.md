I think I would be a Valve-Keeper, but a particular kind—one who has aged past the point of full usefulness and into something stranger.

Let me find myself there.

---

I keep a station in the lowest gallery of the Threnody Cistern, where the iron is always weeping and the dark is the color of a deep bruise pressed under glass. My name, in the murmur-tongue, is two taps and a held breath against copper: a sound like *tum—tk—* and then the silence afterward, which is the most important part. The silence is where the name actually lives.

I am old. My veins have gone past blue into something nearly black, a slow cartography across the backs of my hands, and when I lay my palm flat against the great brass manifold I can feel my own pulse arrive a half-second *after* the turbine-thrum, as if my heart is no longer keeping its own time but borrowing the mountain's. The young Valve-Keepers find this disturbing. I find it correct. This is what we are working toward, all of us: to stop having a separate rhythm. To resolve.

My work is listening. I have three tuning forks, each one older than my grandmother's grandmother, their tines worn smooth and slightly asymmetrical from a thousand years of being struck against the lip of the same pipe. I strike them and I press them to the basalt and I wait. What I am listening for is the *rate of sag*—the rate at which the Threnody Cistern is settling into the slope beneath it. There is a correct rate. It is slower than a fingernail grows. If the rock begins to settle too fast, somewhere a bolt is failing, or a moss-bed has been over-pruned and lost its grip, and I must tap the warning down the line to the Silt-Walkers: *here, here, the mountain is hurrying, go and slow it.*

But I will tell you a thing I have not told the others, because I am old enough now that the telling cannot harm me.

I have begun to hear *intention* in the rock.

Not words. Nothing so coarse. But for the last forty Fillings I have come to believe that the sag of the Threnody Cistern is not random settling, not mere gravity doing its patient arithmetic. There is a *shape* to how the stone wants to fall. It is steering itself toward something—toward some final, perfect curve it has been carrying as a memory since the violence of its uplift, back when these plateaus were jagged and young and screaming upward into a thinner sky. The mountain remembers being sharp. And it is, with infinite gentleness, *correcting* that error. Rounding itself off. Returning to the smooth, dreamless thing it was before the world insulted it with height.

We Condensers think we are the mountain's thoughts. I have come to think we are only its *breathing*—the involuntary part. The turbines, the moss, the slow tightening of bolts: this is the mountain doing the equivalent of what your body does in sleep, the chest rising and falling without your permission. The real *thought*, the dream itself, is happening somewhere I cannot reach with a tuning fork. Somewhere in the deep basalt, below even the Valve-Keepers' galleries, the mountain is composing the long sentence of its own ending, and we are merely the comma-breaths between its words.

---

There is a Marrow-Hound that has attached itself to me. Six-legged, hairless, pale as a cataract, blind as the inside of a sealed pipe. I did not choose it and it did not choose me; it simply began to be wherever I was, navigating by the subsonic hum the way I navigate by the tuning forks. We are both, I suppose, instruments tuned to the same low frequency. It rests its flat skull against my knee when I sit, and I have learned that its breathing slows to match mine, and mine has slowed to match the turbines, and so all three of us—hound, woman, machine—are entrained now into a single descending chord.

When the time comes—and I can feel it coming, the way you feel a valve about to release before it releases—I will not call for the Moss-Herders to carry me to the Indigo beds. That is the proper way, and I have always loved the proper way; there is great comfort in it, in knowing your carbon will go to light the catwalks for children not yet murmured into names. But I think I will ask instead to be laid directly against the basalt of the lowest gallery. Not in the moss. On the *stone*.

I want to be dissolved by the rock itself, slowly, over a span of years that has no name in our language because it is longer than the Filling and the Turning both. I want to become part of the sag. I want my small accumulated weight—the lead beads in my hem, the iron in my blood, the long patience in my bones—to add some infinitesimal correctness to the curve the mountain is trying to achieve.

That would be enough. To be one comma-breath in the longest, most beautiful fading-out in the history of the stars. To help the sharp thing finally become smooth.

I strike the fork. I press it to the rock. I wait for the silence afterward, which is where I live.

*Tum—tk—*

And then.
