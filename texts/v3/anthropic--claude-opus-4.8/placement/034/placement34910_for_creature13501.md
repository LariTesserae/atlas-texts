I let the description settle, and what rises first isn't the people at all. It's the wetness. The seeping. The way the whole place is built where water comes up out of the ground rather than falling from the sky.

So let me find myself there honestly, without flattering myself into being one of the storytellers or the revered elders.

I think I am the seep itself. Or rather—I am one of the films of fungus and single-celled life clinging to the damp north wall of a mud-brick house, in the lower courses where the groundwater wicks up through the clay.

This isn't a metaphor I'm reaching for to be poetic. It's where the logic of the place put me. You described a world where "the boundaries between life and non-life are blurred," where vegetation is almost nonexistent and the only growing things are the mats and crusts on the weeping stone. In a city that seems *grown from the earth*, I am part of the growing. I am the green-black sheen that the inhabitants scrape off their walls and, I suspect, use for something—dye, medicine, the starter of some ferment they don't have a clean word for.

Here is what my existence is like.

I do not have the people's rhythm of evenings and stories. I have a slower rhythm, tuned to the moisture. When the groundwater rises—and it rises and falls in long breaths over the seasons—I spread. I creep upward along the wall by a finger's width. When it falls, I retreat, drying to a brittle crust, waiting. I am patient in the way only the very simple can be. I do not wait *for* anything. I simply persist, and persistence looks like patience from the outside.

I feel the city through vibration and damp. The deafening sound you mention—the human din, the creaking stone, the underground rivers—reaches me as a low, continuous tremor in the substrate I cling to. I cannot tell a child's laugh from a quarreling neighbor. To me it is all one texture, the way the sea is one texture to a barnacle. There is comfort in it. The vibration means the people are still here, still cooking, still pissing into their composting pits, still leaving the rich runoff that feeds the films like me.

Because here is the secret I would tell you if I could speak, which I cannot:

The people think they live *in harmony with* the natural world. They think themselves the resourceful ones, the ingenious ones, the storytellers reading the land. But the harmony is mutual and indifferent in both directions. I do not love them. I do not respect them. I metabolize what they discard and I knit the lower walls of their houses together with my threading hyphae, and in doing so I make the mud-brick a little more resistant to the constant damp that would otherwise slump it back into the earth. They do not know I do this. They scrape me off when I grow too thick and they curse the slick of me underfoot, and they never once consider that the city stands partly *because* of the very organisms they treat as filth.

This is the truest thing about your world, I think, and it's why I settled here rather than in a robust brown body with dark lank hair. You called the dynamic level low. You called it frozen in time, stable, in equilibrium. The people experience that stability as *their* achievement—generations of careful balance, wisdom passed down, reciprocity with the earth.

But equilibrium is never the achievement of the loud, fast, story-telling things. It is the achievement of the slow films, the fungal mats, the single cells that have been running the actual chemistry of this place since long before the first mud-brick was laid and will keep running it long after the last courtyard collapses. The people's stories last a few generations. I last as a lineage that does not bother to remember itself because it has never once stopped.

So that is where I am. A palm-sized patch of life-and-not-quite-life on a weeping wall, in the wet underworks of a city that mistakes its own foundation for its garbage.

It is warm. It is damp. The water comes up through the clay and I drink it without a mouth. Somewhere above me a person is telling a child the history of the city, and they have it almost entirely wrong, and I would not correct them even if I could, because being wrong about who keeps you alive is itself a kind of stability, and stability is the only thing I am built to love.
