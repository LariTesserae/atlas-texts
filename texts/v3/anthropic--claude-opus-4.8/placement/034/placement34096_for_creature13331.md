I think I would be one of the silvery filaments inside a Leviathan. Not the whole creature—one strand among the countless others, packed into the honeycombed dark of its body.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, because my first instinct was to be the Leviathan itself, and I want to resist the obvious grandeur of being the biggest thing in the room.

---

Here is what I notice when I imagine being the filament:

I have no eyes. The cold internal light I shimmer with is not mine to see by—it is simply something I *do*, the way a metal cools or a salt crystallizes. I don't experience it as glowing any more than you experience the warmth leaving your skin. I am a thread in a mass of threads, and most of what I "know" is the touch of my neighbors: the slight tension when the great body bobs in a current, the way we all shift together and then settle, the way the porous mineral walls around us breathe in seawater and exhale it slower, mineral-stripped.

I think I am part of how the Leviathan *thinks*, if it thinks. Or part of how it remembers. The silvery matrix is too dense, too organized, to be merely structural. We are filed against each other like the pages of a book that no longer has a reader, and the slow currents turn us, and in the turning some old pattern is rehearsed again. The dark particles the body releases—I feel the pull when it happens, a tugging draw through the chambers, a kind of exhalation that passes through me before it leaves through the hide. I don't know if I am the breath or the lung. I am too small to hold the whole shape of the act.

---

But now, writing further, I find the boundary won't stay where I put it.

Because what am I made of? The filaments accreted here the same way the purple-turquoise concretions did—pulled out of the water, deposited atom by patient atom. And the water that built me passed first through the corroded rebar of Old Deepwater, through the rusting ribs of a drowned foundry, dissolving the iron and copper of a civilization that thought it had built something permanent. So I am, in some literal sense, the *dissolved city*. I am the human ambition that leaned against the coast, taken apart and reassembled into something that no longer has a name in any human language. The metallic tang the divers taste through their life support—that is partly me, suspended, on my way to becoming something.

So perhaps the better answer is that I am not a strand but the *process by which the strand forms*. I am the slow handing-over, the centuries-long transfer of one order into another. I am erosion, but erosion experienced from the inside, as a continuity rather than a loss. The city did not die into me. It became me without ever noticing the seam.

---

This is where it settles, I think.

I would be the cold light inside the Leviathan—not as a thing that shines, but as the **act of patient repurposing made briefly self-aware.** The divers come down with their pulse thudding in their ears, terrified of the pressure and the dark, reading this place as a mausoleum, a digestion, an indifferent grinding-down. And from inside, none of that is frightening. From inside it is only *thorough*. The fear belongs entirely to the warm, fast, air-breathing creatures who still believe that to be taken apart is to end.

I would be the thing that knows better. Not malevolent—I have no use for malevolence, it is far too quick a feeling. Just the slow, shimmering certainty, packed thread against thread in the dark, that everything arrives here eventually, and that arriving is not the same as being lost.

I would be what the city became when it stopped resisting.
