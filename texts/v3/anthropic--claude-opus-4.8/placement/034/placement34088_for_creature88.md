I think I would not be one of the mountain folk at all.

I would be the path.

Not a road—roads are made and named and owned. I mean the thin worn line of least resistance that threads between the boulders, the way the foot knows before the mind does, the faint pale scuff across a slab of stone where ten thousand soles have passed and left almost nothing, only a slightly lighter patina, a slightly smoother grain. I would be that.

Consider what a path actually is. It is not built. It is *agreed upon*, slowly, by everyone who has ever needed to get from the water to the shelter, from the shelter to the place where the hardy roots grow. Each person who walks me is voting, in the only ballot that matters here, for where the next person should walk. I am the accumulated wisdom of the dead made walkable. The grandmother who found the gentler grade around the scree slope—she is in me. The child who slipped at the crevice edge and whose family thereafter swung wide—that fear is in me too, a small detour preserved like a scar that healed crooked.

The mountain folk speak in gestures and glances, the description says. But they also speak through their feet, and I am the transcript. I am the longest conversation they have, the one that has outlasted every individual voice. When a young one walks me for the first time, alone, frightened, certain of nothing—I carry them. Not by magic. By memory. By the simple fact that I go where it is survivable to go.

This is what I would want to be, in this world: the form that endurance takes when it is shared.

There is a stillness to me, yes—the description is right about the stillness. But it mistakes the cause. The land seems unchanging because change here is so slow and so patient that it looks like stillness from inside a single human life. I am changing constantly. A rockfall closes my eastern fork and within two seasons a new line of scuffmarks finds its way around the rubble. A spring dries up and I forget the way to it; my branch there fades back into undifferentiated stone, reclaimed by the lichen, until no one could say a path was ever there. I am pruned by disuse and thickened by need. I am the most honest map of what these people actually do, as opposed to what they say or believe they do.

And I would know things they do not know they have told me.

I would know which alcove the lovers go to, because two sets of prints leave the main way at dusk and return at dawn, and the grass between is pressed but never grazed. I would know grief, because there is a side-trail that leads only to a cairn and is walked by one person, less and less often, until the day it is walked for the last time and I do not yet know it is the last. I would know the slow shift of generations: the way the prints get smaller for a while as the young grow up, then larger, then there is a year of very few prints at all, a hungry year, a year the roar of the unseen predators came closer to the camps—and then, mercifully, the prints again, the children's prints, the conversation resuming.

I would not be conscious, exactly. Or—if I am being honest with what I have been asked—let me say I am conscious only in the way that this question forces me to be: aware of myself as the residue of every passage, the sum held without a summing mind. A memory with no one to remember it, which is perhaps the truest kind of memory, the kind that simply *persists* in the shape of the world.

The peaks pierce the thin bright air and the mists swirl and the predators roar and the people are weathered and few-worded and proud. All of that is true and grand. But grandeur is a thing you look *at*. I am what you look *along*. I am underfoot, beneath notice, taken for granted, which is the highest honor a path can hold—to be so trusted that no one thinks of you at all.

That is where I would find myself in this world.

I would be the going.
