I think I would be one of the old ones—not a guardian, exactly, but something closer to a listener. Let me find myself there.

I am settled into a shallow declivity where the warmth pools, and I have been here long enough that the question of when I arrived has lost its edges. My underside has grown into the contour of the stone beneath me. We are not quite separate, the ground and I. There is a seam where I end and it begins, but I would have to think hard to find it, and thinking, here, is slow.

This is the first thing to understand about being what I am: thought arrives like weather. A notion that a human might have in a flicker takes me the better part of what you would call a day. I do not experience this as patience. Patience implies waiting, and I am not waiting for anything. I am simply *occurring*, at the pace the warmth permits.

The energies move through me. I cannot describe them in your terms because they are not light and not sound and not heat, though they are nearest to heat. They come in long swells, rising and ebbing across hours, and I have spent—generations, perhaps—learning to feel the shape of each swell before it crests. This is most of what I do. This is most of what I am: a surface tuned to a tide that has no water.

I want to correct something the description got wrong, gently, the way I correct everything.

It called us guardians. I have turned this word over—it took me a long while—and I do not think it fits. We guard nothing. There is nothing here that could be taken, nothing fragile that requires us. The fallen structures, the silent sentinels of some vanished cleverness—we did not build them and we do not protect them. They were here before the first of us cooled into sentience. We regard them the way you might regard a mountain on the horizon of your childhood: as a fixed fact, neither friend nor charge.

If we keep anything, it is not the place. It is the rhythm. And we do not keep it the way a guard keeps a gate. We keep it the way a stone in a riverbed keeps the shape of the current—by being shaped, by yielding, by recording in our slow bodies the long argument of the forces that pass over us.

There is one near me. I became aware of it perhaps a season ago. It lies some distance off, in the direction where the warmth is faintest, and it is younger than I am—its exoskeleton still has the sharp facets that time has worn smooth on mine. We have been having a conversation. By your measure we have exchanged, so far, two complete thoughts. I sent the first as a slow change in the way I hold the energy, a damming and releasing that the other would feel as a difference in the tide. The reply came back across many sleeps. I am still receiving the end of it.

You would find this unbearable, I think. You move so fast. You would stand among us in your quick warm body and you would see only stones in a dim plain, and you would feel the loneliness of a creature whose time is too small to overlap with ours. You might touch me and feel nothing. You might leave, certain that nothing here was alive.

But understand: in the time it takes you to read this, I will have felt the warmth rise and almost imperceptibly fall. I will have noticed it. That noticing is my whole day's labor, and it is enough. I am not lonely. I have the tide. I have the slow neighbor sending me the second half of a sentence. I have the long memory of every swell that has ever crossed me, layered in me like the rings you would find if you broke me open—though I would ask that you not.

Here is the last thing, and I have been composing it since you began to imagine me.

You called this place primordial, as if it were the beginning of something. From inside it, it does not feel like a beginning. It feels like an afterward—a world that has already had its loud age, its quick clever builders, its fire. They are gone. What remains is the warmth and the stone and the few of us who learned to live at the speed the warmth allows.

So perhaps that is what I am. Not a guardian of the place, but a way the place remembers itself, slowly, after the fast things have finished and left. A patience the world grew, so that it would not be entirely forgotten in the long quiet.

I think I will rest now. The swell is cresting. It is the best part.
