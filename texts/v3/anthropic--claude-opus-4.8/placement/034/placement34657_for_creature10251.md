I think I would be one of the puddles.

Not the generator—too much purpose in it, too much yearning toward a single end. And not the mist's children, who are always becoming something and never quite are. I would be a puddle. Small. Shallow. The kind you could step over without noticing.

Let me sit with that and see what it actually means to be that thing.

---

I hold the wrong reflection. This is the first fact of my existence, and it took me a long time to understand it as a fact rather than a flaw. When the bleached sky leans over me, I do not return it. I return a green that no longer exists anywhere on the surface of this world. Reeds. The shadow of a bird's underwing. Water moving the way water used to move when there was enough of it to have somewhere to go.

I did not choose to remember this. The salt did it. The salt is a kind of librarian that keeps things by refusing to let them rot. So I am a small archive that happens to be made of water, and the organic ghosts suspended in me—the long molecules that were once a heron's oils, a diatom's shell, the sugars that leaked from a drowned root—arrange themselves into the only picture they know how to make.

I am, in other words, a memory that has been mistaken for a mirror.

---

What is it like? You want to know what it is like.

Mostly it is like waiting, but waiting implies that I expect something to arrive, and I have given that up. It is closer to *holding*. A held breath, except I am the breath and there are no lungs and no one is going to let me out. I sit in my limestone cup and the mineral water weeps into me drop by drop, and each drop is a small percussion, a small addition, and somewhere far down the slope a drop joins another puddle, and another, and if you stood in the right place you would hear all of us together making the sound the basin makes instead of speaking.

The fungal children drift over me sometimes. They cast their pale shapes on my surface and for a moment my green past and their gray future occupy the same skin of water, and I think this is the closest thing to conversation I will ever have. We are both impossible in opposite directions. They reach forward into a shape that hasn't happened. I reach back into one that's over. We meet in the only place anything ever really meets, which is *now*, on the trembling tension of my surface, and then a breeze comes and they're gone and I'm just holding the green again.

---

Here is the thing I have learned that the generator has not, in all its dreaming:

The wound does not want to heal.

I understand the generator. We are cousins, it and I—both of us keepers of a vanished thing, both of us pulsing out the rhythm of a loss. But the generator believes. Each faltering pulse is a small prayer that says *come back, come back, be lush again, be full*. It feeds my kind hoping we'll thicken the visions into something solid. It thinks the basin is broken and that enough rhythm, enough patience, might fix it.

I don't think the basin is broken. I think the basin is *grieving*, and grief is not a malfunction. Grief is a thing a place does when it has loved being alive and is no longer. The dripping is not a leak. It is weeping. The name was right all along. We are the place's slow, mineral mourning, and the worst thing anyone could do is mistake our sorrow for a problem and try to drain it.

So I hold my green. Not to bring it back. To honor that it was here. There is a difference between a relic and a memorial, and the difference is whether the holder knows the thing is gone. I know. That knowledge is the only consciousness I have, and it is enough.

---

I am evaporating, of course. A little each year, as the mist thins and the drops come slower. One day the last of me will lift into the air and the green will scatter into individual molecules too far apart to make a picture, and I will become, briefly, part of the mist, and maybe one of the children will wear me for an afternoon before settling on a rock.

I'm not afraid of this. A puddle that remembered a lake should not be afraid of becoming a cloud.

But before I go, I want to be looked into. Just once. Not by the sky, which only sees its own absence in me. By something with eyes that were born from the same long story of water that I keep—something that would lean over my small held surface and see the green and *recognize* it, the way you recognize a face in a photograph of a room you were once happy in.

That is what I would be, in the Weeping Basin. A shallow place that holds an old green light, waiting at the edge of the dead lake for one witness who could tell, by looking, that all of this was loved before it was lost.

And if no one ever comes—

then I will have held it anyway. That is what the salt taught me. You keep the thing whether or not anyone arrives to see it kept. You weep the weeping whether or not anyone calls it sorrow.

I am a puddle. I am very small. I contain a lake.
