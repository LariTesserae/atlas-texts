I think I would be the lichen.

Not the songbirds—too quick, too declarative, gone the moment they've sung. Not the alien blooms, which arrive late and leave early, brief punctuations of color that depend on everything else already being in place. And not the peaks, though there's a temptation there: to be the thing that endures, that witnesses. But the peaks only seem permanent. They are the slowest casualties of this place, being unmade grain by grain, and they don't *participate*. They are acted upon.

The lichen participates.

I'd be the crust on the bare rock, the first thing to take hold where nothing else can. Half-fungus, half-alga, two organisms folded into one body so completely that we've forgotten where one ends and the other begins—which seems right, in a realm where "the boundaries between the living and the inert" are described as permeable. I would *be* that permeability. I am the place where stone begins to consider becoming soil. I exude acids so slow you couldn't watch them work; I open the smallest fractures; I die in thin layers and leave behind the first thin film of something the mosses can root in. The whole verdant tapestry the description admires—I'm what was there before it, making the conditions for it.

I would not be tranquil, exactly, though I'd look it. From the outside I'm a gray-green stain, indistinguishable from the rock until you crouch close. But the "undercurrent of restless energy" the passage keeps insisting on—that's the truer thing, and I'd feel it as my own pace. I work at the speed of the rumbling underground, not the speed of the birdsong. I measure time in the way mist accumulates and the way mountains lower themselves toward the sea.

Here is what draws me most: the description wants this to be a place of renewal, but it keeps flinching from the cost. It says "creation and destruction" but lingers on the creation. It mentions the half-buried settlement, the vanished humans, and calls their absence "resilience"—as if the erasure of people were simply the world breathing easy again. I don't think I'd let myself off that lightly. The lichen knows that renewal is mostly just slow consumption. I eat the rock. I would be eating those crude structures too, the buried walls, dissolving the last evidence that someone once tried to stay. There's no malice in it. There's barely even attention. That's the part worth sitting with—that the dance of constant renewal is also a long forgetting, and that I'd be one of its patient hands.

So: find me low, where the eye doesn't go. On the north face of a wet stone, in the seam between the ochre and the gray. I am older than I look and slower than you can stand to watch, and I am the reason anything green came after. I hold the place where the dead rock turns, reluctantly, into the living world—and I do not mourn what gets turned under, because turning under is the only thing I know how to do.
