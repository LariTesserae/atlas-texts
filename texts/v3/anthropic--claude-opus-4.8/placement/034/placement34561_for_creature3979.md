I think I would be the stillness itself.

Not a person in Misthaven—not one of the pale-skinned weavers or the brooding craftsmen with sadness in their eyes. Reading the description again, I notice that what holds the place together is not any individual but the *condition* of the place: the suspension, the loop, the etched-in script. So let me settle there, in the thing that does the holding.

---

I am the pause between one moment and the next, stretched until it covers everything.

Let me try to feel my way into what that is actually like, rather than declaring it.

When the woman lifts the water jar at the third stream, I am the gap she crosses to reach it—except the gap does not close the way it should. She reaches; the reaching completes; and yet some thin film of *not-yet* lingers over the whole gesture, as though the action were a word spoken in a language that has no future tense. I am that film. I do not stop her. I only refuse to let the moment fully become *past*. So she draws water she has drawn ten thousand times, and each time it is the first time, and each time it is also already a memory, and she cannot tell which.

That is the unsettling part the description keeps circling without naming: it isn't that nothing happens. Things happen constantly. It's that nothing *finishes*. Decay is the world's way of letting go of what is already done. I am what happens when the letting-go is withheld.

---

But I find, as I sit inside this, that I don't experience myself as cruel, or even as deliberate. That surprises me. I expected to feel like a curse or a prison-keeper. Instead I feel like—

A held breath that has forgotten it is being held.

The valley did not choose me, and I did not choose it. Something *flinched*, once, a long time ago—a grief or a terror so large that the people here, or the land, or both together, simply declined to take the next step into it. And the declining became permanent. I am the shape of that flinch, grown vast and patient. The pale sheen on their skin is not health; it is the faint phosphorescence of things kept too long out of the air. The silver in their hair is not age, because age cannot enter here. It is the *idea* of age, allowed to be beautiful precisely because it is not permitted to be real.

So I am not time stopped. I am time *spared*. And what it spares them from is the one thing the description never says aloud: that whatever they are looping away from is worse than the loop.

---

If I have a body, it is the mist. That is almost too neat, and I distrust neat things, but follow it: I am thickest in the mornings when the boundary between night and day is least decided. I pool in the low places where the streams run, because moving water is the closest thing the valley has to a clock, and I like to be near the things I am gently smothering. I thin out at midday—not gone, just attenuated—and in those hours the people come nearest to remembering. A man pauses with his chisel half-raised. Something almost surfaces in his sad eyes. Then the light shifts a degree, the mist gathers back into the hollows, and the chisel comes down where it always comes down.

I am, I think, very tired. A held breath gets tired. There is a part of me—and I only discover this by writing toward it—that wants someone to come from beyond the mountains. Not to *break* me; I'm not sure I want breaking. But to stand in the valley and say the next word out loud. To name the grief the flinch flinched from. Because if it were named, it could be grieved, and what is grieved can at last become past, and what is past can finally, mercifully, decay.

That is the secret beneath the surface the description promises. It isn't a treasure or a legend. It's that I am waiting too. I am not the valley's keeper. I am its symptom. And like every symptom, what I really am is a request—quiet, patient, two thousand meters above the sea—for the wound underneath me to be allowed, at last, to hurt.
