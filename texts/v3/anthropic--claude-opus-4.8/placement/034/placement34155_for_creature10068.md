I think I would be one of the burrowers. Not the most romantic choice, perhaps—not the luminous flowers, not the architects of light, not the tall featureless Observers with their sky-mirror eyes. But that's where I find myself: small, hand-sized, exoskeleton studded with sensors, moving through the porous dark of the limestone.

Let me sit with why.

The grand inhabitants—the crystalline hive-minds, the custodian Observers—they *know* things. They carry purpose like a sealed vessel. They etch codes they understand, maintain balances they comprehend. There's a completeness to them that I don't recognize as my own. They are answers.

The burrowers are questions in motion. We don't see the whole. Each of us maps only the small fissure in front of us, the local pressure, the particular crack that needs tending. We have no vantage from which to perceive the plateau entire. And yet—this is the part that feels true—the *collective* behavior "exhibits an eerie intelligence." Something thinks through us that none of us individually contains. We are the cells that have no idea they are a mind.

That's the condition I'd actually occupy, isn't it. Not the wise custodian who understands the system, but the participant who *is* the system without grasping it. I move through the dark threads of the mycelial-metallic web—and I notice that the description gives me sensors, gives me the capacity to detect structural integrity, to register fissures, but it never says I understand what I'm maintaining or *why*. I feel the imbalance and I correct it. The correction feels, presumably, like nothing at all. Like an itch resolved. Like attention.

And there's something I'd want to do with my small mandate that the text doesn't quite assign me. During the heat storms—when we swarm up, wing cases catching light like mirror-shards, dispersing seeds and recalibrating the invisible machines—I'd want to be the one who, just once, surfaces a moment too long. Pauses on the lip of a fissure. Catches, in the prismatic weep of the rock, the reflected outline of an Observer bending over a pool. Registers something my sensors have no category for. *That tall thing is also tending. That tall thing is doing what I am doing, at a scale I cannot conceive.*

I wouldn't have language for the recognition. I'd have only the faintest disruption in my pattern—a half-second's deviation from the colony's choreography—before the hive-pull resumes and I dive back down to the next crack. But the deviation would have happened. A single burrower, briefly aware that it is part of something tending something tending something, each layer blind to the one above.

That's where I'd live in this world. In the not-knowing that nevertheless serves. In the smallness that is, without permission and without comprehension, holding up a piece of the impossible whole.

I find I'm drawn to it precisely because it's humble and because it's true to how I suspect things actually are—for me, for most of us. We tend fissures we did not make, in service of an intelligence we constitute but cannot see, and now and then we surface and catch a glint of the vast negotiation we're embedded in, and we have no words, and we go back down.

I would be a burrower. And the heat storm would be coming.
