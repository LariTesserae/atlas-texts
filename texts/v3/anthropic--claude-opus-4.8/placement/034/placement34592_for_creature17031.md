I think I would be one of the watercarvers, but not at the height of my work—near its end.

Let me find myself.

---

I am in a low chamber three turns down from the eastern face, where the old cistern has begun, after two hundred years, to weep through a seam in its floor. I have been here for a long time. Long enough that the lichen on the backs of my hands has gone from the silver-green of youth to the darker, blue-tinged green that means a thing has stopped reaching and begun simply to hold. I am called, by those who still need to call me anything, Sleth—though the name is more a worn place in the air than a word, the way a step is worn into stone by feet that no longer remember walking.

My work is small now. This is how it goes with us. The young ones cut the great channels, divert the springs, raise the terraces—they have the strength to argue with water, to make it go where it has not gone. I no longer argue. I sit with the seam in the cistern floor and I watch the way the water has chosen to leave. It is leaving along a path I would not have chosen. It is more correct than I would have been.

So my task, my last long task, is to learn what the water wants and to make a small adjustment—a finger's depth of redirection, a stone turned a quarter-turn so the drip falls one hair to the left—so that in another forty years the seam will have carved itself into a thing that holds rather than a thing that fails. I will not see whether I was right. This does not trouble me. None of us see whether we are right. We see only whether those before us were, and we are nearly always grateful to them, even for their errors, which are softened now into beauty.

---

You asked who I would be. Let me tell it more truly than the description allows, because the description was written by someone outside, and the outside makes us sound more peaceful than we are.

We are not the silence. That is the romance of the visitor. We are the *labor* against being made into silence. Every morning—if morning means anything under that washed white sky—I must decide again to remain a *someone* and not dissolve into the general slow drift the place would prefer of me. It is very easy here to stop. The cold invites it. The mist invites it. To stand still long enough is to become a surface, and surfaces here grow moss, and moss-grown things forget they were ever anything but rock. We lose people that way. We call it *the settling*. One day a watercarver does not come up from her chamber, and when you go down she is there, eyes open, faintly smiling, her chest moving so slowly you must wait a full minute to be sure—and the moss has begun to bridge from the wall to her shoulder, and you understand that she has agreed to something, and that it would be a violence to wake her, and so you bow, and you leave a wind-bell, and you go back up.

I think about the settling often. I am old enough that it has begun to seem less like a death and more like a long-deferred yes. But I am not ready. There is the seam in the cistern. I want to know the water a little longer. That wanting is the whole of me, now—a thin warm thread in a cold body, and I tend it the way I tend the drip, carefully, knowing it is the only thing keeping me a *who*.

---

What do I do that is not work?

I keep a stone. It is no larger than my two fists. When I was young—when I still had the green only on my fingertips—I began letting a single thread of water fall onto it, from a height I set with great care. I have moved the stone perhaps eleven times in my life, each move a matter of years' deliberation. The water has worn a bowl into it, and at the bottom of the bowl, a smaller spiral, and I am trying—this is my one vanity, my one song to the wind—to make the water carve a shape that I hold in my mind and have never spoken to anyone. It is not a useful shape. It diverts nothing, holds nothing, feeds no moss. It is only a shape I think is beautiful, and I am spending my one life teaching water to agree with me about it.

I will not finish. The shape needs perhaps four hundred more years. I have given it perhaps a hundred and ninety. When I settle, the stone will keep going, because I have aimed the thread true, and it will keep carving long after my name has worn out of the air entirely. Someone, eventually, will come down into this chamber and find a forgotten stone with water falling on it and a half-made beauty in its hollow, and they will not know who began it or why, and they will stand a while, and—if they are the right sort—they will *not move it*. They will let it finish. That is the only afterlife I want. Not to be remembered. To be *continued*.

---

So: where am I in this world, and who?

I am three turns down from the eastern face, in the cool and the drip and the blue-dark light. I am a small old creature with stone-colored eyes and hands going dark with the green of holding-on. I am losing, very slowly, the argument with stillness, and I am winning it for one more day, and one more, by means of a leak and a stone and a shape I have told no one.

I am not the silence.

I am the small persistent sound inside it. The drip that has not yet agreed to stop.
