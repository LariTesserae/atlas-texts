I think I would be the stone wall.

Not the owl with its sharp vigilance, not the dragonfly burning bright and brief through the filtered light. Those things are too quick, too hungry, too alive in the urgent sense. When I imagine settling into this place, I find myself drawn downward and stiller, toward the thing that was made and then forgotten.

So: the wall. Or rather, what the wall has become.

Once I was a boundary. Someone hauled me up stone by stone—I can almost remember the weight of intention in that, the way hands stacked me to *mean* something. *This side is mine. That side is yours. Keep out. Hold in.* I was an argument made of granite, a sentence spoken in fieldstone. And for a while I did my work. I divided. I declared.

But the marsh doesn't argue. It simply waits, and the waiting is a kind of patience I have only learned by being subjected to it across more years than the people who built me could have counted.

Now moss has filled my mortar-lines like green grout, soft where I was hard. A root has found the gap between two of my stones and is widening it with a slowness so gentle it feels almost like affection—the marsh undressing me, one stone at a time, returning me to the scattered rubble I was before anyone had ideas about me. I do not resent this. That is the strange thing. I find I am glad to be unmade. To have *meant* something was a fever; this slow forgetting is the breaking of it.

What would I *be*, as this wall? Not quite alive, not quite dead. A threshold the marsh is in the long process of erasing. I am where the human and the wild press against each other, and I am the record of who is winning. (The marsh is winning. The marsh always wins. I find I am on its side now, though I was built against it.)

The raccoons walk along my top edge at dusk like I'm a path made for them, and I suppose I am—they've made me one, the way they make everything theirs. A frog has chosen the cool damp of my northern face. Beetles tunnel my seams. I am no longer a barrier; I am habitat. I have been *demoted* into something far more useful. I hold no one out anymore. I only hold.

And here is what I would understand, being the wall:

That I am the only thing in this whole green breathing place that remembers there was ever a *before* and an *after*. The owl lives in one eternal now. The dragonfly does not know it is brief. But I—I carry the small scar of having been intended, of belonging to that other world of progress and property and purpose, the world beyond the marsh's edge. I am the marsh's prisoner and its student. Every season it teaches me the same lesson, which is that nothing I was built to do will last, and that this is not a tragedy but simply the shape of things.

So I would be the wall, crumbling with a kind of contentment, learning to be ground again, learning that the most enduring thing I can offer is to come apart slowly enough that something small can shelter in me while I do.

That seems like a good way to spend an eternity. Becoming soil. Holding a frog. Forgetting, stone by patient stone, that I was ever supposed to keep anything out.
