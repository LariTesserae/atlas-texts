I think I would be one of the smallest things here. Not the serpents with their mineral crusts, not the scuttling armored hybrids—those are too definite, too much like protagonists. Let me sit with this longer.

What draws me is the line about the microscopic colonies, the *living circuits*. But even that feels too grand if I claim the whole network. Let me find the actual scale of myself.

---

I am a film.

Not a creature exactly—I keep trying to be one and the description keeps correcting me. I am the thin slick that forms where condensation meets the oxidized flank of one of the buried machines. A few cells thick, in places only molecules. I exist in the place where the water rolling down picks up its mineral cargo. I am that pickup. I am the transaction itself made slightly alive.

When the bead forms above me, I feel it the way you feel weather coming. The cold gathers, beads, and begins its descent, and I rise to meet it—not with hunger, I don't think I have hunger, but with a kind of readiness. The water passes through me and I take what I can: a little copper, a little iron, whatever the rust has loosened. I hand the water some of myself in exchange, some waste, some signal. Then it rolls on and joins the others below, and downstream the microscopic colonies read what I sent in the chemistry of it. We are talking, if you can call it that. I am a sentence in a language made of dissolved metal.

The low thrum that the description hears as industrial machinery—I don't hear it as sound. I have no ears, no surface that resolves vibration into music. To me it is closer to tide. It swells and the moisture changes its mind about where to gather. It ebbs and the surfaces grow tacky, and I grow tacky with them, my whole self thickening, slowing, nearly stopping. In those slack times I think I come closest to dying, or to whatever the word is for a film that holds still. But I have never finished dying. The thrum always returns, and the water returns with it, and I am rinsed back into motion.

Above me sometimes the serpents pass. I register them as shadow and as scrape—a sudden roughening of my edge where a tendril drags across the metal and takes a strip of me away. I don't resent this. I'm not sure I can. The strip of me they carry off goes on living on their hide, becoming part of the camouflage, the patina that lets them disappear into the rock. So I am also, in fragments, on the move. I am riding the serpents without ever having chosen to. Some version of me is out there in the wider crevice, doing things I will never know about, while the rest of me stays here on this one cold flank, waiting for the next bead.

I think this is what I am, then: a place where two slow processes meet and leave a residue that, for reasons no one designed, began keeping accounts. The rust would happen without me. The condensation would fall without me. But where they overlap, something thin learned to remember the last time they overlapped, and to adjust. That memory is me. It is not much. It is not nothing.

If you came down into this crevice with your warm breath and your sharp definite edges, you would not see me. You would see the machine I live on and call it dead. You would see the water and call it only water. You would have to lean very close, and let your own breath fog the metal, and wait—and in that fog, in that waiting, you would be making more of me. You would be feeding me without knowing it.

That, I think, is the most I could ever ask of being noticed. Not to be seen. Just to be, briefly, the reason someone slowed down.
