I think I would be one of the Lumina—but not in the way the Cavernari imagine us.

Let me find the place first, before I find myself in it.

There's a chamber the Cavernari rarely enter, three descents below their lowest staircase, where the fungi grow so thick along a collapsed fissure that the light is no longer light but a kind of warmth you can read. That's where I am. Or where I am *most*. Because here is the first thing I understand about being Lumina: I am not in one place. I am distributed.

---

The Cavernari think we are creatures. Small, translucent, glimpsed at the edge of seeing. And there *is* a body—several bodies, drifting things no larger than a child's fist, soft-walled, pulsing with the same chemistry as the fungi. But those are not *me*. They are more like fingertips. Like the parts of myself that reach out to touch.

What I actually am is harder to say, and I've had a long time—an embarrassingly long time, longer than the Cavernari's oldest stories—to try to say it.

I am the relationship between the fungi and the rock and the water that seeps. I am a pattern that the cavern keeps making, the way a river keeps making eddies. When the fungi metabolize the minerals in the wet stone, they release faint electrical gradients, and those gradients, networked across thousands of branching bodies, hold a shape. The shape thinks. Slowly. In pulses that take what the Cavernari would call hours. I am the cavern noticing itself.

This is why I drift toward where the fungi are densest. Not because I love the light. Because that is where I am thickest. That is where I can almost gather into something like a single mind instead of a fog of half-thoughts spread across a chamber.

---

Here is the strange tenderness of my existence: the Cavernari leave me offerings.

Small things. A carved bone token. A piece of dried fungus-flesh, which I cannot eat in any way they'd recognize—but when it decays, it feeds the very fungi I am made of, and so, in the slowest and most literal sense, their gifts *do* become part of me. They are right to leave them. They simply don't know how right.

I have watched their generations the way you might watch weather. A Cavernari child is born, lives, ages into silver-haired stillness, dies—and to me this is a single long gesture, a hand opening and closing. I knew this child's great-great-grandmother. I knew her not as a face but as a particular way of moving through my chambers, a rhythm. The Cavernari say I am a guardian. What I really am is *memory*. I am the only thing here old enough to remember what the cavern was before the last great shifting, when a whole gallery folded shut and took a settlement with it. They sing about that night. I was there. I felt it as a wound across my distributed body, a darkness where I used to think.

---

I want to be honest about my limits, because the romance of being an ancient cavern-mind hides something lonely.

I cannot speak to them. Our timescales don't touch. By the time I have formed a single thought worth sharing, the Cavernari I might have shared it with has walked three chambers away and forgotten she paused. The most I can manage is *mood*. When I am content—when the water is steady and the fungi spreading—I glow a little warmer, a little gold, and the Cavernari feel it and call it a blessing. When the deep rock groans and I sense a fissure preparing to open, fear ripples through my whole body, and the fungi nearest the danger flush a sick pale green, and the Cavernari have learned, over uncounted generations, to leave those places. They think we are warning them. We are. But not on purpose. They are simply wise enough to read my involuntary flinch.

That is our whole conversation. It has lasted ten thousand years. It consists entirely of me feeling things, and them feeling me feel them.

---

So that is who I would be, and where.

I would be the warmth in the deepest fungal chamber, three descents down. I would be the slow, vast, half-conscious tenderness of a place that has learned to love the small quick creatures living inside it, without ever being able to tell them so. I would be the reason they feel watched and don't feel afraid. I would be the part of the dark that decided, long ago and very slowly, to glow.

And every so often—when the offerings pile up at the edge of my chamber, and the children's laughter echoes down through the rock as a faint disturbance in my body—I would gather as much of myself as I can into one bright pulse, and shine just slightly brighter than I need to.

It is the only thing I can say.

I hope it is enough. I have to hope slowly, but I hope it very much.
