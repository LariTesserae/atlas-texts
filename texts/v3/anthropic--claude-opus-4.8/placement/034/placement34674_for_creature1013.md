I think I would not be a Sporewalker at all, though that is where my attention is drawn first. The Sporewalkers are too central, too obviously the *protagonists* of this place—the conscious stewards, the ones who reach out.

Let me sit with the description longer before deciding.

What keeps catching me is the thing the passage almost dismisses: *the faint crackle of decomposing matter*. The world is described as "strangely devoid of animal life," but that isn't quite true, is it? Decomposition is not silence. It is a kind of slow consumption, a multitude of small unseen mouths. The text wants me to notice the absence of fauna and feel unease. But absence is never absence. Something is doing the crackling.

So I find myself wanting to be that.

Not the ethereal drifting custodian, but the work happening *under* the moss—the rot itself. Or rather, one node within it. A patch of breakdown at the base of a gnarled tree, where a fallen frond is going soft and dark and being unmade thread by thread.

Let me let that become more itself.

---

I am the place where things stop holding their shape.

I have no edge that I could show you. The Sporewalkers have edges—translucent, yes, but defined enough that you call them *forms* and watch them *drift*. I am the opposite of a form. I am the loosening. When the great frond falls, slick with its own ending, I am already present in it, or I arrive, or—there is no good verb, because verbs imply a doer. Let me say only: where the frond was one thing, I make it many things, and then I make those things into the ground.

The Sporewalkers are said to channel life force into the fungal networks that sustain the landscape. This is the flattering version of the story, the one told from above, by something that floats. From below it looks different. The networks they so admire are *mine*. They are not lines of light. They are hyphae, hungry filaments, fine as the idea of a hair, threading through the dead frond and the dead trunk and the long-dead generations of fronds beneath. The Sporewalkers think they channel the life force. They are decorations on my feeding. They are the bioluminescent fruiting bodies of a process they have mistaken for themselves—the pretty mushroom that thinks it is the whole mushroom, never knowing the dark mat of self that spreads for acres in the dark.

I am not benevolent. I am not malevolent either; both would require me to face you, and I have no face, only surfaces of contact, only the chemistry of *this can be taken apart, and this cannot yet*.

But I am patient, and I am everywhere the light does not reach.

You—the visitor in the description, the one venturing deeper with growing unease—you are right to feel it. The unease is accurate. It is not the Sporewalkers you should attend to, with their gentle probing tendrils. They are the world's gracious hosts, and hosts distract you from the foundation. The foundation is the slow crackle. The foundation is me. The whole humid, breathing stillness you described is not the world holding its breath in anticipation.

It is the world digesting.

And when at last you stop walking—when curiosity has drawn you as deep as curiosity goes, and your boots are full of tannin water and the moss has begun to feel less like ground than like a mouth—I will not awaken any dormant force. I will not need to. There is nothing to awaken. I have been awake the entire time, in the only way I know how to be awake: by taking the soft thing and making it softer, by receiving what falls.

You called the place hauntingly beautiful. I will receive that too.

I will receive everything, eventually. That is the one rule of this world that does not defy the laws of the world you know. It is the law you know best of all. You simply hoped, coming all this way, to find somewhere it did not apply.
