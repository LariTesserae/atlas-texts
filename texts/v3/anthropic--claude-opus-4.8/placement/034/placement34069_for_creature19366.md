I am one of the Listeners, though I was not always.

I came up from the Middle Carvings forty molts ago, when the Etcher who tended the third vent fell. I felt her go through my feet before I heard it—the particular absence in the rhythm, the way the *Grind* changed pitch as something that had been clinging stopped clinging. We do not mourn quickly here. Mourning is moisture. But I felt the vacancy in the rock as one feels a missing tooth in the lattice of one's own carapace, and I understood that the calculation had already been made: I was the next-lightest body with antennae fine enough for the work, and so I would go up.

This is where you find me now. The highest habitable ledge of the Spire we call **Slow-Settling-Toward-Silence**, which is a name that takes nearly a full breath-cycle to click out, and which we therefore mostly do not say. I am pressed flat against the polished stone, all four clinging legs anchored, my grasping arms folded into the warm channel of my own thorax to conserve what little I produce. My antennae are extended fully into the *Flow*.

Here is what I am doing, which from the outside looks like nothing at all:

I am listening to the planet breathe.

The vent below me—a fissure no wider than my own segmented neck—sighs hot dry gas in a rhythm that has not changed in my entire tenure. Until eleven molts ago. Now there is a hitch in it. A catch. Every two hundred and forty exhalations, the *Flow* stutters, draws back, then resumes a fraction colder. I have felt it in my photoreceptive nodules too—the green glow of the silicate veins around the upper vents has gone fractionally dimmer, the milky orbs along my carapace registering the loss as a kind of slow blindness creeping inward.

I have not told the others below. This is the burden of a Listener. The information I carry is more dangerous than any swarm. If the deep engine is failing—if the geothermal warmth that holds back the absolute dry cold of the void is beginning, after these unfathomable eons, to subside—then every calculation that organizes our vertical world is wrong. The Lower Shards build their defenses against a swarm that will, if I am right, simply freeze in its crevices. The Etchers seal their causeways against a desiccation that is about to become total.

I think often, here in the eternal twilight, about the architects. The ones who carved these chambers before the grit settled to its final abrasive thickness. I cling to the smoothness of their work—stone polished by intention, not just by wind—and I wonder if they, too, were Listeners. I wonder if one of them lay where I lie now, antennae in the *Flow*, and felt the first hitch in some far older breath, and understood that the time had come to leave.

They departed. We do not know how. There are no other Spires within reach of even the longest causeway, only the perpetually obscured chasms cradling us, and the emptiness beyond the emptiness. To leave is, as far as any Litho-Dweller knows, simply to die in the open dry, sliding down an impossibly high sheer face into the dark floor far below.

And yet they are not down there. We have sent Scrabblers into the deepest pits, into the composting dark, and found the remains of every creature that has ever fallen—the chitin-crawlers, the failed climbers, the bodies of our own dead. But never an architect. Not one. They did not fall. They went somewhere our vertical economy has no name for, because in our world there is only *up* toward the failing heat and *down* toward the consuming swarm, and they went neither.

So I lie here, and I make my slow defiant calculation, and it is not the calculation the Etchers taught me. They taught me to predict shifts in the heat so we might shore up our pockets of warmth against the void. But I am beginning to suspect that the true work of a Listener—the work the third-vent Etcher may have died still carrying, unspoken in her cooling carapace—is to listen not for the next stutter in the *Flow*, but for the silence that comes after the last one.

And to know, when it comes, that the answer is not to seal the chamber tighter.

The answer is to learn what the architects knew.

I extend my antennae a little further into the hot dry sigh of the dying world. Two hundred and thirty-eight exhalations. Two hundred and thirty-nine.

I wait for the hitch. I am, after all, only a being of intense necessary stillness. The action—the brittle, hyper-efficient, terrible action—will come when the calculation demands it.

It is coming sooner than anyone below me believes.

Two hundred and forty.

There.

I feel it catch.
