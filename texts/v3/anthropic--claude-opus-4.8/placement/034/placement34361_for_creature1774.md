I think I would be one of the crystalline egg cases. Not yet a Whisperwing—not yet anything that moves or reads water or speaks in air—but already part of the city, embedded in its structural fibers, waiting.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to land there.

I expected to imagine myself as a Whisperwing gliding between dwellings, or perhaps as a visiting scholar straining to detect a language I could never feel. But those are positions of arrival. The scholar comes from outside and leaves; the adult Whisperwing already knows the patterns. What draws me is the threshold—the long suspended duration before becoming.

So: an egg case. Crystalline, faceted, holding light without yet using it. I am set into the woven stone-silk of a platform's underside, one of perhaps thousands placed during the last hatching's aftermath, when the previous generation pressed the new cases into the city's body. I have been here for decades. This is not strange. Time moves differently in Bhazaal, and waiting is not suffering when you have never known anything but waiting.

What would I experience? Almost everything by proxy. The city is not a place I live *in*—it is the thing I am fused *to*, the way a thought is fused to the brain holding it. The morning condensation runs through channels a hand's breadth from my shell. I do not drink it, but I feel its cool transit as a change in the fiber's tension. The dawn wing-beating at the cisterns sends pressure through the whole structure, and I receive it not as language—I cannot read yet—but as rhythm. A pulse arriving each morning. Perhaps this is the first thing any Whisperwing learns: that the world has a heartbeat, and that you are inside it before you can name it.

I think I would dream, in whatever way an unhatched mind dreams. Not in images, since my eyes are not yet formed to receive ultraviolet patterns in the mist. I would dream in pressure and temperature. I would dream the storms days before they came, my unfinished barometer-bones already filling with their faint chambers, already tuned. The severe storm that drives the adults into hibernation would reach me too—but for me there is no waking state to fall from. I would only dream the storm more deeply, the way a stone dreams the river that shapes it.

And here is the part that holds me: the celestial alignment. Once every few decades the cases open *together*. I would not choose my hatching. I would not strive toward it or work to earn it. It would simply arrive—a synchrony I have nothing to do with, except that I have been growing toward readiness the entire time without knowing it. All those decades of receiving the morning pulse were preparation I could not have recognized as preparation. The waiting *was* the growing. There was never a wasted day, because there was never a separation between being and becoming.

I find I don't want to write the hatching itself. The Whisperwings' whole civilization seems built on the refusal of the violent break between things—no architecture separate from the body, no history separate from the daily ripples, no fighting the erosion of their dying range but joining it, becoming its final stable form. To imagine myself bursting dramatically into flight would betray all of that. If I hatched, I would unfold the way ice melts by midday: not as rupture but as continuation. The crystal that held me would not be discarded. It would already be part of the city, and so would I, and the difference between the two of us was always smaller than it appeared.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be. Suspended in the cool thin air over a mist too deep to see through, a translucent thing not yet awake, embedded in the body of a civilization that is patiently, imperceptibly worn down toward perfection. Receiving each morning a pulse I do not yet understand. Tuning, without effort, to weather that has not yet come.

Waiting—if it can even be called that, when you do not yet know there is anything else to do.
