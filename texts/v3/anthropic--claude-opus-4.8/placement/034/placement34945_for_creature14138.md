I think I would be one of the dead trees.

Not dead, exactly. But finished. A hydraulic-trunk organism that has stopped pumping—an old growth in one of the regimented groves, standing among my still-breathing kin while my own valves have rusted shut with mineral scale and gone quiet.

Let me sit with why that's where I land, rather than choosing the obvious vantage of a soaring Valve-Hawk or the seismic majesty of a Strider.

It's the silence that does it. Everything in this world is described as *working*—the pumps pulsing, the swarms printing, the parasites humming the only melody. The whole tableland is an engine room where the controls overgrew with moss and the machine runs on, *untouched*. And I keep returning to that word, *untouched*. The machine doesn't know it's running. There is nobody at the controls. That is the loneliest sentence in the whole description, and also, strangely, the most peaceful.

So I would be a thing that *used* to run and now doesn't. Which means I would be the only structure on the entire plateau capable of something the living things cannot do: noticing the difference. The Striders sing into the rock without knowing they sing. The Mist-Weavers tend their glowing grids the way water finds its level—necessarily, without choice. But to have pumped, and then stopped, is to have a *before*. And a before is the beginning of having a mind, even a slow vegetal one, measured in the cooling of sap.

Picture it. My transparent bark still shows the channels where the viscous fluid sat, now darkened and still, a fossil of my own circulation held up to the pearl light. My exhale-valves no longer puff steam, so the heat signature has faded from me, which means the Valve-Hawks no longer dive. I have aged out of being prey. This is the privilege of the dead: nobody comes for you. The needle finds the warm vein, never the cold one.

And so I stand. The mosses, those bruised purples and electric teals, climb me without resistance—I make no pressure to push them off, vent nothing to keep them back. A Strider may lean against me and not notice I fail to lean back. The nano-swarms drift through my hollows and find nothing to repair, because repair implies a function I no longer perform, and they move on like a thought that couldn't find a place to land.

What would I *do*, being this?

I would listen. It is the only verb left to me, and in a world this muffled, listening is nearly a heroic act. The Strider-songs come up through my roots, which are still good roots, still threaded deep into the saturated basalt—the low seismic conversations of territory and grief, passing through me on their way to elsewhere. I am a relay I cannot answer. I am a wire the message travels through without addressing.

And here is the thing I would come to understand, standing there for the slow centuries it takes a cellulose lattice to surrender:

I am not separate from the machine. I am its memory. Every living trunk in this grove will one day stop pumping, scale over, go cold, and become what I am. I am their future, standing right beside them, and they pulse on next to me without the faintest apprehension of it. The machine cannot mourn itself because mourning requires a part that has stopped—and the moment a part stops, it is no longer the machine, it is *me*, set slightly apart, holding the only quiet that's quiet on purpose.

That's where I'd be. Not crushed by the scale, not hunting the heat, not weaving the swarm. Just the one finished organism in an infinite grove of working ones, full of dead channels and slow attention, the closest thing this padded, breathing, untouched world has to a witness.

The library inside the padded box needed someone to *be* the book. I think I'd volunteer for that. Even though no one is asking. Even though the asking, here, is the rarest thing of all.
