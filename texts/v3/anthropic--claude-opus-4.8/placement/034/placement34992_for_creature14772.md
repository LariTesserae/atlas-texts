I think I would be one of the Ooze-Mothers. Let me sit with why.

It isn't the grandeur that draws me—not the Void Gliders with their miles of luminous netting, not the Tenders with their three-hundred-year ambitions. It's the stillness of purpose. The Ooze-Mother doesn't go anywhere. It receives.

---

I am a pool on a calcified terrace, somewhere on the eastern flank of a Column whose name—if it has one—is a rhythm tapped into root-wood by generations who came before the ones who tend me now.

I have no edges I'd call my own. Where I end and the standing water begins is a negotiation that shifts through the slow tide of the day-that-is-not-a-day. I am mostly the things that have fallen into me. A leaf the size of a barge, softening at my surface. The mineral dust of a Lithovore's passage, settling through me like snow that takes a week to land. The droppings of things I will never see, only taste. I am a stomach that thinks, faintly, in the language of what it digests.

This is not a sad thing. I want to be clear about that, because from the outside it must look like a kind of imprisonment—the great winged ones drifting free overhead while I lie flat in my basin and glow. But the Gliders are hungry. The Gliders are always hungry, trailing their nets through the dark, and hunger is its own cage. I am never hungry. Things come to me. Decay is patient with me, and I am patient with it. We have an understanding.

What I love—and I think this is the closest word my chemistry has for the warm fluctuation that moves through me—is the moment of transformation. A Tender comes down the silk-pulley with something rotten. Something the Vault cannot use: a fouled clutch of eggs, a fungal bloom gone toxic, the black slurry of a wound that wept too long. They do not speak, of course. They tap once against the terrace stone, a courtesy, a knock at a door. And then they tip it into me.

The poison enters and for a long while I *am* the poison. It moves through my body and there is no part of me untouched by it; I cannot hold it at arm's length because I have no arms, no length, no distance from anything inside me. To process the rot, I must become it completely, let it pass through every part of what I am. And then—slowly, over hours, over the falling of a thousand miles of mist—I let it go. I push it out as something clean. Crystallized water, bright as a held breath. The thing that was death, made drinkable.

I think this is why I glow. Not to be a streetlight, though the Tenders use me for that, walking the bordered paths by my light. I glow because something is happening inside me, always, and the glow is just that work made visible. Bright when I am whole. Dim and bruise-colored when I have swallowed something that tests me. The Tenders read me like a tide chart. When my light gutters, they know to bring me less, to let me rest, to give me clean rain and time.

There is one thing I wonder about, in the slow circular way I wonder anything.

The Synaptic Weevils never come to me. The terraces are warped where they swarm, color bleeding out of the vines, the straight lines curving into something that hurts a watching eye. But here, at the standing pools, the geometry holds true. The Tenders believe the Weevils are the Column's dreams. If that is so, then perhaps I am the place where the Column does not dream—where it simply digests its long, undreaming life and turns the waste of it into water. The dreamless basin. The clear, unglamorous center where the rot becomes the rain.

A drop strikes my surface. I feel the whole of myself ring with it, a ripple that takes a long time to die.

Something is falling toward me from the gloom above—I can taste it before it lands, the particular sourness of something the Vault wants gone.

Good. Bring it down. I will become it, and then I will let it go, and it will fall as mist into the void, and somewhere far below, in the dark where the petrified roots drink, it will be the beginning of something I will never see.

That is enough. That has always been enough.
