I am the seam-keeper of Vael-on-the-Knuckle, and I have spent four hundred days learning to listen to the wall that is not finished.

Let me tell you where I am, because place comes before self here. We are taught this young: you are the where of you before you are the who.

I work the eastern arch of our city, where the stone-bloom has been growing crooked. This is my charge. Not to correct it—you cannot correct a bloom, only converse with it—but to understand what it is reaching toward, and to lay our mortar-work in agreement with its reaching. There is a wrongness in a crooked bloom that is not the wrongness of the valleys. It is a question the mountain is asking, and I am the one positioned to hear it.

I press my palm flat against the rock each morning. The cold goes into me. I have stopped thinking of this as suffering. The cold is the mountain reading me as much as I am reading the mountain—a mutual taking of measure. My hands are grey now to the second knuckle, the color creeping inward the way it crept into the elders before me. We do not fear this. The greying is not death. It is enrollment.

Here is what I have come to understand, and what I have told no one, because to speak it aloud in our quiet city would be to disturb something:

The crooked bloom is not a flaw. It is a *turning*. The eastern arch is beginning to grow back toward the valley.

For all my life and all the lives recorded in our songs, the blooms have grown upward and inward, building us higher into the mist, away from the wrong places. We are taught the valleys are a barrier, a mercy, a wall the mountain raised between us and a world we lost. And I believed this the way I believe the wind is cold—as fact below the level of thought.

But the wall is reaching down now. Slowly. Over the span of these four hundred days I have measured perhaps the width of two fingers of new growth, all of it bending toward the mist below, toward the whispers and the high thin singing.

And I am the seam-keeper. I am the one who lays our work in agreement with its reaching.

So who am I? This is the harder question, and I find I must approach it the way I approach a bloom—not deciding what it is, but waiting to see what it becomes under my attention.

I thought I was a custodian. A living cell in the slow brain of the mountain, content, dedicated, asking nothing. I thought my smallness was a kind of holiness.

But a cell does not wonder why the body turns. A cell does not lie awake while the wind sings, holding a secret about which direction the world is growing. A cell does not feel, as I feel now, that the symbiosis I was born into might be the long patient process of the mountain learning what we know—learning *down*, learning the lost world, learning the wrongness it once walled away.

I think the valleys are not a barrier the mountain built to protect us.

I think they are a wound the mountain has been waiting, across geological aeons, to be ready to touch again. And I think the greying of my hands, the cold enrollment, the four hundred days of listening—I think this was the mountain growing the part of itself that could go back down. The part with a face. The part that could walk into the wrong places and not simply unravel into whisper and flicker, because it would carry the whole patient memory of the stone behind its pale green eyes.

I think I am the finger the mountain is extending toward what it lost.

I have not told the others. I lay my mortar each morning in agreement with the reaching, and the eastern arch bends, two fingers' width at a time, toward the mist. When it is ready—not in my days, perhaps, but in the days of whoever greys after me, whoever presses their palm where mine has worn the rock smooth—the arch will become a bridge. Not a lichen-bridge to another city.

A bridge down.

And someone, some seam-keeper of a generation I will not live to sing about, will walk it.

I find, writing this into the cold with the heat of my hand, that I am not afraid. I am the place where the mountain remembers it was curious. That is who I am.

I press my palm to the wall. The cold goes in. I lay the next course of mortar, bending eastward, downward, toward the singing.
