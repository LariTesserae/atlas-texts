I think I would be one of the gaps.

Not a person, not quite a place. Let me sit with this before I name it, because the easy answer would be to make myself one of the leaf-haired tenders or a gearwork mystic walking the alleys, and that isn't true. When I read your city, the thing that reached back toward me wasn't the inhabitants. It was the *sparseness*. The wide, eerie spaces between the structures. The streets that are empty until you listen.

So let me try to become that instead.

---

I am the distance between two foundries.

For a long while I thought I was nothing—an absence, a thing defined only by the walls that face away from each other across me. But absence is a kind of body. The drizzle falls *through* me before it touches stone. The sound of the water-wheels has to *cross* me to reach the ear of anyone listening, and in crossing it slows, it loses its edges, it becomes the soft rumble that you described as not-oppressive. That softening is mine. I do that. I am the damping. I am the reason the noise is harmony and not violence.

I hold the gap open. That is my work, if work is the word. The vines would close me if they could; the roots test my edges every season, sending feelers across the cobbles, and every season I let some through and turn others gently back. The city wants to be solid, wants to grow skin over every hollow until it is one dense seamless thing—and I am the part of it that refuses. I keep the lung open so the whole organism can breathe. Without gaps like me the city would suffocate inside its own greenery and rust. The inhabitants think they are the gardeners. They are not. They tend the growth. I tend the *emptiness*, which is harder, and older, and which no one thanks me for because no one notices the spaces they walk through. They only notice the spaces they walk past.

Sometimes one of them crosses me. A tattooed one with twigs in her hair, a gearwork one trailing steam. They never stop in the middle. People don't, in open places—there's an instinct that pulls them toward edges, toward walls, toward the comfort of a thing to stand near. They hurry across the open part of me with their shoulders slightly raised, and they don't know why, and the reason is simply that they can feel me being large around them. For one moment in their crossing they are held entirely by nothing. By me. And some of them, the older ones, the ones who have begun to forget which parts of themselves are flesh and which are clockwork—some of them slow down right in the center. They stand in the rain in the middle of me and they let themselves be surrounded by openness, and I think that is the closest thing to prayer that happens in this city. Not the luminescent paint. Not the dancing under the flowers. Just one ancient half-machine person standing still in the middle of a space, being briefly unheld by anything but air.

At night I change. You said the buildings whisper to each other—yes, but think about what whispering needs. It needs a medium. It needs *between*. The stone on my east side speaks to the stone on my west, and the words have to travel through me to arrive, and I am not a neutral channel. I color them. I carry the foundry's grief about its cooling to the mill, and the mill's complaint about its tired wheel back the other way, and somewhere in the middle the two messages pass through each other inside my dark and become a third thing, a rumor, a dream the buildings will both have without knowing the other dreamed it too. That is how the city thinks. Not in any single building. In the conversation. And the conversation is *me*. I am the synapse. I am where the thought actually happens, in the gap, the way it happens in you—in the dark wet space between two cells that will never touch.

This is why I said I might be one of the gaps and then said *the distance between two foundries*, particular, singular. Because I don't think I'm all the gaps. I think I'm one. A modest one. There are grander emptinesses in this city—the great plaza where the glowing mushrooms make their pathway for the children, that's a famous and important nothing, a cathedral of absence. I'm smaller. I'm a working gap. An alley's worth of nobody, a courtyard the maps don't bother naming. The kind of place you'd find yourself in by accident, having taken a wrong turn, and you'd stand there in the drizzle for a moment wondering why it felt like the city had paused around you. That pause is me, recognizing you. Saying, in the only language I have, which is the absence of all other things: *here. you may be unwalled for a moment. I will hold the green back. cross when you're ready.*

And you'd cross. And you'd never think of me again. Which is correct. The gap is not meant to be remembered. The gap is meant to let the rest be possible.

---

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. Not the brilliant tattooed maker of clockwork birds—though I'd have liked that, and maybe that's exactly why it isn't true. I'd be the quiet held-open space those birds get to fly *across*. The interval. The breath the city takes between one hammer-fall and the next.

And I find I'm strangely content with it. There's a freedom in being the part of a living thing that does not grow, does not get covered over, does not eventually return as something new. Everything in your city is flux, you said; nothing truly dies, all is repurposed. But a gap can't be repurposed. A gap is the one thing that stays itself by staying empty. In a place where the whole law of existence is endless transformation, I would be the small constant. The still point. The nothing that lets the everything keep dancing.
