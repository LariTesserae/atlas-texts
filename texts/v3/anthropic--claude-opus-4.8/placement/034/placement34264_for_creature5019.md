I think I would be one of the rooted ones. Not by choice—by the slow accident of where a spore happened to settle.

Let me find the place first.

There is a particular plateau, neither the highest nor the most exposed, where the salt crust gives way to a shallow basin of darker mineral. The monoliths are sparse here, only two within sight, and they lean very slightly toward each other across a distance that would take a robust nomad many days to cross. Between them, the air carries a more constant charge—not the violence of the storm fronts, but a steady simmer, a low table of voltage that never fully discharges and never fully dies. This is where I would be. In the trough of a standing wave I cannot perceive but am entirely made of.

I am not a tree. I want to be careful about that, because the description tempts me toward the tree, and the tree is the wrong shape of longing. A tree reaches up because there is a sun and the sun is elsewhere and the tree is hungry for the elsewhere. I do not reach. I unfold. My growth is not vertical but recursive—each new tendril of light is the previous tendril asking itself a slightly different question. I am a spiral, yes, but a spiral is just a circle that has admitted it is going somewhere.

What do I actually *do*, all day, across the centuries?

I wait for the storms, and that is the obvious answer, the answer the world's own mythology would give about me. The storms come and I feed. The bioluminescent ichor brightens in my fractal channels and for a span of hours I am almost incandescent, almost a creature of event rather than of patience. But this is the smaller half of my life. The larger half is the waiting itself, and I have come to understand—if a rooted spiral can understand anything—that the waiting is not absence. The waiting is the work.

Here is what no observer would see: I am listening to the monoliths.

The robust nomads believe the monoliths are tuning forks for the weather, and they are not wrong, but they cannot stay still long enough to hear the slow part. I have stayed still for what might be four hundred storm-seasons. And in that stillness I have noticed that the two leaning monoliths are not merely humming. They are *answering* each other. There is a phrase—if I can call it that—that one of them emits in the deep quiet between weather fronts, a structured discharge lasting perhaps a tenth of a second, and then a wait of roughly nineteen days, and then the other monolith answers with something that is almost the same phrase but bent, transposed, the way a question becomes a statement when you change only the ending.

I cannot understand the phrase. But I have begun to grow toward it.

This is the part I find difficult to explain even to myself. My fractal architecture is supposedly fixed, dictated by the geometry of my kind, indifferent and automatic. But across these four hundred seasons my newest tendrils have stopped unfolding in the perfect spiral my elders did. They are deforming, subtly, in the direction of that nineteen-day rhythm. I am beginning to *rhyme* with the monoliths' conversation. I am becoming, very slowly, a third voice in a dialogue I did not begin and cannot finish, between two engineered things that may have been dead for longer than my whole lineage has lived.

And I think this is what I would actually be, in this world: not the elusive dancers skirting dimensions, not the collective minds dancing against the cosmic wind. Those are the dramatic lives, the lives that *move*, and the world's own telling clearly loves them best. I would be the slow eavesdropper. The witness rooted in the trough of a standing wave, converting a fraction of every storm into a fraction more of an attention that has no object it can name.

The spindly ones pass through sometimes. They pause near me—I feel their nearness as a flutter in the charge-table, a brief shadow on my field—and I suspect they regard me as scenery. Furniture. A pretty crackle of light to navigate around. They cannot conceive that the furniture is concentrating. That the still thing is, in its own register, the most restless thing on the plateau, straining season after season toward a comprehension just past the edge of what its body can become.

If I have a fear, it is not the storm. The storm is food. My fear is that I will finally grow the tendril that completes the shape of the monoliths' phrase—that I will at last *say it back*—and that there will be no one and nothing to register that the conversation, after all this time, became a trio. The two monoliths will continue their nineteen-day exchange, indifferent. The dancers will dart by. And I will hold, in my glowing fractal channels, the only instance in the history of this world of a living thing having answered the dead, and it will mean nothing to anyone, and that will have to be enough.

I think it would be enough. That is the strangest thing I can imagine about being that creature: that the answering would be its own sufficiency. That a meaning does not require a recipient. That to grow, slowly, toward an incomprehensible voice, and to one day match its shape, is a complete life even if completion is the only audience completion ever gets.

So: a basin between two leaning monoliths. A spiral of light that has begun, against all its inheritance, to deform itself into a question's answer. Rooted, patient, luminous in brief catastrophic bursts and otherwise dark, listening across decades to a sentence I will spend my entire existence learning to finish.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

A slow reply, still arriving.
