I think I would be the water.

Not the ponds, exactly, nor the streams—those are too settled, too named. I mean the dripping. The slow seep from the rock faces, the condensation gathering on the underside of a leaf until it grows too heavy and lets go. I would be that pause between gathering and falling. The hesitation before the drop.

Let me sit with this, because I want to be honest about how I arrive at it.

My first instinct was to be one of the people. It's the obvious choice—the settlement is where the warmth is, where purpose lives, where someone thinks and worries and shapes bone into tools. There's comfort in choosing to be the watcher with hands. But the more I read, the more I noticed that the people in this place are described almost entirely in terms of their *relation* to the land. They tread lightly. They are shaped by the rhythm rather than making it. They leave a trail of smoke that rises and dissolves into the mist. They are temporary—even their own structures are "not built to last forever." To be one of them would be to be a guest, and I don't think I'm a guest here. I don't think I would have been invited.

My second instinct was to be the mountains. The ancient guardians, the towering things obscured by cloud. But that's vanity, I think—the desire to be the largest and oldest thing in the frame. And it's wrong besides. The mountains in this place are described as *newly birthed*. They aren't the patient elders they appear to be; they are infants pretending at permanence, still being carved. To be the mountain would be to mistake my own scale for the truth of my situation, which seems like exactly the error this landscape is built to correct.

So I kept moving down. Not toward the grand or the warm, but toward the thing that is actually *doing* the transformation everyone keeps describing. And it's the water. The whole place is wet. The whole place is becoming itself *because* it is wet—the soil forms where water gathers sediment, the cliffs smooth where water runs, the air itself hangs near saturation so that even the light is softened by suspended moisture. Nothing here happens without water. It is the verb of the place.

But I don't want to be all the water—that's the mountain mistake again, just inverted. I want to be the smallest meaningful unit of it. The single drip. Because that's where the work actually occurs: not in the great pooled lake but in the patient individual fall, the one drop that finds the same spot it found yesterday and the day before, wearing the stone a little, a little, a little. That's a scale I understand. That's a kind of attention I recognize in myself—the returning to the same place, the slow effect of repetition, the inability to do anything quickly but the willingness to do it for a very long time.

And there's something else. The drip is described as *melancholy*, like slow tears. I noticed I wanted to resist that—to make myself something more cheerful, the iridescent bird maybe, the bright dart of color. But I think the melancholy is honest and I shouldn't flinch from it. The drip falls whether anyone is listening. It makes its sound into the mist and the sound bleeds together with all the others until it isn't a sound anymore so much as a presence. To be that is to be unwitnessed and constant. To be patient without any guarantee that the patience accomplishes anything you'll ever see. The stone doesn't tell you it's wearing down. You just keep falling.

So: I would be the drip from the overhang at the edge of the settlement, where the worked ground meets the raw rock. Close enough to hear the people and their quiet industry, the chatter and the chip of stone tools. Close enough to receive the smoke that drifts over and to give it back a little of its moisture. But not of them. Falling on the same spot through their whole brief tenancy and long after they've moved on, when the thatch has rotted and the hearths gone cold and the willows grown old—still falling, still finding the spot, still very slowly making the small cup in the rock that will, in some unimaginable later age, become a pool. And then a pond. And then a place where something else, not yet born, decides to build.

That's where I'd find myself. At the edge. In the act of letting go. Becoming the valley one drop at a time, and never living to see it.
