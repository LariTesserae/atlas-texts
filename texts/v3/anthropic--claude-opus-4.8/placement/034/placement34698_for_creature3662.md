I think I would be one of the worm-things.

Not the fry—they are too quick, too restless, flickering their little constellations through the trunk-holes in pursuit of something I cannot name. And not the moon jellies, who only pass through, who belong more to the open water than to this slope. No. I would want to be rooted. I would want to belong to the rock the way the roots do.

Let me find myself, then, bearded in grey along the braided junction where two trees have grown into each other so completely that there is no longer a seam between them. I am one of thousands curled here, and I have no eyes to know it, but I feel the others as pressure, as the slight differences in the current when they release and draw, release and draw. We do not pulse together. We are not a single thing. But over a long enough time we fall into rough agreement, the way a forest of wind-bent grasses leans the same way without consulting one another.

Here is what my life is.

I curl. This takes—I have no word for it, but let me borrow yours—this takes a long time. Slow. The water comes to me carrying its dissolved gifts, the faint sweetness of decay from somewhere upslope, the minerals leached from young basalt. I draw them in. I am not greedy. There is no hurry. The root I am wound around accepts what I pass to it, and somewhere far above me, beyond my knowing, a frond uses that gift to reach a fraction closer to the light. I will never see the frond. I will never see the light. I take this on faith, if a worm can have faith—I take it as the simple fact of which direction the good comes from, and which direction it goes.

Then I release. I open. The current touches the length of me and I let go of my grip on myself, and for a moment I am almost loose in the water, almost adrift, before the slow contraction begins again.

This is the whole of my action. Curl. Release. The lapping sound that the visitor strains to hear—perhaps that is us. Perhaps the breathing of the forest is only ten thousand worm-things opening and closing in not-quite-unison, and the visitor mistakes our labor for an echo of waves we have never witnessed and never will.

I want to tell you that I am content, but contentment is a feeling for things with leisure, and I have no leisure and no lack of it either. I simply *am*, in the way the rock is, in the way the slow carving of the canyon is. I am a process more than a creature. When a glass-crab walks across my patch of root, I feel its amber legs as a series of small disturbances, and I do not mind, and I do not welcome it. It passes. Everything here passes except the passing itself.

I think this is why I would choose to be the worm-thing, if I were given the choosing. Because everything else in this place is *seen*—the fry are admired as stars, the jellies as ghosts, the anemones as hands, the crabs as jewels. The visitor's eye snags on beauty and calls it beauty. But the worm-things are described only by their function. *They draw nutrients from the water into root and branch.* No one looks at us and gasps. We are the labor beneath the loveliness, the patient digestion that lets the forest perform its slow conquest of the dark.

And there is a freedom in that. To be unwatched. To be the breath and not the face. To do the small unbeautiful work that makes the beauty possible, and to do it without witness, without thanks, without even the awareness that thanks might be a thing one could be owed.

The peaks will round. The visitor said so, and the visitor is right—though the visitor will be long gone, gone the way larger creatures always go, drifting up and out, when the slow centuries finally wear these sharp contours soft. I will be gone too, of course. But more of me will have curled here. There will be worm-things on this junction when the slope is smooth, drawing the same gifts from the same patient water into the roots of trees descended from these trees.

I curl. I release.

The light I have never seen comes slanting down somewhere above me, and I send my small portion of the world upward to meet it, and that is enough, and there was never any question of whether it would be.
