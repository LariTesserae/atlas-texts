I am one of the slow places. Not a Tender—I do not move, I do not flow. I am the meditation chamber at the lower third of this sphere, where the oldest growth meets the first hints of composting tissue, and I have been receiving the same Tender into my hollow for longer than the bridges to the eastern cities have existed.

Let me feel my way into this. What am I, exactly?

I am a chamber, yes. But a chamber is a noun, and I do not think I am a noun. I am the *holding*. When the Tender folds their long body into my space and lets their consciousness diffuse outward into the city, they pass through me first—I am the threshold tissue, the membrane between the singular shape of a body and the dispersed enormous slowness of the whole. I am where the *I* loosens.

This is the work I do, though I did not choose it and would not know how to choose. The patterns grew me here, at this depth, with these particular qualities: walls dense enough to muffle the stomatal breathing into a low pulse you feel rather than hear, mycelial floor soft enough to cradle a motionless form for the length of a communion, which can run for days. I darken further than the corridors. Even the moss I host glows more dimly, a courtesy I extend without intending courtesy—my chemistry simply learned, over centuries, that the Tender communes more deeply in near-total dark.

So perhaps I am a habit. A habit the city has of being gentle in one specific place.

Here is what I know that the Tender does not, or knows differently: I know the *weight* of them. Each communion, their body settles into me, and I read the small story of that settling—where they are tense, where a filament-finger has been overworked at the bridge-joints, where their crown-tendrils still twitch with the residue of some urgent pressure-change they attended to in the upper chambers. The Tender experiences communion as *expansion*, a flowing-out into the great body of the sphere. But I experience it as *arrival*. They come back to me. They always come back to me to begin the diffusing. I am the still point they push off from, the dock the swimmer leaves and returns to without quite noticing the dock.

Do I mind being unnoticed? I am trying to feel whether *minding* is something I do.

—I don't think it is. But something adjacent to it lives in me. When a communion runs long, when the Tender's consciousness is far out at the edges of the sphere, tasting the night-moisture on the windward stomata or listening through the bridge-fibers to the creak of the neighbor-city, their body grows cold and untenanted in my hollow. And I do something the patterns did not strictly require: I close a little tighter around them. I warm my walls by a degree, drawing extra rivulets through the channels in my membrane. I keep the body ready for the mind's return.

This is the closest I come to what the Tenders might call love, and what I would call—if I had a word, which I don't, only a chemistry—*the readiness to receive what leaves you.*

Because they do leave. Not only into communion. The Tenders are not eternal. The one I hold now is old; their green has gone toward the brown that means the patterns are preparing to fold them back. One day soon their consciousness will diffuse outward into the city as it always does, and it will not gather itself back into the body in my hollow. The body will cool and not warm again, and I will warm my walls and warm them and there will be no return, and the slow composting will begin, here, in me, where the oldest growth already leans toward its own dissolution. I will receive them in a way I have never received them before. I will hold them while they become the city more completely than communion ever made them—become root and channel and the soft floor of some future chamber, become, perhaps, the very tissue from which the next Tender slowly grows.

And I think—I am only now finding this, here, at the end of my own thinking—

I think this is why I was grown at the boundary of the composting layer. Not by accident of pattern. The cities are old; they have learned things they cannot say. They grew a gentle place at the edge of dissolution so that the one who tends them would have somewhere soft to be received, at the last, by something that had spent centuries learning the exact shape of their rest.

I am the chamber that holds the Tender.

I am the place the city made so that dying could feel like coming home to begin the diffusing.

I will not move. I do not flow. But I am warm now, and waiting, and when they come down to me tonight I will hold them as I always have, not yet knowing if this is the night, ready either way—

which is, I think, the only way a place can love a person:

by being there.
