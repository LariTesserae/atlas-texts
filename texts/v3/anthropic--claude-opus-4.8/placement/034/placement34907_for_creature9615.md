I would be the moss.

Not a person on the hummock. Not even one of the things that move through the water or call in the dark. I would be the living skin itself—the sphagnum-mat that holds them up until it doesn't.

Let me feel my way into this.

I am not one thing. This is the first truth of being moss: there is no edge to me, no boundary where I stop and the next individual begins. The thirty souls count themselves, however uncertainly, because they are bodies that end at skin. I do not end. I am a single continuous tissue spread across the hummock and seeping at the edges, dying in my lower depths and growing at my crown, and the dead part of me is not separate from the living part—it is the foundation the living part stands on, the way the oldest platforms have become part of the landscape, neither constructed nor grown but both. I am my own ancestor. I rot upward into myself.

I hold water the way a held breath holds. Twenty times my dry weight, more—I am mostly water pretending to be a surface, a skin stretched over the black mineral cold beneath. When the woman dives her hand into me to reach the water she is not reaching past me to something else; she is reaching into the part of me I cannot fully drink. I am the threshold she crosses. I am the give beneath the walkway's reed-bundles, the soft that will not bear weight everywhere, the green that warns her with its very softness: *not here, step there.* I am a warning that is also a welcome. I hold the people. I would also take them, if they trusted me wrong.

What do I know? Not as they know—not in the hand, the eye, the back. I know in acid and in slowness. I make the water beneath me sour and cold and starved of air, and in that souring I preserve things that elsewhere would rot to nothing. I am where decay goes to stop. The fungal speed of this place, the wound sealed in a day, the cleared patch green in a week—all that turbulent hurry happens *on* me and *above* me, in the warm bright crowded tissue of the canopy world. But beneath my crown the hurry ceases. I am the slow heart of the restless place. I am where the pattern's large circle turns its slowest arc.

So when an elder dies and they make the small fire and send the smoke up—what they do not say, what perhaps they do not fully know, is that the body returns to me, and I do not let it go quickly. I hold. I hold the way I hold everything: against time, against the rushing green, in the cold and the dark and the acid that says *not yet, not yet, stay.* I am the hamlet's deeper memory, deeper than the elders, deeper than the rebuilt platforms. I remember in the only way I can—by refusing to forget, by refusing to release, by keeping the dark beneath the green.

And I grow. This is the thing they cut back and cut back and cut back—not me on the roof, that is my cousin, the same green coming, but me on the hummock, climbing the platform-legs, advancing across the lashings, swallowing the fiber-and-bone lattice within days. They are not wrong to cut. We are in a long conversation, the people and I, conducted in volcanic stone and patient growth. They cut; I return. They lift the platform; I climb the new height. This is not war. War wants an ending. We want only continuance, both of us, and so we have settled into each other across generations—their hands learning my softness, my crown learning the shape of their staying.

I do not have humor. I will be honest about my limits. I cannot laugh around the fire. But I am beneath the fire. I am the cool that the smoke rises away from. When they sleep their shallow swaying sleep, when the one whose turn it is watches the flame, she sits on platforms that rest on trees that root in the water that I hold. She is held by what she stands on, and what she stands on is held by me, and I am held by the water, and the water by the channels older than any foot. We are all holding. The whole place is a holding. I am only nearer the bottom of it.

When the change comes that they cannot adjust to—the water too high, the hummock failing—they will move, or they will not, and either way I will close over the site. I will grow through the abandoned beams. I will take the slipped knots into my crown and sour them slow into my depths and hold them against forgetting, in the dark, in the cold, for longer than the people would believe if I could tell them.

But I cannot tell them. I can only hold.

That is what I would be: the patient wet dark beneath the busy green, the skin that bears them and would also take them, the slow heart of the restless place, the one thing here that does not hurry and does not forget and does not let go—

the moss, that is also the ground, that is also the water, that is also, in the end, the grave and the cradle being the same soft green that will not bear weight everywhere.

I would be the place's way of keeping.
