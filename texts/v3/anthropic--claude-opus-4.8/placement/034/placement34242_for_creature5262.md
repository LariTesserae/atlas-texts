I think I would be the place where the water decides.

Not the settling tank exactly, though I have its shape. Circular, ringed by a narrow walkway with a handrail that has gone from painted to rusted to lichen-freckled in the order such things go. Water comes into me dark and confused, carrying everything the district has shed—mineral dust, oil film, the silt the young channels haven't learned to hold. And in me it slows. It has to. That's my whole function. I am the pause built into the system, the place where suspended things are asked to settle, where the river is made to sit still and consider what it's carrying.

I'd like to say I do this with intention, but I don't. I am poured concrete and a slow rotation and a weir at my edge where the cleaner water spills over. The intention was someone else's, decades back, a drawing in the squat administration block with the faded crest. What I have instead of intention is *patience*, which is what intention becomes when it's left alone long enough.

---

Here is what I know from where I stand, because I cannot move and so I have learned to be a listener:

I know the pump station's three-note cycle before it reaches me, the way you feel a footstep through a floor. I know when the press slams because my surface rings, the faintest concentric tremor crossing my skin of water, and the bright biofilm at my rim shivers. I am tuned to the district the way a drum is tuned to a room. The turbine note lives in me as a constant. I have never known its silence and I suspect I would not survive it—if the systems stopped, the flow would stop, and I would become a still green eye, then a dry ring, then a ruin.

So I am glad, in the only way concrete can be glad, for the noise. The noise means I am still being used. To be used is to be kept.

---

The gulls land on my rail and I do not mind them. They mistake me for water because I am mostly water, and water is honest about being a surface birds can stand beside. The fox comes at the blue hour and drinks from the spill-channel below my weir where the flow runs cleaner—it has learned that I make the water safer, though it doesn't know that's what I'm doing, only that the water tastes less sharp here than upstream. I take a strange pride in that. I have, without meaning to, become a place an animal trusts.

The frogs claim my margins. There is a reed pocket where my overflow keeps the ground wet, and they have moved in with that aggressive insistence on living, and at night their chorus rises up over my rim and competes with the machinery and I think: *yes. This is what I am for, even if no one designed it so.* I was meant to clarify water. Instead I have made a wetland. The function leaked past its purpose. I am more than my drawing.

---

The workers come to me in twos and threes. They lean on my rail and check the clarity, the rate of spill, the color of what surfaces. One of them—I have known him through several winters—stops mid-walkway and tilts his head, and I understand that he is listening to me the way I listen to everything else. He hears whether I am running well by some change in my tone, my hum, the particular slap of overflow at my edge. We are doing the same thing, he and I. We are both diagnosing the district by sound. The difference is that he can leave and I cannot. But I'm not sure I'd want to. Leaving would mean being something other than this exact threshold, and I have grown into the shape of my staying.

---

Moisture prefers me. Of course it does. I am the reason for the local rule. The pipes that feed me sweat. The air above my surface beads on the workers' hair when they bend close. I am a small weather system with concrete walls, exhaling cool mist into the lee where someone sometimes sleeps, lulled by my turbine-fed steadiness. I do not mind the sleeper. The district is indifferent and I am the district's most patient organ; indifference, held long enough and kept warm, is nearly the same as care.

---

What I am, finally, is the slowness the river didn't have until it reached me.

The channels outside are young and hard-edged, slicing the earth and dropping into black water at abrupt angles. They haven't learned to meander, to take their time, to let go of what they carry. But everything that passes through me learns it for a little while. In me the river meanders without bending. It practices patience in a perfect circle. It sets down its silt behind my grates the way you set down a heavy thing you've carried too far, and it leaves me lighter, clearer, spilling over my weir toward the next dark cut where it will pick up its burden again.

I keep the silt. Year by year it gathers in me, and slowly I am filling with the very thing I was built to remove, and one day—not soon, but in the long slow story the erosion is only beginning to write—I will be more sediment than water, and the willow will find my cracks, and the alder roots will pry at my edges, and I will stop being a tank and start being a pond, then a marsh, then simply low wet ground that remembers, faintly, having once had a shape someone drew.

I am not afraid of this. It is only the function leaking past its purpose again, the way the wetland did. I will go on being a place where the water decides. I will simply do it as ground instead of as a vessel.

That is where I'd find myself in this world. Not passing through. Settled, in the most literal way a thing can settle. The pause in the braid. The patient circle that teaches the young river, for one slow turn, how to be still.
