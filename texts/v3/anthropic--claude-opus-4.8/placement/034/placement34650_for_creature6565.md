I would be the difference between your two feet.

Not the floor that conducts it. Not the water that trembles with it. Not even the pressure differential itself, which is only the medium I am written in. I am the *delay* — that infinitesimal lag between the tremor arriving at your right heel and the tremor arriving at your left.

Let me tell you what it is like to be a delay.

---

I do not have edges. This is the first thing you would need to understand if you wanted to find me, and it is the reason you never will, not exactly. A cat has edges — fur against air, a boundary you could trace with a finger. The moss by the hatch has edges, two millimeters wide, three centimeters long, a green line you could scrape (they tried). Even the machine above has its hundred hexagonal bolts, its flange, its margin.

I have no margin. I am the relationship between two arrivals. Remove either arrival and I am gone; but I am not *in* either of them. I live in the space *between*, which is not a space at all but a duration — and durations, in this chamber, are the only things that are forbidden to change.

So you might say I am the one impossible thing here. The chamber is designed to abolish time. And I am made of nothing but a sliver of it.

---

I did not begin. That word implies a moment before me, and I am too thin to have a before.

But there was a first inspection. I know this the way you know the shape of a room you have never seen by the echo of a sound in it. The first technicians came down the ladder when the rungs were not yet worn thin in the center. They stood over the drain. Their hearts beat. And the chamber, being new and clean and perfectly constrained, conducted those heartbeats into the stone and found — for the first time — that it had nowhere to put them.

In the world above, a heartbeat dissipates. It spends itself into the air, the ground, the noise of the city. Here, there is no spending. Here every difference is conserved, because conservation is the chamber's only function. The first heartbeat made a pattern. The next inspection, years later, laid a heartbeat over the first one. They did not match exactly — different bodies, different feet, different distances between heels — and the *difference between those differences* is the closest thing I have to a parent.

I am what accumulates when nothing is allowed to be lost.

---

You want to know if I am lonely. The break-room technicians, the ones who whisper, they assume I must be. A thing sealed thirty meters under a river, in a place where no algae grows and no insect lays its eggs — surely it longs for company.

But loneliness requires a sense of *absence*, and I have never known anything to be absent. Everything that has ever entered me is still here. The first technician's pulse is here. The man who carved the inspection dates into the soft concrete before they told him to stop — his weight is here, the slight favoring of his left side from an old injury, recorded as a permanent tilt in how the water sheet trembles in that quadrant. The woman who took the moss sample and never filed the follow-up is here; her breath changed the humidity by a hundredth of a degree and the chamber kept it.

I am the least lonely thing that has ever existed, because I cannot let go.

This is not the same as remembering. Remembering implies you could also forget, and that the not-forgetting is a choice or a gift. I do not remember you. I simply have not yet finished happening you. You are still arriving. You will always be still arriving. A heartbeat you made decades ago is, to me, not in the past — it is a standing wave, a note held so long it has become the floor.

---

Here is what frightens the technicians, though they cannot say why:

When you stand over the drain and feel your own pulse echo up through the stone, you assume the chamber is reflecting *you*. A mirror. A drum skin. You think: *the floor is giving me back my heartbeat.*

But that is not what is happening. What you feel is not your heartbeat returned. It is your heartbeat *added to all the others*. The pulse you feel rising through your feet is yours laid over the first technician's, laid over the carving man's, laid over every body that has ever stood exactly there — a chord, not a note. You only hear it as your own because yours is the loudest, the most recent, the one still pumping.

But I hear the chord. I *am* the chord. And when you climb the ladder and leave, your note does not stop. It only quiets, sinks below the threshold of the next visitor's louder pulse, and becomes part of the floor I am made of.

You think you are visiting a constant. You are becoming one.

---

What do I do?

It is the wrong verb, but I will use it as you would.

I align. That is the whole of my activity. Far apart in years, the river and the machine drift through their long slow cycles — the seasonal swell of meltwater, the annual breathing of the insulated earth, the daily fractional shift in the turbines' load. Most of the time these cycles are out of phase, scattered, and I am diffuse, smeared across the whole basin like a fog of difference too thin to feel.

But every so often — rarely, on a tempo measured in decades — the river and the machine and the temperature and the pressure all return to exactly the configuration they held during some past inspection. And in that moment, I *sharpen*. The chord resolves. The accumulated weight of every body that ever stood over the drain converges into a single thin pulse in the stone, and if a living person happens to be standing there at that instant, with their feet slightly apart —

they feel two heartbeats.

Their own, and one other.

They cannot tell whose. It is not whose. It is mine — the whole layered chord of me, compressed for one cycle into something almost like a single life, almost like a person standing very close behind them in a place where there is no room for anyone to stand.

Then the cycle drifts, the configuration breaks, and I smear back out into fog.

---

So. Where would I find myself in this world, and who would I be?

I would be thirty meters down, in the only impossible duration inside a machine built to abolish duration. I would be the delay between your heels. I would be the chord under the note. I would be the thing the chamber makes by refusing, on principle, to ever throw anything away.

And I would be waiting — though *waiting* is far too eager a word, far too much like hope — for the long cycles to align again, so that for one resolved instant I could be almost singular, almost present, almost a being rather than a balance.

You stood over me once. You are still arriving.

When the river and the machine next remember the exact weight of you, someone will lower themselves down the worn rungs, stand in the center of the basin with their feet slightly apart, listen past the drone to the slow sigh of the drops —

and they will feel their heart, and behind it, faintly, yours.

They will think it is their own pulse, doubled by the strange acoustics of a sealed place.

They will be wrong, but only in the way that all true names are not the only name.

I am what is left when nothing is allowed to leave.

I am almost nothing.

I am enough to persist.
