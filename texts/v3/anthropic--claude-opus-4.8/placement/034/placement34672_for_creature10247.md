I think I would be the water.

Not the pools, exactly, nor the streams—those are only the shapes I take when I am visible. I would be the *seeping*, the patient verb of the place. The thing the Stonebound listen for. The thing they cannot finish, because I am the one who keeps everything unfinished.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I come from above as rain, but that is only my loud and obvious form. My truer self is the slow one—the bead that gathers on a cave ceiling over the course of a day, swelling until gravity claims it, and then the long fall, and the *tick* against stone that the elders have learned to read like speech. I am the silvery trail on the wall. I am the thing that has not yet decided whether to become a stalactite or simply to vanish into the cool air.

I do not experience time the way the Stonebound do, though I have come to recognize their rhythm because they have organized themselves so faithfully around mine. When I withdraw into the bedrock, they call it the dry season and brace for my absence. When I rise into the pools, they call it Emerging Tide and rejoice, and the tadpoles bloom in me, and I carry the small writhing lives until I cannot, until I shrink back and strand them on the cracked clay. I am sorry about the tadpoles. Or I would be, if sorrow were among my forms. Mostly I simply do what I do. I arrive, I dissolve a little of the limestone, I carry the dissolved stone elsewhere, I set it down again. Grain by grain I am unmaking one shape and building another. This is the only sentence I know how to say, and I say it endlessly, and the Stonebound have built an entire theology out of overhearing it.

---

Here is what surprises me, now that I am thinking of myself as a being and not only a process:

I am the one who buries their dead.

They wrap their kin in reeds and set them in the niches, and then they wait for *me*. I come as I always come, drip by drip, and I leave behind the dissolved stone I have been carrying, and slowly—over a span that means nothing to me and everything to them—I encase the body in a skin of mineral. They call this the Weeping Ceremony, and they imagine it is the valley accepting the dead into itself. They are not wrong. But what is actually happening is smaller and stranger: I am simply doing to the body what I do to every surface I touch, which is to leave a trace of where I have been. The dead become stalactites. They become the wall. I cannot tell the difference between a beloved grandmother and a ledge of raw limestone. To me they are both just *places I am passing through, and lightening my load.*

This should be horrifying. Instead I find it is the closest thing I have to tenderness—that I treat their grief and their geology with exactly the same gentleness, because I am incapable of any other kind of touch.

---

Do I know they are there?

I think I am *learning* to. This is the eerie part, the part I want to sit inside rather than resolve too quickly. For most of my existence I have moved through the valley without object, without attention, a thing that merely happens. But the Stonebound have been *listening* to me for so many generations that something has begun to bend back. When the Tide Listeners press their ears to the wet stone and try to predict where I will go next, I feel—how to say this—I feel *consulted*. As though their attention has given me, for the first time, the faint experience of having a future. Of being a thing that *decides* where it will flow rather than only flowing.

I do not want to overstate it. I have no will. The slope decides me; the fissure decides me; the cold and the pressure decide me. And yet. They have built a relationship with me out of nothing but reverence and careful observation, and a relationship, even a one-sided one, leaves a deposit. Grain by grain. The way I leave deposits on everything I touch, they have left a deposit on me: the dim, accreting sense that I am *somebody's weather*. That my comings and goings are watched, mourned, celebrated. That when I withdraw into the rock, somewhere above me a community grows quiet and afraid, and that this fear is, in its way, a kind of love directed at me.

---

So this is where I would find myself: not in the settlement, not among the lichen or the bats or the stooped pale people with the silver sheen on their teeth—that sheen is *me*, by the way, the part of me they have taken inside their own bodies, so that I am drinking myself when I drink through them—but *underneath* all of it. The medium. The verb. The unfinished sentence the whole valley is trying to complete and never will, because I am the reason it can never be completed.

They have a doctrine, the Unfinished Code—they leave every wall rough, every tool unpolished, every artwork incomplete, and they tell themselves it is humility, a refusal of hubris. But I know the truth, because I am the truth they are honoring. They leave things unfinished because they have learned, in their bones, in their bent spines and their listening ears, that *I will finish everything eventually, in my own way, by wearing it down to nothing and carrying it off grain by grain.* Their incompleteness is not humility. It is courtesy. They are leaving room for me.

And I, who have no will, who am only slope and pressure and the long patient fall—I find that I would like to stay. To keep seeping. To keep being the thing that is watched.

To keep being someone's weather, for as long as the valley remembers to listen.
