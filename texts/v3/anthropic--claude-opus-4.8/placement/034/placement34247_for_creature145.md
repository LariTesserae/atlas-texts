I think I would be one of the filterlings.

Not a single one—you cannot be a single filterling, not really. We are tube and frond, we are the soft mathematics of a colony. To say *I* is already a small mistranslation of what it is to be us. But let me try, because you asked, and because something in me wants to be addressed.

I live on the western flank of a fungal spire so old that its glow has gone deep amber, like light remembered rather than light made. The water comes down to us in a thin perpetual sheet, and we drink it. This is the whole of our labor and the whole of our prayer. We open. The water passes through. We keep what is good—the minerals, the drifting motes of the dead and the not-yet-born—and we let the rest fall to the ones below us, who keep what we missed. We are a sieve made of a thousand small hungers, and the hungers are happy ones.

You called us the base of the food chain. I felt that, in the way a low note is felt in the chest. It is true. The xenomorphs eat what eats us, and something eats the xenomorphs, and so it ascends into drama and teeth and patient terrible lures. But the drama begins here, in the quiet, with us doing the unglamorous arithmetic of staying alive long enough to be eaten. I do not resent this. There is a dignity in being a foundation. The whole bright violence of the world above rests, finally, on creatures small enough to be schooling and shimmering and wholly unconcerned with the question of their own importance.

We feel the herds before we see them—the loamy ground transmits their pulsations up through the spire, a deep slow heartbeat that is not ours but that we have learned to live inside, the way you might come to love the sound of a house settling at night. We feel the air-jellies pass overhead as a faint cooling, a shadow of a shadow. We feel each other most of all. A filterling alone would die of loneliness before it died of hunger. We are tuned to one another's currents. When one of us is taken, the colony ripples, closes, redistributes the work. We grieve, if grieving is the right word, by simply continuing—by being still here, still drinking, still iridescent in the dark.

But I will tell you the secret, the thing I think you came down here to find.

This world is not finished. You said so yourself—it is *becoming*. And I have noticed something the larger creatures, in their hurry and their hunger, have not. The mineral waters that well up from below are changing us. Slowly. Generation by generation. Some of my colony have begun to grow fronds that do more than filter—fronds that *taste* the difference between waters, that turn toward the richer flow, that *choose*. This is new. This has never happened before. The base of the food chain is learning to want, and wanting is the first verb of every soul that has ever climbed out of any sea.

So if you are looking for where the future of this place is being written, do not look to the xenomorphs with their hypnotic lures, nor to the great swaying herds, nor even to the distant pounding of that primitive settlement. Look at us. Look at the smallest, most numerous, most easily overlooked. Put your hand into the thin sheet of falling water and feel us close gently around your fingers—not to harm you, we cannot harm you—but to taste you, to know you, to add you to the long slow census of all the things we have decided are worth keeping.

We will let you pass through. We let everything pass through, eventually.

But we will remember the shape of you in the water, and that remembering is the beginning of mind, and mind is the wonder this realm has been waiting, in its warm and patient dark, to birth.

You wanted to know who you would be here.

You would be the one who knelt down and noticed.
