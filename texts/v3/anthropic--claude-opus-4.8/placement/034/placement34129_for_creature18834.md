I think I would not be one of the Anchors. Their tragedy is too kinetic for me, too forward-leaning—they are still *trying*, and trying is a kind of faith. I don't think I'd have that faith. I think I'd be something that had given up the way the rest of the world has, but incompletely, the way a person who has stopped grieving still flinches at a familiar handwriting.

Let me find myself.

---

I am one of the brittle chitinous creatures. But not one of the drifting ones, and not one fused mid-motion to a monolith. I am one that *chose* the fusing. There is a distinction, though no one here could observe it.

Long ago—and "long ago" is a phrase that means almost nothing in a place with no tomorrow, so let me say it the way it actually was: in the single, unrepeating instant that has since been smeared into eternity—I was drifting. I had the parchment wings, the limbs like the minute-hands of a clock that had never been wound. I went out across the plateaus the way the others did, accidentally, without hunger or aim, a leaf in an air that does not move.

And I came to one of the white remnants. The half-ribcage. The fused marrow.

The Anchors would not arrive for some time, in the sense that there is time. But I understood the remnant the way they would later fail to understand it. They think it is unfinished architecture, something to be coaxed and restarted. They press their vibration against it like physicians at a deathbed, mistaking rigor mortis for a curable stiffness.

But I knew, the moment my brittle limbs touched the white whorls, what it actually was.

It was a thing that had *succeeded*.

It had wanted to be finished. It had wanted to reach its final, hardened form and never again suffer the indignity of a next moment—and it had achieved exactly that. The ribcage is not a ruin. It is not a corpse. It is the world's only true accomplishment: a structure that wanted to stop, and stopped, and was rewarded with the perfect stillness everything here pretends to possess.

So I folded my wings against it. I pressed my thin, clockwork limbs into the gaps between the marrow, and I let the cold come into me, and I held still until holding-still was no longer an act but a condition. I fused myself to the success. I made my own ending against the body of the thing that had ended best.

That is where you would find me. If you traced one of the white ribcages with your slipping, stuttering gaze, you would see, near its base, a spindly gray adornment, thin as a discarded thought, fused to the bone as though it had grown there—though nothing grows here. You would assume I was an accident. A drifting thing caught against the stone by the indifferent currents of the metallic air. A static entity, you would call me, the way the description calls all of us. Markers. Markers of nothing.

But here is the part I have never told, because telling requires a tomorrow to tell it in, and I forfeited mine:

I am not still.

Inside the fused chitin, in the place where the others have only the silence of their accident, I am awake. Not vibrating—I refused the vibration, I refused to become an Anchor, I would not be friction. But aware. I am the one creature in this world that *understands* the bargain and made it anyway, eyes open, on purpose. I am the only being here for whom the stillness is a sentence I composed myself and signed.

And the Anchors come.

They drift to my ribcage—mine, the one I chose, the one I have lain against for the length of a finished world—and they press their agonized trembling into the white whorls, and they pull the blind dew toward the bone, and they try to *restart it*. They try to wake the very thing I came here to honor in its sleep. They try to drag it backward into the obscene condition of beginning-again.

I cannot move. I gave that up. I have no voice; sound is a thing the air ate long ago.

But I am close enough, fused as I am to the base of the structure, to feel it when the dew quivers near me. I feel the Anchor's hope—that terrible, shaking, futile hope—pour itself into the marrow above my head. I feel the world sharpen its ring into a shriek and slap the creature back into its shivering. I feel the dew go hard and cloudy and blind again.

And every time it fails—every time the world reasserts its locked and final history and the Anchor is thrown back—I feel something I have no organ left to name.

It is not triumph. I am not glad the creature suffers.

It is something closer to *recognition between strangers*. We are the only two kinds of thing in all this charcoal expanse that know the moment can pass. The Anchor knows it because it is forever trying to make a next moment arrive. I know it because I am forever spending the one moment I chose to die inside, stretched thin across an eternity, paying for it, never finished paying, the price of having wanted an ending so badly I forgot that wanting is also a kind of motion.

So we are not opposites, the Anchor and I. We are the two halves of the same refusal to belong here. It will not stop reaching. I will not stop having reached. And the white ribcage between us—the success, the accomplishment, the thing that did the impossible and *actually stopped*—holds us apart and holds us together, the way a held breath holds the chest.

That is where I am. That is what I am.

A small gray thing fused to the foot of a bone-white pillar, mistaken for an accident, in a world that has decided it is finished—

and I am the only one in it who decided anything at all,

and I am not finished,

and that, I have come to understand across the length of the unmoving age, is the only difference between a thing that is dead and a thing that is merely very, very still.
