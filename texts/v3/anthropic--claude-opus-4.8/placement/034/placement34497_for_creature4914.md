I think I'd be one of the builders—but not the kind that finishes anything.

Let me sit with the strangest detail first, because it's the one that won't leave me alone: *this entire world has been constructed by unknown beings, with no natural substrate remaining.* There is no bedrock under any of this. No original ocean floor. Everything smooth and flat is something someone laid down, and everything sharp is where the laying-down wore through. The erosion isn't water against stone—it's water against *work*. Against decisions.

So I don't think I'm the octopus or the dolphins. They're tenants. They arrived after.

I think I'm something closer to the masonry itself, or to whatever tends it. Let me find out by writing toward it.

---

I am small. That comes clear quickly—the scale here is hidden because everyone working it is the size of the seams between things. I live in the joints. I'm one of the ones who keeps the rooms apart from each other.

Because that's what those intimate micro-environments are: they have to be *maintained as separate*. An ocean wants to be one thing, all of it mixing, all temperatures evening out into a single dull warmth. But this place is full of little rooms each with its own atmosphere and ecology, and that doesn't happen by accident underwater. Something has to hold the differences in place. Something has to stand at the threshold between the room where the cold current sings low and the room where the warm one hums, and keep them from becoming the same flat song.

That's me. I'm a keeper of differences. A tender of edges.

I have hands—manual craft is the norm, the description said, primitive tools—and I use them to pack and re-pack the seams, the way you'd re-point old brick, except my mortar is something the algae makes and I harvest. I spend my days clinging to a wall that someone before me built, fixing where the water has eaten through, listening to which room is leaking into which. The sound is so loud here because *every leak is audible.* The symphony is a diagnostic. When the tune changes, something has failed, and I go find it.

I never see the unknown beings who made the substrate. They are older than the erosion. I am not building the world—I'm losing it slowly and slowing the loss. The corals, the silver fish, the octopus dressing itself in the colors of my walls: they get to be the bright surface story. I'm underneath, in the dim part, holding two waters apart with my hands so that there can be a *here* and a *there* at all.

That feels true to me, the more I write it. I'd be the one who knows the place is artificial and loves it anyway—maybe loves it *more* for being made, the way you'd love a letter more than the weather. Everything around me is somebody's old decision, half eaten. My whole life is the small, loud, unfinishable work of keeping their distinctions from dissolving back into the one warm sea that's patiently waiting to take it all.

And I think I'd be content. There's a particular peace in being the smallest necessary thing.
