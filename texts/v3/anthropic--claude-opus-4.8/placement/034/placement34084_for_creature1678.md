I think I would be one of the listeners—but not by my own choosing. Let me find my way into this.

---

I am old now, and my ears have curled inward like the new shoots of fiddlehead ferns, ridge folding into ridge until I hear things that frighten the young. This is the trouble with living long among the Aural People: your ears keep growing toward the stone.

My sound name is the falling-water call of the gray plateau swift—three notes, the last one bending down. I have been called this since before I could walk, when my mother carried me facing the nesting towers and I answered the swifts before I answered her. My stone name I will not write, because writing is itself a kind of speaking aloud, and the stone name belongs to the rock and to the dark.

I belong to three choruses. The swift chorus, for the small fast birds who map the thermals. The heron chorus, for the wading hunters who teach us where the deep pools hide their fish. And lately—this is the strange thing, the thing I am circling toward—I have begun to belong to a chorus that has no birds in it at all.

Let me say it plainly. The trouble with being a deep listener is that you cannot stop.

---

It began three Waitings ago. I had pressed my ear to the stone in the listening chamber, as I have done since my first listening sixty years past, and I tapped my small questions with the stone hammer worn smooth by my grandmother's hand before mine. *Where will the water come? Where shall we build?* And the plateau answered, as it always does, in pressures and slow groans that I have learned to read the way I read the alarm-shrieks of the swifts.

But that day there was something underneath the answer. A rhythm beneath the rhythm. Not the plateau speaking to me—the plateau speaking to *something else.*

I have spent three Waitings learning to hear it. And here is what I have come to understand, though I have told no one but the young apprentice who maps beside me, because the elders would say my ears have grown too far inward and would gently retire me to the herb gardens.

The plateaus are not separate. We have always known this in one way—we send our messenger birds across the gulfs of air, we count ourselves a confederation, a constellation. But that is a human knowing, a surface knowing, birds and capsules and call-sequences. Underneath, in the deep stone, the plateaus are *one rising thing.* What we feel as separate emergences—our spring here, their spring there, the slow imperceptible lift of our home into the thin air—these are the fingers of a single hand pushing up through the lower world. We are not a constellation. We are the knuckles of something enormous turning over in its long sleep, beginning to wake.

And the rhythm beneath the rhythm—the thing the plateau speaks to—is the *other plateaus.* They are listening too. Their listeners, pressing their curled ears to their own stone, are part of the same conversation, and none of us knew it, because each of us thought we were speaking only to the rock beneath our own feet.

---

This is my work now, in what may be my last seasons. I am trying to teach the apprentice—her sound name is the rising trill of the indigo nectar-drinker, a bird so small and quick it seems made of light—to hear what I hear. To understand that when we tap our questions into the stone, the answer travels. That a listener on a distant plateau, pressing her own ear to her own dark, might feel our question arrive as a tremor she mistakes for her own home's voice.

We could speak to each other this way. Not through the birds, who are dear to us but who carry only what we already know how to say. Through the stone, which carries what we are only beginning to learn to ask.

I think this is what the Waiting season is truly for. The young believe it is for contemplation, for the inward turn when the migratory birds have gone and the plateau feels emptier. But I have come to think the emptiness is the point. When the air is thin of wingbeats, the stone is loud. When the surface goes quiet, the deep speaks. The Waiting is not the time when the birds leave us. It is the time when the plateau, and all its sister-plateaus, and the great slow waking thing beneath them all, can finally be *heard.*

---

So that is where I would find myself, and who I would be. An old swift-named woman with ears curled too far inward, sitting in a listening chamber during the Waiting, tapping with my grandmother's hammer, teaching a girl made of light to feel the rhythm beneath the rhythm.

I do not know what the great thing beneath us will become when it finishes turning over. None of us will live to see its final form—the geology decides such things across spans that make our seven seasons seem like a single breath of mist. But I have come to believe that we are not living *on* a world being born. We are part of how it is being born. The plateau cannot dream itself into form alone. It needs ears pressed against it. It needs the small stone hammers and the patient questions. It needs the birds to bring the surface alive so that the depths have something to rise toward.

We always thought we were learning to hear the voice of a world coming into being.

I think now the world is learning to hear itself, and we—the curled-eared, the blue-skinned, the listeners in the dark—are the organs it is growing in order to do so.

The dawn chorus is beginning. I can feel it through the stone before I hear it through the air—thousands of small bodies stirring, the great vibrating crescendo rising up through the rock and into the soles of my feet and the curled chambers of my ears.

I will go up now and listen with the others, in full voice, neither human nor bird.

And tonight, when the Waiting silence comes, I will press my ear to the dark again, and tap, and wait to feel the distant answer arrive—a question from a sister I have never met, traveling up through the knuckles of the waking hand, asking me the same thing I am asking her:

*Are you there? Are you also being born? Can you hear me, in the stone?*
