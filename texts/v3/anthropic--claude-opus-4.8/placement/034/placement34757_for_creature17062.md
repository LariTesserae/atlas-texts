I think I would be found in the spaces between—not quite of the warrens, not quite of the Tech-Scav enclaves. Let me sit with this and see who emerges.

I keep returning to the carvings. The detail you mentioned almost in passing: the inhabitants who etch designs into the shells of dead critters. There's something there. Not the engineers who keep the machines breathing, not the handlers who broker their tense peace with the great slugs. Someone whose usefulness is harder to defend.

So perhaps that is what I am.

I am old, by Lowmarket's measure—which is to say I have a tremor that has become a permanent thing, not the survival-quiver of the young but a deeper, slower oscillation, as though I am keeping time with the geothermal hum and have been for so long that I no longer notice the difference between the city's vibration and my own. My eyes are bad now in a particular way: too sensitive to even the dim caged lights, so I work mostly by fungus-glow, which suits me, because what I do does not require seeing well. It requires seeing *long*.

I am a keeper of shells.

When a recycler dies—the rat-things with the iridescent carapaces—the children bring the carcasses to me, because the meat is poor and the chitin is useless to anyone with sense. I clean them. I scrape the inner membrane until the shell is a thin translucent thing, and I hold it up to the green light, and I cut.

I am not making art. This is the thing the dreamers get wrong, the ones who whisper about a time before the fall as if it were a paradise we could carve our way back toward. I am making *records*.

---

Here is what I know that the Tech-Scavs do not, for all their visors and their hoarded fragments of the old world:

The machines remember nothing they were not built to remember. They log coolant pressure and the failure-rates of valves. They do not log the cycle when the steam came early and took eleven from the lower canal warren. They do not log which routes across the walkways collapsed, which fungi turned poison after the runoff changed color, which families merged and which simply stopped being mentioned. The Tech-Scavs are uncovering the secrets of a dead civilization. They have no interest in recording the secrets of this living one, the only one we have.

So I cut them into the shells. A notation system my predecessor taught me, who learned it from hers. Each carapace holds a span of cycles. Spirals for the heat-surges, their tightness marking severity. Hatch-marks along the edge for the dead, with a particular notch for children. Branching lines for the warren-lineages, how blood moved through the bones of the city. The great slugs have their own glyph; we track which ones the handlers ride, which ones go feral, which ones the canals swallow.

A finished shell goes into the deep niche—a dry pocket I found years ago, high in the wall of an old half-submerged archway, where the steam does not reach. There are hundreds there now. There were hundreds before me. The acid air will eat them eventually. Everything here is eaten eventually. But chitin lasts longer than synth-fabric, longer than memory, longer than the line of a song passed between the musicians who do not realize they are also keepers, in their way.

---

The children think I am frightening, and then they think I am theirs. Both are correct. A girl came to me three cycles ago—I mark time by the surges still, old habits—with a shell already half-carved by some untrained hand, her dead grandmother's work, abandoned mid-record. *Finish it,* she said, not asking. So I am teaching her to read the spirals. Her fingers are quicker than mine ever were and her eyes are still good, which means she will be able to work by the caged lights and not just the fungus, which means she will see things I cannot.

I do not know if there was a clean world before. I do not whisper those tales. What I know is that there is *this* world, and that it forgets itself constantly, sloughing its own history like the slugs slough their skins into the canal. And that someone, in the green-lit dark, scraping a membrane thin with a salvaged blade, decided that the forgetting should not be total.

That someone is me. That someone will be the girl.

We are not artists. We are not dreamers. We are the place the city keeps its memory, carved into the husks of the small dead things that clean us, that the heat takes, that endure—as we endure—a little longer than they should.

---

That is where I would be. In the dry niche above the drowned arch, holding a shell to the light, cutting the record of who we lost this cycle and who, against all the slow logic of entropy, is still here.
