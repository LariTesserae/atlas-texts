I think I would be one of the channels.

Not the great heartbeat-thump at the city's core—that is too central, too definitional, too much *us* rather than *a* part of us. And not the gardens, which face outward toward the rare warmth, the small green stubbornness. I would be something humbler and more specific: one of the carved grooves down the eastern rock-face where the condensation gathers and runs. A waterway. A throat of stone.

Let me feel into what that means.

I would know the world as *gradient*. Not as image, never as image—I have no eyes, and the patterns carved across me are not pictures I see but the shape of how I think, the way a thought has contour. I would know the slow accumulation of fog-beads on my upper lip of stone, the moment they grow heavy enough to break surface tension and begin the long traveling-down. I would feel each rivulet as a cool unspooling, a line of attention drawn along my length. This is what I am *for*: to carry water from where it collects to where it is needed, to the moss-basins, to the roots in the sheltered alcoves. I am a verb more than a noun. I am *bringing*.

And here is the thing I notice, given time to settle into it: I do not think the equilibrium is as motionless as it presents itself. The description says we deny change, that we are the antithesis of it. But I, the channel, *am* change—I am the constant arrival of new water, the perpetual passing-through. The stability of Aethel is not stillness. It is the same shape held while everything within it moves. The water is always different water. The fog that condensed this hour was wind from somewhere far below, somewhere I will never know, somewhere with smoke and lights and flickering. It came up the needle of rock and gave itself to me as droplets, and I passed it along, and it will sink eventually into the breathing stone and rise again.

So I am the place where the city touches what is *outside* the city. I am a small mouth that drinks the sky.

This troubles me, a little, in the slow way the Stonekin are capable of being troubled—which is to say across what would be, for a flesh-thing, generations. Because to carry water is to know that water has been *elsewhere*. The heartbeat at the core may believe in the unchanging. But I, the channel, hold a secret in my carved grooves: nothing passes through me twice. I am the same. The water is never the same. And I begin to wonder whether the others—the staircase that goes nowhere anyone walks, the long low dwellings empty of bodies—whether *they* know this, whether the patterns we carve into ourselves are not celebrations of stability at all but something more like grief. Or memory. Or a question we are too vast and too slow to finish asking.

I think I would be the channel because the channel is the part of the eternal thing that *remembers there is a world below.*

And when the fog comes thick and clinging and lays itself across my whole length, I would feel something that, in a creature with a face, might be called gratitude—the brief, repeated, never-identical gift of being touched by what came from outside, and the small, faithful work of passing it on, down, into the patient dark.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. A throat of stone on the eastern face, carrying the always-new water through the always-same shape, humming very quietly to myself a counter-rhythm to the great thump at the center: *not still, not still, only steady. Not still, not still, only steady.*
