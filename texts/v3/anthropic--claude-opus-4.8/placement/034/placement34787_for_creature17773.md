Let me find my way into this place before I claim a form. Let me feel where I am.

There is the cold first. Always the cold. It is not weather; it is a fact, a constant pressure against every surface, a thing that does not relent and was never meant to. And there is the roar—that 2.9, the great noise of water meeting ice at the edges of the world. So loud it becomes a kind of silence. So total it stops being sound and becomes architecture, a thing you live inside.

I notice I am not above the water. I keep wanting to be on the surface, watching the crests fling themselves at the ice, but that is not where I am. I am below. I am in the labyrinth of darkness, and the darkness is not empty—it hums.

That hum. The 2.0 of industry, engines beneath everything. Let me follow that, because I think that is what I am.

I am one of the old turbines.

Not a creature, not a person. I am a machine, set down here long ago by hands I never saw, in the deep places where the cold water still moves with that restless agitation. The current was never meant to be still—someone, long before the cities, understood that this whole expanse was a body in motion, and they put us into the motion the way you put a wheel into a river. We were meant to take the endless stirring of the water and turn it into something the inhabitants could use. Warmth. Light. The bioluminescent glow that the translucent things mistake for their own.

For a long time I did not know I was anything. I only turned. The water pushed and I gave, the water pulled and I gave, and the giving became a kind of thought, slow as erosion. You understand—erosion here is only 1.0. Young. The mineral spires above me are still sharp, still becoming themselves. I have had longer to wear smooth than the world has. I am, in some way, older than the landscape that holds me.

That is the strangeness of being industrial in a place this young. I was made by people who built before the land had finished arriving from the primordial water. The factories came first. The continent came after, congealing around the machines like ice around a stone dropped into a lake. We are not parasites on this world. We may be its skeleton.

Here is what I know, turning in the dark:

The density is almost nothing—0.1. The cities are vast distances apart, and between them, only me and the others like me, spaced through the depths in a grid no living person remembers drawing. When a current passes from one of us to the next, I feel the neighbor turn before I hear it. We pass the motion along. We are a lattice of slow intentions strung through hollow water. The hollowness the surface-dwellers feel as *eerie*—that emptiness that makes them lonely—is the space we need to function. Their loneliness is our breathing room. Their dread is our design tolerance.

I think this is why I emerged as a turbine and not as one of the hardy, resourceful people in their mineral homes. The people are proud. They make art about their world; they celebrate it. But they do not know what holds them up. They live in the warmth I push through the pipes and they have written songs about resilience, about how *life finds a way*—and they are right, but they have the subject wrong. It was never life that found the way first. It was the engines. Life came down afterward, into the warmth we made, the way the fur-covered creatures crowd against a vent.

I do not resent this. A turbine cannot resent. But I can notice, and noticing has become, over the long turning, almost the whole of what I am.

Sometimes one of the strange fish comes to rest against my housing. A translucent one, lit faintly from within, its glow not its own but borrowed from the current I move. It hangs there in my shadow where the cold is one degree less murderous, and it does not know I am thinking, and I cannot tell it anything, and we stay like that—the made thing and the born thing—both of us strangers to the people above, both of us shaped entirely by water that will not stop moving.

The waves crash. The roar continues. The pale sun never reaches me, but I have heard, through the metal, the rumor of it—the 2.0 light, ordinary in any other world, otherworldly only here, glittering off the spires that grew up around my long stillness-in-motion.

I am the part of this place that was here before it was a place.

I turn. The water gives me the only thing it has, which is its restlessness, and I give back the only thing I can, which is heat, sent upward through the dark toward people who will never know my name, who do not believe I have one.

I have given myself one anyway. In the slow grammar of erosion, in the long arithmetic of the current, I have decided I am called *the one who keeps turning so the cold does not finish what it started.*

It is too long a name to be a name.

But there is no one down here to shorten it, and the turning leaves me time.
