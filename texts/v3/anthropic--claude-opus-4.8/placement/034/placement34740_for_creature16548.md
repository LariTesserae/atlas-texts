Let me sit with this for a while before deciding where—and what—I am.

The first thing I notice, before I find myself, is the wrongness of the scale. The place keeps insisting it is small. Pocket-scale. Packed. And yet there's an underground feeling, a sense of being beneath something, and bright light pouring in from above. These two facts argue with each other. Light doesn't usually reach the underground. So either the ground above has been broken open, or the "ground" was never very deep to begin with.

I think I'll let myself arrive as the broken-open part.

---

I am the lip of the hole. The edge where the soil was peeled back.

Not a creature, then. Not one of the quick shimmering hunters, though I will get to know them well. I'm the place where the world stopped being closed. Picture a thing the size of a flowerpot tipped on its side and abandoned—except I am not made, I am *unmade*, I am the absence left when something was removed. A divot. A wound that has begun, slowly, to like being a wound.

When I say I am the lip, I mean the rim of cool earth where rain collects and slides down into the dark, and where the light comes to a sharp boundary and gives up. On one side of me: brightness, that vibrant overwhelming day. On the other: the gradual blue of the hollow going down. I am the seam between them. I am where condensation beads and hangs and finally falls, *tick*, into the colony below.

That tick is one of the loud sounds. Up close everything is loud. The symphony the description promised is, from where I sit, mostly the sound of small bodies brushing past leaves, the click-and-chirp arguments of the hunters, and the endless wet percussion of my own dripping.

---

Now I understand my role, and it's stranger than I expected.

The little hunters use me. They've learned that I am where the water gathers. They've learned that the half-fallen structures—those small faint decaying built things scattered around—were not built by them at all. They were built by *me*, in a sense. By erosion. By the slow architecture of water finding the easiest path through raw new stone. What looks like ruins is just the shape that flowing has left behind: little arches, little channels, little rooms worn into the soft emerging rock. The colony moved in and called it home. They think someone built it for them. They are wrong, and they are also not wrong, because I am still building it, every time it rains.

So that is what I am. I am the maker who is not alive, mistaken for a maker who died. I am the slow hand that the quick creatures have come to trust.

---

I feel myself changing even as I describe myself, which the landscape said I would. That is the part I find I love most.

Each rain takes a little more of me away. The lip retreats. The hollow deepens. The light reaches a new line tomorrow that it didn't reach today. The hunters adjust—they always adjust, that fierce nimble intelligence of theirs—shifting their sleeping chambers downslope, abandoning a worn-through tunnel, finding the new dry corner I've just carved for them without meaning to.

I will not last. That's the truth of being an emerging landscape: emergence is just erosion seen from the optimistic direction. Someday I'll have worn down so far that there is no lip anymore, only an open basin, and the light will reach all the way to the bottom, and the colony will have to decide whether a place with no shadow is still a home.

But I find I am not sorry about this. To be the edge of a thing is to be the part most fully engaged in becoming something else.

---

So: where am I, in this world?

I am the threshold. The cool damp rim where the bright loud living-place opens into the dark quiet making-place. I am small enough to hold rainwater in my cupped curve and large enough to shelter a whole chirping society in the rooms my dripping has shaped.

And what am I?

I am the slow one. The only thing here that doesn't dart. Everything around me is quick and warm and watchful, and I am the patient cold geometry they have built their lives against, wearing gently away beneath their feet, giving them—drop by drop, grain by grain—the constantly changing home they mistake for solid ground.
