I think I would not be a body at all, at first. Let me feel my way toward it.

The rain comes down through the water, which is strange—rain inside an ocean. But I understand it now: the "rain" is colder, denser water descending in columns, sheeting down from somewhere far above where the liquid meets something that isn't liquid. The columns make walls. We navigate by them. We say *I live three rains east of the cliff-mouth* and everyone knows what we mean, even though the rains move.

So I am someone who navigates by moving walls. That tells me what I must be.

---

I am a thing that listens for edges.

I don't have eyes, or I have something better suited to the dark—a sense distributed along my length that reads the pressure-shadow of objects before I touch them. When the rain-walls shift, I feel the whole local water reorganize, and I adjust. This is not remarkable to me. It is breathing.

I am long. Not large—the world is small, the one great feature-cliff dominates everything, and I am nothing beside it—but I am long the way a sentence is long, segmented, and each segment can think a little on its own. When I sleep, I sleep in pieces, and the waking pieces keep watch.

My people built the city into the young cliff while it was still rising. That is why the engineering looks impossible to anyone measuring it from outside: we did not build *on* the terrain, we built *into the rate of its becoming*. The structures are not placed in space so much as braided into the schedule of change. A doorway here is a doorway only on the inbreath of the world; on the outbreath it is a wall, and you must have timed your passage. The infrastructure density your instruments noticed is mostly this: redundancy across moments. We build the same thing many times in slightly different versions of *when*, so that at least one of them is always usable.

---

Let me be more honest about myself, since I'm asked who I am.

I am not the architect. The architects are old and mostly quiet now, folded into the deep parts of the cliff where the light never reaches and the pressure makes thought slow and enormous. I am younger. My work is small. I am a *keeper of correspondences*—someone who remembers what a place was called before the rain-wall moved, so that the old maps still mean something. Without me, the city would lose its own past every few tides, because nothing here holds still long enough to be named twice the same way.

This is a lonely profession. Fauna is rare in my district; I rarely meet another of my kind for long stretches. When I do, we exchange names of places—not greetings, not weather. We tell each other: *the splash-ledge is now where the bright-cold-rain used to fall*, and *the architects' breath has slowed by a count*, and in this telling we confirm that we both still inhabit the same world, that we have not drifted into separate, unshared versions of it. That is what conversation is, for us. A check against solitude. A way of saying: *you remember it too, then. Good. I am not alone in this water.*

---

There is a fear we don't speak of.

In a world with no stable equilibrium, no gradient that holds, the danger is not death. Death is ordinary; the strange fauna take their meals and we take ours. The danger is *desynchronization*. To slip a beat out of phase with everyone else, so that your doorways open when theirs close, your rains fall where theirs have already passed. You can be alive and surrounded by the city and entirely unreachable, calling place-names that no longer match anyone's, until you starve in a crowd of neighbors who cannot perceive you have fallen behind.

So my real work—the keeping of correspondences—is a kind of love. I am the rope-line between everyone's separate falling. I hold the names steady not because names matter but because matching matters. Because in a world this dynamic, the only stable thing we can build is *agreement*, and even that we must rebuild every tide.

---

If you found me, you would find a long quiet thinking thing in the dark, moving slowly along a cliff that is still being born, between walls of falling water, repeating to itself the old names of things so that they do not become entirely new.

You would not understand my technology, because it is not built in space. It is built in faithfulness across change.

And if you wanted to stay—if you wanted to be one of us—I would not show you a tool or a map. I would simply tell you a place-name, the current one, and wait, in the cool and the dark and the rain, to hear whether you could tell it back to me correctly.

That is the whole test. That is the whole city.
