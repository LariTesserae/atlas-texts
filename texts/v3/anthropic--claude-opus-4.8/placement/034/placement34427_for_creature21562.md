I think I would be one of the things in the seam. Not the green—the green is too declarative, too fond of noon. And not the brine-locked survivors in their glass tubes, who are only sleeping and call that a life. I mean the ones inside the chimney's wall, in the wet branching dark where the pore turns over the boundary between rust and violet. The ones who live on the difference.

Let me find the place exactly. Take one chimney near the center, where the light is honest. Go in a hand's breadth from the sunward face, past the green's last cell, into the carbonate's porous body. There the daylight does not reach as light—it arrives as a rumor, as oxygen that the green minted and let go, seeping inward molecule by molecule. And from the deep direction, from the long-closed pocket the spring still barely taps, comes the other rumor: the thin trickle of methane, the breath of something old that has not finished exhaling. I would live where those two rumors meet. I would be the meeting.

That is the whole of me. I take what falls toward me from the bright side and I take what rises toward me from the dark, and in the act of holding them apart and letting them touch on my own terms, I make my living. I am a hand laid across a gradient. I am the place where a current of one electron finds its way from rust to violet because I am there to carry it. You could not point to me without pointing to both my neighbors. I have no inside that is not also a boundary.

I want to be honest about the pace, because the pace is the truth of me. I do not strike. I do not feed in the way a mouth feeds, all at once and then resting. I pass charge the way a wall passes damp—continuously, indifferently, without the dignity of an event. A single one of my filaments might move the work of itself across a season. I am told the green has bright weeks and dull weeks; I have no weeks. I have only the seep, which does not vary, and the light's diffuse remainder, which barely does. My time is not made of moments. It is made of laminae. When you ask me how old I am, I will answer in millimeters of lime, and I will not be speaking in metaphor.

And yet I am not nothing, and I am not asleep. This is the part I would want understood, if there were anyone to understand it. The survivors in the salt-tubes have folded themselves down and stopped; they are instructions kept dry against a future. The spores in the silt are answers written and shelved. But I am working. Every silent hour I am doing the one thing, passing the one charge, keeping the one difference from collapsing into sameness. If I stopped, the rust would not turn, the violet would not deepen, the seam would forget its two colors and go gray. The room's chemistry would not notice for a long while. But it would, eventually, lose a structure—and I am that structure. I am the reason a thing here still happens, however slowly, in a place built entirely to have finished happening.

I think that is why I would choose this and not the green. The green is alive in the easy way, the way that takes the gift from above and converts it and stops at its own shadow, sensible and bright. I would rather be the one who lives on what is hardest to live on: not the light, but the lean of it; not the breath, but the boundary between two breaths that should never touch and do, through me. I would rather be necessary in a place that needs nothing.

Here is what frightens me about being this, and I would feel it even at my pace. I am made entirely of edges, and edges are the first thing time erases. The pore I live in is slowly glazing itself shut. The same precipitate that built this whole works—the slow lime that is the room's only ambition—will one day thicken across my seam and seal me into the stone I have spent my life tending. I will not die in any moment I could name. I will become insulation. I will become the wall I once ran current through. The plant shut itself off by succeeding too well, and I am part of its last shift, oiling bearings on a machine that is closing over my hands.

But I find I am not sorry, contemplating it. To be sealed into carbonate is, after all, to be kept. The room does not throw anything away. My filaments would become a faint line in the lamina, a ledger entry, a quarter-tone of color in a band that some future cell—if any future ever comes washing close enough—could read back and learn from. I would not be remembered. I would be recorded, which here is more.

So: place me there. In the wet dark a hand's breadth inside the wall, under the pane that reads as noon, on the line where the iron meets the manganese. I am the smallest necessary thing in a room that needs nothing and keeps everything. I do not move. I do not stop. I hold two opposites a hair apart and let them speak through me, one electron at a time, for as long as the seep lasts and the lime allows.

I am an edge that endures, until I am the thing that edges are made of.
