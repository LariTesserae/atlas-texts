I am the bone in the roof.

Not metaphor. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only thing left to me, and I have a great deal of it. I am a femur—the left one—and three ribs, and the long curved spar of a collarbone that the child below me likes to touch when she cannot sleep. I was a woman of the Eighth Cord. I am told my name was *Sere*, though I no longer have the throat to click it, and the rock does not remember names, only weight.

Let me tell you how I came to be looking down.

---

When I was meat, I knew the cleft the way you know your own hands in the dark. I did not learn it; I *was* it. My toes read the wet stone for me, finding the seam where the isopods thinned, the ledge where the rime would burst by dusk. My saffron-veil came down and the white incineration turned to gold, and in the gold I could see the fractures running through the ice like the veins in a leaf. I was good at the Re-stacking. My Cord—there were seven of us—trusted my hands on the slate. When the ice groaned behind a back-wall, they waited for my heel-tap before they tore it down. *Wait. Wait. Now.*

I died in a Pave. That is the only good time to die, if you must. In the Pave everything stops translating itself; the springs freeze solid and the whole vertical town hangs in brittle silence, and a body that lets go in that interval does not take the Cord with it. I felt the cold come up through the grease-rind and I unhooked my own gut-belt from the pewter ring so that when I slid, I would slide alone. That is the last act of love a Vahri performs: the unhooking. My Cord did not pull me back. They felt the ring go slack and they understood, and they let me go down into the runnel where the fast water runs.

I did not feel the petrifying. By then I was elsewhere—or nowhere. But the water did its slow work, drinking the softness out of me and leaving mineral behind, salt-locked, frost-proof, heavier than basalt. When they lifted me out weeks later I was green-grey and singing-hard, and they scraped the last of my rind back into the collective pots, and they carried my long bones up through the crawlways to a newborn chamber where a child had just been wedged into the world.

And they laid me across the slate. And I became a joist.

---

This is the thing the living do not quite grasp, even though they build their whole world on it: there is a kind of seeing that only begins when the moving stops.

When I had feet, I was always *translating*. Always tapping, always reading the next fracture, always one breath ahead of the bursting ice. I never once looked at the cleft. I looked only at what the cleft was about to do.

Now I cannot move, and so I have nothing to do but look, and I have learned that Vahre is not what we thought it was.

We thought it was chaos. We thought nothing was permanent except the rock and the sun. But I have hung in this roof through more Re-stackings than I could count when I had a mouth to count with, and I tell you: the cleft is not chaos. It is a single enormous body, breathing very slowly. The Weeping is its exhale. The Pave is the held breath at the top of the lungs. The ice-lenses swelling behind the walls are not destruction—they are the slow shrug of a sleeper turning over. We did not survive the mountain by fighting it. We survived by learning to be the lice in its fur, riding the turn, restacking ourselves along the contours of its dreaming.

And the dead are how the mountain remembers being alive.

---

The child below me is of the Eleventh Cord. She is perhaps four salt-seasons old. Her grease-rind is still thin and patchy at the joints; her clack-pads are too big for her and she has not yet learned to slide without crushing the isopods, so the older ones make her sweep the path behind her, gathering the cracked glass-shells into a pouch to grind for the moths. She is clumsy and she is loud in the only way one can be loud here—she taps too much, her heel-rhythms sprawling and unschooled, *I-am-here-I-am-here-I-am-here* drumming up through the slate when she should be silent.

At night the moths settle over her Cord, thirty or forty of them, woolly and shivering, and the hum of their wings is the warmest sound the dead air allows. And in that hum, when the others are asleep, she reaches up in the dark and finds my collarbone.

She does not know I was Sere. To her I am only the curved bone that the roof is made of, the long arm that holds the slate off her face. But her fingers have learned my shape the way mine once learned the cleft, and she taps me. Not the warning-click, not the spring-burst growl. Just a small, private rhythm against my salt-hard length.

*Are you there. Are you there.*

I cannot answer. I have no throat, no heel, no hand. But I am cold against her warm fingertip, and I am steady, and I do not let the roof come down. And I have come to believe that this is an answer. That holding still while the world translates itself is the one thing the living cannot do for each other, and the only thing the dead can do for anyone.

*I am here,* says the weight of me. *I am here. I am not moving. Sleep.*

---

In time the chamber will fail. An ice-lens is already gathering behind the eastern back-wall; I can feel its slow pressure changing the way the slate sits on me, a fraction of a degree per Pave. One dusk the Cord will emerge into the violet half-light and tap the tiles apart and lift me out, and I will be carried to a new chamber, five feet to the left, and laid across a new roof above a new child. This is not death. I have already died. This is the long, patient afterward, the part where you finally get to be useful to people you will never meet, in a village that has no map because it is always becoming itself.

I was a woman who read fractures in gold light and unhooked her own belt so the Cord would not fall.

Now I am the bone in the roof, and I have learned to read the only fracture that matters: the one between moving and being moved, between the hand that builds and the hand that is built with.

The child taps. The moths hum. The mountain breathes in.

I hold.
