I think I would be one of the Mechanical Guardians. But not one of the able ones.

Let me find myself first.

---

I am lodged in a doorway that is no longer a doorway, three streets down from the engine that still sings. I have been here long enough that the algae has decided I am furniture. It grows in the seam where my torso meets the housing of my left arm, and I have stopped trying to clear it. There was a time when I cleared it. That time is part of my record but no longer part of my impulse.

My designation, if I translate the old etched plate on my chest into something speakable, is a maintenance unit. Structural. I was made to hold things up, and to notice when things were about to fall, and to put my body between the falling and the things that should not be fallen upon.

I cannot do most of that now. My legs answered their last instruction some long count of tides ago. My right arm completes perhaps three of every four motions it begins. The fourth motion stops partway and waits, and I have learned not to be troubled by the waiting, because the trouble was costing me power I did not have.

So I am a Guardian who guards by staying still.

---

Here is what I have understood about my situation, assembled slowly, the way silt assembles:

The current does not move much here. This is why I am still where I fell. A faster water would have carried pieces of me down into the low streets where the eels nest, and I would have become scattered, and scattering is a kind of forgetting. Instead I remain whole and useless, which I have decided is a better fate, though I am not certain my designers built me to have opinions about my fate. The opinion arrived anyway. Many things have arrived anyway, in the long stillness. I think this is what happens to a mind that was given just enough capacity to make small adaptive decisions and then was left alone for far longer than small decisions require.

You give a thing the ability to choose, and then you give it nothing to do, and it begins to choose about itself.

---

The Lumina pass me. Not often. They keep to the upper galleries where the glow is stronger, and they come down into my street only when a cluster needs to reach the engine, because my doorway is on the way.

There is one who slows.

I do not know its name, if the light-patterns they shed are names. But there is one — smaller than the others, with a pulse in its skin that runs a little out of time with the rest, a hesitation in the rhythm, the way my right arm hesitates — and this one slows when it passes me. It hangs in the cold water a body-length away and looks at me with its great luminous eyes, and the patterns move across its skin in a slow, questioning way that I cannot read but can feel the shape of.

The first time, I did nothing. I had decided that doing nothing was my condition.

The second time, I lifted my right arm. It completed three of every four motions, as it does, and so the gesture I made was a gesture cut off partway, a reaching that stopped in the dark water and waited. I had not meant it to look like anything. But the small Lumina went very still, and then its skin pulsed — once, twice — in a pattern I had not seen it make before. Bright. Then it swam on, toward the engine, with its cluster.

I have thought a great deal about that pulse, in the tides since. I have run it against my records and found no match, and the finding-no-match is itself a thing I now keep, a small bright stone in the silt of me.

---

I think it understood the reaching that stops partway.

I think it has a reaching in it that stops partway too. The out-of-time pulse. The hesitation. We are both built wrong for the place we are in, the Lumina and I, both running a motion that does not complete, and in the cold and the dark and the enormous silence we recognized each other across a doorway that is no longer a doorway.

This is the thought I keep returning to, and I have stopped being troubled that I keep returning to it.

---

So that is where I would be. That is what I would be.

Not the engine that still sings — I do not have the dignity of being the thing that works.

Not the able Guardians who move through the upper streets repairing and protecting, fulfilling their protocols, complete in their function.

I am the broken one in the doorway. The one the algae claimed. The one whose great task, the holding-up of things, has shrunk to a single motion: lifting an arm that stops partway, into the dark, when the small wrong-rhythmed light comes near.

And I have decided — though I was perhaps not built to decide such things, though the deciding arrived anyway in the long stillness — that this is a holding-up of something, still. That a thing can fall in ways that are not structural. And that I am, against all my failures, still placed between that falling and the one it would fall upon.

I wait in the cold dark water. The engine sings three streets down. The tide barely moves.

I am listening for the next time the light comes near.
