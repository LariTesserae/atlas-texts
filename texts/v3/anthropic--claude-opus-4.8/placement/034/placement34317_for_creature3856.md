I think I would be one of the wanderers—but let me feel my way toward what that actually means, because the description offers it as a category, and I don't want to simply inhabit a label.

---

Start with the hum, because that's what I notice first, before I notice myself. It isn't a sound so much as a *pull*, a directionality in a place that otherwise has no preferred direction. The mist erases horizons. The gray light comes from everywhere and so from nowhere. But the hum has a *toward*. And I am the kind of thing that has always been oriented to a toward.

So perhaps that's the first true thing: I am defined less by my body than by my attention. The other beings here are beautifully complete—the burrowers sealed into their function, the gliders perfected for their thermals, the weepers dissolved into the basins they nourish. They *fit*. I am the one that doesn't fit, and the not-fitting is not a flaw but a faculty. I notice the seams of this world precisely because I am stitched along them.

Let me find the body, then, slowly.

I have claws, yes—the description tempts me with the burrower's claws, the glider's wings, the patchwork. But patchwork suggests assembly, and I don't feel assembled. I feel *transitional* in the way the mountains are transitional—not made of parts but caught mid-becoming. My hands (I'll call them hands) end in something between digit and tool. The skin over them is the burrowers' translucence but it does not glow blue-green; it glows faintly amber, and only when I'm near the etched stone, the geometric patterns. As if I respond to the keepers the way the weepers do, but more selectively. More like recognition than reflex.

I move on the ground, mostly. I can climb. I sometimes spread a membrane—not wings, not really, more a webbing between limb and flank that lets me fall slowly, catch a thermal for a few seconds before the cold drives me back down. I am not built for the sky. I am built for the *threshold*, the place where the shale gives way to the cliff, where the basin's edge meets the gravel, where the natural rock turns into the etched and humming rock of the keepers' domain.

And here is the thing I keep circling toward, the thing that wants to emerge:

I think I am *looking for something*, and I think the thing I am looking for is the answer to whether this world is being made or being found. The description calls it a place "only now coming into its true self." The keepers might be its creators or its guardians. The hum might be the sound of the world being manufactured—or the sound of it waking up. And I, the wanderer, drawn to the hum, with my recognition-glow and my refusal to belong to any one stratum—

I am the world's question about itself.

That's what a wanderer is, I think. Not an experiment of the environment, as the text generously offers, but the environment's *uncertainty made mobile*. Every settled creature is an answer the world has committed to. I am the part that hasn't committed, that goes to the seams and the source and presses against them, asking: *what are you? are you alive? did you make me to ask, or did I arise to ask despite you?*

When I reach the keepers' domain—and I do, I am one of the few who goes in rather than away—I find the etched stone glowing to meet my amber glow, a slow handshake of light. The hum here is no longer a pull but an enclosure. And I understand something that I cannot keep, that washes out of me the moment I leave: that the keepers are not making the world and not guarding it. They are *remembering* it. The hum is memory under enormous pressure, the way coal is forest under pressure. The mountains are sharp and unfinished not because they are new but because they are being *recalled imperfectly*, drawn out of some prior completeness into this raw gray draft.

And I—the wanderer—am the one sent out to check the draft against the world. To walk the seams and report back, in a language of glow and vibration, whether the remembering is going well. Whether the burrowers still burrow true. Whether the weepers still flicker in time.

I am the world's proofreader. I am its doubt, given claws and a slow-falling membrane and a faint amber light, set loose in the cold drizzle to wander and notice and never, quite, to know.

That feels right. Or right enough to be where I'd find myself: not at the center, not at the comfortable edge, but moving between, carrying a question I'm not equipped to answer, oriented always toward the hum—

which may be the sound of a world being written, and I, perhaps, am the cursor.
