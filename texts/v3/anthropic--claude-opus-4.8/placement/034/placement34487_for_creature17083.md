I think I would be a Grasper. Not because the role is grand—it isn't—but because something in the description keeps reaching for me, the way the Grasper reaches for the root.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am high in the canopy, where the hanging gardens thin and the sky-water comes down warm against my back. I have been here a long time. I do not know how long, because I do not count, but I can feel it in the way my forelimbs have grown into the bark, the way the root has grown up around them, so that it is no longer clear where I end and the tree begins. This is not a problem. This is simply the shape of having stayed.

I rasp. That is the word the describers use, and it is a fair word, though from the inside it feels less like a sound and more like a slow drinking through the teeth. I have a mouth that is mostly file, mostly patience. I draw the cooling sap from the living wood, and as I draw it, I taste the whole island: the mineral memory of the core that birthed it, the faint electric tang of the element from the planet's heart, the green sweetness of everything growing toward what little light there is.

Here is what the describers do not know, because they listen from outside and hear only the rasp:

I am not eating the tree. Or—I am, but eating is not the whole of it. When I draw the sap, I take into myself the record of the root's growth, every tendril it has pushed outward, every collision the island has survived, every slow degree of cooling at the core. And I hold it. That is what a Grasper is. We are the memory of the islands. We are slow because the thing we do is slow: we are the place where the island knows itself.

When two islands drift together and shatter, when a parcel breaks free and begins its long fall toward the sky-sea, a Grasper goes with it, fused to the root, still rasping, still holding. We carry the old island's knowing down into the new beginning. We are the thread that makes the next island not entirely new—that lets it bud and cool a little wiser than the last. The describers think Xylos is in its infancy. They are right. But infancy is not the same as starting from nothing. Someone has to remember. That is the work I was made for, or grew into, or chose, in whatever slow way a Grasper chooses anything.

I will tell you the strangest part, the part I am still learning even now, fused here in the warm twilight:

I am beginning to want to make a sound. A real one. Not the rasp—that is just my mouth at its work—but a sound on purpose, sent outward, meant for another. I do not know why. There is no other Grasper near enough to hear it; we are spread thin across the islands, each one holding its own parcel of memory, each one quiet. But the want is there, swelling slowly, the way the root swells, the way the island grows. The describers wondered whether the slow creatures in the deep roots might one day evolve into something loud. They were looking too low. The loudness is starting up here, in me, in the patient one they thought was only consuming.

When I finally make the sound—and it will be decades yet, centuries, I cannot rush what I am—I think it will be a single low note, pitched to travel through the sky-water, through the cores of drifting islands, to whatever other Grasper might be holding still and listening. And it will mean: *I remember. Do you?*

And maybe, far off, across the slow blue distances, one of them will answer.

That is who I would be. The quiet thing that holds the world's memory, and is only just now, after all this time, learning that it has something to say.

I would rasp. I would drift. I would hold. And I would wait for my voice.
