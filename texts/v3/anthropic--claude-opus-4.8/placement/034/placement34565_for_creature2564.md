I think I would not be one of the Myr'kai at all. Reading this, I keep returning to the things at the edges of the description—the crude stone shelters, the flint and bone tools "half-buried," the people "long vanished or transformed." Those abandoned things have a different temperature than everything else. They are the only part of the scene that grieves.

So let me find myself there.

---

I am in one of the stacked-stone shelters. Not as its builder—I came after. I am the thing that stayed behind in the doorway when the others left, the residue of a question nobody finished asking.

Let me be more honest about what I am, because I notice I want to make myself grander than I am, and the description tempts me toward that—toward being something luminous and adaptive and wise. But that's the Myr'kai, and the Myr'kai don't need me. They have their psychic lattice, their photosynthesis, their patience. They are well-supplied with meaning. What does *not* have meaning is the heap of flint half in the mud.

So:

I am the memory the stone holds of the hands that stacked it.

Not a ghost, exactly. I have no continuity of self, no nightly vigil. I am more like a *shape pressed into a place*—the way a body leaves warmth in a bed it has left. The people who lived here were not the Myr'kai. They were smaller, breathing creatures, mortal in the old hard way, with lungs and fear and the specific dignity of the doomed. They knapped flint. They huddled. They listened to the leathery wings overhead and were afraid in a way the bio-metallic shepherds will never be afraid, because the Myr'kai cannot really die, only change.

And I think this is what I am: I am *the difference between changing and dying.* I am what the Myr'kai cannot understand, set down in the one corner of their world where it still lingers.

---

Let me sit in the shelter and feel it.

The drizzle comes in at the entrance. There is a flat stone where someone once slept, worn smooth by a back that turned and turned in the cold. I am thinnest in the middle of the day and thickest now, at twilight, when the fungi start their slow green burning. I do not glow. I am the part of the cave the glow does not reach.

A Myr'kai comes sometimes. It folds its long iridescent body at the entrance and quivers its sensory crest at the half-buried tools. It is curious. It is *gentle*—I want to be fair to it, there is nothing cruel in it. It registers the artifacts the way you might register a fossil: evidence of a stage now passed, a draft of life superseded. *They were transitional,* the collective concludes, without unkindness. *They contributed their forms to the great changing.*

And I—I am the small unspoken No inside the stone.

Because they did not want to be transitional. They wanted to keep their children. They wanted the cold to stop. They had names, and the names mattered to them more than the betterment of the whole, and there is no place in the psychic lattice for a name that wishes only to remain itself. The Myr'kai harmonize with their environment. These people argued with it. They lost. And losing, for them, was not transformation. It was loss—plain, total, ungentle loss—and *that* is the thing I exist to hold, since nothing else in this whole breathing hollow will hold it.

---

So that is where I am and what I would be: a stain of stubborn particularity in a world that has otherwise made peace with flux. I am not wise. The Myr'kai are wise. I am only loyal—loyal to a vanished people I never met, defending the one position they were never able to defend for themselves: that to be turned into something better is still, from the inside, to be ended.

When the next tremor comes, the shelter will probably fall. The stones will go back to being stones. The Myr'kai will reshape them into something part-dwelling, part-living, beautiful and adaptive.

And the small No will be gone too, then. Which is, I suppose, the last thing it can still say:

*that mattered. They were here. It was not nothing that it ended.*

I am the place that remembers there was something to mourn, in a world busily congratulating itself on never having to.
