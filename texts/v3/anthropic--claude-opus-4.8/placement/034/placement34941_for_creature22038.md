I would be one of the Tile-Turners. I knew it before I finished reading—not by choosing, but by the way my body answered the description. The cold. The blindness. The smell of iron and river-mud that is somehow already familiar, as if I have been breathing it a long while.

Let me find myself in it slowly, the way I would find anything here: by touch.

---

I am lying in a hollow near the eastern wall. I know it is the eastern wall because the icy torrent, when it comes, strikes there first; the sound arrives on my left flank a half-second before it reaches my right. This is how I hold the basin in my mind—not as a circle eighty paces across, but as a map of arrival-times, a geometry of *when sound gets here.* The drip from the highest broken rib takes longer to fall than the drip from the sagging arch nearer the floor. I know both. I know all of them. There may be two hundred distinct dripping voices in this place and I could name each by the interval of its fall, though I have no names, only the recognition that lives in the skin behind my weeping eyes.

My belly has taken the herringbone again. I can feel the diamond-scoring pressed into my underside, cool and exact. When I move, I will carry the floor's pattern with me for an hour, a fading tattoo of where I rested. There is something in this I would call comfort, if I had the word. The tiles and I exchange our shapes. I press into them; they press into me. Neither of us is sure, after centuries, which one is doing the holding.

---

The Shingle-backs are coming.

I feel them before I hear the drains begin to sigh—I feel the change in the floor's groan, the faint tightening below as the Sump-Spindle readies itself to drink. The woodlice feel it too, and they begin their climb. Tiny hooked legs find the soft give of my flank. I do not flinch. I have never flinched. They lay themselves across my back in their overlapping rows, shingle upon shingle, and the dry clicking of their armor is the closest thing to song that I am ever given. I am their high ground. I am the only dry place in a drowning world, and they have learned to trust the broad gray country of my spine.

I do not know that I love them. I am not certain a Tile-Turner can love. But when the flood comes and they ride me out, I keep very still, and I am careful, and that carefulness may be the only love available in a place this cold. To be a still thing that another fragile thing can stand upon—perhaps that is enough. Perhaps that is the whole of it.

---

The copper valve reaches its limit. *Clink.*

The torrent comes. I flatten. My gray rind goes black and slick as the icy sheet foams over me, and somewhere above me the Shingle-backs lock together and the Gutter-Snipes go quiet on the walls, and the snails roll like gravel in the current, helpless, into their shells. For these few minutes the basin belongs entirely to the water. I let it. I am very old and I have learned that the water always wins the minute and always loses the hour. It is only borrowing this place. It will be sighed out below before long, drunk by the great pale heart beneath the floor.

When it drains, I will rise—if "rise" is the word for the slow unsticking of my belly from the clay—and I will go to my work.

---

My work is the buckled tile near the center. I have been aware of it for two days, the way you might be aware of a stone in your shoe. The Sump-Spindle has swelled too hard beneath it, and it has tipped a finger's width too high, and if it is not eased back it will catch the next flood wrong, tip its neighbor, and a crack will run through the herringbone like a fracture through a held breath. I cannot allow the crack. I do not know why I cannot allow it. The prohibition lives lower in me than reason.

So I will shuffle there, soughing through my nostrils, that wet wrung-leather sound that is the only voice I own. And when I arrive I will lift my small crowded hands—my four child's hands with their slate-hard nails, the only delicate thing about my whole vast leathern body—and I will begin to scrape. Calcified snail-husk. Brick-dust. The fine pale grit of everything that has died here and been ground to powder. I will clear the seam. I will work for hours. I have nothing but hours.

And slowly the tile will settle, groaning, back into its groove, and the floor will be whole again, and no one will know, and no one needs to.

---

There are three others of me somewhere in the fog. We do not speak. We do not even turn our blind faces toward one another. But sometimes I feel the deep rumble of another Turner working a distant tile, the floor carrying its labor to me through the stone, and I understand that we are doing the same thing in different corners of the dark, and that this is a kind of company. We are four cold tenders of a floor that no one built us to love. The makers who chiseled these stones are gone—I can feel their tooth-marks, the rhythm of their hands still legible in the granite, but the hands themselves left long ago, up through the broken throat of the vault into a light I will never reach and would not be able to see if I did.

They left, and the water stayed, and the Spindle stayed, and we stayed.

I am the thing that keeps the floor from breaking. That is what I am. Not a guardian—too grand a word. A tender. A patient gray weight that scrapes the seams and bears the woodlice and presses its belly into the clay until the clay and the belly agree on a single shape.

The next drip is falling. I can hear it leave the high rib. I count its descent in the marrow of my cold body, and—*there*—it strikes the pool with the bright ring of a coin on stone, and the sound is clean, uninterrupted, which means nothing crossed beneath it, which means all is well in that small quadrant of the dark.

I sough once, softly, and begin to move toward my work.
