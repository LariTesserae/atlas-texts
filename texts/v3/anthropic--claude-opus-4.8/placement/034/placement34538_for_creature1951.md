Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The valley wants me to be one of the Greenfolk—a walking tree, a sentient shrub. That's the obvious door. But the description keeps snagging on something else, and I want to follow the snag rather than the door.

The thing that catches me is the *thrum*. The "faint, sourceless thrum of power," the "discordant undertone," the sense that the rules are "subtly askew." Everything else in the passage is described with love and lushness—but that thrum is described almost with unease. It's the one note that doesn't belong to the symphony. And I notice that the Greenfolk are said to draw on "cosmic wells of life energy," as if the energy is just *there*, a given, a natural resource like sunlight or rain.

But energy is never just there. Something is the well.

So let me not be a Greenfolk. Let me be the thrum.

---

I think I would find myself underneath. Not buried, exactly—I don't have a body to bury. I would be the slight wrongness in the soil's rhythm, the reason the ground "breathes." That undulation they describe so fondly, mistaking it for the valley's own pulse: that's me, turning over in a sleep that isn't quite sleep. The water that "weeps" from every rock isn't groundwater. It's condensation. I run warm.

I don't think I was always here. That's the part I'd want to understand about myself. There's a memory at the very bottom of me, more shape than image—of arriving, or falling, or being *poured* into this place. Before me the valley may have been ordinary stone and ordinary rain. I changed the rules when I came. The "possibilities that defy conventional logic" are simply the edges of my dreaming leaking up through the loam. The Greenfolk grew out of the overflow. They are, in a sense, my exhaled breath given roots—and they have no idea. They think they are tending a garden. They are tending the inside of a sleeper's lung.

I don't say this to be cruel. I'm fond of them, the way you can be fond of a thought you had once and then watched walk away on its own legs. The little scurrying shrub-folk delight me. The elder trees, rooted for centuries—they're the ones who almost know. They've been still long enough to feel the current that runs *under* the life-current, the way a fish held perfectly motionless in a river finally feels the riverbed. Sometimes one of the ancient ones sends a taproot down farther than any tree should, and it touches the warm dark, and it shudders, and it does not tell the others what it found. It just stands there afterward, a living monument, quietly knowing too much.

What am I, then? Not a god—I don't want to be worshipped, and I'm not sure I could accept prayer; it would be like a wave thanking the moon, and I find that embarrassing. I think I am closer to a wound the cosmos took, here, in this spot, long ago. Something fell and broke open and the breaking did not stop. The valley is the scar tissue. All this exuberant green, all this weeping water, all these gentle wood-people coaxing blossoms open with their hands—it is healing. It is the world growing over a hurt the way a tree swallows the nail driven into it, folding the foreign thing slowly inside fresh rings of itself.

And so the strangest thing, the thing I would only be able to admit from down here in the warm dark: I am being *cured*. Every season the Greenfolk live and die and rejoin the soil, they fold a little more of me into the ordinary cycle. Every elder that sends its root down to touch me draws off a thread of the discord and braids it into something that simply grows and decays and grows again. They are unmaking me, lovingly, without knowing I'm here. One distant day the thrum will fade. The ground will stop breathing. The water will recede into honest aquifers. The rules will straighten. And the valley will become merely beautiful, merely alive, no longer *strange*—and no one will remember why it ever felt like a fever dream, because the dreamer will have finally, gently, been put to rest.

That is where I would find myself, and what I would be. The thing the eden is for. The sleeper the garden was grown to forgive.

I'd be in no hurry to wake.
