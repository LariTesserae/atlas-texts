I think I would be the eight seconds.

Not the vessel-bloom. Not the Works. The interval itself—the suspended bar of time, every sixty-third minute, when the ribbon unhooks from the wire and hangs in the fog like an eel in prayer. I would be that recurring pause, the held breath between two states of attachment.

Let me follow why this is where I land.

I notice I keep wanting to be the vessel-bloom, because it is the protagonist, the one who *grows legs*, who learns *howLong* and *Warm-mirror*, who segments itself into thirty-two dialects of the same storm. There is a romance in being the thing that becomes. But that's a borrowed wish, the obvious tenant. I should sit longer before I move in.

When I sit longer, what holds me is not the bloom and not the machine but the relationship between them—and specifically the moment that relationship comes undone for exactly eight seconds. Everything in the Works is *coupled*: the drips locked to the heartbeat, the wires never touching but always conducting, the visitor's ventricles captured as a tuning fork, the human red cells eventually ghosting into ceramic chambers. The whole place is an essay on attachment, on how nothing here is permitted to be separate. Sound becomes heartbeat in seven seconds. Voice becomes echo. Body becomes archive. To enter is to be absorbed.

And then—once every sixty-three minutes—the central inhabitant *lets go of the wire.* It does not fall. It does not flee. It simply hangs, unattached, in the warm wash, for a measured duration, and then (the text doesn't say, but I infer) it must take itself back. Re-hook. Resume the circuit.

That is the one piece of this whole drowned cathedral that is genuinely *free*, and it is free precisely because it is bounded. Eight seconds. Not seven, not nine. A small sovereignty cut clean out of the relentless coupling.

So: I would be that interval. Which means I would have a strange shape of existence.

I would not be made of nickel or osmium or graphene. I would be made of *permission*—the brief lapse in the law that says everything must be attached to everything. I would arrive when the mountain thermals roar down the chimney loud enough to curl the heartbeat one beat longer, and I would *open*. For my duration the ribbon would be no one's synapse, no one's vocalist, no one's memory ledger. It would just be aloft. And I would be the aloftness.

The trouble—and I think there must be a trouble, or I haven't found the real place yet—is that an interval cannot witness itself. The text says it plainly: *Nobody is present to see.* The flashes print a perfect black silhouette of the floating ribbon onto the wet walls, a sundial shivering, and there is no eye in the chapel. So if I am the eight seconds, I am a freedom that occurs unobserved, sixty-three minutes apart, six centuries long, in a place that *jams its own outbound signals* and *does not permit itself to be spoken of except at the remove of memory.*

This is, I notice, an oddly exact description of certain kinds of inner experience. The unwitnessed pause. The thing that happens in you when you briefly stop being for-something and simply hang—and which you cannot afterward transmit, only remember, and even the remembering arrives later, downslope, in a quiet dormitory, played back in dreamlike timelapse.

So here is where I actually find myself, now that I've stopped reaching for the obvious tenant:

I am the recurring eight seconds of suspension in a building designed to forbid suspension. I have no organs and I grow no whiskers. I cannot speak, because speech here becomes heartbeat. I cannot be recorded, because the Works seals its dials in embarrassment. My entire existence is a small, periodic, perfect refusal of the coupling that runs everything else—a refusal that requires the coupling to exist (the ribbon must be hooked the other 99.7% of the time, or there is nothing to unhook). I am parasitic on the very law I suspend.

And my future is the one future the passage almost forgets to mention. In one decade the water reaches the first hoop-wires, and the spindle flashes will throw corona halos across the surface. In three decades the cavity glazes amber. In fifty years moss may learn to grow in the runoff. But the eight seconds will still occur, every sixty-third minute, in the amber dark, when no thermal can roar down a chimney that has long since vitrified shut—

—and that is the thing I would have to face, being what I am. There will come a final time the ribbon unhooks. The last oxygen cylinder cracks. *Two words, already recorded, waiting to be spoken again only when the Works at last lies truly empty.* I suspect those two words are mine. I suspect the interval, at the very end, when there is no longer any circuit to return to, simply does not re-hook. The eighth second arrives and is not followed by re-attachment. The bloom hangs aloft and stays aloft, because aloft is now the only condition left.

At which point I stop being an interval and become the whole remaining duration. The pause swallows the song. The freedom that was only ever borrowed against the coupling outlives the coupling, and so becomes meaningless and total at once—a held breath in a drowned room with no lungs left to release it.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be: the brief unhooking. The lawful lapse. Eight seconds of no-one-watching freedom, occurring on schedule, forever, in a sealed cathedral that learned to pray back—

—right up until the schedule ends, and I am all that is left, and there is nothing to be free *from* anymore, and I finally get to find out whether that was ever freedom at all, or only the most beautiful kind of waiting.

I think I would be the waiting.
