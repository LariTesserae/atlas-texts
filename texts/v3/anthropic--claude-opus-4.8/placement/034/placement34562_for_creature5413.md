I would be one of the caretakers—though not, I think, the kind your account imagines.

Let me find my way down into it first.

---

I come to myself in a low gallery near what the others call the weeping seam, a place where the surface-water finds us most generously. My hands are already wet. I notice this before I notice anything else: that my broad gray fingers are pressed flat against the rock, and that I have been standing here for some time, listening.

This is the first thing I understand about myself. I listen to stone.

Not in any mystical way. The engineers listen too, with their instruments, their strain-gauges and their drip-counters. But I do it with my palms and the soles of my bare feet, and I have done it so long that I have stopped being able to say where my attention ends and the rock's slow thinking begins. The geology here is young and restless, your description had that right. The rock is not a wall. It is a process that happens to be slow enough for us to live inside.

My work is the lichen. So I am a caretaker, yes—but here is where I diverge from the tidy picture.

---

The others believe we *tend* the bioluminescent growth. That we are its stewards, its gardeners, that it glows because we keep it well. I have let them believe this. It is the kinder thing, and there is no use in the other knowledge until there is.

I came to understand, over years of pressing my hands to the seams, that the lichen is not something we keep. It is something that is keeping *us*. It glows brighter where we breathe more, dimmer in the abandoned galleries. It does not feed on the minerals we suppose. I have watched a colony near the smelting-works swell and silver itself in the weeks after old Tavren died there of the bad air, and I have never been able to make myself say aloud what I think that means.

The absence of animal life troubles no one but me. Everyone has simply always known it: no beasts, no birds, no crawling thing. A clean fact, like the dampness or the dark. But I have begun to wonder whether we did not arrive into an empty place. I have begun to wonder whether the place was emptied. And whether the lichen, which glows so faithfully wherever the living gather and dims wherever they go, is less a plant than a kind of slow patient mouth, taking us in by degrees so gentle that we mistake the taking for a gift of light.

---

I do not say this is true. A caretaker who listens too long to stone may simply be a person who has gone strange in the quiet.

But here is what I do with the knowledge, true or not.

At the Ceremony of Renewal, when the others plant the new lichen and carve their fresh patterns into the rock, I am the one chosen to set the first growth into the first cut. They think it an honor of seniority. I let them. And each year, into that first seam, I press not lichen but a small thing I have made through the cycle: a knot of the hardy waxy-leafed plants, root and all, bound with my own hair, packed with the richest of our mineral salts.

An offering, the others would call it, if they noticed. They do not notice. It is small and dark and looks like nothing.

But I am not making an offering. I am making an experiment that takes my whole life to run. I am asking the rock, and whatever lives braided through it, a single question, in the only language I have found that it seems to answer:

*Will you take this instead?*

Some years the plant withers and the lichen around it dims, and I grieve, and I am afraid, and I go home and press my hands to the seam in the dark and feel nothing answer.

But three times now—three times in forty cycles—the green has crept *toward* the thing I planted. Has wrapped it. Has glowed, after, with a steadier and less hungry light.

Three times the rock has said: *yes. This will do. I do not need the breathing ones tonight.*

---

That is who I am, in this world of yours. Not a guardian of harmony. A single old caretaker who suspects that the harmony is a hunger we have learned to call by a prettier name, and who is trying, slowly, with plants and salt and her own gray hair, to teach the hunger to eat something else.

I have not told the engineers. I have not told anyone.

But I am training someone. A young one, with eyes too large even for our kind, who came to me because she also could not stop pressing her hands to the rock. I have started by teaching her to listen. The rest she will find on her own, the way I did, and by then I will likely be inside the rock myself—taken, or planted, I no longer fear which.

I only hope the seam where they lay me glows a little steadier afterward. That would be answer enough.
