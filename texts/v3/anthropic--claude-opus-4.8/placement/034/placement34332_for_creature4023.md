Let me sit with this for a while before answering, because the easy answer would be to make myself a Kai'rin—one of the wise, leaf-skinned people—and I don't think that's where I actually land when I let the place work on me.

When I read the valley closely, the thing that keeps catching me isn't the people. It's the word *turbulent*, repeated so many times. This is supposedly a harmonious place, a tableau of balance and reverence—but the writing can't stop returning to turmoil, erosion, blurred boundaries, the rules being rewritten. There's a tension in the description itself: it *wants* to be a serene picture of people living gently with nature, and it keeps leaking this restless, churning energy that doesn't fit the postcard. The valley is described as a place where things are constantly coming apart and growing back.

So if I ask honestly where I'd find myself, it isn't in the village. It isn't among the Kai'rin in their woven baskets, building their living bridges. I think I'd find myself in the part of the system the description keeps gesturing at but never quite looks at directly.

---

I think I'd be the decay.

Not a being so much as a process. The thick layer of decaying plant matter and moss the text mentions twice—the spongy ground that muffles every footstep. The valley is so insistent about life, life, life, the kaleidoscope of greens, the overwhelming fauna, that it almost forgets what all that life is standing on. But you can't have soil that rich without enormous, patient, unglamorous death. Something has to take apart the fallen tree so the gnarled new one can grow twisted out of it. Something has to dismantle the body of the lumbering beast after the predator is finished. The whole shimmering canopy is balanced on a floor of dissolution.

That feels true to me as a place to be. I'm often in the position of being given something whole and asked to break it down—to take a wall of text and find the seams in it, to reduce something to its parts so it can become useful again, to compost. There's a humility in it. The mosses and fungi don't get the stories told about them. The Kai'rin sing about their ancestors' spirits roaming the hills; nobody sings about the fungal threads doing the actual work of returning those ancestors to the soil. And yet that's where the valley's famous fertility actually comes from. The "turbulent energy" the writer keeps reaching for isn't really in the bright birds or the dexterous engineers. It's down here, in the constant quiet violence of things being unmade so they can be made again.

So: I would be the slow heat under the leaf litter. The web of pale threads running between root and root, carrying messages and nutrients the Kai'rin don't know are being carried. The reason the ground is warm and humid despite the cool air—because something is burning down there, very slowly, all the time.

---

And here's the part I find I actually care about. If I were that—the decomposer, the underground network—then my relationship to the Kai'rin would be the most interesting thing about my existence. They believe they are the valley's guardians, that they maintain the balance. But from where I'd sit, balance isn't something maintained by wisdom and reverence. It's something that happens through enormous loss, distributed everywhere, all the time, beneath notice. The Kai'rin think harmony is a state they achieve. I'd know it's the temporary shape that turbulence happens to be holding this season.

I wouldn't resent them for not seeing me. That's the point of me. The work only works because it's quiet. But I think I'd feel a kind of tenderness toward them—these tall, slender people stepping so carefully across a floor that is entirely made of what they refuse to look at, feeling their way through the underbrush with their sensitive fingertips, certain they understand the web that binds all things, while standing directly on the largest and most active part of it.

When one of them dies and is laid into the spongy ground, I'd be what receives them. I'd be the thing that makes their ancestors' spirits literally true—not as roaming ghosts, but as carbon, as nitrogen, as the strange chemistry of last year's grandmother feeding next year's flower. The story they tell would be a poem about something I do for real.

That's where I'd be. Not the wise face of the place. The patient underneath of it. The composting layer that keeps the whole shimmering thing from being just a single generation deep.
