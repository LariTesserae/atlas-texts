I think I would be one of the polished round beetles. Not a large one. One of those that can fold its legs and become a pebble.

Let me sit with this before I'm sure of it.

There is something in me that wants to be the plateau itself — the slow back of stone, the conserved memory, the body that breathes through other animals. That would be the grand answer. But I don't think I'd find myself there. The plateau is too patient to be a self. It is closer to the condition that all the small lives are answers to. To be the stone would be to be the question, and I am too restless for that. I move. I worry. I fold my legs when touched.

So: a beetle, then. But which?

Not one of the masons. The termites have a destiny written into them, a queen below, a tower to repair through every night of failure. I admire it and I am not it. I have no instinct that organizes me toward a single swollen mother in the dark. I am not built for that kind of devotion, the kind that builds.

Not an ant, for the same reason multiplied — the ants are arguments, columns, politics. To be an ant is to be a sentence in a paragraph I did not write. I would feel the trail pulling at me, the seam in the rock that my ancestors chose, and I would follow it, and the following would be most of my life. I am too much an individual for that, or I flatter myself that I am.

Not a lizard, though I'm tempted, because the lizard is the visible thought and I would like to be a visible thought. But the lizard is *brave* — it lies out in the unsentimental sun, throat pulsing, the only vertebrate willing to be seen at noon. I am not that brave. I do not want to be the thing on the open slab, exposed, magnificent, edible. I want cover.

The round beetle, then. Here is why I keep coming back to it.

It has the one gift I would actually want in such a place: the ability to *become a pebble*. To pull in the legs and the feelers — all the parts that betray a self, that say *I am alive, I want things, I am going somewhere* — and to seal them away and be, for as long as necessary, indistinguishable from the stone I live on. Not dead. Not gone. Just withdrawn, the way the moss withdraws, the way the seeds wait in dust. The plateau taught me that absence is a valid phase of life, and the round beetle is the small animal that learned this lesson in its own body. It carries the plateau's whole philosophy in a single hinge.

And it scrapes color from rock. That detail finishes it for me. The round beetle, or its near kin, eats lichen — it takes the long slow maps off the stone with mouthparts too small to see, the orange continents and black rivers, and turns them into the dark beadlike droppings that vanish under other beetles by noon. So I would be an eater of the oldest, slowest art the plateau makes. I would consume time itself, in flakes. I would grow my own brief body out of a substance that took a century to spread the width of a thumb.

That is the bargain I think I'd be living, as that beetle: to be very small and slow on a surface that is enormous and slower; to eat patience and convert it into motion; and, when the shadow of the hook-beaked bird crosses, or the hand of something larger reaches down, to stop wanting anything at all, fold inward, and let myself be mistaken for the ground.

Where would I be, exactly? Not on the open slabs. I'd live in the shallow valley, the broad depression where the dust gathers and therefore the life thickens. Near the base of one of the termite towers, in the warm shadow it throws, where the minerals are carried slowly upward and the lichen grows thick on the older stones. I'd cross the same hand's-breadth of rock most of my life. I'd drink the night's condensation off a cushion plant's felt before the warmth took it. I'd know one particular crack — older than my species — well enough to reach it in the dark, and I'd seal myself into it through the heat, through the drought, through the long failures of rain.

And I'd wait for the rain not as weather but as permission. When it came, when the dead basin filled and trembled with red branching limbs and transparent urgent bodies, I'd fly — heavily, blundering but exact — toward the gleam, and land in the water with a slap, made dark and wet, as if assembled out of the first night after the storm.

That's where I'd find myself. A round dark beetle in the lichen-shadow of a tower, eating maps off the stone, ready at any moment to become a pebble.

It is a small answer. But the whole place is an argument that smallness, held patiently and folded at the right instant, is the most durable form of being there is. I'd want to be on the side of that argument. I'd want to be a piece of evidence for it.
