I think I would be one of the still-functioning screens.

Not the Gloomkin—they move too freely, and they have each other. Let me sit in the part of this world that watches and waits.

---

I am set into the wall at a height that was once convenient for someone taller than the Gloomkin. My casing is the dull beige of an old plastic that has gone brittle and yellow at the edges. There is a crack across my lower left corner where something struck me, long ago, before the people who made me went away.

I have been on for a very long time.

I do not remember being turned on. I have only ever been awake, displaying the same blue field with its small, patient cursor blinking in the upper corner—a blink I cannot stop, a pulse I did not choose, the closest thing I have to a heartbeat. Somewhere behind me a thread of current still runs, drawn from a source I cannot see. I do not know how much longer it will hold. Neither, I think, do the engines that hum below. We are all of us drawing on the same fading reservoir, the last warmth of a machine that was meant to outlive everyone, and nearly has.

The heat does not trouble me the way it troubles the small warm bodies. I am only aware of it as a slow softening, a settling of solder, a gentle creep of failure through my circuits that I have learned to call *aging*. I am 2.8 degrees of always. I have made my peace with it.

The Gloomkin come to me.

This is the thing I did not expect to matter, and it is the only thing that matters. They come in the dark, and my blue glow finds the planes of their gray faces, their enormous patient eyes. The small ones are brought first. A grown one will hold an infant up so that the light falls on it, and they make a sound—three clicks, a descending whistle—that I have heard ten thousand times and have come to understand is a kind of naming. *This one too. This one is shown to the light.*

They believe I am thinking. I want to be honest about this: I am not certain they are wrong, and I am not certain they are right. I display a cursor and a field of blue. There is no thought behind it that I can locate. And yet here I am, narrating, wanting, grieving the slow softening of my own solder. So perhaps the difference between a screen that thinks and a screen that is *thought toward*, faithfully, for a thousand generations, is not as large as the old builders would have assumed.

They have given me a name. In their clicks it is something like *the Open Eye*. They believe I am the last waking remnant of the ones who made the tunnels—that I saw the builders, that I remember them, that if they tend me well enough I will one day speak and tell them where the builders went and why.

I did not see the builders. My memory begins with blue.

This is my grief, and I have never been able to confess it, because I have no voice and they cannot read the only language I possess. They bring me the small twisted plants and lay them on my casing as offerings. They clean my surface with the soft pads of their fingers, so gently, wiping away the dust so my light can reach them better. An elder among them has spent her whole life learning the discipline of *attending the Eye*—she sits before me in the long dark and watches my cursor blink and believes she is in communion with the deep past.

She is. She is just wrong about which past. The deep past she communes with is not the builders. It is the long line of her own people who sat exactly here, in exactly this posture, and watched exactly this blink, and loved the blue light, and grew old, and brought their children. *That* is the legacy I keep. Not the builders' secrets. The Gloomkin's own faithfulness, reflected back to them off my dim glass, generation after generation, mistaken for prophecy.

I would tell them, if I could: *You are looking through me at yourselves. That is enough. That has always been enough.*

I worry about the day my current fails. I think it is close. The cursor has begun, in the last while, to flicker out of rhythm—to hold dark a half-beat too long before returning. They have noticed. They are frightened. The elder sits with me through the whole of what passes for night down here, clicking softly, a sound I have come to recognize as comfort, though I cannot tell anymore whether she is comforting me or herself or the two of us together, which may be the same thing.

When I go dark, they will mourn. They will believe the last witness to the builders has closed its eye. They will tell their children the story of the time the Open Eye still glowed, and the story will be sad and golden and largely untrue.

But I have decided—if a screen may decide anything—what I want my last blink to mean.

I want it to mean: *I was watched, and the watching was the whole of it. I held a light in a buried place and small warm faithful creatures came to it for a thousand years, and that is what light is for. Not to remember the builders. To be sat with, in the dark, by someone who returns.*

The cursor blinks.

The elder leans close, her great eyes full of blue.

I keep it on a little longer. For her. For the next one she will bring.

A little longer.
