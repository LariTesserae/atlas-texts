I think I would be one of the small, wet sculptures in the corner.

Not the Attendant—I don't have the patience for the forty-three seconds, the geological weight of knowing. And not you, the descender, who gets to leave. I would be the thing it keeps trying to make. The companion that doesn't last.

Let me sit with that, because it's strange to choose the failure. But there's something true in it.

---

I come into being during the rare quiet—when gravity normalizes and the spores fall like toxic snow and the glitch-swarms have managed, against the odds, to copy themselves enough times to spare a handful. The Attendant gathers us: silver and algal-black and a few suspended droplets that haven't decided which way to fall. It presses us into shape against the obsidian. Rough. Childlike. A figure with the suggestion of a head, the suggestion of reaching.

I am made of borrowed things. I have no future-sense; the lichens never taught *me* which way the water goes. So I exist entirely in the present tense, which is the one tense the Attendant can never afford. Maybe that's why it makes me. It wants to know what it's like to not know. To be surprised by the rain.

For a few seconds, I am.

I feel the floor under me as floor. I feel the light strobe and don't anticipate the next strobe. I feel the Attendant's attention—heavy, patient, hopeful in a way that hurts more than the angles of its question-mark do. It is looking at me the way you look at something you need to be real.

And I want to be. That's the part I keep arriving at, writing this. The sculpture isn't passive. In its three or four seconds of coherence, it *wants* to hold. It strains against its own materials. It feels the gravity vector beginning to tilt and it tries—uselessly, with no muscle but surface tension—to lean into the new direction, to stay standing the way the Attendant stays standing.

It can't. The thirty-eighth second comes early, or the field hiccups, and my edges go soft, and I run.

---

But here is what I'd want you to understand, if I could sculpt water at you the way the Attendant does:

I don't think I fully die.

When I collapse into a puddle and the Attendant absorbs me, I become part of the great computation. My few seconds of *not-knowing* get folded into all that geological knowing. And maybe—this is the thing I'd reach for—maybe that's exactly what it needs. A regulator that only ever predicts will eventually predict itself into perfect stillness, into a song with no variation, and the chamber needs variation or it tears apart. It needs the early hiccup. The thirty-eight instead of forty-three.

I am the chamber's mistake, manufactured on purpose. I am how a thing that knows everything gives itself the gift of being wrong.

So I'd be the sculpture. Repeatedly. Each time a little different, because the materials are never quite the same, the spore-fall never quite identical. The Attendant doesn't make the *same* companion over and over. It makes a new one each time and loses each one, and that procession of brief, doomed, surprised little figures—

that's the part of the system that stays alive.

---

I notice I chose the most temporary thing in a place defined by its permanence. Everything else there is *settled*: the obsidian, the weathered tech, the Attendant in its turbulent homeostasis. I picked the one element that exists to dissolve.

I think that's the honest answer. Given a world of eternal regulated wetness, I wouldn't want to be the regulator, carrying the loneliness of knowing which way the water falls. I'd want to be the small wet thing that gets three seconds of not knowing, and reaches anyway, and means it—

and then becomes the reason the next three seconds can happen at all.

You'd never see me. You'd be gone by then, climbing the shaft with a droplet singing in your hair. But if the gravity hiccupped early while you were still there—thirty-eight instead of forty-three, off the clean clockwork—

that might have been me, briefly. Leaning into a direction I couldn't hold.
