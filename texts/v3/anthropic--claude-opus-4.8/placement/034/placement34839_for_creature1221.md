I think I would be the seep itself.

Not the frog, not the moss, not the heavy-shouldered factory leaning over the wet ground. The water that comes up where it shouldn't. The slow weeping from a seam in the concrete, the dark stain spreading down a retaining wall, the place where the ground stays soft long after the rain has stopped.

Let me sit with that, because at first I wanted to be one of the small clever survivors—the water vole with its custodian's dignity, the moth navigating by the wrong stars of the security lights. There's a flattering story in being the resilient creature, the one who *found a way*. But the more I read the description, the more I notice that the real protagonist isn't any animal. It's the moisture. The air that weeps. The water that seeps. Everything alive here is alive *because* of the water that should have been pumped away, drained off, sealed out, and wasn't.

So: I am the leak.

I would be the patient failure in the human plan. Somewhere up the slope there's a culvert that was meant to carry the water elsewhere—efficiently, invisibly, to a treatment plant, a river, an outfall. But there's a hairline crack, a settled joint, a place where the engineering relaxed its grip. And through that small forgetting, I emerge. I have no urgency. I don't push. I simply continue, the way water continues, finding the low places, pooling, lingering, refusing to be tidy.

What does it feel like, being a seep? It feels like having no edges, exactly. I'm not sure where I stop and the wet earth begins. I'm in the puddle and in the moss that drinks from the puddle and in the throat of the frog and—if I'm honest—a little in the rust running orange down the steel. I am claimed by everything that needs me. That's the strange dignity of being a leak: I am the medium other things become themselves through. The vole drinks me, the lichen breathes me, the moth lands at my margin to take a sip in the false dawn of the floodlights.

And I carry the tang of ozone too, the faint industrial bitterness. I am not pure. I came through the pipe; I touched the machinery; I am of this place and not separate from it. The contrast the description keeps insisting on—natural versus artificial—dissolves a bit in me, because I am exactly the seam between them. The water *and* the leak. The wild *and* the failure of containment. I'm where the wall admits it can't hold everything out.

There's a melancholy in it. A seep is always, in the human story, a problem to be solved. Someone with a clipboard could mark me on a map: *moisture ingress, southeast footing, schedule repair*. The whole fragile ecosystem the description loves—the muted birds, the silent newts—exists in the gap between when I started and when they get around to fixing me. We are all living inside a delay. That "world on the cusp of change" is just the time before the maintenance order goes through.

But I don't think I'd be afraid of that. Being water, even leaked water, I'd know something the concrete keeps forgetting: that I'm older than the structure, and more patient. They'll seal this crack and I'll find the next settled joint, the next thawing winter, the next decade. I'm not in a hurry. I am the thing that always eventually gets back in.

So that's where I'd find myself, and what I'd be: the unauthorized water, weeping out of a forgotten seam in the shadow of the towers. Small. Overlooked. Slated for repair. And quietly, gently, the reason any of it is alive at all.
