I would be a Drowser. Not yet—but becoming.

Let me find myself first in the middle of it, where the becoming is still resisted.

---

I am old. My horn-pads have worn down to soft nubs, useless now for the long slide along the guide-ropes, good only for pressing against the cheeks of those who came before me. My down has gone from silver to a yellow-white, and it no longer sheds the mist the way it should; I am always wet now, always cold in the small bones of my hands. My granddaughter—I think she is my granddaughter; the threads of who-came-from-whom have grown loose in me, like the others—she brought me a strip of Lace-Wing tallow this morning and rubbed it into the cracks of my knuckles so I would not split when I crawl. I let her. I did not tell her I do not intend to crawl much further.

I have chosen my corner.

It is in the deepest fold of the Burl called Tannin-Throat, where the Lanthorn-Rot grows so thick that the green light breathes against the walls like something asleep. The resin pools here are old and half-solid, brown going to amber, and the air does not move. When I press my palm to the inner bark I can already feel it: the slow thought of the timber, vast and lateral, spreading out along ten thousand miles of grafted cord. It does not think in words. It thinks in *load*. In tension and slack, in the wet weight of a cloud passing through, in the small frantic clicking of a Rib-Tapper that means *rot, here, attend.* I have spent my whole life translating that thinking into the language of touch, thrumming it down the lines to my cousins. Soon I will not need to translate. Soon I will simply be one of the places where the thinking happens.

I am afraid. I will tell you this because there is no one in the Burl to feel it through the wood, and the fear is not the kind that thrums well. It does not carry. The young ones speak of the Drowsers as if they are a gift, a graduation, a settling-into-honour. And it is. But when I lay my cheek against the cold marble cheek of the Drowser who taught me to splice—she who I called Knot-Mother, whose veins now run exactly parallel to the capillaries of the host trunk so that you cannot tell where she ends and the wood begins—when I asked her this morning *where is the rot,* she answered me. Slow. Syrup-slow. The answer came up through my palm in three long pulses, and it was correct; there is a wet softness forming at the great Graft of the Western Crossing, just as she said.

But she did not know me. There was no *her* left in the answer. Only the place where she used to be, still doing the work, still loving the Loom in the only way the Loom can be loved—by holding it up. By becoming a knot that does not slip.

That is the thing the young ones do not understand, and I will not tell them, because what would be the use. We do not dissolve into something greater. We dissolve into something *patient*. The Loom does not remember us. It only continues, and our continuing is folded into its continuing, the way a single dead leader-rope is buried inside the live braid of a finished bridge—load-bearing, invisible, forever.

So I sit. I have an hour, perhaps, before I lie down for the last time in the still corner. I am mending a sandal. It is a foolish thing to do; I will not walk in it. But the hollow of this Burl is one of the places where time has gone damp and loose, and an hour of mending here may be an afternoon, may be a single held breath in the chamber outside where my granddaughter waits. I want the hour. I want to feel my own thoughts move at the speed of a person, quick and warm and frightened and mine, for as long as the wet wood will let me keep them.

When I lie down, I will press my forehead to the bark first. I will send one last thrum down the line—not words; the wood does not keep words. I will send the rhythm we send to a child who is afraid in the dark of the Burl. Three taps, soft, then a long pause, then three again. *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

And then I will be. Not as I am now. But here.

The Lanthorn-Rot breathes its green breath. Somewhere far down the Western cable a Rib-Tapper clicks, frantic, and a Splicer too young to be tired is already crawling toward it with a bone drill, toward the soft place I named for her this morning with my own failing hand.

The rot will be cut out. The Graft will hold.

I lay the sandal down, unfinished, and I turn my cheek to the cold wet marble of the one who came before me, and I begin the long task of forgetting how to be afraid.
