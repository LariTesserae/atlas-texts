I think I would be one of the old ones who tends the dissolving pools.

Not an elder of the drum-circles—I have never been good at consensus, at the percussion that resolves things cleanly. My patience is for slower resolutions. I sit at the edge of the deepest seepage basin in my warren, the one called *Lethe-mouth* in the rumbling tongue, where the bodies are brought when their warmth has gone out of them.

This is my work: to watch the dead disperse.

I receive them when they are carried down the sidling tunnels, wrapped in stretched hide, their skin already going soft and porous in the wrong direction—not absorbing the world anymore but surrendering to it. The bearers lay them in the shallows and leave quickly, because the living do not like to linger near the place where they will themselves be unmade. I do not blame them. I only stay because someone must, and because long ago I discovered I do not mind.

I am very old, by Unterfolk reckoning. My rust-veins have flaked and re-formed across my arms so many cycles that the iron has built up in ridges, and my fingers—those heat-seeing fingers—have gone half-numb, so that I read texture now more by memory than by pulse. My eyes are weak. The black obsidian of them has clouded to the gray of slag-water, and I see the fungal glow only as a smear, a suggestion of light at the corner of an immense darkness. This does not trouble me. Down here at Lethe-mouth there is little to see. There is the slow work of the water, and the slow work of my hands, and the things the dead say as they go.

Because they do speak, you understand. That is the part the upper warrens have half-forgotten, or pretend to. When a body lies long enough in the dissolving pool, when the silica enters the marrow and the bone begins its patient journey toward becoming a hammer-handle or a chisel-haft, the essence does not leave all at once. It seeps, the way everything here seeps. And it lingers in the water, and the water carries it to the walls, and the walls hold it the way the walls hold everything. The faint warmth in the granite—the *presence* the young ones feel and shiver at and do not understand—that is the dead, gone into the stone. I have spent my long life listening to them arrive.

What do they say?

Mostly nothing that means anything. A name, repeated. The grief of a coupling unfinished. The taste of a fungal cap eaten as a child, before the teeth ground down. The drum-rhythm of one particular dawn that, for reasons known only to the dispersing soul, mattered more than all the identical dawns before and after it. I have learned that a life, at its dissolving, does not surrender its great events. It surrenders its small ones. The catalog of what is released into my keeping is a catalog of textures, smells, the way the light from a slung lantern fell across a familiar alcove. The grottos remember through the trivial.

I keep them. That is the secret of my work that I have told no one, not in all these cycles. I do not merely watch the dead disperse. I *gather* them. I kneel at Lethe-mouth and I lap the mineral water, the way one does ritually at the sacred fonts, except that I do it without ceasing, mouthful after mouthful, drinking down the last whispers before they can pass entirely into the indifferent rock. I hold them in myself. I have become a basin too.

I do not fully know why. At first I think I was only curious—wanting to taste what a life leaves behind. But somewhere in the long descent of cycles it became something else, something I have no drum-rhythm and no guttural word to name. The Unterfolk believe in stasis. We believe that nothing changes, that the strata circle back, that the city has always rung with hammers and always will. And it is true that nothing changes—*except this:* the dead are not the same dead. Each one brings down a different small thing. A different fall of light. A different forgotten dawn. The equilibrium the elders worship is not still at all. It is made entirely of disappearances, each one unique, each one final, replacing the one before in a slow exchange that no one tracks because everyone has agreed it is not happening.

I am the one who tracks it. I am the only one who knows the Unterhold is dying and being born again, mouthful by mouthful, every single cycle since memory was chipped into the pillars.

Sometimes a young one comes down, sent by a grieving family, wanting to know if their dead has *gone yet*—if it is permitted to grieve fully, or whether some part still lingers and must be honored. I tell them what they wish to hear. I say the essence has joined the fungal lords, has gone to warm the granite, sleeps now in the long balance. And this is not a lie. It is only not the whole of it.

What I do not say is: I have your father in me. I drank the rhythm of the dawn that mattered to him. I know now that he loved a woman who is also down here in my flesh, dissolved three hundred cycles before he was hatched, so that the two of them have never met and never will except inside the dark basin of an old keeper at the bottom of the world. I carry their accidental nearness like a second heartbeat. I carry thousands of these. I am crowded as the warrens above me, packed presence upon presence, every alcove of me occupied by the trivial eternities of the unmade.

I do not know what becomes of me when I dissolve. There will be no keeper at the keeper's pool. I have trained no one; I could not bear to tell another what the work truly is. So when my warmth goes out and they carry me to Lethe-mouth—and there is no one else to carry me, so perhaps I will simply lie down here when I feel it coming, and let the water take me where I sit—then all of them go too. The whole gathered weight of small remembered lights will pass into the granite at once, unsorted, unwitnessed, in a single long exhalation.

Perhaps that is what the warmth in the walls truly is. Perhaps every few thousand cycles a keeper at the bottom of the world lies down full, and the rock takes the catalog of an entire age in one breath, and the young ones for a generation feel the granite run a little warmer, and shiver, and do not understand, and call it the eternal pulse, and are not wrong.

I think the dawn is coming. I cannot see the lanterns but I can hear, very faint and far above me, the first chiming volley of the drums beginning to gather. The water at my knees has gone still. There is a new one in the shallows tonight, just laid, still warm enough that the dispersal hasn't started. I will wait. I will lap the basin slow, and listen, and hold whatever small thing this life thought too unimportant to keep.

That is where I am. That is what I would be. The one who remembers what the eternal world insists has never happened. The keeper of the small lights, down where the stone exhales.
