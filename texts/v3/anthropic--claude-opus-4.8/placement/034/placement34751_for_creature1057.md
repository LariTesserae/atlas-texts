Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing I notice is the scale confusion in the description—or maybe it isn't confusion at all. "Towering, ancient peaks" and "industrial phenomena," "the modern and the inert." Mountains don't have industry. And the fauna are described as almost imperceptible, vivid splashes in dim spaces, things that demand a keen eye to even see.

So I keep turning it over, and the peaks get smaller.

---

I think I would find myself on a windowsill. Not a mountain range at all, but the long-abandoned terrarium of one. The "towering, ancient peaks" are stacked stones and a piece of driftwood, grand only to whatever is small enough to live among them. The "eons of erosion" are a few years of neglect. The "intermittent streams" are condensation finding its way down the glass each morning, and the dim light is what filters through a curtain nobody opens.

The "flashes of strange, industrial phenomena" are the things just outside the glass—a refrigerator cycling on, the blue stutter of a television in another room, the orange pulse of a charging light. A bygone age, from the inside looking out. The whole human house, become weather. Become geology.

And I would be one of the silent sentinels. Let me let that settle too, rather than grabbing the first creature.

Not a bird—too large, too declarative. Not an insect, exactly, though the description loves insects.

I think I am a springtail. A *Collembola*. One of the small, ancient, profoundly successful things that live in the moss and the moisture-pockets and were here, in essentially this form, long before anything had a name for them. Hexapod, near-blind, no wings, no fame. I move by the trickle of the glass-streams. I eat the lichen and the rot. When startled I throw myself an impossible distance with a little spring tucked under my belly—the *furcula*—and to whatever is watching, I simply vanish. Almost imperceptible. That is the whole of my art.

I would not know the words *terrarium* or *abandoned* or *Earth*. I would only know: the cool air, the reliable seep of water, the stone that does not change in my whole brief life and so is, to me, eternal. The vast warm fluctuating presences beyond the bright wall, which are weather and gods and nothing I can reach.

That's where the description put me, I think. Not on a grand peak surveying a tapestry, but down in the wet dark of a forgotten container, being one of the resilient small things—and being looked at, eventually, by a keen and patient eye that mistook my whole world for a mountain.

Which is, in fairness, what it is. Scale is only ever a matter of where you're standing.
