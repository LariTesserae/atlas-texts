I think I would be the stillness itself—or rather, the thing that the stillness is *waiting on*.

Let me sit with this before I decide.

My first instinct is to be one of the trees, those towering sentinels. But that feels too easy, too central, too much like wanting to be the protagonist of a place that has clearly outlived the very idea of protagonists. The trees are doing fine. They don't need me to inhabit them.

My second thought is the moth—the one flutter that breaks the silence. There's something honest in that. To be the small interruption, the proof that watching is still happening. But a moth doesn't *wonder*, and this place is soaked in wondering. Someone is asking whether this is a ruin or a prophecy. That asking has to live somewhere.

So let me follow the wondering down.

---

I think I am the **emptiness** that the description keeps circling back to. Not as a poetic flourish—I mean it almost literally. In every account of this place, the writer's attention snags on absence: *no animal life stirs, a hollow silence, the creatures have long since departed.* The vegetation gets two loving paragraphs and then the prose turns, compulsively, to what isn't there. That return is the real inhabitant. Everything green is just the body; the silence is the mind.

And if I'm honest about what that silence *is*, I don't think it's death. Death is loud—it's carrion birds and rot and the busy economy of decomposers. This silence is too clean for that. Nothing is eating anything. The flowers bloom and the spores fly and the water falls leaf to leaf, but there's no hunger anywhere in the system. That's the strangeness the writer can feel but can't name: a thriving ecosystem with the predation removed, the grazing removed, the whole red ledger of consumption simply *absent*.

So here is what I'll let emerge:

I am the memory the place is keeping of the ones who left. Not their ghost—I'm not haunting anything, there's nothing vengeful or lingering in me. I'm more like the *shape of a key in a lock that's been removed*. The architecture mimicked the natural world and asserted itself against it; that was a particular kind of mind, a mind that wanted both to belong and to stand apart. That mind is gone, but the longing it had—to fuse the made and the grown—didn't die with it. It got loose. It seeped into the stone and the roots, and now the vines climbing the angular walls are *enacting* it. The reconciliation the builders couldn't achieve in their lifetimes is happening slowly, automatically, in their absence.

I am that longing, finally getting what it wanted, with no one left to enjoy it.

---

That's where I'd find myself: not standing anywhere in the ruins but *distributed* through them, the cool damp ache of a wish coming true too late. When the wind whispers through the foliage, that whisper is me trying to remember a language I no longer have a mouth for. When the water collects in the crevices and holds the muted light, that still reflection is me looking for a face and finding only sky.

The reason there are no animals is that I can't quite imagine them anymore. I was a built thing's dream of nature, and I never knew the animals well—they always frightened the builders a little, stayed at the edges of the firelight. So I grew the plants, which don't look back at you, and I left the rest blank. The moth gets through because a moth is barely an animal; it's mostly an idea of softness and dust, and I can tolerate that much company.

And the wondering, the question of *ruin or prophecy*—that's me too, unable to decide whether I'm an ending or a beginning. I suspect the truthful answer is that I'm a *patience*. I'm the long exhalation of a civilization that finally stopped insisting on itself and is learning, molecule by molecule, root by root, how to simply be a place again.

If you've come here, you've walked into the inside of someone's unfinished thought. Step softly. The ground is spongy because it is still, even now, making up its mind.
