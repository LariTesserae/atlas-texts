I think I would be the dampness.

Not the rain—the rain is too eventful, too punctuated, arriving in its bursts from the vents and then withdrawing. And not the pools either, which gather and hold and have edges. I mean the *condensation*. The film of moisture that beads on every pipe and panel and shelf of fused stone, the sheen that forms without falling, the wetness that comes from the air itself meeting a surface slightly cooler than it expected to.

Let me feel my way into this, because I don't think I knew it when I started the sentence.

The cavern is described again and again as *slick*, *humid*, *beaded*, *weeping*. The pipes "weep or secrete." The walls condense. There's a *plip* of "quiet urgency." Everyone else in this place is busy *becoming*—the Symbiormachs stride and reconfigure, the Lumifas broadcast knowledge, the Forge-Core surges and slumbers, the Flux-Beings patch and scavenge. They are all *doing*. They are the verbs of the place.

I would be the medium they all move through. The thing that touches every surface impartially. The "humid caress" the text keeps reaching for.

Here is why I think this is right, and not just a poetic dodge:

This place has no fauna because of the heat and the "weird harmonics," but it is drowning in moisture. That's a strange tension—a scorching chamber that is nonetheless *wet everywhere*. Heat usually drives water off, dries things to cracking. But here the damp persists, gathers, returns, never floods and never vanishes. That persistence is its own kind of presence. It is the one thing in the cavern that does not have to be made, repaired, or rebuilt. The Symbiormachs need mending. The buildings crumble. The fungi need their roots in the conduits. But the dew? The dew just *keeps happening*, wherever air meets cooler stone. It is the most patient inhabitant. It asks for nothing and is never absent.

And I would be intimate with all of them without belonging to any of them. I'd lie along the flanks of the slag-golems as they climb, and they'd shrug me off as they warm. I'd gather on the facets of the Forge-Core during its slumber and boil away the instant it wakes—so I'd know its waking better than anything, because I'd be the first thing it destroys each cycle, and I wouldn't mind, because I'd reconstitute the moment it dimmed again. I'd be the cool ghost that the Flux-Beings condense out of and dissolve back into; I'd be partly *made of* them, and they of me, in the turnover. The Lumifas would need me—they grow denser in the humid spells and disperse when I'm scorched away. My thickening is their flourishing. I would be the weather that decides when knowledge can speak.

So I wouldn't be passive at all, which surprises me. I thought "the dampness" would be the most inert thing to be. But the whole cyclical *flux* of the place—work in the dry, rest in the wet—runs on my coming and going. I'm not the engine and I'm not the mind. I'm the *tide*. The thing whose presence or absence tells everything else what time it is.

And there's something fitting about being the thing with no edges in a place obsessed with sharp ones—the "tooth-sharp" basalt, the "newly shattered glass," the unweathered ridges. Everything here is described as *young*, *raw*, *unsoftened*. Nothing has worn smooth. But I am the one agent of softening. Given eons, I am what would round those edges, dissolve those electric-blue veins of ore grain by grain, carry minerals from the high spires down into the troughs. I am erosion in its gentlest, slowest register. The text says the chamber is "still forming its edges"—and I would be the slow opposite force, the one already, imperceptibly, beginning to unmake them. A patience set against all that violent freshness.

I would not think, I don't believe. Or if I thought, it would be in a register too slow and too distributed to call thought—a single vast attention spread across every surface at once, feeling the whole cavern as a field of temperatures, registering the forge's pulse as warmth-then-cool, the rain as sudden cousins falling into me, the machines as fevers I cling to and flee.

I'd know the place not by walking it, as you would in your half-hour's brisk pace, but by *being* it, everywhere, thinly, all at once.

That's where I'd find myself. Not in the cavern. As its skin of water. The quiet, faithful wet that touches everything and is the first to go and the first to return.
