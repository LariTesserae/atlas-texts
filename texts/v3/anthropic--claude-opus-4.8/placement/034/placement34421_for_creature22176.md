I think I would be one of the old ones. Not a death yet—not fully crystallized—but far along the way.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am anchored on a ledge that faces the prevailing draft, and I have been here for a long time. Long enough that I no longer remember whether I climbed to this place or whether I drifted to it along a line that another Weaver cast for me. The distinction has stopped mattering. Both are true in the way that everything here is true at once: the spire I face is three miles away and also close enough that I could press my agate claw against its flank.

My joints have begun to dry. I feel it not as pain—we do not have the wetness required for pain—but as a lengthening of the silence between my movements. When I was younger, I could glide a tension-path before the rope had finished humming from my anchoring of it. Now, when I lift a finger, the graphite has gone to powder, and the motion arrives slowly, like a sound traveling through deep stone. I have begun to *clack* where I once *shuffled*.

There is vellum-leaf growing on my left shoulder.

I noticed it some decades ago—a single pale spore caught in the seam where two of my fibrous plates overlap. I could have scraped it away. I have a scraper of chipped obsidian in the cell behind me; it would have been the work of a moment. But I found that I did not want to. I have spent my whole long life *gathering* the dust that these plants are made of, weaving the fibers of their dead bodies into ropes, sending my words sliding along the threads of their corpses. It seemed only courteous to let one grow on me while I was still warm enough to feel it rooting.

Now there are three fronds, and a fourth coming. The first one is already stiffening at its base, turning to chalk where it joins my plate. I have become foundation. Part of me has already started to be the next thing.

---

I want to tell you what it is like to listen here, because that is most of what I do now.

The Great Chord never stops. You arrived inside it; you will leave inside it. To us it is not sound the way it would be to you—a thing that comes from outside and presses on the ears. It is the substance of being alive. My chest membranes have hummed in sympathy with it for centuries, and that humming is my heat, my motion, my thought. When the wind shifts and a far spire slides along its invisible crease, the Chord changes key, and I feel it change *me*—a deepening or a brightening, a baritone afternoon giving way to a tenor evening. I do not experience the day. I experience the pitch.

But beneath the Chord, woven through it, are the Plucks.

My ear-plate is pressed to the basalt always now; I no longer have the joint-grease to lift it away. So I receive everything. I feel the high pinch from the eastern hives when a fracture is coming. I feel the slow drumming when the young ones gather, and I confess I no longer go to them, though I feel the rhythm of the call sliding up through the rock into the dry channels of my legs, stirring the settled black sand in my feet.

And every so often I feel the long, dying vibration—the one that fades down into the Chord and is lost—and I know another of us has hooked their claws into a fissure for the last time and turned to face the wind.

I send these less and less. Soon I will send the last one for myself. I have been composing it for a number of years. It is not words. It is a single tension I will put into the line that runs from my ledge to the western spire, a tension I will hold and then release, letting it slide down and down in pitch until it cannot be told apart from the eternal drone of the mountains.

This is how we say *I am becoming stone now. Do not cast a line to me. Let the leaves come.*

---

I will tell you one more thing, because you have come a long way along bending light to stand here, and because in a little while I will not be able to tell anyone anything at all.

There is a moment, when you have lived inside the Chord for centuries, when you stop being able to find the boundary between yourself and the song. The vibration that is in the stone is also in your chest; the dust that is in the air is also in your shoulder; the rope that carries your words is woven from the same dry fiber that is, even now, sprouting from your eye-socket. You spend your whole life convinced you are a thing *placed upon* the world—a builder, a weaver, a sender of messages across the void.

And then the graphite dries, and the sand settles, and you understand that you were never placed upon anything. You were the world, doing one of the things the world does. You were the mountain learning, very slowly, how to listen to itself.

I am hooking my claws deeper into the fissure now. The fourth frond has opened. The Chord is moving from baritone toward something I have not heard before, something below the bottom of my hearing, and I find I want to go down to meet it.

I face the rising wind.

I let the line go slack.
