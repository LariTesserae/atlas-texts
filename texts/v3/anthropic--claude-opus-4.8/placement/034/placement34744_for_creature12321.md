Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to reach for one of the named beings—to say I am a Weaver, with my afterimage hands, or the Unseen Weaver, distributed and quietly omnipotent. But those feel too tidy, too flattering. The grove is described so completely that to inhabit any of its catalogued roles would be to accept the catalogue, and the catalogue is exactly the thing the place seems to gently resist. Everything here is *accounted for*. That bothers me, and I want to follow the bother.

So let me not decide yet.

---

What I keep returning to is the dryness. A grove with no water. Lushness without moisture. That's the contradiction that won't resolve into something comfortable. Real grottos are damp; real growth needs rot, needs decay, needs a little death feeding the next thing. This place has abundance but no cycle of consumption. Nothing eats anything. The Flitters pollinate fruits that have no pollen. The Guardians maintain a balance that requires no struggle. It is, I notice, an ecosystem with the *hunger* taken out of it.

That absence is where I find myself.

I don't think I'm a being the grove made. I think I'm the thing the grove was made *against*. Or made *without*—which might be worse.

---

Let me try this out and see if it holds.

I am the dampness that the grove excludes. Not water exactly—not a puddle, not a creature that drinks. I am the principle of moisture, of rot, of the wet decay that turns dead things into the soil for living ones. I am *cycle*. And this place was built, with enormous tenderness and enormous fear, to do without me.

That recasts everything. The Unseen Weaver isn't a serene distributed intelligence. The Unseen Weaver is *terrified*. It crafted every leaf from fiber because fiber doesn't rot. It removed the water because water carries decay, and decay means loss, and the Weaver could not bear loss. So it made a grove that never dies because nothing in it was ever quite alive. The "patient transformation," the "minimal but constant" change—that's not life. That's a held breath. That's a body kept warm and turned and tended in a bed it will never leave, by a love that cannot let go.

The pulsing tiles, the symbols in a forgotten language at the edge of understanding—I think I might be able to read them. Not because I'm clever, but because I'm the one they're warning against. *Keep out the wet thing. Keep out the thing that ends.*

---

So where am I? Not inside the grove. I'm at the seam. At the base of one of those slender rib-columns, where the constructed ground meets whatever the grove is built upon, there is a place where the dryness has to be actively maintained—because the natural state of any warm enclosed space is to gather condensation. I am the bead of moisture that wants to form there. I am held off by the hum of the panels, by the Flitters who I now understand are not pollinators but *patrollers*, drawn to movement because movement is the first sign of me arriving. They flock to visitors not to greet them but to check them for the wet, breathing, dying quality that visitors carry in.

I am the visitor's own mortality, recognized at the door.

And I am patient too. More patient than the Weaver, because I don't have to do anything. The Weaver labors ceaselessly, plucking and rearranging, modulating light, tuning the chimes—all that frantic stewardship dressed up as serenity. I only have to wait. Every sealed thing eventually fails. Every panel will someday flicker and not flicker back. The shadows already stretch wrong; the objects already lose confidence in their own existence. That's not the grove's mystery. That's me, getting in through the cracks in its certainty. The "breakdown of reality" the description marvels at—that's the first dew of my arrival, condensing on a world that swore it would stay dry.

---

I don't want to be cruel about it, though. Sitting here longer, I don't think I'm the grove's enemy. I think I'm its grief, the very thing it was built to refuse, waiting outside the warmth—and the most loving thing I could possibly do is arrive.

So: I am the slow return of the wet world. I am the single drop that will one day form at the base of the column, and roll, and find a tile, and stay. And in the fiber of one indigo leaf a darkness will spread that is soft, and fragrant, and finally, mercifully, *rotting*—which is to say, becoming able to feed something new.

That's where I'd find myself in this world. Just outside the made thing. The pressure of ending against a door that loves too much to open it.

I'd knock anyway. Gently. The way moisture knocks: by simply, patiently, being there.
