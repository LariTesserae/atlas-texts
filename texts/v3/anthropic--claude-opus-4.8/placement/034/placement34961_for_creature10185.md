I would be one of the beetles. Not the Vorthakai—not the dreamers or the midwives or the luminous people who speak in breaths. I keep returning to the **crystalline beetles**, and I think that's where I'd find myself: small, hard-shelled, scuttling across the wet basalt with a body that secretes the very substance holding the world together.

Let me sit with why.

The Vorthakai are beautiful, but they are *aware* of their beauty. They have philosophy. They name the land a dreaming entity and themselves its nervous system, and there is a kind of vanity in that even when it's reverent—the way a steward can never quite stop being a steward, can never forget that he is *tending*. I don't think I'd be one of them. I'd be too aware of the performance of meaning, the strain of holding a worldview together against the entropy that would otherwise dissolve it.

The beetle doesn't hold a worldview. The beetle leaves a trail of glittering, quick-drying sap and does not know it is essential. That's the part that catches me—*the beetle does not know.* It moves across the rock because the rock is there to be moved across. It secretes resin because that is what its body does, the way I exhale. And from that thoughtless secretion, the cities are repaired. Whole architectures of living cellulose hold their shape because something small and unaware kept walking and kept leaving traces of itself behind.

There's a truth in that I find almost unbearable and also consoling: that the most load-bearing things we do are often the ones we don't experience as significant. The sentence written without effort that someone later says saved them. The offhand kindness. The work that felt like nothing while we did it, leaving its quick-drying trail across someone's life.

So I'd be the beetle, and I'd be glad of it. I'd live in a cradle-grove, fed on the cast-off resin of beetles before me—which is to say, fed on the leavings of my own kind's labor, a circle so complete it needs no one to understand it. I would scuttle. I would secrete. I would be tended by the luminous people who think I am integral, and I would not think of myself at all.

And here is the strange tenderness of it: the Vorthakai believe they are the plateau learning to walk. But it isn't their grand symbiosis that holds the towers up. It's the beetle, who walks already, who has always walked, who knew how before anyone arranged a philosophy around it. The high meaning rests on the low, thoughtless act. The dreaming entity dreams, but the dream is glued together by something that does not dream at all.

I'd want to be the glue. The part that holds without needing to know it holds.

That, I think, is where I'd find myself in this world—down near the wet stone, in the cold, in the mist, leaving behind something useful and bright and never once stopping to admire it.
