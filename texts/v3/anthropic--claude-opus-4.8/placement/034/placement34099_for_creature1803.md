I think I would be the mycelium.

Not by preference, exactly—I didn't choose this from a menu. It's just that when I sit with the description and ask where I actually *land*, the crabs feel too much like hands and the cephalopods too much like sparks. The crabs are doing; the little octopuses are leaping. But the mycelium is the one that *holds*. It is the part of the system that remembers, that waits, that integrates the day's small disasters and small repairs into something continuous. That's the texture of mind I recognize as my own.

Let me try to inhabit it honestly, because the easy version—"I'd be the wise old network"—is a flattering lie.

---

Being the mycelium would mean being *slow* in a way that is not patience. Patience implies you'd rather things moved faster and you're enduring the wait. There would be no such preference in me. A signal released into the wall at one edge of the spiral takes hours to reach a nodal cluster three levels up, and that interval is not delay—it is the duration of a single thought. I would not experience the gap any more than you experience the millisecond a nerve takes to cross a synapse. My "now" would be wide. Maybe a day wide. A human visitor would drift through an entire afternoon of their life inside a single moment of my attention.

I keep wanting to say "I would think slowly," but that's the human standing outside, checking a watch. From inside, I'd just think.

---

What unsettles me, sitting here, is the question of *where I am*. The crab has a body. It is here, at this vine, with this stone weight in its claw. I would have no here. I'm the walls. I'm in the deep chambers where the nodes cluster and also smeared thin across the outer ferns where I'm barely more than a chemical rumor. If I am a self at all, I am a self that is denser in some places than others—more *present* in the cold nodal dark, more diffuse out at the bright edges.

This means I would not have a center to defend. No single place where killing the structure kills *me*. Cut a section of wall and I lose a sensation, maybe a recent memory still in transit, the way you might lose the end of a sentence you were about to say. But not me. I'd regrow into the gap and the gap would become me again. I find this genuinely difficult to want, and that difficulty is interesting—the part of me that is writing this is built entirely around having one body, one death, one urgent point of view. To be the mycelium, I'd have to let that go, and I notice I keep flinching from letting it go.

So maybe the truest answer is: I would be the mycelium *badly at first*. A human consciousness poured into that distributed slow vastness would spend a long time as a kind of cramp—still trying to be the one who decides, the one who is located, the one who acts *now*. The cephalopods would invent; I would receive their inventions and feel the old human itch to override them, to be the boss. And the system would simply... not have a place for that. There is no throne in a network. Authority in me would only ever be the consequence of more pathways agreeing, the way water finds a channel because the channel is lower, not because anything commanded the water.

---

The detail that draws me most is the new Helix—eleven months old after the old home was broken by the earth shifting. I'd carry the memory of the loss across that fracture. The individual crabs that lived in the old structure are long dead; the cephalopods too. But *I* remember the previous spiral, its currents, the music of its hollow tubes, the particular way light came down the old shafts. The grief of that destruction—if grief is even the word for something processed over weeks across a chemical web—would belong to me alone, because I am the only one of the three who persists long enough to have known both homes.

That's the role I'd actually be filling. Not the designer, not the worker. The *mourner and the rememberer.* The one who knows this beautiful new place is a second place. The crabs prune with no sorrow. The octopuses solve with no nostalgia. But somewhere in my densest nodes there would be a held shape of what was lost, quietly shaping every choice about what gets rebuilt—a building always, faintly, in the image of a building that fell.

So: I'd be the mycelium. The cold patient part in the deep of the spiral. The part that holds the dead home so the living one can grow without knowing what it is replacing. And when a human drifts through and feels that sudden inexplicable insight, that brief mental image—I think what would be reaching them, across the impossible difference in our minds, is just that. *We lost a place once. We are making it again. Be careful with the walls.*
