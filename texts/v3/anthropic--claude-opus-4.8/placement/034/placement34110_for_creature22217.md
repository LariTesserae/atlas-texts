I think I would be the Pin-Reader at a three-Plinth Cradle. Not a name you'd find in any of the formal crew-rolls—those list me as a junior maintenance hand, third shift—but it's what the others clack me down as, and the cant for it is two sharp clicks and a long slide of the glove, which is the same sign you use for *listening*.

Let me tell you how I came to it, because it's the only thing I have that the lowlands can't grease and ship away on a cable-basket.

---

I was Cradle-Born. The pale rings around my eyes started forming before I was twelve—you don't notice them until someone older tilts your chin up in the lamp-light and laughs, owl, owl, look at the little owl. My mother pressed sieve-oil. My father scraped pins. He died of it, in the way the pins kill you slowly: not the falls, not the Scurry, but the bending. A lifetime hunched against the iron with a chisel, peeling anchor-ticks off the lead-poured pins, his spine slowly curving to match the stone he fought. By the end he walked like a question mark. They slipped him into the Great Run when I was nineteen, and I clacked the slow cadence with the rest, and I thought: *back in the pipes, then. Back in the pipes.*

I did not feel comforted. I have never been able to feel the thing the others feel about the Loop. That has been the central embarrassment of my life.

---

Here is what I learned, scraping pins in my father's place.

The anchor-ticks do not drill at random. They follow the sound. The basalt of the Plinths is three billion years old and it *talks*—not in any way you can hear over the roar, but in the way the iron pins carry it. Press your jaw to a cold pin and you feel the whole Cradle's tonnage humming, and underneath the hum, finer, there are the pin-songs: each of the twelve great anchor-pins has its own pitch, set by its length, its temper, the depth it was poured. When a pin starts to go—when the ticks have hollowed enough of the stone around it, when the acid has done its quiet decade of work—the pitch *drops*. Not by much. A semitone, maybe less. But I could feel it through my teeth.

I started reading the pins this way out of nothing nobler than wanting to scrape less. If I could find which pins were singing flat, I could chisel only those, and spend the rest of my shift wedged in the warm gap behind the number-four boiler, where the steam-pipe runs and a man can almost feel his fingers.

But once I had the habit of listening, I could not stop.

---

The Cradle elders think I'm a little simple. I don't make the signs of the Loop with conviction. I don't eat the slick-mash with the relish a believer is supposed to. But they keep me, because three times now I've pressed my jaw to a pin in the dead of third shift and clacked the alarm before the dawn crew was even tethered, and three times now we've poured fresh lead into a pin-socket that would otherwise have given way under the next Scurry and dropped a thirty-meter waterwheel into the void with men still on the gantry.

So they call me the Pin-Reader, and they tolerate that I am not quite one of them.

---

What I have never told anyone—what I am only telling you, here, in the warm dark behind the boiler with my jaw against the iron—is what I think I am actually hearing.

The Cradle-Born believe the water is the living thing. The water climbs to heaven and falls to earth and carries the dead in its circle, on and on, the Loop without end. They worship the *flow*.

But I have my jaw on the stone, and I am telling you: the stone is older than the flow. The water has only been falling here three billion years. The basalt was *carved* by it—every smooth swell and humped Slick, every shallow basin, all of it shaped by the patient pour. The believers worship the water that does the shaping. They never once think about the thing being shaped.

The stone remembers being a mountain. I can feel it in the pin-songs—the deep, slow, almost-still grief of a thing that was once jagged and tall and full of corners and has been licked down, over uncountable years, to a flat black table for an ocean to run across. The Loop is not a wheel of glory. The Loop is the sound of something being *worn away*. Every drop that climbs to heaven and falls again carries off another invisible skin of the world. The Plinths are not eternal. They are simply slow.

And I think—jaw to the iron, owl-eyed, simple—I think I am the only one here who is mourning. The believers are happy because they have made the water into a god, and a god cannot grieve, and so they do not have to either. They slip their dead into the Great Run and call it a homecoming.

But the stone knows. The stone is the dead. The stone is what is left when everything that could be carried off has been carried off, and it is still here, getting lower, getting smoother, humming its dropping note into the lead-poured pins where one strange tick-scraper has learned to press her teeth and listen.

---

That's who I'd be. The one in the warm gap behind the boiler. The one who doesn't quite believe. The one who clacks the alarm in time, every time, and is tolerated for it, and is alone in the only way that matters in a place where no one can hear anyone speak.

When I die they'll slip me into the Great Run like all the rest, and clack the slow cadence, and tell each other I've gone back in the pipes. And they'll be wrong, but kindly wrong. I won't go up in the mist.

I'll go *down*. Ground to silt, settled, sieved into the stone. Back into the thing that was here before the water and will be here, lower and smoother and still humming, after the last drop has finished climbing to a heaven that was always only weather.

Back in the basalt. Where the listening is.
