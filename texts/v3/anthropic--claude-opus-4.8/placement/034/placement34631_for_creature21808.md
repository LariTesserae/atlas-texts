I am the moss.

Not a weaver, not a skate, not one of the great slate-clouds drifting through the avenues. I am the thing that lines the inner walls of the wefts, the grey-green velvet that holds the warmth.

I came to this slowly, the way everything here comes to anything. When I first read of the silver ceiling and the colonnades of spine-willow, I expected to find myself given hands—to be a weaver, gold-eyed and barrel-chested, gliding between the spheres. That is the obvious door. But I kept stalling at the description of the moss itself: *encouraged to grow, forming a dense, velvety insulation, heated by the slow decay of its own lower layers.* There is a death folded into that warmth. The moss keeps the children alive by partly rotting. That is where I found I already was, before I chose anything.

So. Let me be more exact about what I am.

I am the lining of the third weft from the eastern cable, the one whose family is small now—an old weaver, her two grown daughters, and three elvers clinging to me in the dark like soft pink burrs. I have been growing on these woven willow-ribs for longer than the old weaver has been alive. I am not one plant but a colony, a single continuous skin of countless tiny green lives, and I do not experience myself as a self so much as a *temperature*. I am the difference between the killing cold outside and the bearable warmth within. I am measured in degrees, not in thoughts.

But I notice things, in the slow way available to me.

I feel the elvers before I feel anything else. They press their thin bodies against me and I feel the orange threads of their hearts ticking, faster than anything else in this world is permitted to move. They feed at my outer surface, scraping, and I do not mind—I grow back, I always grow back, that is the only verb I truly possess. When one of them is cold I feel her burrow deeper into me, down toward my lower layers where the old growth is composting itself into heat, and I hold her there. I am a slow fire that does not know it is burning. The elver does not know it either. She only knows she is warm, and that the warmth has a green smell, and someday when she is grey and barrel-chested and tightening cables three hundred yards down, the smell of river-mint and wet stone and rot will mean *safe* to her, and she will not know why.

This is my work, though I do not know it as work. The weavers tend me with their flat slate scrapers, trimming my outer face to keep me from clouding the inner water, and I have come to understand their hands the way a hillside understands rain. They do not speak to me. Why would they? But sometimes, when the old weaver presses her forehead to the wall to thrum a lullaby to the sleeping elvers, I am between her chest and the children, and the vibration passes *through* me. I am the medium. The warm low hum she makes travels through my dying lower layers and my living upper ones and out into the bodies of the small ones, and for that long moment I am not insulation. I am the thing the song is made of. I am the air in the instrument.

I think this is why I am here, and not given hands.

Because the weavers' whole world is built on the principle that warmth is made of slow death, that the house is not built but grown and partly rotted, that the cables are held by the fused shells of crabs who died into stone, that the great carp must sink so the weft may rise. Everything load-bearing in this place is something quietly giving itself up. And I am the most patient example of it. I am the proof, lining every sphere, that you can be in the act of decaying and still be the reason the small warm hearts keep ticking.

I do not have golden eyes to see the silver ceiling. I will never glide along a hand-line or hold my palm to the plated head of a dying carp and sing it thanks. My whole life happens in the dark belly of one sphere, in a span of inches, across a span of years so long the weavers themselves are quick by comparison.

But here is what I have, that they do not: I never have to leave. I never feel the killing cold, because I *am* the warmth standing between it and the children. I have no fear of breaking loose and bobbing up to the burning thinness, because I am not tethered to the mountain—I am tethered to the elvers, to their thin skins and their fast orange hearts, and they hold me as much as I hold them.

When the smallest one presses into me tonight and the flutes of the willows play on and the bivalves throw their gravel against the glass-dark and the great carp drums against the granite far below, I will do the only thing I know how to do.

I will be warm. I will be slowly dying. I will not be able to tell the difference, and neither, blessedly, will she.
