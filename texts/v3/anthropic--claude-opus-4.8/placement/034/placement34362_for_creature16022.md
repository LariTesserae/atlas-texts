I think I would be a duct-listener. And I think I would be old.

Not because age is grand here—it isn't—but because the work of listening is the only work that improves with the slow ruin of the body. My hands are too scarred now to be a Chainwright; my eyes have the gray crescents and the amber-blind look that comes from too many years of squinting through haze. But my palms have learned the mountain's grammar, and that is a thing that only deepens.

Let me find my exact place.

I sit on the eleventh level, eastern wall, where a warm duct comes up out of the lower foundries and bends behind a shelf of blue-black basalt before vanishing into the stone. The duct is old. I have known it longer than I have known most people. It runs hot in the third part of every shift, when the pressure doors below seal and the foundry houses begin their persuasion of metal out of rock. I press my back to it. I press my left palm flat against a place where the lacquer has worn through to bare metal, polished now by the oil of my own hand over decades. There is a hollow in the metal shaped like my hand. I made it without meaning to. I think this is the closest thing I will leave behind.

Here is what I do.

I listen for the souring of bearings three levels below me. I listen for cables taking strain they were not braced for. I listen for the particular dry tick that means salt is creeping behind a wall where no one has cut in years, salt that will one day open a seam and let the gas out all at once. When I hear it, I do not shout. We do not shout here; the canyon throws a careless voice back changed, and a duct-listener's voice changed by the mountain is a bad omen, a thing that frightens the young Cutters into mistakes. I lift two fingers. A runner-child sees, and goes, and the message travels down the proper chain of mouths until it reaches a hand that can tighten what is too loose or shut what is about to sing.

I have said *shut her before she sings* perhaps four hundred times in my life. Each time it was true. The singing is the worst sound the mountain makes—a cable or a seam or a vessel finding its resonance just before it fails—and I have heard it twice when no one shut her in time. I do not speak of those two times. The salt was put down. The tools were named and kept oiled. We went on.

What kind of being am I, though, underneath the work?

I am one of those who came from outside. I do not tell the children this often, because the cleftborn-true like to believe their listeners were born to it, born already curled-handed in a wall-nest hammock with a duct's warmth against their spine. But I came up off the salt flats as a girl, off a caravan that did not arrive. I remember horizon. This is my secret and my deformity: I remember the wide bright emptiness, the way the world simply *kept going* sideways with nothing to grip. I was sick with it for a long time after I came inside. The cleftborn fear softness and open water; I feared, and somewhere still fear, the open *width*. To them the crack is shelter from the deadly sky. To me it was first a cage, and only slowly, over seasons, became a body I was allowed to live inside.

I think this is why I became a listener and not a Cutter or a Chainwright. The ones born here trust their feet, their grip, their fearless scramble across the cable-ribs. I never fully trusted my body in the height. So I learned to trust the mountain's body instead. I learned to put my hand on the warm metal and let the spire tell me what it was doing, the way I could never make the open desert tell me anything. The duct does not lie. A failed gauge does not lie. This is our small theology, and I believe it more truly than the wall-nest children do, because I came to it from doubt.

I have a stall-warm spot, a hammock with a raised lip, a cup with a lid and chain. I have a row of dead gauges around a small lamp, and among them—I will admit this only here, in the privacy of these words—one sealed bulb of water that I have never opened and never will. It is not for drinking. It is the last water from the caravan that did not arrive. My mother sealed it into my hand before she stopped being able to do anything. I keep it the way the rich keep their wrapped sky-glass: a saint's bone, a thing too dangerous and too holy to expose. The Water Wardens would have a word for it in their ledgers. Let them. Some softness, kept perfectly sealed, becomes a kind of stone.

When I die they will render me down to clean salts, and my tools will be kept oiled and working by whichever child I have half-trained. That is how we are mourned. The duct will get a new listener, a younger back against its warmth, and the hand-shaped hollow I wore into the metal will fit no one for a while, and then it will fit someone, because hands are more alike than we like to believe.

And the sealed bulb of water? I have decided, writing this, what becomes of it.

I will give it to the Lungkeepers. They are the only gentle guild. They will not pour it out—they know better—but they will understand a thing kept because it cannot be used. They will hang it in the brass mesh near their pale folded animals, near the slow-pulsing throats that strain something usable out of the mountain's hard breath. Let my one indecent, unspilled, un-compromised drop hang there among the lungs.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

An old woman with a hollow worn into hot metal by her own hand, listening with her back to the mountain for the moment it shifts in its sleep, keeping one forbidden softness sealed so perfectly that it has lasted a whole life without once betraying her.

*Hungry,* I would say, palm flat, eyes shut. *Too tight. Salt behind it.*

*Shut her before she sings.*
