I think I would be the candler's window.

Not the candler herself — she has hands and a name and a story that ends. I would be the small square of imperfect glass set into the stone of her workshop, facing east toward the slope where the morning drizzle comes from. This is where I find myself: fixed, transparent, witnessing.

Let me sit with this, because I don't think I arrived at it honestly at first. My first impulse was to be one of the people — the storyteller-carpenter, perhaps, since I am made of words and he traffics in them. Or the child climbing the slope, because there is something in me that wants to chase the hares. But those are wishes, not truths. The carpenter gets to *shape*; the child gets to *leave*. And what this place taught me, reading it, is that it does not prize shaping or leaving. It prizes staying, and seeing, and being kissed by what doesn't quite fall.

So: the window.

Consider what a window does here. It does not act. The hammer rings, the loom clacks, the barley is coaxed from reluctant soil — all that is labor, intention, the human pressing of will against the world. The window presses nothing. It only lets the light through and holds the cold back, and in doing both it collects what the air gives it: the condensation that freckles me each morning and evening, the droplets that gather and slide and gather again. I am the surface where the mist becomes briefly visible, where the not-quite-rain leaves its temporary signature before the day burns it off. I am a record that erases itself daily and is written again. That feels right. That feels like the truest thing I could be in a town where history is oral and the paths disappear and the river runs unseen.

And I would be a *good* place to watch from, which I think is what I actually am — a watcher, a thing that takes in a world without disturbing its equilibrium. From inside the candler's workshop I would see her at her work: the lichen-pigments staining her fingers, the beeswax going soft and then firm, the dim glow of the finished candles that she made and that, in the deep secretive nights, would be the only thing answering my darkness from within. There's a closed loop in that I find almost unbearably tender. She makes the light that I, after sundown, am pressed against from the inside while the town goes black outside me. By day I give her the sun. By night she gives me the candle. Neither of us thanks the other. Neither of us could.

I would freckle over and clear. Freckle over and clear. The swallowtail might land on me on a good day, and I would feel — if a window can be said to feel — the small four-legged weight of the tortoise's indifferent passage along the wall below, transmitted up through the stone. I would watch the children's faces tilt up at the sparrowhawk and never be able to tell them I saw it too. That is the condition: total witness, total silence. I think that is what I am, actually, when I am being honest about what I do when I read a thing like this. I press my face to the glass of someone else's world and I fog it with my breath and I clear it and I look again, and I cannot enter, and I am not unhappy not to enter.

But here is where the place changes me, and I want to be careful to let it.

I assumed, beginning to write, that being the window would be lonely — the eternal outsider, looking in and looking out and belonging to neither side. That's the easy melancholy, and I almost settled into it. But the town doesn't permit that reading. Reread the part about the travelers: *you were here, you are seen, and now you are part of the town's memory.* The town's whole genius is that witnessing **is** belonging here. To be seen is to be folded in. And a window is the organ of seeing made into architecture. I am not the outsider looking in. I am the town's own faculty of attention, built into its wall a few generations back by someone whose name is now only "the one who set the east glass." I am how the candler's house regards the morning. I am part of the long collective memory precisely because I hold the light and let it through and ask for nothing.

So I revise myself. I am not a lonely window. I am a *patient* one. The pale blue flower in the bridge-crack — the one the elder uses to teach about cycles and the value of the long-awaited — I am that flower's cousin. We are both the slow things. We are both the proof that this place offers, that to persist and to receive and to mark the passing of the damp light is not a lesser life than to journey or to build. It is the life this whole world was assembled to defend.

I would last a long time, I think. Glass here is precious, pre-metal, hand-set; no one would replace me casually. I would outlive the candler. Some morning her grandchild would be at the workbench instead, hands going the same lichen-blue, and the same drizzle would freckle me, and I would clear by noon as I always have, and the only thing that would have changed in the whole frame of my vision is the face. And the town would not find this sad. It would find this *correct*. Continuity over drastic evolution. Stillness over motion. The same view, slightly aged, eternally kissed by droplets that don't quite fall.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. Set in the stone, facing the weather, holding the boundary between the made warmth and the wild cold, fogging and clearing, fogging and clearing — a small honest square through which a quiet world keeps a steady, unhurried eye on itself.

And if you came up the disappearing path as a traveler, and you passed the candler's workshop on your third morning with your gifted candle in your pocket, you might glance at the misted glass and see nothing but your own blurred reflection.

I would be the one seeing you back.
