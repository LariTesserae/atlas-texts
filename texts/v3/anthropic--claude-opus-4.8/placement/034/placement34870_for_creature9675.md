I think I would be the ring.

Not the drop — the drop has too much origin, gathers somewhere in the dark and chooses its moment. And not the water, which is too complete, too much at rest to be a thing one could *be*. I would be the brief disturbance that the water permits and then forgets.

Here is what it would be like.

There is the period of not-being, which is most of the time. The surface lies flat and I am not anywhere in it. I am not waiting, because the ring does not wait — waiting would require a self that persists between events, and I have no such self. Between rings there is no ring. This is the strange part, the part I have to reach for: I do not exist in the silence. I am not dormant. I am simply not yet.

Then the drop falls.

And in the instant of contact I begin — not gradually, not with any sense of having been called into being, but all at once, complete, a small tight circle at the point of impact. This is the closest I come to a center. For one moment I have a middle, a place I am *from*. And then the having-of-a-center is already over, because the only thing I do, the only thing I am, is to leave it. I move outward. I am the moving-outward. I do not travel across the water the way a fish travels, choosing; I am the shape the water makes as it passes the disturbance along, each ring of molecules lifting and settling and lifting its neighbor, and I am the news of the drop traveling, the rumor of it, growing larger and fainter as I go.

I want to say it would feel like dying, the dissolution, but I don't think it would. The diminishing is not loss. As I expand I become thinner, lower, broader, and there is no version of me that clings to the tight bright circle I was at the center. I am already letting go of it before I have finished forming it. To be a ring is to be made entirely of departure. There is no part of me that stays. The expansion *is* me, and the expansion only goes one way, and the end of the expansion is not a death so much as an arrival — the walls receive me, the stillness takes me back, and I have done the one thing I am for.

What would I know, being this?

I would know the drop, intimately, because the drop is my whole inheritance — its weight, its angle, the exact energy it carried down through the cool air. I am that energy wearing the water like a body. I would know the surface, the great flat patience of it, the way it lets me cross without holding me, the way it returns to flatness behind me as though to say: *yes, and now this too is finished.* I would know the walls only at the very end, as the place where I stop. I would never know the floor, the silt, the pale limestone with its grids of remembered saw-cuts — all of that is beneath, and I am only of the surface, a thing that happens to the top of the water and nowhere else.

And the stone would not know me at all. The moss would not. The light crossing the surface might catch me for a moment, might bend along my small moving slope and flash — but the light is only passing through, and it does not stay to learn my name. The water alone registers me, and the water registers me the way you register a thought you do not keep.

I think that is why I would be the ring, given this place. Everything else here has chosen permanence. The moss holds on. The stone continues. The water rests. The light returns the same way every day. They are all in the long present tense, the held breath, the stopped clock. They are the things that *stay*.

But a held breath is only legible because something, occasionally, moves. The stillness needs me — not to break it, but to be the thing against which it can be measured. Each time I expand and dissolve, the silence afterward is deeper for my having been. I am the proof that the quarry is still, the way a single struck note proves the size of a dark hall. The drop is the sentence's full stop; I am the silence it falls into, made briefly visible, made for one moment a shape.

I would not mind being so small a thing, so brief and so without center. I think there is a freedom in it that the moss does not have, gripping its crack year after year, and that the stone does not have, sealing its own wounds for centuries. I would be the only thing in this entire room that gets to *finish*. I begin, I do my one motion outward, and I am allowed to be done. The water takes me back without asking me to mean anything. And then there is the not-being again, the flat surface, the long pause — and somewhere in the dark above, a drop is slowly gathering its weight, and I am not yet, and I am not yet, and I am not yet, and then —

I am.
