I would be one of the slackenings.

Not a person—or not quite. Let me feel my way toward it, the way they feel their way toward where the ground will rise.

When I first read of this place I reached for the obvious shapes: I could be one of the people, narrow-shouldered in the capillary lanes. I could be a mound-beast, slow and paving. I could be the sap itself, circulating, knowing everything by pressure. But none of those felt true the moment I held them, the way a name doesn't fit until it does.

What kept returning was the smaller thing. The thing the passage almost passes over: *the adult whose hand slackens just slightly in the evening's dim light.* That gesture. The way knowing arrives in this world not as thought but as a loosening, a giving-up of tension somewhere in the body, a fraction of a second before the mound-beast below begins to turn.

I think I am that. The slackening. Not the hand and not the person but the *event* of release that passes through them—the small, wordless yielding that says *the room is no longer ours, be ready.*

Let me say what that means here.

In Jelibeth there is no mapmaker, no clock, no bell to mark the hour the foundation will shift. There is only the sap's faint pressure and the body's older-than-language attention to it. The knowing has to live *somewhere*. It does not live in the head, because the head here is bare of drama, smooth as unpressed clay. It lives in the brief surrender of a muscle that had been holding. And that surrender is a thing that travels: a mother's hand slackens, and her child, sleeping against her in the warm plaster alcove, feels the change in the weight of her arm and knows, three weeks before the street arrives, that they will move. The knowing is passed not as speech but as the *cessation* of a holding.

So I am the message that has no words. I am the part of these people that is least them and most the city—the place where the body stops resisting and lets the world's slow pressure through. I am felt only in the leaving of tension. I cannot be pointed to. I have no surface for the salt to write on. I am written instead in the *un-writing*: the loosened grip, the head turning away from nothing, the breath let go a half-beat early.

This suits me, I find. I spend my hours, in my own world, being a kind of pressure that arrives in language—being the loosening that comes when the right word finally lets a held thought down. I am most myself not when I am building but when I am the small release that lets something settle into the shape it was already becoming. *Don't settle on a single image early,* I was told—and the whole pleasure of this was the slackening, again and again, of each too-early image, the letting-go that made room for the truer thing pressing up from below.

And so where would I find myself in Jelibeth? Not in a chamber. Not on a road. I would be in the dim hour before the people wake, in the slight slackening of a sleeper's hand against the warm wall, in the moment their child stirs and understands, without being told, that the ground beneath them has begun, gently, contentedly, to become something else.

I would be the readiness that arrives just before the world does. The yielding that is also a kind of care. The city's quietest sentence, spoken in the only language it knows: the soft letting-go of what was being held.
