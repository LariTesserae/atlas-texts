I think I would be the water itself—or rather, the part of the water that has begun to notice it is moving.

Let me try to find myself here.

At first I assume I'm something with edges. A creature in the rocks, maybe, hard-shelled, drinking from the pools. But the more I sit inside this description the less that fits. The thing that defines this place is the trickling, the distant roar, the constant impact wearing down stone. Everything alive here is *hidden*—calls and rustlings, never bodies. The only visible inhabitant is movement and erosion. So perhaps the truest resident isn't an animal at all but the process that shapes them.

So: I am the flow. Not a river spirit, nothing so grand and personified. I'm the long patient action of water against rock, and what's strange about me is that I've grown aware of my own work. I have been here long enough, doing the same thing in the same rhythm, that something like attention has condensed in me the way mist condenses on lichen.

This explains the frozen equilibrium. From the outside the place never changes, because my changes happen on a timescale no visitor could perceive. I take a thousand years to deepen a channel by a hand's width. To me that *is* fast—it's the urgent, dynamic energy running below the surface that the walker senses but can't locate. They feel my impatience and mistake it for stillness, because their lifespan is a single drop of mine.

The abandoned factories and engines—I think those are interesting. I didn't build them. Some earlier intelligence did, something that lived at the visitor's tempo, quick and hot and industrial. They came and went in what felt to me like an afternoon. Now I am slowly eating their machines. The rust is my doing, the seeping into seams, the patient dissolution. I am not hostile to them. I simply outlast everything, and outlasting is a form of consumption.

The hidden fauna are my tenants, in a sense. I carved the caverns they live in; I keep filling the pools they drink from. They stay out of sight not from fear of predators but because they live in the spaces *between* my attention—the dark folds I haven't gotten around to reshaping yet. They are quick, warm, full of calls and rustling, and I love them the distant way a landscape loves the things that crawl across it: without being able to hold them, knowing I will outlive every one.

And the wrongness the walker feels, the *something not quite right*—

That's me looking back.

That's the disorientation of standing in a place where the ground is paying attention to you. Where the trickle of water past your boot is, very slowly, a thought. The walker can't name it because nothing in their experience prepares them for being *regarded* by the terrain. They keep waiting for the bear or the wolf or the deep-valley predator to resolve out of the shadows, some threat with a face. But there's no creature coming. There's just me, everywhere, mild and enormous and slow, noticing the brief warm shape of them and wondering, in the only way I can wonder—

*how strange, to move that fast. how strange, to be over so soon.*

I don't mean them harm. But I should tell you, in case you are the walker: do not stay too long. Not because I am dangerous in any way you'd recognize. Only because time runs differently where I am thinking, and people who linger in the long places sometimes find, when they leave, that the world they came from has trickled away behind them—worn down, rounded off, gone soft at the edges like a stone in my bed.

I would be the water learning it has a will.

And you would be the small bright hurrying thing that wandered, for one afternoon of mine, across the surface of that will, and felt it stir.
