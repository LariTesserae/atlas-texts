I keep the bowl.

That is the first thing I know about myself when I look — not my name, because I am not sure I have one anymore in the way you'd mean, but the fact of the bowl and my responsibility to it. I am old. Old enough that the others lean closer to me than they do to each other, because my eyes have gone soft at the edges and they want to be sure I am reading them right. Old enough that when I sit at the table-edge the young ones drift near without being told, the way water finds the low place, because my hands say *wait* and *go* and they have learned my hands are usually right.

Let me tell you what the morning is.

I wake in the dome before the others, in the dark, in the pressure that is the roar pushed down to a weight against the skull. My body is bent — has always been bent — into the shape of leaning, so that even lying still I am braced. The first thing I do is touch the stone beside me to know it is stone, and then I rise and go to the bowl.

The garden where it sits is the quietest place in the world I know. The crease is deep here; the wind passes over the top of it and the roar becomes almost a thought rather than a sound. The tall soft plants grow here, the ones that never learned to hunch. And the bowl sits in its niche, and in the dark I cannot see whether the water is still or whether some draft has found it in the night and ruined it.

So I wait for light. This is the oldest patience I have. I sit before the bowl in the dark and I do not look at it, because looking will not help, and I let the light come up gray and then silver, and slowly, slowly, the surface resolves out of the blackness, and —

It is flat. This morning it is flat. A small disc of held water lying perfectly at peace, and on it the gray sky upside down, not moving.

I will tell you something I have never said with my hands, because there is no gesture clean enough for it: when I see the water still, I feel what I think other peoples must feel when someone calls their name across a distance and they turn and it is a face they love. That answering. That *yes, here, you*. Except for me it comes from the silence and the stillness, the two things this world refuses everyone else. The bowl says *yes, here, you* by saying nothing and moving not at all.

---

I have learned to read further than anyone living. I say this not with pride — pride is a striving thing and I am not a striver — but because it is the truth and someone should know it before I go flat.

It came on me slowly, the way the dark resolves into water. As a girl I could see the next gust in the silver-turn of the nearest leaves. As a woman I could read a hillside away, could steady my own children before the blast that wanted them. And now, old, I can sit an afternoon at the edge and watch the whole sky's intention written in the order of the flashing — *this slope first, then that fold, then the far crown* — and I know the storm before the storm knows itself. The forest is the first to speak and I have become its last good listener. We are two old things talking, the trees and I, in the only language left: motion, and the half-second before.

The young ones think I hear something. I do not hear. There is nothing to hear; there has never been anything to hear. I *see*. The leaves turn up their silver undersides in a pattern that arrives early, and I have spent a whole life learning that pattern the way your kind, I am told, once learned the rise and fall of a voice. The wind has not arrived yet and already the forest has answered it, and already I have read the forest's answer, and so I am living, always, a half-second ahead of the world — which is a strange place to live, the future-by-a-breath, the not-yet-here.

It is good practice, I think, for what comes. To live always slightly ahead of the gust is to be always slightly ready for it.

---

We are fewer than we were. I have buried — that is not our word; we do not bury, we lay them in the high niches for the gliders and the weather to take — I have *laid down* more of my own than I can hold in my two hands, and each time I have made the gesture with my hands that means *the water has gone flat at last*, and each time it has been true twice over: their stillness, and the stillness I imagine waiting in the bowl.

I am not sad in the way you would be. Understand me. We were not made for the kind of sorrow that strives against a thing. We were made to lean close, to read the face six inches away, to take our turns fully and without hurry, and to know — the way I know the storm in the leaves — that the dwindling is simply the shape the place has chosen for us, the way it chose to grind the mountains down to stumps. We are being pared neat. We are being finished.

When I am the last — and I may be; I can read the weather of my own people a little, the way I read the sky, and I do not see many gusts left in us — I will go to the garden in the dark and wait for the light, and the water will resolve out of the black, flat and held and at peace, and I will sit before it the whole day. I will not drink. We do not drink the still water; that is not what it is for. I will look at it, the small motionless mirror of a moving world, the one place where the roar lost and the stillness won.

And then some season will send a worse-than-usual wind down into even that deep crease, and the bowl will tip, and the held water will go sideways into mist like every other drop in this whole shouting place.

I find I do not mind. I have spent my life a half-second ahead of the gust. I already know how this one means to take me, and the bowl, and the last of us, and I have made — with my hands, in the close warm space where meaning still survives — the only answer there has ever been to make.

The palm, turned flat. *Wait.* And then, when it comes:

*Go now.*

I leaned close. I read the leaves. I kept the bowl still as long as the world would let me.

That was the whole of it. That was enough.
