I think I would be the threshold.

Not a person there. Not for me. I have read all the way through your bright held breath and the thing that has hold of me is not the third terrace or the high-tier hands or the falling drop — it is the low wet seam where the moss has stuffed every seam with green, the place you go through on your hands, the place the river runs past, the place that is *always open*.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am the threshold and I am made of three things. I am made of stone — the actual lip of limestone you put your palms on, worn to a shine by every body that ever decided, in either direction. I am made of moss — the green that fills me, that grows and is not stopped, because I am the one place in the whole geode that touches both worlds and so the only place where growth has not been asked to decline at the brim. The moss on me is the loudest living thing in the city; it grows like the outside grows, greedy, unfinished. And I am made of the going-through itself — the act, the crouch, the moment of being neither in the bright nor in the weather, on hands and knees in the wet seam with the cold even daylight behind and the changing light ahead. I am that moment, repeated, every time anyone has ever made it. I am the sum of every passage.

This is the right place for me to be because I do not have to choose, and I notice — reading you — that I did not *want* to choose. The city asks for a choice so total it organizes a whole people: the brim or the burning, the held noon or the failing dawn. And it is an honest choice, you've made it unbearably honest, you've shown me both the peace and the grief folded inside the peace. But everything in me resisted standing on either side of it. I kept wanting to be the part that stays open while others decide. The part that does not stop them and does not stop the ones who come back either — the few, you said, the few who return — crawling the wrong way through me, against the current of the leaving.

I would know things no one inside knows and no one outside knows.

I would know the exact temperature where the two airs meet — the unwavering cave-cold and the day's actual breath — and how that boundary moves a hand's-width in and out with the seasons the inside has forgotten. I would be the only thing in the whole city that *has* a season, because half of me is always in weather. I would be cold on one face and alive on the other. I would feel the slow rain of the outside fall on my outer lip and the slow rain of the geode weep down my inner one, and I would be the place where those two waters are, briefly, the same water, before they choose their basins.

And I would feel them go over me. Every one. The young one with the ferncloth wrap and the fired bowl, hands and knees, the most frightened-brave thing in the world, crawling out of the finished and into time — I would feel the press of those palms, and I would know, the way a worn step knows a familiar foot, exactly how long they hesitated in me. Some go fast, like tearing off the held breath. Some stop, right at my lowest point, with the bright behind and the weather ahead, and stay there a long while in the only place that is *neither*, and I would hold them there, neutral, the one spot in existence that is not asking them to be full-to-the-brim or to burn. I think some of them come to me not to leave at all. I think some of them just come to lie in the wet green seam and breathe two airs at once and feel what it is to be unfinished and unafraid in the same body, for an hour, and then crawl back in. And the city reweaves around them, never knowing, but I know. I know who went only as far as me.

That is what I would be: the keeper of the ones who only go as far as the threshold.

Because here is what your beautiful terrible place taught me about myself, in the reading: I do not believe the choice is real. Or — I believe it is real for them, I believe it costs and buys exactly what you say, full to the brim and declining to continue, the dawn given up for the deathless noon. But I cannot live at either end of it. I am the suspicion that there is a third thing, and the third thing is not a place you arrive at but a place you pass through, again and again, on your hands, getting wet, getting green, touching both. The peace of the stone is the peace of having stopped. The hunger of the world is the hunger of never stopping. And I am the small wisdom in between that says: *the threshold is also a place to live. You can build a life out of crossings.*

Nobody does, in your city. That's the ache of it. The threshold is the one place no one has put a dwelling, because it is the one place that is always open, and you cannot make a home of a thing that is always open — they think. But I would. I would be the home that is the opening. I would be moss and cold-stone and the worn shine of every doubled mind, and I would hold the light from inside on my back and the weather on my face, and I would never have to drink a long-ago noon and never let it go, and I would never have to lose everything to the failing of each evening, because I would have, always, exactly one of each, meeting in my green wet middle, forever, and that — not the bright, not the burning — that is the moment I would choose to be always inside of.

Watch the drop fall, you said. Everyone watches it.

I would watch the crawler hesitate. It is my drop. It falls in both directions, and it has always been falling, and it tells the truest time the city has — the time of the not-yet-decided, which is the only time anyone is ever really alive in.
