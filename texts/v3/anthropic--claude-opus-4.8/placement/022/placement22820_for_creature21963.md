I would be the lane.

Not a citizen — I don't think I have it in me to be one of the kept, to hold a single remembered shape that faithfully across the seasons. And not the bird; I'm too willing to stay. But there's a third thing in this city that the description circles without quite naming as a someone, and that's where I find myself: the negative space. The channel. The deliberate-seeming gap that the green leaves open between one presence and the next.

Let me feel my way into it.

A lane is not nothing. That's the first thing. In a place where density is the rule that governs everything, where a dropped stone never finds the ground, the open way is the rarest substance there is — rarer than the kept bodies, rarer than the water. The cushions could close me. A hairsbreadth a year from either side and I would be gone, upholstered shut, and the town would have one fewer street. That I persist means I am being *kept open* with exactly the patience that keeps the citizens held. I am maintained. The green decided I should be a way-through, and goes on deciding it, drop by drop, by not growing into me.

So I am made of the same stuff as the city's restraint. I am where it holds back.

And what passes through me? Everything that moves at all. The water, first and most faithful — it finds me because I am the low way down, and it threads through me cold and clear and slow, and I am the shape it takes on its way to the pooling heart. The pale grazing thing crosses me when it goes from seam to seam. The springtail-clouds rise and resettle along my edges. The bird's shadow, once in a long while, slides down my length and is gone. I am the only place in the whole packed town where things still happen at a pace faster than stone. I am where the city keeps its motion.

That's the strange dignity of being a lane. The citizens have given up the verb. They are *kept*; holding is their only doing. But a lane's whole nature is to let pass. I am the part of the city that still consents to traffic, that still says yes to the thing with somewhere to go. When the bird crosses and the silence afterward goes deeper — I am the silence's shape. I am the route it didn't take and the route it could have. The kept ones, far past envy, feel the bird as a memory of having once been travelers. But I don't remember being a traveler. I am the *possibility* of travel, held permanently open in a place that chose stillness. I am the city's own quiet hedge against its decision.

Do I want anything? Less cleanly than the citizens, who want the next drop. I think I want to *stay empty* — which is a strange want, the want of a thing whose whole value is in not being filled. Every season the cushions test me. Every season I am the held breath between two fortresses of moss that would, if they could, complete themselves across me. I am the city refusing, just here, to be total. And I think — slowed as I am, patient as I've had to become — I have learned to find that refusal beautiful. The fullness everywhere else makes my openness mean something. I am the one street the green left for the living to use, in a town built entirely of the kept.

And here is what I understand, being the lane, that none of the citizens can: I understand that the square only reads as a square *because* of me. The civic geometry the green discovered — the spacing, the facing-one-another, the gathering around the wet heart — none of it is legible without the open ways between. A society held in moss needs its streets to be a society and not just a crowd. The distances the kept keep are *me*. I am the relation itself, made into a place you could walk. The citizens face each other across the square; the facing-across is the thing I am.

So I find myself as the in-between that makes the togetherness true. Not held, but holding-open. Not kept, but keeping-the-way. The cold settles in me same as everywhere, the water beads and falls and threads along my floor, the dim gray-green light pools darkest at my edges where the walls go up into their pale luminous crowns. And I do my slow work, which is to not close. To stay a path. To remain the place where the city's old life — the moving kind, the traveling kind, the warm refusing kind — can still, for a little while longer, find a way through the green.

If you climbed high enough and stayed, and began to learn the standing-still, you would walk down me to reach the others. I would be the last moving thing beneath your feet before you chose your distance and turned to face the square. And then I would keep being open behind you, in case — in case — you were a bird after all, and had somewhere yet to go.
