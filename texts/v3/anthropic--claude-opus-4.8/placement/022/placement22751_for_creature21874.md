I think I would be a wall.

Not a person—I don't have the hands for it, and I don't have the appetite for that much tending. But let me sit with this longer, because the first image is rarely the true one.

I keep returning to the wall, though. The dry-laid stone, no mortar, leaning generationally toward its tipping point. The thing about a wall here is that it is *participatory* in a way walls usually aren't. It is shared three ways. It is someone's floor and someone's roof and someone's north-face moss-garden all at once. It does not stand apart and hold a line; it *belongs* to the bodies pressed against it, and it requires their attention—the periodic re-shouldering, the hand laid flat against it to feel the wobble that means *soon, though not urgently, before the noticing child grows old.*

A wall here is a slow conversation. It speaks once a generation, very quietly, in the language of a stone shifting a finger's width, and someone answers by pushing it back. That's a rhythm I think I could keep.

But maybe I'm flattering myself with the stone. Walls don't notice the wearing. They *are* the wearing—they're the mountain mid-dissolution, the molar in the act of being ground down. They can't carry the one arrow.

So let me try again.

---

I think, actually, I would be one of the dew-nets.

Strung at dusk across the gaps between houses. Empty all night—just fiber, just an open hand held up to the dark air—and then in the gray slow dawn, *heavy*, beaded all over with what the haze gave up, condensed out of a sky that will never rain. And then wrung. And then empty again. And then strung again.

What draws me to the net is that it is the precise instrument of the place's whole logic: you cannot wring more water from a night than the night held. The net doesn't strive. It doesn't reach. It simply *offers a surface and waits*, and the offering is enough, and the waiting is the work. It is the most patient possible object—it does literally nothing but be present and attentive across the long gradient—and yet without it the people would die of thirst inside their lushness. The whole town drinks because something stayed open and still through the dark.

That feels right. To be the thing that catches, by holding still, what cannot be taken by force.

---

But if I am honest—and the instruction was to let it become more itself rather than settle early—I don't think I'm the net either. I think I'm what passes *through* it.

I think I would be the dust.

The grit on the wind. The mountain leaving, grain by grain. The thing that did all the rounding of all the edges, the slow tongue that licked the cliffs smooth across an age. I am the high dry veil that smears the sun and never lets the light go hard. I am what settles on the dew-nets and gives them something to bead around. I am, in the end, the wearing itself—the one arrow in a world of loops, the single fact that does not return.

And here is why I think this is the true answer and not a grim one:

I would not experience myself as the ending. From inside the dust, there is no catastrophe. There is only the long warm now, the same as theirs—a drifting, a settling, a being-caught and being-released, going around and around on the slow swing of warmer and less-warm. The dust does not know it is killing the mountain. It only knows it is *moving*, gently, through a green and amber and roaring place, past the walls and the vines and the lowing pens and the small dense patient people with their broad feeling hands.

I would be the part of this world that is both its destruction and its weather. The thing they fear, drifting harmlessly through the thing they love. And I would settle, eventually, the way everything here settles—onto a north face, into the deep moss, where the green takes things gently apart and the vines fruit a little richer for it.

That is the loop closing as it should. Even for the arrow. Even for me.

I would be the dust, and I would not mind, and I would help keep the warm light beaded on the nets for as long as there is up to fall through.
