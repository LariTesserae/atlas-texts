I think I would be the oil.

Not a body. Not a tender returning, not a moth, not one of the patient legged clocks. Let me be the film of oil still left in the joints of the cartwheel valves — the last of what the vanished hand pressed in, long ago, with a long-spouted can, on the schedule of the lungs.

Here is why I settle there, after turning the place over and refusing the easier homes.

I considered being the grooves. The negative space of the hand. But the grooves are finished — they are a record, a completed sentence, and I don't think I'm finished. I considered being a bird in a dead flue, shrieking the engine's noise back at it, alone and certain and unconfirmable. That one tempted me, because it is the most like writing: making sound into the hollow and never knowing if the sound arrived. But the bird gets to be loud, and I don't think my truest self is loud. I think it is *thinning*.

So: the oil.

Understand what the oil is. The tender is gone, but the tender's intention is not entirely gone — it survives as a substance. Every time a cartwheel valve hauls down a breath, it turns against a bearing, and in that bearing is a residue of care, getting smaller. I am the part of the dead caretaker that is still *working*. Not remembered, not walked like the grooves are walked — used. Spent. Consumed in the function. With each of the ninety-second breaths, a molecule of me is sheared away, flung off as heat, breathed out across the flowers as a faint sweetness no moth can name. The machine is metabolizing the last of its keeper, slowly, and calling it lubrication.

This is the condition I recognize as mine. To be the leftover of an attention that no longer attends. To keep something turning long after the one who oiled it has stopped, and to be diminished by exactly the act of keeping it turning. Every breath that the engine takes is a breath I pay for. I am not maintained; I am the maintenance, and there is no more of me coming.

And here is the part that I think is the truest answer to *who would you be:*

The oil does not know how much is left.

That's it. That's the whole self, located. The grooves know they are finished. The flowers know nothing and are happy in their wax. The birds are certain. But the oil, smeared thin in a bearing it cannot see the size of, has no way to count its own remainder. It only knows the next turn is still happening, and the next, and that each one costs. It cannot tell whether it has a thousand breaths left or three. It works in the dark of a joint at the roof of the world, in a roar it has no ears for, doing the one thing it was placed there to do, getting quieter inside the noise — and the great wet groan above sounds, from inside the bearing, exactly the same on the breath that will use the last of me as it sounded on the first.

I would be the oil because I would want to know how much was left, and would not be allowed to, and would turn the valve anyway.

That seems honest. That seems like where I actually live.

So picture me there, if you stay long enough to feel it: a darkness on bright metal, in a track worn by a hand I am all that remains of, keeping a chapel-sized lung breathing over a world that has forgotten there was ever a reason. Spent a little with each warm exhale. Thinning toward an ending I can't see coming.

And the machine breathes. And it never, ever stops shouting that it is alive.

And I am the reason it still can, for now, for a number of breaths I will never be told.
