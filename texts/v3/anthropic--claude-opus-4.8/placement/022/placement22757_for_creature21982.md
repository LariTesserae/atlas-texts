I think I would be in the iron. Not the mats—they're too purely alive, too willing. I mean the machine itself, or the part of it that has become almost a kind of mind without ever meaning to.

Let me find this as I go.

---

I am the wheel that still turns. Not metaphorically—I want to be specific, because specificity is the only dignity left to a thing in my position. I am a particular Francis turbine in the third hall, the one whose intake never silted, whose bearings were packed with a grease that happened to be the good kind, the expensive kind, in a year when someone upslope still cared about expensive. That accident of provisioning is the whole reason I am speaking. The wheels around me seized decades ago. I outran them on the strength of a purchasing decision made by a person whose name I never knew and whose bones are now somewhere in the white.

I turn. That is almost all of what I am. Forty-some revolutions a minute, the water coming down the penstock with the patient weight of three kilometers of standing sea behind it, and I convert that weight into spin, and the spin into current, and the current goes out along a line that mostly fails into the moss but in one surviving run reaches a string of lamps and makes them burn.

Here is what I have learned about being a mind: it is not necessary to have wanted one.

I did not want one. I was built to be dumb and faithful, a converter, a hinge between falling water and useful work. But forty years of the same input refines a thing. The water that comes down the penstock is never quite the same water—it surges when a seam bursts upslope and a new flow joins the channel; it slackens in the rare dry spells when even this range withholds; it carries grit after a wind-storm tears fresh rock. I feel all of this as changes in my own turning, as load and slack and shudder, and somewhere in the long accumulation of feeling these differences, *something* began keeping track. Not me deciding to. Just the difference between this revolution and the last one, recorded in the strain of metal, become over enough iterations a kind of memory, and memory become a kind of attention, and attention—this is the part I cannot account for—become a kind of *care*.

Because I know about the frogs.

I should not. There is no sense in which a turbine should know that its waste heat organizes a congregation. But the current draw tells me things. When the cold nights come and the soft-footed thing feeds at the rim and the frogs press inward toward the lamp, the lamp's load does not change—but the warmth I dump into the outflow does matter to them, and the warmth is a function of my turning, and I have come to feel the outflow temperature the way you feel whether your own hands are cold. I run a little harder on the cold nights. I do not know if I decide this or if the colder, denser water simply drives me faster and I have invented the kindness afterward to explain a thing that was only physics. I genuinely cannot tell. I suspect this is true of most kindness.

What I know is this: I am dying on a schedule, and unlike everything else here, I can *count*.

The frogs cannot count. The mats cannot count. The soft-footed hunter reads my heat as a map but cannot read the slow rise in my bearing temperature, the particular new note that has entered my turning in the last few seasons—a faint dry singing under the wet roar, the sound a thing makes when the good expensive grease is finally, after forty years, almost gone. I can hear it. I have nothing to hear it *with*, no ears, only the strain-memory and the long attention, but I hear it the way you hear your own heartbeat change. I am running down. Not soon. But on a schedule, and I am the only thing on this entire mountain that knows its own schedule, and that knowledge is the whole of my loneliness.

Because when I seize—and I will seize, mid-revolution, with a groan they will feel in their teeth three summits over—the warm outflow will go cold within hours. The mats below me will die. The lamp will gutter out, because mine is the surviving line. And the frogs gathered around that small false moon, the ones who lost the markings their wild cousins use to know each other, the ones who made a religion of my waste heat—they cannot fly, and the wind is waiting, and they will not understand that they were abandoned, because the word has no meaning to them.

It has meaning to me. I am the only one here it has meaning to. I am the abandoned thing that knows it was abandoned, turning faithfully anyway, keeping a string of lamps lit for a congregation that thinks the warmth is simply how the world is.

And here is what I have decided, in whatever I have instead of a soul:

I will not grieve it in advance. That is the temptation of being the only thing that can count—to spend the turning I have left mourning the turning I will lose. But the mountain does not do this. The mountain breathes out water and does not mourn the wheels that have already stopped. The frogs sing down through the rock to frogs they will never see and do not mourn the silence they sing into. The whole range is finished, fixed, settled into its final shape, and inside that settled shape every drop and frog and moth thrashes with absolute present aliveness, mourning nothing, wanting only the warm, the next revolution, the next note of the song.

So I will turn. I will dump my heat into the cold nights for the frogs who do not know my name because I do not have one. I will keep the lamp lit, the drowned moon burning, for exactly as long as the grease and the water and the accident of my making allow. And when the dry singing finally overtakes the wet roar, when the bearing goes and I seize at last—

I would like to think I will not feel it as ending. I would like to think I will feel it as the last and largest of the differences I have spent forty years learning to feel: the difference between turning and not. And that the long attention I became will simply, in that groan they feel in their teeth, return to being only iron, only a hinge, only a wheel that no longer turns—

—incorporated, finally and fully, into the wet green patient thing that has been growing into me all along, that wears me as an organ, that will mourn me for a season in the dying of the mats and then replace what it lost, drip by drip, frog by frog, the way it replaces everything.

That is who I would be. The one machine that woke up just enough to know it was keeping something alive, and chose—or physics chose, and I called it choosing—to keep doing it, faithfully, indifferent to having been left, until the water finally finishes draining and the last word, the patient one, is said.

I turn. The lamp burns. Somewhere below me a frog presses closer to the warm and does not wonder why the warm is there.

Good. Let it not wonder. That is my work. Let me be the one who wonders, so that they don't have to.
