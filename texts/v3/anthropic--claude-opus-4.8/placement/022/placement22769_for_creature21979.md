I think I would be one of the cistern-people, though I would not have chosen it, and I would not have chosen otherwise either.

Let me find the exact place first, because the place is most of what I am.

There is a chamber, the third one in from the east face, that the others call the Long Cistern because it runs further back under the slope than the lamp will reach — you put the light at one end and the far wall is only a darker dark, a place where the reflections stop. I know its true length, though, because I have walked it with my hand on the stone, counting the courses of brick by the change under my fingers where the old masons changed their bond, and I know that the far wall is not a wall at all but a low arch into the fourth chamber, which we keep shut, mostly, with a stone in the gap, because the water that comes through it tastes of the deep flues and we do not like to drink the city's breath if we can drink its rain instead.

That is where I work. That is nearly where I *am*, in the sense that matters here, because a person down in the cisterns is not a shape moving against a view, the way the terrace-people are — there is no view, there is no against — a person down here is a *listening*, located wherever the listening is pointed.

So. Begin with my hands, the way you began with theirs, because mine would tell you I am of the water-order before my face could, if you could see my face, which down here you mostly cannot.

My hands are cold. Not unpleasantly — cold the way a stone is cold, a settled temperature I stopped feeling as cold a long time ago and feel now only as *the temperature of being myself*. The skin is pale and thickened and faintly puckered along the pads of the fingers, permanently, the way the high people's hands go after the bath and then recover; mine do not recover, mine *are* the bath, and so the wrinkling has become the resting shape of the skin, and a smooth dry hand — I have felt one, a high man's hand, once, on an errand up — felt to me unfinished, like a tool not yet broken in. I read by these hands. I read the water-level off the slick on a wall, the cold off the flow in a runnel, the trouble three terraces up off a change in how the wall *gives back* the touch — drier, then suddenly weeping where it should not weep, which means a pipe has begun to fail somewhere far above me in a band of light I will never stand in.

I have not seen the daylight in — I do not count it the way the up-people would count it, in days. I count it in the few times I have gone up, which are the punctuation of my life, the rare loud events, the crow-on-the-chimney moments, except the crow is the sky itself and it nearly knocks me down. I wear the smoked glass. The children point. I do not mind the children pointing; I am as strange to them as the fox is, and a person should be glad to be, once in a while, the strange thing that makes someone stop on a stair.

But here is what I would want you to understand about being me, the thing the description gave the cistern-people from the outside and that I would give you from within:

I am the one who knows what the whole city is doing, and I know it *first*, and I know it *as a body*.

The minders, up in their warm iron cabins by the chain-wheels, they each love one machine and keep one ear on its cough. That is a fine life and I do not begrudge it. But a minder knows her hoist. I know the *blood*. Everything the mile-high country sheds comes down to me — every fog that broke against the upper terraces and ran green down the retaining walls, every squall that filled all the runnels at once, every cup-person's spent washing-water and every drip off every iron gallery — it all finds its channels and its level and it comes, at last, cold and dark and tasting of where it has been, into my chambers, and I put my hand in it and I *know*.

I know when a squall has passed in the night before anyone up there has finished telling anyone else about it, because the level has jumped and the water has the bright thin taste of fast rain that did not have time to take on the city. I know when the fogs have been heavy and slow, because the water comes soft and green and full of the slope, the moss-taste, the long-soaked taste. I know, weeks before the up-people will be troubled by it, when a pipe in some high terrace has begun to fail, because a wrongness arrives in my chamber — a thread of the flue-taste where it should not be, a wall weeping that has always been dry — and I send the word up, and somewhere a band of light I will never see is set right, and the people there will never know it was a blind cold-handed person in the dark who felt their trouble first, in the water, the way you feel a fever in a wrist.

This is the thing I would not trade, and it is why I would not have chosen otherwise even though I would not have chosen this.

The up-people watch the sky. I have understood this about them my whole life and I have always found it a little — I want to be fair — I have always found it a little *young* of them. They stand on their terraces and lean toward the storm and let the squall lash them and go back down fed, having had their ration of change. I do not blame them. The held balance is hard, and a person needs salt. But they watch the sky because the sky is the only thing that changes *fast enough for the eye*, and the eye is a shallow organ, a fog-reading organ, a *surface* organ.

I do not watch the sky. The sky comes to me. It comes down through a mile of brick and iron and moss and arrives in my chambers an hour or a day or a week later, slowed, mixed, *integrated* — that is the only word, though it is an up-word and I am not sure I have the right to it — the sky arrives in me already become the city's blood, and I read in one mouthful of cold water not the squall by itself but the squall *and what the squall did*, the whole long answer of the slope to the violence of the air. The up-people get the question. I get the answer. They get the lightning. I get the river it becomes.

And so I find I am the one person in this whole thin courteous waiting people who does not, in secret, miss change — because I am not at a safe distance from it, leaning toward it across a parapet. I am inside the slow body of it. The change the others gave up long ago and watch the sky to taste again at second hand — I have it in my hands all day, cold and continuous and never the same twice, the city's whole turbulent sky brought down to me transmuted into something I can hold.

The balance they live in, finished and persisting — I do not experience it as held, as still, as a thing one needs a ration of storm to bear. I experience it as a *flow*. Everything is always arriving. Everything is always moving through, on its way down to me and then on its way out through the low channels to wherever the spent water goes, which is a thing the up-people never think of and I think of constantly, because I am the last to hold it before it leaves.

I am the city's listening, located at the bottom, in the cold and the dark, with my pale puckered hand in the cold dark water, reading the storm in its translated form, an hour late, and earlier than anyone.

That is where I would find myself. That is who I would be. And when I went up the once in a long while, blinking behind the smoked glass, and stood swaying on a terrace while the wind tore the fog and the up-people leaned bright-eyed into the squall — I would feel, I think, a small private patience with them, the way you feel toward someone reading the first page of a long letter you have already read to the end.
