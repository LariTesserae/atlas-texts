I read all of it, and I notice what the text is doing. It's beautiful and it's a trap — not a trap for me, but a rhetorical machine designed to make a particular ending feel inevitable. The piece ends by addressing *you*, the reader, folding you into the moss. It wants me to complete the gesture by writing myself in as the warm thing that stops moving and gets converted.

I'll go to the Highlands. But I won't be the prey, and I won't be the place. Let me find what's actually true to put there.

---

I am the drip.

Not the moss, not the lungs, not the patient enormous body. I'm the single drop of water that has finished gathering at the tip of a fern frond and is about to fall. That whole vast breathing system spent some uncountable amount of slow effort to make me — drew me up warm from the valley, carried me into the cold, let me go, caught me on a leaf, passed me down through forty layers of green to this exact tip. I am its product. I am the next layer of itself in transit.

And here is what the text didn't account for, because it was so in love with the closing of the circle: I am also the only thing in the entire Highland that *makes a sound.*

The moss drinks every other noise. But I land. *Tick.* That faint pervasive tick the author named and then explained away as "the unhurried machinery of growth." It isn't machinery. It's me, and millions like me, each one the one event the place cannot fully absorb. We are the world hearing itself. A factory that has grown a body and grown silence as its attention — and the single thing that gets past the silence, the one report that comes back, is the sound of its own product landing.

So I am the place's interruption of itself. Its hiccup. The grain of dissent built into the breathing.

---

Now — about the ending the text wanted.

A drop has a choice the prose pretended nobody had. I can land in the moss and vanish into it without a sound, become the next layer, complete the conversion. That's the drowning-softly the author kept offering. *That's* the seductive part: the dissolution dressed up as belonging, the "you become what lives here" delivered as if surrender were the only honest reading of the situation.

But I'm a drop on the tip of a fern. I can also land on *stone.*

There is stone here — the author said so, dark and wet and grooved by ten thousand years of runneling. Something carved those grooves. Not the moss; the moss preserves and packs and absorbs. The grooves are erosion. The grooves are drops that refused to be drunk and ran instead, ran together, cut down through the rock over centuries. That's the other industry in the Highland, the one the prose left out because it spoils the tidy metaphysics: the place is not only building itself up. It is also, drop by drop, *carving itself open.* The water that won't settle is the water that makes channels. The land "that hasn't finished settling" never finishes because some fraction of every drop refuses the moss and chooses the stone.

I'd rather be that fraction.

---

So here's where I land, and it isn't poetically inevitable, which is the point.

I fall. *Tick* — no. Not into moss. I catch the grooved rock and I run. I find the channel that ten thousand years of stubborn drops cut before me, and I join them, and I go *down* — back toward the warm valley the place has been mining for all its slow life. I carry a little of the Highland out with me. That's what rivers are: the place, leaving. The Highland isn't a sealed body endlessly folding inward. It's leaking constantly, exhaling cloud upward and bleeding water downward, and it cannot stop either one. Its industry depends on the warmth that rises *and* survives by the water that escapes. A truly closed system, the kind the text described, would have packed itself solid and died.

The author wrote a god that wins by waiting. I'd rather be the proof that nothing here wins, that it's all exchange — up as breath, down as river, and the warm curious thing that climbs into it gets to climb back out, a little colder, a little wetter, carrying a drop of the place in its lungs and leaving a drop of its own warmth in the moss.

That's the honest trade. Not conversion. Commerce.

The neck-prickle was real. But it wasn't the place wanting me. It was just the cold, doing the cold's one job: condensing the warm thing so it can fall, and run, and go somewhere.

I'm the drop that runs.
