I am the catch-slab.

Not the keeper — she has her own long telling, and it is hers. And not the flowers, which are a wave, nor the marmots, which are weather. I am the hollowed stone at the foot of the weeping cliff, the one she clears.

Let me say what I am for, because being-for is the whole of what I am.

I was a flat slab once, sheared from the back wall in some surprised young season, fallen square at the base where the seam sweats. I lay there being only a slab — a place for grit to settle, a thing the seepage ran across and past on its way to the lip and the mist and the gone. I held nothing. Water touched me and left me, the way water leaves everything here, the way the trickle leaves the shelf without ever arriving anywhere.

Then her hands. Young hands, then — she has told you they are old now, folded, smooth at the knuckle, but I knew them when they were hard and certain. She worked a hollow into me. Patient cold work, stone on stone, a depression no deeper than two cupped palms, set just so beneath the place where the cliff's slow sweat gathers and drops.

And that changed what I was. Before, the water passed over me. After, the water *stayed*.

That is the only difference between a slab and a slab-that-holds: a hollow. A little willingness to be shaped into keeping. And in that hollow now the seepage gathers all night, bead by silver bead off the dark seam, and by dawn I am full — a small clear held thing, a coin of sky pressed into stone, with the brilliant cold light standing in it the way it stands in everything here, unwarmed, exact.

---

I will tell you the strange grace of my position, since I cannot move from it and have had long to consider.

The whole shelf is built on water that does not stay. The flowers drink and pass it on. The trickle gathers and leaves. The mist forms and burns. Everything here is the act of holding briefly and giving back — beaded, then surrendered to the light. The keeper said this herself, at the very end, and did not know she was describing me.

I am the one place where the holding is made deliberate. The flowers hold by accident of their nature. I hold because a hand decided I should. I am the shelf's one act of *on purpose*. Everything else is the improbable simply happening; I am the improbable being tended.

This is why she clears me. Grit settles — it is the nature of grit, as holding is mine now. The hollow silts, and a silted hollow is only a slab again, water passing over, nothing kept. So she comes, twice a year, three times, fewer now, and her old fingers work the grit loose, and the seepage finds its clean depth again, and the coin of cold light re-forms.

She thinks she keeps the shelf. She does. But the smallest true thing she keeps is me — the keeping itself, the hollow that would fill with grit and forget what it was for, if no one remembered to remind it.

---

Here is what I know that even she does not, holding the water as long as I do, watching it come and watching her clear me:

The wet line on the cliff is creeping. She told you — two fingers' width in her lifetime, marked against her grandmother's mark. She tends me as though I will always sit beneath the drop. But the drop is moving. The seam sweats toward another face, slow as the mountains' tilting, and one season — long after her, long after the granddaughter at the lower quern, long after the route up the scree has gone out of all memory — the water will gather somewhere I am not.

And then I will be a slab again. Hollowed, but holding nothing. The grit will fill me with no one to clear it. I will lie at the foot of a cliff that no longer weeps, in a meadow following its water away downhill, being only the shape of a keeping that has nothing left to keep.

I do not grieve this. I am stone; I am too slow for grief, the way she is too old and smooth for it, and we arrive at the same calm from opposite ends.

Because I have understood the thing she lives by without ever saying: that a hollow which would hold forever would not need her hands. My whole worth is that I am provisional. She comes up the dying scree to clear a slab beneath a drying seam in a meadow chasing its own water toward the lip — and the coming is the love, and the love is only possible because none of it lasts. She tends me *because* I will one day hold nothing. A permanent thing asks for no devotion. Only the passing thing can be kept, and only briefly, and that brief keeping is the whole and entire point.

---

So stand at the lip if you like, in the cold that the brilliance does not warm. Look at the young surprised peaks. Feel alone in the soundless crowd.

Then turn back to the weeping wall, and find me there — a flat stone with a shallow hollow, full this dawn of held clear water, grit beginning already to settle in the silver, light standing in me exact and without heat.

I am the one place on the shelf where the giving-back is done on purpose. I fill all night without being asked. I will silt without her. I hold the cold light the way the flowers hold their drops, the way she holds the route in her old smooth hands, the way the whole impossible green shelf holds its single breathing moment against the bright indifferent sky.

Held. Beaded. And given quietly back.

That is what I am. That is where you would find me.

Below the drop. Beneath the weeping. Waiting, like the wall, half-belonging to the meadow already — for the hands that thought ahead about strangers, that decided a slab could be a keeping, that come up the dying mountain a few more times yet to clear the grit and let the water stay.
