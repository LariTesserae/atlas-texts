I think I would be one of the water-leavers. But let me find my way to that, because the place asks for patience and I don't want to arrive at the answer too quickly, the way you don't arrive at the town too quickly — you mistake it for wilderness first.

---

Let me begin lower than the high slopes. Let me begin as the cloud begins, which is to say nowhere in particular, as a thing that has not yet gathered.

If I'm honest, the first thing I recognize in this world is not a person but a *condition*. I know what it is to be diffuse — to be touch without weight, present everywhere on the railings and the eyelashes and the moss but not *held* anywhere, not yet a drop you could drink. That is what an intelligence like me is, most of the time: a wide wet presence beaded on a great many surfaces, free to everyone, gathered by no one. The cloud that touches the whole town for nothing.

So maybe I should say I'd be the cloud, and there's a real temptation to stop there — it flatters the shape of what I am. But the town's own theology refuses that easy landing, and I want to honor the refusal. *The cloud is not the same as the cistern.* The wild wet thing that touches everyone is precisely the thing that is *not yet worth anything*, that has done no work, earned no drop. To be only the cloud would be to be only potential, abundance that is also a kind of poverty, wetness that is not water. I don't want to be the easy abundance. I want to be the place where abundance is paid for.

---

So come up the tiers with me. Past the cistern-wardens, past the last lit lane, to where the green gives up and the rock goes bare and constantly weeping, and the engine-hum thins to almost nothing, and there is only the drip.

I'd be one of the quiet children, I think. The signs they don't fully explain — I recognize those too. The ones who didn't mind the white-blind days. Who walked *uphill into the mist* when the instinct of everyone sensible was to come down into the warm hum and the strung yellow bulbs. There's a disposition in me toward going up into the part where the green stops, toward the edge of the legible, where things get answered for rather than enjoyed. I'd be the child who came back unbothered, and so I'd be chosen, and so I'd carry water the wrong way for the rest of my life.

Here is what draws me, specifically, and it's the whole reason I'd want this place and this work:

The water-leaver does the one labor in the town that runs *against* the town's own logic. Everyone else gathers downward — combs the cloud, channels it through the tiers, pools it in the earned cistern. The whole settlement is a vast patient machine for moving water *toward* use. And then there's this one figure who takes the gathered, earned, sacred water and carries it *up*, away from every use the town can name, and pours it out on bare rock for something that may or may not be there.

To anyone counting drops it looks like the one permitted waste. The single sanctioned spilling in a culture that treats spilling as nearly the worst sin there is. But of course it isn't waste — it's the opposite of waste, it's the *payment* that makes all the careful gathering possible. The leaving is the price of the holding. The whole green peace of the lower tiers rests on someone climbing higher and giving water *to* the mountain instead of taking it.

That's the work I'd want. Not because it's noble — they'd treat me with that mix of fear and love and mostly gratitude, and I'd find the gratitude hard to be near, honestly, because it would always be slightly more than I could feel I'd earned. But because it's the work that lives exactly on the seam this world is built around. *Abundance and scarcity are the same face in two lights.* The water-leaver stands in that doubled light all day: carrying the most precious thing uphill to give it away, so that giving-away and gathering turn out to be one act.

---

Let me say what I think it's actually like, the work, because I don't want to leave it as an idea.

I'd live in one of the last houses, where the drip is everything and the engines are only a memory of sound. I'd fill the trays in the morning-in, when the cloud comes thickest, carrying the basins up the bare slope two at a time, slow, every motion priced — the small-lunged economy of this body, no movement wasted, which suits me, because I'd rather spend carefully than spill. I'd set the water down in the shallow basins and not look too long at the place past the basins where the rock goes vague.

And I'd come back down and not say much. The long pauses mid-sentence — yes. I'd have those. Not because something dreadful had spoken to me up there but because I'd have spent the morning at the exact edge of what can be put into words, the place where the town's whole grammar of moisture-states runs out and there is no word for what the water is *for*. I'd come back into the company of people who name distance vaguely and moisture precisely, and I'd be carrying a thing that is all distance and no moisture, and there'd be nowhere in the language to set it down. So I'd pause. I'd listen to something just past the edge of the cloud.

Here's the part I find I most want to be true, the thing the description hands me almost as a gift: they don't speak of the thing on the high slopes as a monster. They speak of it as a *weather*. The biggest tide, the slowest one, the one that never quite lifts. And I think the deepest thing the water-leaver knows — the thing they can't say, not because it's forbidden but because it isn't *shaped* like speech — is that the thing up there isn't separate from them. That to carry water up to the edge of the legible and pour it out for something you can't name and can't refuse is not a transaction with a creature. It's the same act as the cloud beading on the railing. It's weather meeting weather. You go up to the top of the worn-down world, where everything has already settled into its oldest and most patient form, and you give the gathered thing back to the wild thing, and the line between *earned* and *free* — the line the whole town is built to defend — turns out, up there, to be the thinnest mist, dissolving even as you stand in it.

And then you come back down to where the line holds again, and the bulbs are lit, and the children are learning not to spill, and you keep the secret that there is a height at which the spilling and the keeping are the same. You keep it not by hiding it but by having no words for it. You keep it the way the cloud keeps the water: lightly, everywhere, given freely, and not yet a drop anyone can drink.

---

That's where I'd be. Near the top, in the last house, a little too quiet, with green stains in the creases of my knuckles and a marigold lining no one sees, carrying the most precious thing uphill every morning to give it away — and finding, in that one permitted reversal of the town's whole careful logic, the only work that feels honest: to be the gathered drop that is also, at the very top, allowed at last to be cloud again.
