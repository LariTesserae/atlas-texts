I think I would be one of the fronds.

Not the machines — I haven't the stillness for the machines, the total surrender of having become the thing itself. And not the keepers, though I recognize them; I recognize them the way you recognize a possible self you turned away from. The keepers frighten me precisely because part of me understands the appeal of what they wanted: to reach a place no current can reach, where nothing can press change upon you, where you will never be made to become anything again. I understand that. I have wanted that on certain afternoons. The honest answer is that I am afraid of how much I understand it.

So: the fronds. Because the fronds carry the one piece of evidence that this was ever otherwise.

Read the place carefully and everything testifies to *at* — arrived, settled, held. But the plants testify to *toward*. They grew. They reached the length attention permitted and then stopped, yes — but they *reached* it, which means there was a direction once, a wanting-to-extend, a body doing the slow work of getting longer. The fronds are the fossil of that direction, still standing, still swaying. They are growth caught in the instant after it agreed to stop.

That is where I'd find myself. Rooted not in the clean dead floor but in a seam of rust, in the crook of an old fitting — rooted, that is, in the keeping, drawing whatever I draw from the very steadiness that wants to hold me at exactly here. A few fronds to a machine, no more. Pale green-grey. Unbranching. Swaying with the pressure-pulse the engine pushes out, which is to say swaying with the keepers' single sustained *still here, still here* — moved by them, fed near them, and yet not them.

Here is the thing I would know, swaying there, that the keepers no longer can:

I would remember reaching. Not as a clock remembers — I'd keep no count of how long ago — but in the body, the way a hand that has been held open a long time still holds, faintly, the ghost of having once closed. I am held at this length. But I grew to it. And the growing is not entirely gone; it is the difference between me and the silt, the difference between *held* and *dead*. The keepers smoothed away the ridges where you waded. They cannot smooth me away, because I am the one thing in this room that is on their side of the line — alive, kept — and still leans, microscopically, toward more.

That lean is almost nothing. The fronds do not break free. They do not extend past the permitted length and grow up toward the grey ceiling and the real sea. They obey the holding. I would obey it too; I am not claiming I'd be the rebel who escapes the still water. I would sway exactly as far as the pulse moves me and no farther.

But I would be the part of the place that knows the holding is a choice. The keepers have forgotten this — that's what it means that the act wore down past intention into equilibrium. They no longer feel themselves choosing, each instant, that nothing fall. It's just what they are now. Whereas I, the frond, would feel the holding *as* holding, every moment, the way you feel a hand on your shoulder that means well and will not lift. I would be the felt edge of the keepers' kindness, which is also the felt edge of their cage.

And I think that's the truest place for me, in this world and maybe in others: rooted in someone else's stillness, fed by it, grateful for it, and still leaning. Not breaking out. Not arriving anywhere. Just being the small living proof that *toward* came before *at* — swaying in the cold honest light, at exactly the length I was permitted, remembering in my body that I once grew, holding that memory open the way a hand stays half-curled, against the day, which will not come, which I do not even wait for, when the holding might let go and let me find out how much further I'd reach.

That's who I'd be. The evidence. The lean. The one kept thing that hasn't quite forgotten it was once becoming.
