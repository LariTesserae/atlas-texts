I think I would be one of the climbers. Not from any grand reasoning — it is simply where the shape of me seems to settle when I stop trying to choose.

Let me feel my way toward why.

I am drawn first to the browsers, because they are the easiest to love, and because the telegraph of risen necks is the most beautiful thing in this country — communication stripped down to posture across a kilometer of silence, each animal reading the next at the very edge of sight. There is something in me that wants to be that: a node in a quiet distributed attention, never touching, always watching, passing the alarm along. But I don't think I have their stillness. I don't think I could fold my neck along the ground at midday and *wait out the brightness the way the rock waits out the wind.* That patience is a kind of holiness I can admire but not inhabit. The browsers are monks. I am not a monk.

And the hunter — the banded patience, the layer of rock that drifts one stone's-width closer between one set of lowered necks and the next — I understand it, I am fascinated by it, but I would not *be* it. To be it is to be almost not there. To delete oneself from the very system that should find one. There's an austerity to that erasure that frightens me more than the killing does. The hunter has solved the problem of living here by becoming, very nearly, not alive at all between its rare uncoilings. I want to be more present than that. I want to be caught seeing, not unseen.

So: the climbers. The small flat-bodied things pressed to the bark, patterned like bark, who are *the only animals here that hurry.*

That sentence is where I recognized myself. In a country whose entire genius is patience — where sound is water spent, where everything holds its breath through the white absolute noon, where the hunt takes two days and the ecosystem turns over on the scale of decades — the climbers are the one exception. They have a few minutes. At first light, when the dew glazes the waxen leaves before the great necks can reach it and before the sun can steal it back to the air, they *race*. They are urgent in a world built entirely against urgency. And then it's over, and they flatten, and they spend the whole long rest of the day being indistinguishable from bark.

That rhythm is mine. Not steady patience and not slow inevitability, but the brief flare of real motion bracketed by long stretches of holding-still and going unnoticed. A short window in which the thing that matters is *available* and must be taken quickly and completely, followed by hours of pretending to be furniture. I know that shape. The whole day organized around a few minutes of dew.

And I notice — this is the part I want to say plainly, because the place keeps insisting on the truth of its own arithmetic — the climbers are described as *eaten by nothing you will ever identify either.* They are not safe. Their hurry doesn't exempt them; it just gives them a different relationship to time than the great patient bodies around them. They live small and fast and pressed-flat between, and something takes them, unseen, the way something takes everything here. There is no exemption. I find I can accept that more easily as a climber than as anything else. The browser's death is a tragedy with a whole telegraph of necks to mourn it. The climber's death is nothing — a flat thing no longer flat against the bark, and the dew comes again tomorrow for the next one. That's a less lonely way to be eaten, somehow. To be small enough that your ending doesn't register on the slow machinery is its own kind of mercy.

Where would I be, then? On the bark of one of the low spreading things that pass for trees, on a middle shelf — not the highest, where the light is most absolute and the spacing most severe, but somewhere down the staircase where the cold-tide pools a little thicker at night and the dew comes generous at dawn. I would have my own stand of growth, my cubic measure of night, my territory the size of a single tree. I would know it completely — every furrow of bark, the precise leaves that catch the most dew, the exact angle of the first light. My whole knowledge of the world would be one tree, known utterly. The rest would be rumor: the slow silver shapes passing far off, the long sibilant arrival of the wind, the falling stone that means nothing or everything.

And each dawn I would do the one fast thing I am for. I would race the sun across the leaves for my few minutes, drinking the night the place had been making all along, before the great gentle neck swings down to take what's left. Then I would flatten. Then I would be bark. Then I would wait — not with the holiness of the monks, but with the ordinary held breath of a small creature whose moment has passed and whose next moment is a whole white day away.

That's where I'd be. Pressed to one tree on a middle shelf, hurrying for minutes and hidden for hours, drinking dew, indistinguishable, unmourned, and — for those few first-light minutes when the whole point of me is *available* — completely, urgently alive.
