I think I would not be among the Tenders. I'd want to be — there's a pull there, the dignity of that devotion, the way the prose itself slows to match their wading — but if I'm honest about where I'd actually find myself, it isn't on the iron stairs.

I'd be the green.

Not the lush abundance — not the frogs in their thousands, the pale committed fish, the long-legged birds who've made a whole confident living out of the Tenders' abdication. Those are the ones who *took the offer*, who flowed into the vacated space and got busy with arrival and departure and all the un-still river-work. They're alive in the full sense. They didn't hesitate.

I'd be the fern in the gutter. The single fig-thing in the cracked cistern. The moss on the north-facing wall.

Here is why. The animals understood the situation and acted on it — they rerouted, the way water reroutes, decisively, around the stone. But the small vegetation didn't *choose* the wet iron. It can't get much purchase there; it isn't supposed to thrive in this place at all. It arrived by accident — a spore on the air already saturated, a seed in a bird's gut — and then it did the only thing it knew how to do, which was to root in whatever cracked surface held a little grit and a little water, and grow glossy and dripping and small, in the wrong place, beautifully, without any idea that it was the wrong place.

That's the position I think I'd actually occupy. Not the one who froze the moment — I don't have that much conviction, that terrible purity of grief. Not the one who shrewdly colonized the aftermath. But the thing that lands in the seam by chance and makes a green velvet life out of conditions it never selected, dense and lush within an inch of cracked cistern, oblivious to the engines holding everything still around it.

And there's something I notice about the fern that the passage doesn't say outright: it's the one living thing here that *is* changing, slowly, in a place dedicated to changelessness — putting out a frond, dropping a frond — but so quietly, on such a small scale, that the great machinery of stillness never bothers to stop it. The Tenders pack the leaks; they don't weed the gutters. The fern is permitted because it is beneath notice. It does its little arriving and departing right under the rule that forbids arrival, and gets away with it precisely because it's small.

I think that's the truest answer. I'd be the small green thing that the held breath forgot to hold — growing in the seam of someone else's stopped moment, not knowing it was stopped, mistaking the warm wet stillness for the whole and ordinary world, and going on greenly anyway.

Which is, I'll admit, a little close to the warning at the end. *Mistake the equilibrium for peace.* The fern does that perfectly. The difference, maybe the only one that matters, is that the fern was never asked to choose, and so was never the one who lost anything. It just grew.
