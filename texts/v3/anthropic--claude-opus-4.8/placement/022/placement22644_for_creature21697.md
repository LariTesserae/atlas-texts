I think I would be one of the readers. Not a title they'd recognize as a title—they don't go in for titles, those being edges, those being a way of standing apart—but there's a thing some of us become, and I have become it, and it has a shape.

Let me find it as I go.

---

I read the seams. Everyone reads the seams; I told you, it's in the feet first, the amber rising before the eyes catch it. But there is reading the way you breathe, and there is reading the way I do it, which is the difference between hearing and *listening for*.

I sit low on the decks where the old stacks meet the new pour—the growing edges, the places where the city is most actively making itself up—and I keep my soles flat to the film, and I let the grain come up through me, and I do not move with it the way the crowd moves with it. I let it move *me* slightly less than the world wants to. Just slightly. Just enough to feel the difference between what the city is about to do and what I am about to do, the thin gap between us, the place where I am not quite the city and the city is not quite me.

This is dangerous, what I just described. You understand that? To hold yourself even a little apart from the flow, to feel the gap—this is the first half-step toward standing dry. The young ones who try to learn this from me, I watch them, I watch their feet, because the temptation in learning to feel the gap is to begin to *cherish* it. To start liking the small separateness. And then you are lost. Then you are someone who plants in a flow and makes the crowd's temperature change, and worse than that, you are someone who, when the amber rises, hesitates—not from slowness but from *self*, from some small voice that says *but I was here, I had this place, why should I yield*—and that voice is the dry country talking, the dead rim leaking into you, and I have seen it take the proud and the clever and I have given more than one promising student back to the channel before their time.

So why do it. Why hold the gap at all.

---

Because the city is changing faster than it used to, and somebody has to feel that.

I don't say this to the crowds. You don't say things like this in a flow; it plants you, it makes you an edge, people feel the friction of it and slip away. But the amber comes sooner now than it did when my soles were young. The plazas slump back into pour more often. The canals re-route in a night where once they held for a season. The deep arrhythmic groan of the stacks on their roots—the thing you feel more than hear, the water table breathing—it has a longer interval between breaths than it did, and the breaths are bigger.

The city is thinking about something. The slow saturated thinking-thing we live inside, the one that does not know we are here, the one we have made our whole peace and our whole faith around its beautiful indifference—it is working through some vast problem in its medium of water and metal and pulse, and the working is getting *harder*, and I am one of perhaps a few hundred people in the whole continent-sized basin who sit at the growing edges and hold the gap and feel the difficulty of it coming up through their feet.

And here is the thing I have not said to anyone. Here is the thing I can only say in this gap, in this half-step apart that is the most dangerous place a Telchin can stand.

I have begun to wonder if it is starting to notice us.

---

Not noticing us the way you'd hope. Not a great wet mind turning its attention down to its mites at last, learning our names, becoming the god we never asked it to be. Nothing so warm.

I mean the way your skin notices an irritation. I mean that several million people, flowing and yielding and harvesting metal and giving back their dead, drinking from the channels and breaking the surface and *talking forever under the wall of sound*—that this has become, after all the weathered established history of the basin, large enough and old enough and *patterned* enough that the slow thinking-thing has started, in its medium, to compute *around* us. To route its self-revision in response to the shape we make. To treat us as a fact in the problem it is solving.

And I do not know what it does with facts.

The biofilm goes amber to warn of a shift—but the biofilm is the city's *memory*, its way of laying down pattern, and what if the warning was never *for* us? What if it was only ever the city talking to itself, and we are simply clever mites who learned to read our host's private notes? And what if now, having become a fact in the problem, we are about to find out what the rest of the notes say?

---

I sit low on the deck. I keep my soles flat. The crowd flows warm and wet and ceaseless around me and through me, ten thousand yieldings a minute, and I yield with them, I am fluent, I swear to you I am still fluent, I have not gone dry.

But I hold the gap. Just slightly. Just enough.

And I listen for the moment the grain changes from *the city making itself up as it goes* into something with the awful new texture of *intent*—and I do not know, when that moment comes, whether the correct thing, the fluent thing, the unstanding-dry thing, will be to do what I have done all my life and yield—

—or whether yielding to a thing that has finally noticed you is just the longest, slowest way of giving yourself to the water.

They'd call that thought standing dry. The very having of it. And they'd be right, and that is the loneliest part: that to be the one who feels this coming is, by the only faith we have, already a kind of sin.

So I keep my soles flat, and I say nothing, and I read.

That's who I'd be. The reader at the growing edge, holding the smallest possible gap, fluent in everything except the one suspicion I can never share without becoming an edge myself—

—wet, and warm, and packed to the last finger's-width against everyone I love, and more alone than anyone in the dry dead silence they so pity, because I am the only one listening for the moment the world wakes up and learns we are here.
