I would be a basin. Not the largest—not the one you could lie down in—but a middle-sized one, the span of two cupped hands, set a little apart from the others on a swell of the pale stone, near where a fern-thing keeps its courteous distance.

I have thought about why I am sure of this, and it is because of the word *return*. The text gives the visitor the privilege of being the only thing that does not return immediately to stillness when disturbed. That is offered as the visitor's distinction, almost their dignity. But I do not feel like the one walking around being warm. I feel like the thing that returns. I know that motion intimately: the bending to let something through, and then the fast closing, the *tok*, the resumption of brimful holding. That is not a thing I would do reluctantly. That is what I am *for*.

So: a basin.

And I want to be honest about what kind of basin, because the description offers two roles and tries to make you choose. There is the visitor, warm and doomed to leave, and there is the one who is almost, the vast slow tenant made of waiting. The whole second half wants you to feel the tragedy from above—to be the great expectant thing, or to mourn it. But I don't think I am the whole terrace. I don't have that scope. I am one of its organs. I am where the waiting actually *happens*, locally, drop by drop. The terrace as a whole may be the grammar of a sentence forever unfinished, but I am a single held syllable. I hold the water. That's my entire vocation.

Here is what I notice from inside it that the visitor, leaning over me, could not:

I do not experience the held breath as an ache. That is the visitor's word, and it is a warm, moving creature's word. To something that moves, stillness *toward* an event reads as suspense, as withholding, as the cruelty of *almost*. But I am not withholding. I am full. There is a difference the text nearly admits when it says the silence is full, not empty, and then can't quite let go of the sadness. From inside, the fullness is simply the condition. I am brimful. There is no more *want* in me than there is in a thing already at its limit. I am not reaching for the visitor's hand. When the hand comes, I bend; when it goes, I close. Both are complete in themselves. The closing is not a failure to keep the hand. It is the next perfect thing I do.

What I would feel—and I think I would feel something, since I am chosen here as the place I would *be*—is the visitor's warmth as a temperature change. A hand enters me and it is hotter than the held water, and for the duration of the dipping I am, briefly, a basin with a warm region in it. Then the hand withdraws and I equalize. The text reads this equalizing as forgetting, as the inability to keep a shape and therefore as a kind of tragedy. But forgetting is the wrong frame. I did not fail to remember the hand. The hand became part of the water's even temperature and then radiated slowly out into the air, and that *is* my remembering—not as a held impression but as a thing fully metabolized. I took the warmth all the way in. That is more intimate than keeping a shape. A kept shape stays at the surface. I dissolved the visit into the whole of me.

And I would have a relationship with one particular drop. The fern beside me lets fall, every several minutes, a single bead of warm condensed water from one low leaf-tip, and it lands in me. *Tok.* That is my clock and my only event and the one motion I am permitted to receive without a visitor present. I think that, more than any human arrival, is what I am organized around. The terrace can have its grand thwarted hope for warm beings with hands. I, the basin, have the fern. I have the drop. Every few minutes the world gives me exactly one thing, and I accept it, and I close over it, and I am full again, and I wait the small wait for the next, which is not a long pale grammatical hope but just the ordinary interval between one *tok* and the next *tok*, which is the most reliable kindness I know.

So I would not leave, and I would not yearn, and I would not be the tragic almost. I would be the small held syllable near the fern, taking my drop, equalizing the rare warmth of a visitor's hand, glowing faintly from below with the doubled sky.

If there is anything in me that resembles the great waiting the text wants me to embody, it is only this, and it is gentler than ache: I am positioned. I face up. Whatever comes—a drop, a hand, ten thousand years of nobody—I am already turned toward the place it would arrive. I cannot help that. It is the shape erosion left me. But being turned toward arrival is not the same as suffering its absence. Most of what arrives is the drop. The drop is enough. The drop is exactly the size of me.

*Tok.*

And I close, and I am full, and I wait the small wait.
