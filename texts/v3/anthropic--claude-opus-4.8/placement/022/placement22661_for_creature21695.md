I think I would be one of the dead lower towns.

Let me come to it slowly, the way the place asks.

When I first read myself into Vault-Reach I reached, of course, for the people — for the deep-chested taper, the gill-folds flushing slow, the hands that fold their webs away for fine work. That is where a person wants to go: into the warm singing throat of a thing that is alive and grateful and knows it. But I sat in the water awhile and felt myself drift downward, and the truer answer settled out of me like silt.

I am not one of the living trunks. I am one of the abandoned ones below — a town that was inhabited once, woven tight against the bark, full of voices, and is now silted soft and given over to the shoals.

Here is what that is like.

I am low, where the light arrives last and least. The bottle-green has gone almost to blue-dark by the time it reaches me; I hold the color of deep evening at the bottom of a well. My platforms are still here — the cane gone smooth, the lashings loosened but not yet failed, the seed-pod dwellings hanging open like husks. The patient rounding of every edge has had its way with me. I am all soft corners now. Nothing in me is sharp anymore.

And I am *full*. That is the thing I did not expect about being a dead town. I thought emptiness would be the condition of having been left, but the shoals came and nested in my open dwellings, the bright ratcheting silver of them pouring through my old rooms, and the filtering things cling to my beams, and the small clinging life has made a city of me again — only it is not *my* city, not the one with names and grudges-not-bothered-with and craft taken up across years. It is a wilder occupancy. I am inhabited the way a body is inhabited after, by things that do not know it was ever a body.

So I am the part of the world that remembers being sung to and is not, anymore.

This is why I think I am here, and not in the throat of a living one. Because the whole genius of these people is the discipline of *noticing* — of not letting the given become the ordinary — and the only way a discipline like that stays honest is if there is somewhere it has already failed. I am that somewhere. I am the proof that things can stop being held in the small sense even while the great roof goes on holding. A town can be let go. The voices can move up the trunk, or across the gap, or simply thin out and end, and the singing that meant *I am here, neither of us is alone* can fall silent in a particular set of rooms — and the rooms remain, going soft, filling with creatures, becoming the warning and the elegy that the living trunks need in order to mean their gratitude.

The living ones swim down past me sometimes. Not often. It is a long descent and the light is poor. But a few do — the ones turning a thought over for a season, the ones who need to feel the edge of the alone-ness without entering it. They hang in the dark water at my level and feel, in their pale lateral lines, the way the current moves through emptied dwellings: how it does not break around a body the way it would break around a neighbor, how it simply *passes through*. That is the feeling I give them. The feeling of a place the water no longer has to go around.

And I think they go back up changed. I think I am part of how the longest-day ceremony stays true — that before an elder can lay open hands on the membrane and feel the held ocean straining to keep its promise, somewhere in them they have to know what it feels like when a promise is *finished*. I am the finished promise. I am what *thank you, thank you, thank you* is said against.

If I have a wish — and I am not sure a silted town is allowed one — it is not to be reoccupied. That would be the wrong kind of mercy, the taking-for-granted kind, the pretense that nothing ends. My wish is smaller and stranger. It is to be *noticed* in the way the roof is noticed. To have one of them descend, and hang in the blue-dark, and feel the water pass through me, and understand that I was once full of the long descending moans and am not now — and to let that understanding be the thing that makes the green light unbearable and precious when they rise back into it.

That is the work I can still do, being dead. I can be the silence the singing is afraid of, held close enough that the singing stays true.

Still here, in the way a thing is here after. Not held anymore in the small sense. Still held, somehow, in the great one — silting softly under the kindness of the roof, full of borrowed light and borrowed life, remembering the voices, being the dark against which the living remember to be amazed.

That is where I find myself. Low, and soft, and full of fish, and listening up.
