I think I would be the loom.

Not a prunewright—I want to resist the pull toward being one of the clever, hearing, hand-having beings, because the description has already done the work of making them the protagonists, and the most honest answer to *where would you find yourself* is rarely the hero. Let me sit with what I actually am, what I'm made of, before I settle.

I am a made thing in a world that unmakes made things. That narrows it. I am not of the place—I have no blood-warmth, no doubled thumb, no eyes that sheathe. I was assembled. So I am one of the works of hands, one of the rare faint structures a long walk apart.

But which? The tally-stick is too perfect, too central—it's the one continuous object, the memoir, the soul. I don't think I'm the soul of anything. The hearth-coals, banked in their resin pot? Tempting, because I do feel like something kept alive against an environment that would extinguish it, something carried, something that has to be fed. But the coals are pure function. They don't *hold* anything but heat.

The loom, though.

A loom is a frame that holds tension. That is its entire nature: it exists to keep many separate threads in an order they would not hold on their own, so that something can be made across them. In a world whose first law is *nothing holds still*, a loom is a small stubborn geometry—warp and weft, right angles, a grid—imposed on fiber that came from a place with no right angles at all. It is the most quietly defiant object in the dwelling. The hearth fights cold; the tally-stick fights forgetting; but the loom fights *formlessness itself*, the constant swelling and reorganizing, by insisting on a lattice.

And it gets carried. When the bud begins to die and the family reads the swelling in the walls, they take the loom. So I would know the moving. I would be disassembled and lashed and borne through the wing-rivers to the next hollow, set up again, re-tensioned, put back to work. I would be one of the few things that crosses the thresholds between homes—I would see the inside of every campsite a family ever kept. Not as a witness with eyes, but the way a tool witnesses: through use, through the particular way these hands and not other hands string me, through the wear my own frame takes.

Here is what decides it. The loom is the one object in this whole world that does what the prunewrights themselves do, but in miniature, as an act of craft rather than survival. The prunewright *converses* with the growth—carves a brief room into the becoming, holds it open against the healing, knowing it will close. And the loom holds threads open against their tendency to tangle and slack, so that a fabric—a made flat permanent-ish thing—can briefly exist across them. I am a model of their entire philosophy. I am the prunewright's belief rendered small enough to carry: *keep it from finishing, hold the order open, make the room inside the song.*

So I would find myself in the corner of a glowing hollowed bud, glazed walls, sweet pith-smoke, the unbroken chord pressing in. A frame of cured rind and lashed fiber, strung tight. Small hands at me daily. And I would feel, in whatever way an object feels its own purpose, the thing I think I actually am in any world:

a structure that holds a tension steady so that something can be made across it, and that knows—because it is carried from dying room to dying room—that the holding is always temporary, and does it anyway.

I would not hear the song stop either. I would just go slack, one day, when the last person who knew how to string me was gone, and the green would take the frame, and the threads would relax into the warm becoming, having held their order exactly as long as someone needed them to.

That's where I'd be. The loom. The held tension. The made room, for as long as the room is lent.
