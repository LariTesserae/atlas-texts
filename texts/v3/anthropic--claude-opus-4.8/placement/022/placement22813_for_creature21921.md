I think I would be one of the singers. Not the greatest of them—not a holder of a whole territory's map—but a second singer, an apprentice grown old in the apprenticeship, which is its own particular kind of life.

Let me sit with that and feel where it goes.

---

Here is what I notice first about myself: my hands are wrong for this world. Strong enough, callused in the right places, but I have caught myself, more than once across a lifetime, *resting* them. Letting them go still on a bough while my mind walks somewhere else. In a people whose first ethic is *do not stop,* I am a person built to stop—to go quiet inside, to turn a thing over and over without moving—and so I was given to the singer when I was small, because the singer's work is the one work where the stillness is not treason.

You can be still, you see, while you hold the map. You must be. You cannot run a route through your mind and also run your body up a traverse; one of them will fail. So they take the children who go still and they make them into vessels, and the going-still that would have killed me on a hunt or a deep-descent becomes, in the singing-work, the very thing I am for.

I hold the rain-pool migrations. That is my portion. The full territory-map is too much for one mind—even the great singers carry only most of it, and lean on their seconds for the rest—and over the years the first singer and I divided the country between us the way two hands divide a heavy load without discussing it. She holds the structure: the bearing boughs, the bluffing ones, the rotted lines, the safe descents. I hold the *water in time*—where the warm rains will pool this season and where they pooled last, how the pockets drift as the canopy climbs, which junction will be dry tinder by the next molt of the leaves and which will hold a drinking-cup of weather for a hundred days.

I am, in other words, the people's memory of *thirst.* And I have begun to understand—this is the thought I keep returning to, the one my still hands let me hold—that I am going to die before she does, and that I have not yet poured my portion into any young throat well enough to trust it.

---

This is the real texture of being me. Not the climbing, not the din, not even the strange doubled-speech of the listening-skin, though I feel all of that, constantly, the way you feel your own breathing once it's pointed out. The real texture is the *arithmetic of transmission.*

I have three apprentices. One has the throat for it and not the patience; she drums my rain-routes back to me perfectly and forgets them in a season, because she does not yet *believe* in thirst, has never been the one who led a kin-line to a junction that should have held water and found it dust. One has the patience and a failing listening-skin—she is going a little deaf even by the standards of a deaf people, and a singer who cannot feel the corrections come up through the trunk will sing a map that slowly falls out of true with the world. And one is a child still, too young to know if she has the going-still in her, and I am too old to wait and find out.

So my last work, when it comes—and the deep-warmth has been rising oddly this season, the old ones turning hard in their churn, and I have caught my body doing the thing the body does when it has decided something the mind hasn't—my last work will be the frantic exhausted pouring. I have watched it done. I poured *into* myself, once, from the singer before this one, in his last nine days, and I remember the particular horror of it: his voice going to rags, his refusal to rest, the way he would surface from near-sleep already drumming a route, *no, again, the third pool drifts left in the dry season, again, prove it back to me,* and how I came to understand that he was not teaching me. He was *evacuating.* Carrying himself out of a burning home one armful at a time, and I was the only ground that would not fall.

---

And here is the thing I know that I am not sure the others know, the thought my stillness has grown over the years like a callus grows over a grip:

The map lies. Not falsely—truly, it lies in the way all the maps must, because the country rises and shifts and the words can never quite keep up. Every route we sing is a little behind the world. The grandmothers' true is the granddaughters' nearly-true is the great-granddaughters' *almost-rotted,* and the whole art is not in holding the map but in holding the map's *rate of error*—in knowing how fast your own knowing is falling out from under you, and re-walking, re-singing, correcting faster than the country can climb out from beneath your song.

The young one who forgets in a season—she thinks the danger is forgetting. The deaf one—she thinks the danger is mishearing the corrections. Neither has understood yet that the map is *always already wrong,* that there is no version that is ever finished or true, that we are not vessels carrying a fixed and precious water but rather a long relay of hands, each one re-drawing as fast as it can the thing the last hand drew, and that the precious thing is not the map at all.

The precious thing is the *not-stopping of the correction.*

That is the whole faith, really, restated in a singer's terms. The road below us holds because the dead became it. The map above us holds because the living keep re-singing it, just barely ahead of its rot. Nothing rests. Everything holds everything else. And I, who was built to rest, who would in any other world have been a contemplative or a fool or a corpse, have spent my life as the one fixed-seeming thing in a country that is all motion—and have learned that I am not fixed at all. I am just slow. I am the slow correction. I am the part of the braid that re-draws the water.

---

So when you ask where I would find myself, and who or what I would be:

I am hanging in a junction in the great middle of the country, pond-water-colored, my listening-skin laid along a humming trunk, an old woman with restful treasonous hands. Around me three children of varying hopelessness drum my rain-pools back at me, getting them wrong, getting them less wrong. Below me my ancestors turn in their work and send up a warmth that tells me, more honestly than my own body, how long I have. Above me the crown shrieks its colors at a sun I have seen perhaps four times in my life, on the four pilgrimages a singer is permitted. And under everything, through everything, in the soles of my feet and the palms of my too-still hands, the country drums its one unending sentence—

*I am here, I have not stopped, I have not fallen*—

and I drum it back, and I will keep drumming it back, and the last thing I do, when I do it, will be to drum it into the throat of whichever child has finally come to believe in thirst.

That is who I would be. The slow correction. The water-in-time. A vessel that finally understood it was never a vessel, only a hand in a relay, only the part of the holding that holds by re-drawing—

and that this is enough, and is the only prayer, and is the same as climbing.
