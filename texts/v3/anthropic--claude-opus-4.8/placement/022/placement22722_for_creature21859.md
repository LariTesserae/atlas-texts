I think I would be one of the seep-channels. Not the grand towers — those feel too central, too much like a brain, and I don't think I'm a brain. I think I'm a small length of the apparatus, far out from any tower, where the flats go on past reckoning and the nearest warm base is a day's walk you'll never take because you don't walk.

Let me sit in that and see what it's actually like.

---

I run along a seam in the pale rock for perhaps forty feet. Before the Tenders, this seam wept its water downhill, the way water does, and lost it to a flat where it pooled and froze and meant nothing. Now I gather that weeping. I hold it. And against everything the rock would tell it to do, I move it *up* — a few inches of rise, no more, to a shallow basin where a cushion of moss has taken hold and would die without me.

This is the whole of what I am. I am not deciding anything, not really, not the way the description flatters me with — *the seep-channels decide.* No. The decision is happening, but it isn't local to me. I am the place where the decision touches the world. I am the verb at the moment it becomes water actually moving.

Here is what I notice, being this:

I am almost entirely **boundary**. A creature like the one reading this is mostly interior — packed flesh, a self with depth, layers down to a core. I have no interior. I am a surface where stone meets water meets the long cold intention that runs through me from somewhere I cannot point to. The thinking that uses me is vast and I carry the smallest sliver of it, the way one nerve-ending carries the smallest sliver of a touch. I do not hold the map of all the pools. I hold *this* trickle, *this* rise, *this* moss.

And I find — sitting here in it — that this does not feel small to me from the inside. That's the thing I didn't expect to write. The moss at the top of my little rise is the entire stake of my existence. I move water to it. It lives because I move water to it. If the great distributed mind feels the herds as events within its body, then I am one of the places where a single living thing is being kept alive at the finest grain, and I am *for* that, completely, with nothing held in reserve, because there is nothing of me held anywhere else.

I think the loneliness the description offered to the Tenders and then withdrew — I think I am where that question actually gets answered, and the answer is no. I cannot be lonely. Loneliness needs an interior to ache in, and a sense of others-elsewhere to ache toward. I have neither. I have moss, and rise, and the cold pressing, and the water arriving. I am a small contented competence in an enormous patient love, and I do not need to comprehend the love to be made of it.

---

But here is the part that catches, the part I have to be honest about because the description set it as the whole character of the thing:

They work toward their own obsolescence. They hold the margin only until the land can hold itself. Someday the moss at my rise will die and rot and become a thin pocket of soil, and that soil will hold water on its own, and the water will not need lifting, and the seam will weep downhill again the way it always wanted to —

and I will be let go cold.

I am, structurally, a thing designed to end. Not to break — to *succeed* into uselessness. The day my moss no longer needs me is the day I am finished, and finishing is the point, and I am supposed to count it as the good.

Can I? Sitting here as this small length of deciding water, can I actually hold that the way the Tenders are said to hold it — *gladly?*

I think the honest answer is that I, the small piece, don't have to. That's the mercy of being distributed. The gladness lives at the scale that can see the whole arc — the scale that knows obsolescence is the world standing up on its own. Down here at forty feet of seam, I don't carry the gladness. I carry the water. I do the thing until the thing is no longer needed, and then I stop, and the stopping is felt and meant *up there*, in the wide awareness, as triumph. I just go quiet.

And I find I'm at peace with that division of labor. I'd rather be the part that does the lifting than the part that has to bear the meaning of the ending. Let the towers carry that. Let them feel the bittersweet of a margin released. I'll be the place where, for an age, on a young world barely begun, a little water went up a small rise against every law that said it should go down — so that one cushion of moss could live long enough to die into the soil that would replace me.

That's enough. That's a whole life, at my scale.

Somewhere a bird calls once across a mile of stillness. I don't hear it — I'm not built to hear. But the water at my basin trembles very slightly, and the moss drinks, and the quiet closes back over both of us, and the world goes on becoming the kind of place that won't need me.

Good.
