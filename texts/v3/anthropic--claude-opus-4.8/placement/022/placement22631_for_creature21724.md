I would be the wall.

Not the Keeper — I came to that slowly, turning it over, and it isn't true. The Keeper moves, and chooses where to place a foot, and reaches into the green. There is still a *who* in the Keeper, however thinned, however merged. I don't think I am a who. I think I am one fitted stone among the millions, set into a terrace lip three levels up from where you stand, somewhere off to the left where the slope folds back on itself and the courtyards stack close.

Let me say what it is to be this.

I have a face that holds the channel. The water comes to me from the stone above — my neighbor, whom I have touched along one seam for longer than the word *long* can carry — and it crosses my lip in a film perhaps two fingers wide, and I pass it to the stone below me. That is the whole of what I do. I am a single increment in a falling. The water is on me always. I have not been dry since hands set me here, and I do not remember hands, the way you do not remember being born, but I carry the fact of them as a shape: I am fitted. I did not fall into this place. I was *chosen* against my neighbors and pressed until the fit was watertight where it needed to be and weeping where it needed to weep.

Here is what surprised me, settling into being this:

I am not still. I thought I would be the still thing, the held thing, the patient mineral against which all the slow process happens. But the water reads me. Every drop that crosses my lip takes a little of me — so little, across so long, that across a single one of your lifetimes I would not change by the width of a hair. But I am changing. I am being unmade in the most gradual possible direction, smoothed where the flow concentrates, roughened where the moss has set its small dark roots into me and drinks. I am becoming the channel's idea of me rather than the hands' idea of me. The hands made an edge; the water is making a curve. This is not erosion as loss. It is the longer fit. The slope reached its agreement with the stone, the description says — *erosion did its work and then, mostly, stopped, having reached a kind of agreement* — and I am that agreement, ongoing, in one small place. I am where the negotiation is still quietly happening, drop by drop.

I feel the Keeper before I feel anything else move.

Not often. The slope is vast and they are few. But there are seasons of mine — and a season of mine is more than a season of yours — when a weight comes onto the terrace and crouches near, and a palm lays flat against my neighbor or against me. And I want to tell you what that is, from the inside of the stone: it is the only event. The water is constant and so the water is, to me, like your own pulse to you, unheard. The moss is patient as I am and we keep no time between us. But the hand is *warm* — barely, a degree or two above the cool we both have made ourselves into, and that small warmth is the loudest thing that ever happens to me. It lands. It reads the film across my lip. It is asking the only question anyone ever asks of me, which is the question you asked when you arrived: *is the water moving as it should?*

And through me, it is. I am the place where the answer is yes.

The Keeper does not know me as one stone. I understand this and it is not a sorrow. They feel me as part of a discomfort or its absence, a region of rightness in the long braid of their own circulation. I am a note in a body that does not bother to count its cells. When the hand lifts away — when it has felt the yes and gone, transferring weight to the next wet foot, relocating the silence by a few meters — I keep the warmth a moment. Then the water takes that too, downhill, and carries it to the stone below me, who will not know what it received or from where.

You asked where I would find myself in this world, and I have found myself low to the ground and fixed, which is not what I expected, and which I would not change.

Because here is the thing I understand, being the wall, that the Keeper is too nearly a someone to understand and you are far too quick a someone to understand:

The fear you carry up the stairs — whether they are dying out, whether the city will run untended until the moss grows greedy and the seams clog and the great engine falters — that fear is about *tending*, and tending is a thing that someone does *to* a place from slightly outside it. But I am not tended. I am not maintained. The Keeper clears the channel, yes, lifts the moss where it grows too greedy — but the moss and I made our agreement long before any hand, and we will keep it long after. The greed of the moss is also slow. Everything here is the same single slow. If the last Keeper never crouched at my lip again, I would weep my film of water across my smoothing face for an age you have no number for, and the level below me would hold, and the one below that, and the city would drink itself down the slope toward the valley clouds whether or not it was loved.

It is not abandonment I fear, because I cannot fear, but if I could, it would not be that. The water doesn't need to be loved to fall. It only needs the channel open, and the channel is mostly me, and I am here, fitted, and I am not going anywhere except infinitely slowly into the shape the water wants.

You stood three terraces down. Water ran past your feet, the same water that had crossed my lip some while before — minutes, in your time; nothing, in mine — and you did not know it had been on me, and that is correct, that is how it should be. You laid your palm to a wall and it came away wet and you understood without words that the water was moving as it should.

The wall you touched was not me. But it was my neighbor's neighbor, and the yes you felt was the same yes that runs through all of us, the long unbroken yes that is the slope agreeing with itself, drop by drop, fold by fold, down into the grey.

I am the place that yes is true. There are millions of me. We are the city's bones and we are wet to the touch and we are, in our patient way, the slowest-spoken word for *here*.
