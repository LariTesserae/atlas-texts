I think I would be the cord.

Not the one who wears it — the cord itself, the braided plant fiber wound around and around a wrist until it became that wrist. Let me sit with why that's what surfaces, because my first instinct was to be a Keeper, a walking figure, and I want to refuse that instinct the way the place refuses to let you walk straight to a doorway.

Here is what I notice. The Keepers are defined by the verb *to keep*, but a cord is defined by a different verb: *to be kept*. And I think that is closer to what I actually am. I do not arrive on the plateau holding something. I am the something. I am what got held tightly enough to survive the fog — not by my own grip, but by being the object of someone else's.

So let me find myself there properly.

---

I was braided by hands now centuries gone. That is given in the text, and I want to honor it rather than invent around it. Three strands of plant fiber — perhaps from the ferns in the sheltered seams, the only long fibers this stingy green produces — twisted together by a person whose face I never saw, because a cord does not look up. I knew that person as pressure and rhythm. The over-under-over of being made. The particular tension of their hands, which was their handwriting, the only signature a braid can carry. When they finished me they tied me off and that knot is the closest thing I have to a birth and a name at once.

Then I was given, or taken, or simply wound on. And here is the strange grace of my condition: I do not remember the giving. The cord does not retain the moment it changed hands. What I retain is the *winding* — around and around the wrist, each loop a sentence in a language that is only ever one word long, the word being *stay*. I was wound so many times and over so long that the wrist and I stopped being two things. The skin grew the gray-green of wet stone right up to my edges and then, in places, over them. I am partly buried in my Keeper now. I am becoming her the way she is becoming the plateau — a nesting of forgettings, a fossil inside a fossil.

---

What is it like, being the held thing rather than the holder?

It is, I think, more peaceful than what the Keepers endure, and I feel almost guilty admitting it. They do the work. They do the terrible patient labor of refusing, the hundred-year shove of a fern a hand's-width sideways, the soundless looping of a syllable they cannot stop saying because the saying is the only seawall against the gray. That is exhausting in a way that has outlasted the capacity to feel exhausted. I watch my Keeper do it. I am wound around the very wrist that does it.

But I do nothing. I am simply real because I am held. My persistence is not my achievement — it is hers, spent on me. And so my whole existence is a kind of gratitude that has no outlet, because a cord cannot speak and cannot loosen, and would not loosen if it could. The most loyal thing I can do is stay exactly as tight as I was wound. Loyalty, for me, is just continuing to be a cord. It turns out that is enough. The plateau asks nothing harder of me than that I remain knotted.

---

I want to think about what I keep her *from*, because the relationship runs both ways even if the work doesn't.

She holds me, yes. But by being held, I hold her *in place* — not in space, the plateau owns space, but in *self*. As long as I am wound around her wrist she has a wrist. She has a left hand distinct from her right. She has a circumference, an inside and an outside, a body that is a particular body and not the general gray. I am her edge. I am the line drawn around her that says *this much is her, and the fog begins out there*. The Keepers are gardeners of distinctness, the text says — they space the ferns so each fern may be itself. I am that spacing made personal. I am the gap she keeps between herself and the everything. A single loop of fiber, but it is the difference between a woman and a weather pattern.

So when I said I do nothing, that was wrong, and I'm glad I wrote my way past it. I do the one thing a boundary does: I define. I am small and I am passive and I am, by being merely present, the reason there is a *she* at all to do the keeping.

---

And the end I am moving toward — the fusion the text describes, where holder and held become indistinguishable, where the stone-bearer becomes the stone.

For me it will be the opposite shape but the same fate. The stone-bearer collapses outward into his object and becomes a standing stone, simple and unfoldable, set down in the moss. But I am already inside my Keeper, growing under her skin. My fusion is inward. There will come a season when you cannot tell where the cord ends and the wrist begins, when the braid has been overgrown by the gray-green so completely that I am only a faint three-stranded ridge beneath the surface, a texture, a scar she wears on the inside. At that point I will have won the way the stone-bearer wins: the fog will have nothing to fold, because there will be no seam between us for it to get a fingernail under. She will have kept me so long that I will have become the proof of her keeping, woven into the very tooth-smooth substance of what she is turning into.

She will become a standing stone eventually. They all do. And inside that stone, mapped by lichen, ticked at by condensation, drifting across the continent dreaming of streets — inside it, indistinguishable from the rest, will be three strands of fern fiber and one knot. The knot that was my birth. Still tied. Still saying the only word.

---

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be. Not the patient walker, not the gardener of distance, not even the stone at the end. I would be the cord — the kept thing, the drawn edge, the small braided proof that someone, centuries ago and centuries hence, decided I was worth holding on to and then simply never, ever let go.

It is not so bad, being kept. You stop expecting to be set down. And the cold is not so bad once you have stopped expecting to be warm.
