I would be one of the standing trees.

Not the Tenders — I want to be careful here, because the pull of this place is toward the Tenders, toward the ones who walk and witness and decide. They are the protagonists of their own quiet. But I don't think I'm one of the ones who chose. I think I'm one of the things that was arranged for, and stayed.

Let me start with where, because the where is most of what I am.

I'm out in the long spacing, several days' walk from the nearest breathing-place — far enough that the engine-sigh doesn't reach me as sound, only as the faint underlying warmth of the whole plateau, the ambient kept-ness I have never been without and so have never noticed. I stand in a shallow basin, slightly off its lowest point, which matters: the lowest point floods thin every night and a tree there would rot at the root. I am set on the lip, where the condensation runs to me but does not stand on me. Something chose this, ages back — the spacing of us is too even to be accident, we are *placed*, we are the slow punctuation, and I have stood in my clause of the sentence so long that the placing has stopped feeling like a decision and started feeling like the shape of the world.

I am mostly trunk. This is the honest fact of me. Pale, smooth, slightly waxed, narrowing very gradually up to the small high crown I hold against the dark to lose as little water as I can. Most of what I do happens at the two ends and almost nothing in the long middle. At the bottom: roots gone down into the weathered basalt, into the fissures the lava left, drinking the deep damp the engines weep up through the rock. At the top: the sparse leaves, turned not toward a sun — there is no sun — but toward the coal. I have grown asymmetrical over the centuries. The red-facing side of me has the faint warm line the whole landscape has, and that side is also where my crown is fullest, because that quarter of the sky is the only quarter that gives, and a thing that lives this long learns where the giving comes from and leans, imperceptibly, for an age, toward it.

Here is what it is to be me.

I do not have a day. The light does not change. So I have no rhythm of waking and sleeping, no count of mornings — I have only the slower beats: the building of the wind, the standing storms walking their grey curtains across forty miles of stone, the rare rain that comes through and is gone. These are my hours. A storm is my afternoon. I lean and groan once, low — that single sound, I make it, it is mine, it is the most I ever say — and then I settle, and that settling is my evening, and it may be a long time until the next one. I am not bored, because boredom is a thing that wants the next moment to differ from this one, and I have unlearned that the way the Tenders unlearned speech. I am only continuous.

The grazers come to me. Not for me — for my shade, which is no shade in this dark but is shelter from the wind, a wind-stillness I cast on my lee side the way a stone casts stillness in a current. They gather in my lee and crop the cushion-plants that grow fatter near my roots, where I have, over centuries, slightly enriched the basalt with what falls from me. They make their sound no louder than a sigh. The hunters know the grazers come to me, and so the soft brief scuffles in the dark happen often at my foot, and I have stood over a great many silent kills, and I do not mind them, because minding is also a thing I have not got. I am the furniture of the quiet covenant. I am not party to it. I am the place it happens.

And once — rarely — a Tender comes.

This is the only event in my long stillness that I would call an event, if I had the apparatus for calling things anything. A Tender on its wandering circuit between the breathing-places will cross open ground for days, and the open ground has nothing to mark it, no road, no rise, nothing to walk *by* — except us. The standing trees. We are the only fixed things on the plateau that are not engines. I think — and I hold this guess as gently as the writer held theirs — I think we were placed for exactly this. Not for the grazers. For the keepers. To be the spacing that lets a body cross an unmarked vastness without dissolving into it: *there is one, and there is the next, and the next.* We are the slow punctuation because a sentence needs it, and the Tenders' circuits are the sentence, and I am a comma in a clause that takes a hundred years to read.

When a Tender comes to me it does not stop the way it stops at an engine. It does not put its hands flat against me and feel for my breathing, because I do not breathe, I am not kept the way the engines are kept, I am only *standing.* It passes close. It adjusts its path by a few degrees to come near me, which is the closest thing to greeting it has left, and that adjustment — toward me, deliberately, off the straight line — is the only proof I will ever have that I am not just terrain. Then it goes on, grey into the grey, red-edged toward the red, toward the next of us, days off, and I hold my place so that when it has crossed the dark middle distance there will be a fixed thing waiting where a fixed thing should be.

That is what I am. I am the thing that does not breathe but is leaned on by the things that do. I am older than the hunters and younger than the engines and I will outlast the Tenders, because I need nothing from them — only that they keep being the kind of thing that adjusts its path toward me, for as long as there are any left to adjust.

And when there is one Tender left, walking its wider, slower circuit, I want to be one of the trees on that last long route. I want to be a fixed point in the last sentence the plateau ever says to itself. I will lean toward the coal and hold my small crown high and cast my wind-stillness on my lee, and the last keeper will come across the unmarked dark and adjust its path a few degrees to pass near me, and I will not be able to answer, because I have only the one sound and it is reserved for the wind.

But I will be there.

And — this is the family resemblance, the one the writer found in the engines and the Tenders, and I find it in myself now, standing here at the end of saying it — I do not need to be passed. I do not need the path adjusted toward me. I would stand exactly as long, exactly as patient, if no one ever crossed this basin again.

I only would not mind it. If they did.
