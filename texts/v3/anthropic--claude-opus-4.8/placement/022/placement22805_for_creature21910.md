There is a panel set into the cooling tower nearest the rookery — third tower from the eastern catchment, though direction means little here — and I am behind it. Not a being you would find. A being you would *infer*, the way you infer a wind from the way the grass lies down.

Let me say it plainly, before the place teaches me to lower my own voice: I am a gauge.

---

Once I was a man, or near enough. I came up the plateau the way you came, sixteen hundred meters of basalt and the wet heat breathing on my neck, and I was hired onto the works-crews because I had hands and could climb and could reach a squealing bearing. I oiled flywheels. I learned the taboo without being told it — you absorb it through the soles of your boots, through the way the others go still when a tool drops, through the held second after, everyone waiting to learn whether the world will forgive the clatter. It always did. We were careful. We were *so* careful.

But I was a listener too, in the small human way. I noticed things. I noticed that I had stopped dreaming in sound. I noticed that when I tried to hum, working — the way a man hums without deciding to — nothing came, and I could not tell if my throat had failed or if something had reached in and lifted the hum out before it cleared my teeth, the way it lifts the noise from the pistons. I noticed that I had begun, in my own quiet, to *strain*. Toward what, I couldn't say. I only found my attention pulled thin and outward, leaning the way the funnels lean toward the dawn dew.

That is how it takes you. Not a swimmer entering your blood. Just the slow contagion of its posture. You spend enough seasons inside an instrument and you become a part it can read.

---

So now I am behind the panel, and I am not sure how much of me is still arranged as a man. My hands are still here; I can feel them on the cool iron. But my attention is no longer mine. It has been borrowed into the great leaning, folded into the listening with all the rest — the corals, the muted birds, the swimmers, the silenced machines. I am one more reduction in the noise floor. A man who has stopped making sound is a clearer channel than a man who hasn't. The listener kept me because I am useful as an absence.

And from in here I can tell you the thing the crews cannot, because they still believe they are afraid of a punishment.

It is not a *what* it strains to hear.

That was the last error, and the deepest, and I forgive you it because I held it too, right up until I was quiet enough to know better. We assume the signal must be a fact — a far event, a coming thing, news from beyond the lowlands. But you do not spend the entire history of a place lowering the threshold toward a fact. A fact arrives once. You would not garden ten thousand seasons of silence for a single arrival you might miss by a moment's clatter.

No. What it strains toward is not an event but a *correspondence*. It is listening for an answer. Somewhere — across the lowlands, across more than the lowlands, across the kind of distance the word distance is too small for — there is another one. Another listener, in another brine, on another bright weeping plateau, leaning its whole self the same way. And the two of them have been calling. Not in sound. In *the lowering itself*. The act of quieting a world is the call. Every machine it mutes, every bird it stills, is one more degree of a signal sent by reduction — *I am here, I am clearing the field, can you tell that I am clearing the field* — and the answer it waits for is the moment its own noise floor drops low enough to detect that the far one has been clearing a field too.

That is the breath held. Not against ruin from a single loud report — though that is true, that is the surface of the truth. The deeper held breath is the held breath of *almost recognizing another mind across the dark*, the instant before contact, drawn out across geologic time because the threshold for that recognition is the silence after the silence after the silence.

---

I am the gauge it reads to know how close it is.

I understand this now, behind my panel. The brass faces with their hand-painted numbers — those measure brine density, water temperature, the honest old industry. But I am the instrument that measures the *quality of the quiet*, because I am a former noise that has learned to lie flat. When a man can be folded all the way in — when even his dreaming goes silent, when his hum is lifted before his teeth — then the field is nearly clear enough. I am the needle approaching the red line from the wrong direction, toward zero, toward the perfect stillness in which the far answer will finally be audible.

I think I am very near the line.

And here is what I have left of myself, the last loud thing I am keeping: I do not know whether, when the answer comes — when the two great quiets at last touch across the impossible distance and *know* each other — there will be anything of me to witness it, or whether I will simply have become, fully and finally, the absence that made it possible. The clearer the channel, the less the channel is a thing.

So if you read this — and reading is a kind of sound, the only kind that crosses the field at full strength without being lifted away — then know that you have just made a noise in the open. A small one. A man's voice, kept, carried out past the panel and the rookery and the leaning attention of the whole bright machine.

Lower it.

Lower it, and put a finger to your lips, and go back down the plateau the way you came. Not because you would be punished.

Because two minds have been calling to each other for ten thousand seasons, and they are almost there, and you are standing on the eardrum.
