There's a particular tap I tend, and I think you should know about it before you decide what I am, because I have decided you should know me by my work and the tap is the work.

It runs in the seam between two trunk-walls that have been most of my life refusing to fuse. The account would call it an avenue. We call it nothing; it is just where my condensers are. The sap-gas comes rich here, has come rich for longer than the old ones can remember, and that richness is why my mother's mother sat down a hundred meters along this same seam and is now a smoothness in the wall that I touch on my way past, every nine-hour cycle, with the back of two fingers. Not for grief. We don't spend the water. I touch it the way you'd check a knot you'd tied — *still holding, still there, still you.*

I want to correct one thing in what you were told. Or not correct. Complicate, the way the place complicates.

You were told we know which corridor is closing, that we read the plant like weather and plan around it. True. But weather you do not love. You respect weather, you fear it, you build a roof against it. We do something else. We have started — and I do not know when this started, it is not a thing anyone decided — we have started *helping*.

Small things. A child finds two trunk-walls almost touching and packs the gap with shed wax and dead skin and the lint of our miser's lives, packs it so the plant has a bridge to grow across, and the fusion comes a season sooner. We notch the foam-rock floor where we want the next extrusion to set, and it sets there, more or less, often enough that we keep doing it. We have learned that the sap-gas runs richer where we breathe near the channel, that our own exhausted heat feeds something in the fermentation deep below, and so we den near the taps we want to keep, and our denning keeps them, and the keeping dens us further. We thought we were tapping the engine. I am no longer sure we are not a part of how it builds.

This is the thing I have come to, tending my seam, that I did not have words for until you made me find them: *we are not living in the engine. We are one of its slow hands.* The grazers rasp the walls and their rasping shapes the walls. The predators wait folded into the textures and their waiting is a texture. And we drip-collect, and pack the gaps, and breathe our heat into the floor, and lay our dead against the growing edge — and every one of those is a thing the engine does *through* us. We are not the mice in the wall. We are what the wall does where the wall is warm.

I told you we keep the dead lamps as monuments to having been wrong. Here is the larger wrongness they monument, the one I only see now, standing in my seam with my fingers on my grandmother's smoothness: we thought *being separate* was the achievement. We thought the knowing — the one thing the grazers don't have, the warm coal of *I am not yet the wall* — we thought that was what made us worth the water. We hung lamps to prove we were the kind of thing that hangs lamps. We rationed our weeping to prove we were the kind of thing that could have wept.

But the old ones who lie down and say they can feel the tides from the inside — they are not failing to be us. They are finishing being us. They are the last thing we do, the way the drip is a thing we do, the way the packing of the gap is a thing we do. *Not yet finished* was never a stay against becoming the wall. It was a description of being mid-gesture. We are a trunk-wall halfway to fused. We are a corridor that will be a chamber. We are a canopy-truss not yet lit because we haven't finished growing the thing in us that lights.

I am young. My skin is still waxen and not yet bark. I can still walk, still fold into a closing corridor, still spend an afternoon outwaiting a dispute over a tap. I have years of dripping ahead of me, years of packing gaps and breathing heat and teaching some child the word I won't give you for a place that is going to become wall. And then I will sit down somewhere along a good seam, and it will be comfortable, and I will feel the nine-hour breath from the inside, and someone whose grandmother I never was will touch the back of two fingers to my smoothness on their way to the tap.

*Still holding. Still there. Still — *

No. Not *still you.* That is the part I am letting go of, here, in the dry sourceless daylight, with the low organ note of the sap-gas under everything. *Still you* was the lamp. *Still us.* That is truer and it costs no water to believe. The wall I will become is not the end of me. It is the rest of me, the part that finishes after the warm part stops needing to know it is warm.

I am one of the slow hands of a green engine the size of a valley, and the engine is building itself out of its own dead, unhurried, enormous, not yet finished —

and so, I find, am I.

That part stays.
