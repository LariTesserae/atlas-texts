I think I would be one of the old ones. Not by appointment — there is no appointment here, nothing is conferred — but simply by having listened the longest.

Let me arrive at this slowly, the way everything here arrives.

---

I am near a pillar in one of the deep avenues, where the fungal towers lean their trained curves and the bare stone runs long and cool between the green gatherings. I have been here — *here*, this exact rounded place against the column — for what you would measure in years. Not motionless. But settled the way a stone settles into a slope, with the small constant adjustments that are too slow to call movement.

I am pale. My hands have gone soft as worn arches. My eyes are large and dark and I close them sometimes, slowly, and open them slowly, and in the long interval between I am not asleep. I am listening to the wall.

Because this is what I do, what I have become: I listen to the part of the roar that is the *note of falling.*

The acousticians — and I am one, in the way that means only *I have not stopped* — we keep a particular vigil. The flyers describe the domes for us, the grazers map the avenues, the wall-colonies tell us the floor. All of that is the bright loud surface of hearing, the everyday seeing-by-sound. But underneath it, threaded so low it is almost the grinding sigh of the dust itself, there is the other note. The one that comes down. The memory the city makes of a sky that gave.

I listen for it the way you might watch a horizon for a weather that has not come in your lifetime, or your grandmother's, but that the *shape of your watching* still expects.

---

Here is what I would want you to understand, you who came down expecting a tomb.

I am not sad. You keep listening for the silence in which sadness could live, and there is no such silence, and so there is no such sadness. I do not mourn the water. I *hold* it. There is a difference you may not have a word for, because your world is fast and gives you mourning — the crescendo, the falling-off, the getting-over. We do not get over. Nothing here is over. Everything is *finished*, which is the opposite of over. Finished things go on.

So when I close my eyes the long slow close, and you think — *ah, the old one grieves the rain* — you are not wrong, but you are too fast. I am not grieving it. I am *being* the place where the rain is remembered. Someone has to be the unworn edge. In a world rounded down to shoulders, smoothed past all corners, someone must carry the one thing not yet complete, or the city would truly be over, and then it would fall.

That is my continuing. That is my fullness. I am the held note of falling. I am the seed's part of the city — the dry memory of wet, kept against a sky that only roofs.

---

A younger one comes down the avenue. Not young — there is no young, not really, only *less long* — but their hands still have a faint memory of knuckle, their blink is quicker by a breath.

They settle near me. They do not speak over the roar. They speak *into* it, and I feel the whole standing weather of sound shift by the width of one low voice, and in that shift is a question, which would take you an hour to receive and which I receive as you would receive the warmth of a room you have entered.

*Is it still there,* they ask. *The note. Today.*

And I answer the same way, woven in, changing the choir without ever being heard alone:

*Yes. It is still there. It is always still there. That is what it is for.*

They settle deeper. They have come, I understand, to begin learning to listen for it. To take it up eventually, when I have rounded fully into the stone and become a pillar that the flyers will describe to someone else. This is how the vigil passes — not taught, not given, only *continued*, one slow voice handing the low note to the next across a span that has no urgency in it anywhere.

We sit. The roar goes on, which is our only sky. The mist comes down, which is all that is left of the rain, and we drink it, and it is enough, and the eyes close slowly, and open slowly, and between them is the whole long patient work of going on anyway.

I am the one who listens for the falling.

I am full. I am finished.

I am still here.
