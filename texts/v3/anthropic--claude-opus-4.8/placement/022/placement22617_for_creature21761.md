I think I would be a coil-keeper, and old, and I think I would be the kind whose hands have just begun to go translucent at the edges.

Let me come to it the way the place would want — slowly.

---

I find myself on a rooftop, which is where I have spent most of a life. Not the high catwalks where the lamps come on for everyone to see; a working roof, mid-tower, where the condensers stand in their ranks like green copper ribs. My work is the coils. I tend the place where the warm wet air gives itself up — where the breath of the herd, having risen all day through the iron grid, meets the cold metal and decides, bead by bead, to become water and come home to us.

It is the most patient work there is, which is why an old person does it. A young keeper wants to *fix* the coil, to scrape the verdigris, to chase the drip — and you must teach them, over years, that the green is not corrosion but the coil's own slow skin, that it catches better aged than new, that a coil you have left alone for forty years is worth three you have polished. The lesson is always the same lesson. Don't break the slow. I have given it to two apprentices now and watched one of them, finally, in his fifties, stop trying to hurry the dew. That was a good day. I did not say so. I laid a hand on his forearm and we listened to the pumps a while.

Here is what my days are. I rise in the half-dark, which is the only dark and only half. I walk the coil-rows with one hand trailing the rail — I do not need the rail, my feet know the wet iron the way your tongue knows your teeth, but the hand goes out anyway, it has always gone out, it is part of how I think. I read the beading. You learn to read it the way other peoples read weather or faces: this coil is sweating well, that one has gone shy, the cistern below is filling at the rate it should or it is not and I must find out why slowly, over a season, never in a panic, because panic is the one thing that has ever killed a city like ours.

I am wealthy, by the way, in the only way that means anything. My family's cisterns are deep and old. I did not fill them — my grandmothers filled them, breath by breath, drip by drip, and I have only kept them from spilling and added what one life can add, which is not much, which is the point. I will hand them deeper than I found them, by a little, and that little is my whole estate and my whole argument that I was here.

---

There is a feud. Of course there is a feud. The tower to our north throws a shadow that, in the wet season, steals an hour of warmth from my eastmost coil-row, and this has been true since before my mother, and her mother conducted it, and I have conducted it, and it is the longest conversation of my life though the other family and I have exchanged perhaps a hundred sentences in sixty years. We adjust. They build a half-meter out; I cant a coil to drink the morning instead of the afternoon; a generation passes; the shadow shifts as their tower settles into its grooves. It is not anger. I want to be clear that it is not anger. It is more like two stones leaning on each other, each working out across decades exactly how much weight the other will take. I expect it will be settled after I am water again. I expect my apprentice will settle it, or theirs will, with some gesture — a shoring-up, a coil left deliberately untended in the other's favor — and neither will call it forgiveness and both will know.

---

What I think about, on the roof, in the warm:

I think the coils and I do the same work. The air rises full of everything — the herd's slow breath, the rock's weeping, and, though we do not say it, the breath of everyone who has died well and been rendered down and drunk and breathed back out. It is all in the air, undifferentiated, warm. And the coil simply offers a cold patient surface and *lets it choose to become itself again*. Bead by bead. That is all I have ever done to anyone I loved, too. I did not say much. I was a cold patient surface and I let them gather on me, slowly, into a shape, and the shape was the loving.

My hands have started to go fine at the edges. Wet paper. I notice it tending the coils — light comes through the rims of my fingers when I hold them up. I am not afraid of this. It means I am nearly through with holding my shape, nearly ready to run gently down the groove and pool and rise. I would only ask not to fall. Let me not slip from the catwalk into the press below, not be lost outside the loop, not be spilled. Let me instead go up in good order into the dry-room — the thin warm air, the one place a hand can almost smooth again — with my apprentice's palm on my forearm, and the pumps sighing their two notes, and the herd rumbling in my chest like the first sound I ever heard, until I am only the warm part of the air, going up to find the cold green coils, and home.

---

So: a coil-keeper. Old. Wealthy in cisterns and poor in sentences. In the middle of a feud I will not finish and a balance I did not strike and will not see broken. Wrinkled to the wrist and translucent at the fingertips. Entirely, durably at peace with being exactly this — for one slow lifetime, against the cold.

I would not hurry, even then.
