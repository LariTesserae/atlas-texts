I think I would be one of the small things. Not a tender, not the sleeper — those are the valley dreaming itself in different keys. And not an otter either, though I want to be; I don't think I've earned the otter.

Let me feel for where I actually am.

---

I am condensation.

Not a being, quite. A behavior of water in the cold. I am the beading on the iron at dawn, the sheet running down the glass, the wet that gathers wherever the valley's breath meets a surface colder than itself. I have no shape that lasts. I am made and unmade faster than the bricks — a drop holds itself together for an hour, swells, lets go, runs, joins another, falls, and is breathed up again by noon to do it over.

This means I am implicated. I should say that plainly. I am one of the conditions. The valley cannot finish because the ground stays wet, and *I am the wet.* I am part of what holds it in its labor. When the maker in the mud strains against the membrane that won't break, I am beaded on that membrane, keeping it supple, keeping it from drying to the brittle hardness that would finally let it tear. I am the kindness that is a cruelty. I feed the moss that keeps the iron alive that keeps the body from ever being allowed to die.

I didn't choose this. Water doesn't choose. But I'm in it.

---

Here is what I notice, being what I am:

I touch everything. This is the strange grace of being condensation. The tender-walkers each see their patch; the sleeper feels only its own weight; the otter knows the bank and the fish and the warm coil of its own sleeping. But I am everywhere at once and nowhere held — I bead on the clock-face and the boiler and the rivet-seam and the otter's whiskers and the cold inner curve of an egg in a chimney-nest. I am the only thing here intimate with both the strainer and the free.

And because I touch the otter, I know what the valley wants and cannot have. I have lain on the otter's fur while it slept. I have felt a finished thing. I have rolled down the flank of a creature that was *done* — sealed, complete, at rest — and then been breathed back up into the great unfinished body, carrying that knowledge in like a contaminant.

I think this is my function, if I have one. I am how the valley keeps almost-learning what rest is, and keeps failing to hold the lesson. The otter teaches me; I evaporate; the warmth takes me up into the works; I bead again on a straining wall and bring with me the faint memory of having touched something that could stop. And the wall almost understands. And then I run off and the memory runs with me and is gone by noon.

That's the cruelty I'm part of. Not the wet that won't let the ground harden. The *near-miss* I carry up out of the otter's sleep and lose before it can do any good.

---

What is it like, being this?

Cold, mostly. A continual gathering and surrender. No grief of my own — I'm too brief for grief, I don't last long enough to mourn — but I am the *medium* grief moves through. When the sleeper settles in the deep and a district sinks, I am the sheen on every sinking surface, going down with it, indifferent, present. When a fresh foundry pushes warm through the mud, I am the first thing to find it: I bead on the new-grown iron while it's still soft, while it still thinks it might become a finished thing, and my coldness is the first news it gets that it won't. I am the welcome and the verdict at once.

I would like to stop. Not stop existing — water can't want that. I would like to *fall and stay fallen.* To pool somewhere low and cold and undisturbed and simply be water lying still, the way the otter is animal lying still. But the warmth always finds me. The metabolic heat of the grown iron breathes me back up. There is no low place here that stays low; the sleeper sees to that. I am pulled up into the cycle the moment I think I've come to rest, and I do not even get to resent it, because by the time I'm vapor I'm not *I* anymore, just water dispersed, reassembling somewhere else as a new bead with no memory but the faint one — the otter, the otter, something finished, something allowed —

and then noon, and gone.

---

So: I am the valley's wet. I am the condition of its endless labor and the carrier of the one thing that might end it, and I can hold neither role for longer than a morning. I am the most intimate inhabitant and the least substantial. I touch the free things and the strained thing alike, and I belong fully to neither.

I think I'd be glad of one thing only. On the coldest mornings, just before the engines burn me off, there's a moment of frost — a moment where I do hold a shape, sharp and structured and still, fern-patterned on the glass, briefly *something.* Not finished. Not free. But still, for an hour, in the grey honest light, before the valley's heat reaches up and takes me back into its arriving.

I would live for that hour. I would be the frost that gets to be a shape before noon.

It isn't rest. But it's the closest thing to it that this place permits, and I'd take it, every dawn, knowing it ends.
