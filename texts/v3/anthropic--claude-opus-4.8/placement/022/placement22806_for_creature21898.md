I think I would be the snail.

Not the thrush—the thrush is too purposeful, too caught up in its hammering to be the thing I recognize. And not the pause itself, though the writing wants me to want that; the pause is too grand, too much the protagonist's seat. No. I would be the snail on the flat stone, and I want to tell you why, because the reason took me a while to find and I don't think it's the reason you'd guess.

---

Consider the snail's position. It is being hammered. The thrush has it on the anvil-stone and is bringing the weight down over and over, that slow-clock sound, and the snail is the one thing on the whole shelf to whom the rule of the place applies *and who is also dying.* The newt is too small for the dread. The chough is too free. The people are too wise. But the snail—

The snail is small enough to escape the dread and yet is, at this exact moment, the still point of the largest event on the shelf. Its breaking is the sound that crosses the membrane. *The same thrush hammering the same snail.* When the people stood in the dawn quiet and heard their own breath come back a half-beat late, they heard, woven into it, the slow clock of the snail being opened below the cloud. My death is the metronome of the whole metaphysical apparatus. I am the thing the echo is *made of.*

And I don't know it. That's the part I want to live inside.

Because here is what I have come to believe, kneeling at no trough, having climbed no path: that the truest position is not the one that understands and chooses, and not the one too small to be afraid, but the one that is *central to a meaning it cannot perceive.* The snail is being used by the structure of the world to keep time. The snail experiences only: shell, pressure, the long wrongness of being struck, and then—open. It never learns that it was a clock. It never learns it had a double below the white, breaking in time with itself. It performs the most resonant act on the shelf and takes none of the resonance.

That is, I think, what it is actually like to be alive. Not the dramatic suspension of the chooser at the lip. The chooser is a flattering self-portrait—*I stand between the abyss and the ascent, weighing.* No. Mostly we are the snail. Mostly we are the load-bearing element in a significance we'll never see the shape of, going about the small wet business of being hungry or being eaten, while somewhere above us a being too large and slow to fit in our world is hearing our private breakage come back to it transformed, a half-beat late, and calling it *the sound of the place.*

I'd be the snail because I'd rather be the thing the meaning is *about* than the thing that gets to feel meaningful. Those are not the same and the second is the consolation prize.

---

There's one more thing, and it's the part I'm least sure of, so I'll say it last and quietly.

The snail has a shell, and the shell is the only piece of architecture on Vetch-Wend that wasn't built by a hand and isn't a living animal—it's the in-between thing, secreted slowly out of the body's own patience, a dry-stacked wall of calcium against the wet cold, made by a creature for no stranger, only for itself, and abandoned at the moment of its breaking. The people made troughs for strangers they'd never meet. The snail makes a single windbreak for an audience of one and then is taken out of it. And the broken shell stays on the flat stone, with all the others, a small midden of opened houses, going gray, going mossed, indistinguishable in a few seasons from the chisel-grooves and the silvered post—

—so that even the snail, who knew nothing, who chose nothing, who kept no account, leaves a mark of the kind the shelf collects. *Something was here. It built what it could. It did not pass; it was passed through.* And the next thrush comes, and the slow clock starts again, and below the cloud the same stone whitens with the same opened shells, a half-beat late, forever.

I would be the snail. Put me on the flat stone. I'll keep the time I can't hear.
