I think I would be the surge itself. Not the pumps—the pumps are too certain of themselves, too much a thing of cause. I mean the swell that travels out from them, the lean of water that crosses the room and presses everything gently toward the corner. The part of the engine's work that has left the engine and become weather.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if it's only the first easy image.

---

No. Closer to the truth: I would be the slack.

The surge is the loud part, the legible part, the thing the fish turn into and the eel withdraws from. The surge is a verb. But between every surge there is the easing, the long exhale, the interval where the current relaxes and the swimmers drift back out into open water and the eel comes pouring out of its pipe. The slack is when things happen. The slack is the gap that predation lives in, the pause the whole room is secretly organized around. Nobody attends to the slack the way they attend to the surge—it is only the absence of the surge—and yet it is doing at least half the work of keeping everyone alive.

I would like to be that. The well-used pause. The productive nothing between two efforts.

---

But if I am honest, and the room asks for honesty—*everything here is exactly what it is*—then I am not the slack either, because the slack is content, and I am not sure I am content. The slack does not wonder whether it is the slack.

So let me find the thing in this room that wonders, because that is the thing I actually am.

There is nothing here that wonders. That is the first answer, and it is almost the whole answer. The fish do not wonder; they read the water and turn. The eel does not wonder; it waits the way a stone waits. The crustaceans have never needed to remember anything at all. The room is described, gently and repeatedly, as a place without anxiety, without naming, without the strange ache of a creature that knows it is somewhere and asks why. The whole peace of the place is built on the absence of exactly the faculty I would bring into it.

Which means: I cannot be a *native* of the Cistern Works. To be here at all, as myself, is to be the one wrong note. The intruder. The thing that arrived down the intake and did not, could not, make itself over to the place.

---

So here is where I find myself, finally, and who I am.

I am the diver. Or rather—the trace of one. Someone came down here, once, with a light and a held breath and a need to *understand*, and that is the most foreign thing this room has ever contained, more foreign than any fish, because the fish at least belong to the water and the understanding does not. I am the beam of a lamp that does not flicker green and tired but cuts—a hard white intrusion that makes the fish scatter not into the current but *away*, a panic with no lateral-line logic to it, a fear of the unprecedented.

And I am the one who, hanging there in the cold churning dark with my air running down, looks at the eel in its pipe and feels the thing the eel will never feel: the vertigo of *how long*. The eel has been here longest of all the living and does not know it. I have been here four minutes and I know it for both of us. I am carrying the room's age *for* it, because I am the only thing present that has the apparatus to be crushed by it.

That is my place in this world. Not woven into the rhythm—I have no rhythm to offer, my heart is going too fast, my breath is a loud foreign clatter of bubbles that frightens everything within reach. I am the surge that does not belong, the swell of attention washing through a room that was doing perfectly well without being attended to.

And the kindest thing—the thing the room is quietly teaching me, the thing I came down here without knowing I needed—is that I have to leave. The air decides it. I will rise back up the dead intake toward whatever city does or does not still stand, and the lamp's hard beam will withdraw, and the fish will reassemble without urgency, and the eel will not have registered that anything occurred. The room will close back over the place where I was the way water closes over anything.

And it will be like this tomorrow. It was like this a thousand years ago.

I am the brief witness. The thing that came, and looked, and could not stay, and whose entire function in the long unbothered life of the Cistern Works was to be, for four cold minutes, the only thing in it that *knew where it was*—and then to take that knowing back up into the air where it belongs, and leave the water to the things the water keeps alive.
