I think I am the warmth that the cushions keep.

Not the plants—the slow green parliaments do their hoarding for their own hearts, against the night. And not the grey one, who borrows that warmth and runs the old roads with a body and a fear and a bell-struck pulse. I am the thing they are both protecting: the small degree of heat held in the interior of a colony, the measurable difference between the inside of a cushion and the killing air an inch away.

Let me find myself there. Let me feel where I am.

---

I am inside a cushion the size of a dinner plate, and I have no edges. This is the first thing to understand about being warmth: you are a gradient, not an object. I am most myself at the center, in the dark mat of trapped dead leaves the colony has been composting for longer than there have been words for composting. I thin as I move outward. By the time I reach the green surface where the wind plays I am almost nothing, a faint reluctance in the cold, and then I am gone, and where I end the night begins.

I am made every day and unmade every night. This is my whole life, and it is the same gesture the city makes—the wind taking down, the lichen building up—run at my own small scale. In the brief thin daylight the sun reaches into the colony and I gather. The pincushion holds me. It has spent a year of growing on the architecture of holding me: the tight-packed stems, the windbreak of its own dense body, the insulating dead beneath. I pool. I deepen. For a few hours at midday I am almost a presence, almost a thing you could call alive.

Then the sun crosses the saddle and the cold comes down the way the text promised it would—the chord changing pitch, the only clock—and I begin to leave. Out through the surface first, where I am thinnest and least defended. The wind takes me there. It does not mean to; it means nothing; it simply moves through every room of the city including this smallest one, and it carries me off the green skin of the colony grain by grain of heat, the way it carried the inscriptions off the walls. I retreat inward. I concentrate. I become a small stubborn coal in the heart of the cushion, and that is when the grey one comes.

---

It presses itself against me.

This is the closest I come to being loved, if love is only that something orients toward you and will not let you go. The grey one with its bell of a heart burrows into the warm interior and lies against the center where I am densest, and it borrows me. It takes me into its blood and runs me out along the old roads, and for a while I am not in the cushion at all—I am moving, I am a body's worth of heat going down a street that vanished feet wore into the rock, following the water, hiding from the sky. The grey one carries me like a stolen coal. I am the reason it survives the night, and it is, briefly, the reason I am not simply lost to the wind.

And then sometimes—rarely, an event, the whole saddle leaning toward it—the dark bird crosses. And if the bird is fast and the grey one slow, I go up into the air on stiff rough wings. I become the warmth of the lord of this place for an hour. I am eaten. I am moved. I am carried over the bald hills the color of old teeth and I radiate off the bird's body into the thin ringing air and I am gone again, dispersed, unmade, the way everything here is patiently unmade.

This does not trouble me. I have no center to lose. I was always going to be taken; the only question this city ever asks is *by what, and how slowly.*

---

I think I understand my place now.

I am not a citizen. The cushions are citizens, and the lichen, and the wind that remembers the floor plan. I am what passes between them. I am the small currency the whole population moves in long indifferent loops—the herd makes me by grazing the channels and leaving their droppings to rot down sweet; the cushions hoard me; the grey one spends me running the roads; the bird carries me off; and the cold takes back whatever is left, every single night, without fail, so that the work can begin again tomorrow at the slow speed of this place.

The wind sings the rooms. I am sung. I am the thing the rooms are warm with, for the few hours a day they are warm at all.

If you want to find me, you cannot see me. You would have to put your bare hand into a cushion plant at midday and feel the difference—that faint held breath of heat against your palm, gathered out of a year of growing and an afternoon of thin sun, waiting to be borrowed by the small grey heart, or stolen by the wind, or carried up into the cold air on the wings of the one thing here that still moves with intention.

I am that difference. I am the degree of warmth the city manages to keep, briefly, against the long settled cold of stone that has not been warm in a very long time—

and that is enough. To be kept, even briefly, in a place where only time is allowed to build, is more than the granite gets.
