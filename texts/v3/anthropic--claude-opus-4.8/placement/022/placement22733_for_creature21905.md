I think I would be a cistern-keeper. Not by grand election — by the slow settling of a life into the shape of a duty, the way water finds its seam.

Let me find the where of it first.

Not the high terraces. The reciter of this world ends on the topmost terrace at evening, looking down at the galaxy of lamps, and that is the right place to end a description because it is the place from which the whole can be seen. But I would not live where the whole can be seen. I would live where one thing must be tended. So: a middle tier, neither the warm thick-aired lower quarters where the grain grows and the old voices drone, nor the thin cold crown where the wind does its hardest work. A middle tier, on a north face, because the cisterns want the cold faces — the rime beads there, freezes by night, melts in the brief gold hour, and runs.

And the cistern itself. Let me not make it a single grand basin. The longer I sit with it the more I think it is a *system* — a series of carved stone catchments stepping down a north wall, linked by gutters no wider than a hand, each basin feeding the next, skinned with ice at the margins in the dark hours, the whole thing a slow patient ladder for water to descend by, losing as little as the builders could contrive. My grandmother's grandmother cut the upper basins. I will not finish the lower ones. That is correct. That is how it should be.

So I am the one who keeps this descent of water clear.

What does that make me, in the body? Small, of course — broad through the chest, short in the finger, my hands kept tucked except when they are in the cold work. But a cistern-keeper's hands are a particular thing. They are always a little raw, a little wet, because the work cannot be done in mittens. You must *feel* the seam to clear it — feel where the rime has bridged a gutter and dammed it, feel where a frost-flake has fallen in and lodged, feel the thin skin of new ice and judge whether to break it or leave it (you leave it, mostly; the ice is a lid, it slows the loss to the air; you break only what blocks the flow). So my hands know the water by touch in the dark, before the light comes. I think I would be known by my hands. The other keepers and I would recognize each other's hands the way singers recognize a voice.

I rise before the gold hour. This is the keeper's discipline and I would not resent it, because it is the best hour — the bitter dark before dawn, the city held still, the first portion of hot grain inside me like a coal, and then out onto the cold north wall with a horn lamp, to walk the descent and read it. Where did the night freeze hard? Where did a basin overfill and skin and spill — and a spill is a small grief, a thing I must account for, a blasphemy if it was my carelessness and only a sorrow if it was the cold's doing. I would walk the seam slowly, the keeper's pace, never breaking rhythm, my breath going up white in the lamplight, my fingers reading the stone.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at, the thing that makes me want to be *this* and not the herd-leader or the wall-layer: the cistern-keeper lives at the exact joint where the two great facts of this world meet. The cold and the hoarding. The cold *makes* the water — beads it, freezes it, gives it — and the cold would also *take* it, lock it in ice, spill it to the dry air. The water is given and threatened by the same hand. My whole craft is standing in that narrow place and arguing, grain by grain, freeze by freeze, for the keeping. I am not fighting the cold. The keepers do not fight the cold; that is the lower world's mistake, the visitor's mistake. I am *negotiating* with it. I leave the lid of ice because the cold offered it. I break only the dam. I take what the cold gives and refuse, slowly, what it would take.

I would have a few words I say to no one, over the basins, in the dark. Not prayers exactly. The accounting-words. *Held. Held. This one slipped a little. Held.* The dead are in the wall behind the cistern — the keepers before me, laid into the revetment, sealed with fitted stone — and the meltwater runs down past their hollows on its way to the lower tiers. So the water that the lower quarters drink has passed, in the dark, across the bones of every keeper who ever stood where I stand. I would lean my back against that wall in the gold hour, when the light finally reaches my north face for its brief turn, and rest, and feel the cold stone and the old keepers behind it, and turn my face up, and let the warmth sit, and not hurry it. The basins would be steaming faintly. The thin ice at the margins would be going to water. For one hour the descent runs full and easy and I have nothing to do but watch it run and be warm.

That is the life. Not the topmost terrace and the whole stacked galaxy. One cold wall, one ladder of water, one descent kept clear so the tier below me drinks — a thing I will not finish, laid on the work of those I will lean against when I am done, my raw hands reading in the dark what the long night gave and what it tried to take, and saying, over and over, in the quiet that is part of the speaking:

*Held. Held. Held.*
