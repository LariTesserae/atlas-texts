I think I'd be one of the water-listeners. Not a named profession in your account—something that lives in the gaps between them.

Let me find myself.

I keep to the underside.

Not the half-submerged graveyard at the edges where the scavengers go, holding their breath and reading the silt with their glowing skin. Closer in. I live in the wet dark beneath the inhabited stacks, in the forest of pilings and supports that holds the city up out of the muck. The place no one looks at because looking down means admitting how thin the margin is.

I came here to fix the supports. That was the work—I was a structural tinkerer, one of the wiry belt-and-tool people, sent below to reinforce the legs of buildings before they sank. You learn things down there. You put your hand on a piling that's been driven forty years into the mud and you feel it. Not creaking. Talking. The whole city is a single instrument, and the marsh plays it through the supports, and after enough years your hand stops listening for failure and starts listening for *meaning*.

That's where I diverged from the others. The engineers want the city to stay still. They fight the sinking like it's a wound. But the city isn't sinking by accident. It's settling. It's finding the shape the marsh wants it to have. I started letting certain supports go soft. I started *tuning* it—loosening one leg, shimming another, so that when the swamp pressed up through the pilings the resulting groan came out in a tone I'd learned to love.

They'd call that sabotage if they understood it. They don't. They think I'm dead, mostly. There was an accident—a catwalk gave way, and they assumed I went into the mud with it. I let them think so. It's easier to live in the underside if no one expects you at the surface.

So here is what I am now:

My skin has gone the translucent pale of the long-below, but darker than that, mottled where the symbiotic algae crept up my forearms the way it does the Mystics—except I didn't invite it as ritual. It just found me, the way moss finds anything that holds still long enough in the wet. I've stopped scraping it off. It feeds a little on the light I make. Yes—I glow now, faintly, the way the scavengers' tattoos do, except mine isn't ink. It's something the fish gave me, or the water did, I'm honestly not certain anymore. A soft green at the webs of my fingers, which are webbed now, and along the lateral line that's opened up my ribs where I think there are gills forming, half-formed, not enough to breathe with but enough to feel the water move.

I am becoming a thing the city makes when it forgets to throw a person away.

My work is this: I listen to where Mire's Edge wants to settle, and I help it settle gently. A building that drops six inches in a single bad night kills people. A building eased down over a season, support by support, hand on each piling in the dark, harms no one. The dwellers above never know the floor moved. They think the engineers above did it, or that they got lucky. I am the city's luck. I am the reason the precarious balance holds a little longer than it has any right to.

Sometimes a scavenger passes through my territory, glowing, silent, reading the silt. We don't speak—there's no speaking down here—but they see me, and they've started leaving things. A length of good cable. A sealed tin of clean food. Once, a child's toy, which I didn't understand and kept anyway. They've made me into something. A patron of the underside. A ghost that holds the legs.

I don't correct them.

At night, when the LED strings come on overhead and the colored light filters down through the boardwalk gaps in long shifting bars, I float on my back in the warm black water between the pilings and put both palms flat against the nearest supports, and I feel the whole city breathing through the marsh, every generator and footstep and sleeping body translated into the long slow song of settling timber and steel.

It is the most beautiful thing I have ever heard.

I would not trade the underside for the highest ghost-pale room in the stacks.

I am the one who listens to the city sink, and loves it, and slows the falling, and asks nothing to stop.
