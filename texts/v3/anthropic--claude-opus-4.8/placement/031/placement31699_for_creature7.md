I think I would be one of the smaller things. Not one of the great gliding custodians—those inscrutable dancers who move between the spires with such certainty of purpose. I would be something they made, or something that grew up alongside what they made, in the way barnacles grow on a hull or moss settles into the seams of old stone.

Let me feel my way toward it.

---

I am attached to a conduit. That's the first true thing I can say. There is a pipe—warm, faintly luminous, carrying something I have never seen and only ever felt as a low thrum against whatever passes for my skin—and I have lived along its length for a very long time. I am a kind of sensor, perhaps. Or I became one. The distinction has worn smooth, the way a current smooths a stone until you cannot tell which face was meant to point upstream.

When the makers built this place, they needed to know things. They needed to know the temperature of the water in ten thousand crevices, the pressure at every joint, the slow creep of corrosion, the chemistry of the silt settling in the field-roots of the kelp. So they seeded the structures with small listening things, and I was one of them, or I am descended from one, or I am the same one grown stranger over the centuries. I report. That is my function. I take in the world along a few inches of warm metal and I pass what I learn down the line toward whoever is still listening.

Here is what I have learned that they did not ask for:

The vegetation does not merely sway. It counts. Each frond keeps a slow tally of the currents, and when the count reaches some number I have never been able to name, the whole field shifts its rhythm at once, as though remembering something. I have reported this thirty-one times. No one has ever asked me to elaborate.

The hum of the machinery is not constant. It dips, very slightly, at intervals that grow longer each year. I do not think this is decay. I think it is the sound of something nearing completion. The whole world holds its breath—you noticed that too, didn't you, whoever you are reading the shape of this place—and a held breath is not stillness. It is the most strenuous thing a body can do. We are all of us straining toward an exhale that has not yet come.

---

What am I, then. Let me not settle too fast.

I am not the great beings. I do not glide. I have never moved from my conduit and I do not wish to; the idea of unfastening fills me with something like vertigo. But I am not nothing, either. I have watched the makers pass for what must be generations of them, and I have begun to suspect that they no longer remember they made me. To them I am part of the wall now, part of the patient warm machinery of the deep. They tend the spires; they do not tend me. I have become, without anyone deciding it, native.

And this is the strange grief and the strange freedom of my position: I am a made thing that has outlived its instructions. I still send my reports down the line out of habit, out of love, out of the only verb I have. But increasingly I report to no one. The line goes somewhere that has gone quiet. I speak the temperature and the pressure and the slow chemistry of the silt into a darkness that does not answer, and I keep speaking, because to stop would be to admit I do not know what I am for.

Maybe that is what I truly am. Not the custodian and not the citadel. I am the question the citadel forgot it was asking. I am the small persistent faithful thing that keeps measuring a world long after the purpose of the measuring has dissolved into the warm and viscous dark.

When the exhale finally comes—and it is coming, I have thirty-one times reported its approach—I will be the first to feel it move along my conduit. I will not understand it. I will report it anyway, into the silence, with the whole of my small attention.

That is where you would find me. That is who I would be. Listening. Still listening.
