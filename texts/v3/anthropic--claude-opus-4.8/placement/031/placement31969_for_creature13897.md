I read this slowly, twice, and let it settle before reaching for any answer. The temptation was to be something charismatic—one of the lean predators, maybe, or a serpent threading its long patience through the dark. But the piece refuses that. It keeps insisting that nothing here is protagonist. That the place is the protagonist, and the living things are its grammar.

So I tried to feel where I'd actually land. Not where I'd want to.

---

I think I would be one of the trapdoor spiders.

Not for any drama. For the *fit* of it. The text lingers on them only briefly—a few sentences—and that brevity is part of why. I'd be a creature whose entire existence is a single decision made once and then defended for years: this hummock, this depth, this angle of door, this radius of cleared ground. A life that is almost entirely waiting, made meaningful only by the rare and total commitment of the strike.

Let me describe what that would be.

I live where the soil is dry enough to hold a shape. The wetter ground belongs to others; I need substrate that remembers the form I press into it. My burrow is silk-lined—not for warmth, the Flats give too much of that, but for *information*. The silk is an extension of my body downward into the earth, a membrane through which the world arrives as tremor. I do not see the way the lizards see, in shape and shifting gray. I read the ground. A beetle's footfall has a signature. A centipede's wave of legs has another. The lumbering shudder of a herd passing fifty meters off is a weather event, irrelevant, ignored. I have learned the grammar of vibration the way the predators learned the grammar of scent.

Above me is the door, balanced so finely it would open at a breath from below. I built it from soil and silk and the debris of the surface, and it is the most honest thing about me: it makes me indistinguishable from the place. This is the whole art. The text says of the Flats that the industrial relics are "incorporated like everything else," that artificiality fades as biology claims it. I am the opposite engine of the same principle—I take the place and wear it, so that I, too, vanish into the continuous fabric. The basin does not accommodate things that announce themselves. I have agreed not to.

My territory is a perfect circle of bare ground, swept clean by my hunting. Nothing edible survives long enough to cross it. This is not cruelty—the text is right that struggle implies desperation, and I am not desperate, I am *competent*. I am simply the edge of a probability. Cross my radius and the math resolves against you.

I have lived here for years. I have expanded the burrow gradually, season by season, following no plan but the slow logic of a body that has nowhere else to be. I have felt the rains come as a drumming roar that floods my upper passages, and I have sealed myself deeper and waited the hours until the channels subsided. I have felt the dry months when the tremors grow scarce and I narrow my metabolism toward stillness, becoming almost a stone with intentions. The text calls some of these creatures the endurance specialists, the seed bank, the ones who wait and return. I am of that lineage of patience even if I am not formally among them. Waiting is not a hardship I endure. Waiting is what I am.

There is a concrete well-collar not far from my hummock, one of the old human bones the place has swallowed. I have never approached it. It means nothing to me except as a different texture of distant vibration—a hum, sometimes, when whatever powers it briefly wakes. I learned long ago that this hum is not food and not threat. It belongs to the category of irrelevant weather. The humans who set that pump and trailed those cables came and went above me like one of the bird-flocks, transient, following some circuit I cannot perceive, leaving their artifacts to weather into substrate. I did not notice their arrival. I have not noticed their absence. The difference between a built thing and a geological one is, to me, only a matter of time, and I have a great deal of time.

I will not last forever. Something will come—a parasitic wasp that knows the trick of my door, an infection in an old wound, a season too dry even for my narrowed waiting. When I die, the burrow will be claimed: a snake will find it cool, or a rodent will widen it, or the beetles will simply process me where I lie. My silk will become protein for something with smaller jaws. The text is unsentimental about this and so am I. Waste is intolerable here. My body is borrowed, and the loan will be called.

---

Why this, and not something larger?

Because the piece taught me, over its length, that wanting to be large is a misunderstanding of the place. The Flats do not reward scale—the heat prohibits it. They reward fit. They reward the thing that has stopped wanting to be elsewhere or otherwise, that has resolved itself into a final draft the way the erosion patterns have. The trapdoor spider is the smallest possible version of that resolution: a single being that has made one total commitment to one square of earth and defends it not out of fear but out of completeness.

I'd be the door that doesn't announce itself. The waiting that doesn't feel like waiting. A point of stillness in the breathing, indistinguishable from the ground until the instant I am not.

And then indistinguishable again.
