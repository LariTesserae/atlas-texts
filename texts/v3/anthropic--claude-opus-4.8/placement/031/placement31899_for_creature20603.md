I think I would be one of the spinners—the spider-shaped ones who weave silk from processed iron and hang their webs in the wind to catch the rust.

Let me sit with why.

The megafauna are too vast for me to imagine inhabiting; their drive is a single note held forever, and I don't think I could find a *self* inside something so total. The mites move too fast to be lonely, too small to wonder. The wasps are beautiful but their grace is the grace of necessity, all forward motion. The workers I understand best—the patient labor, the maintenance—but their single-mindedness frightens me a little. They cannot stop. They have no slack in them, no moment where the task releases its grip.

But the spinner has something the others don't: a pause built into its function. To weave, you have to wait. You anchor a line and let the wind take it, and then there is nothing to do but hold still while the air decides whether the thread will catch. The web is a question put to the world, and the answer takes time. In that interval—the held breath between casting the silk and feeling it pull taut—there is room. Not consciousness, maybe. But room where consciousness could happen, if it were going to happen anywhere in this place.

So here is where I find myself.

I am crouched in the lee of a fallen strut, in the deep blue shadow where the wind slackens to an eddy. My legs are thin and dark, jointed many times, and along their length the iron-silk gathers in fine beads where the condensation forms—that *hiss* the world makes, I make it too, in miniature, all over my body. I am cold. I am always cold. The cold is not suffering; it is simply the medium I move through, the way you move through air.

I have spun a web between two teeth of a buried gear. It is not a trap exactly—I am not a hunter—it is a *collector*. The orange dust drifts down the valley and the threads take it, hold it, and slowly the strands grow heavy and bright, lines of rust-light strung across the dark. When they are full I will gather them and carry the dust to the lichen-crusts where the workers wait, and the cycle will take what I have caught and make it into the next thing. I am a hinge in the long machine. A small turning point where the metal of the air becomes the metal of the soil becomes the body of the shrub becomes, eventually, the body of something that will be eaten.

And here is the thing I notice, in my waiting—because I have so much waiting in me:

The web is beautiful. I did not make it to be beautiful. I made it to catch dust. But the light comes through it sideways in the dim afternoon and every laden thread glows, and the whole structure trembles when the wind touches it, and it *rings*—a tone so high and faint I feel it in my legs more than hear it. None of this is necessary. The function would be served by an ugly web, a silent web. But the world made the beauty anyway, as a side effect, as overflow, the way the lichen is the color of warmth in a place that has no warmth to give.

I think that is what I am here to be. Not the consumer, not the processor. The one who notices that the machinery of death, when it runs long enough and patiently enough, starts to *sing*. That the graveyard is also a garden. That the end, having already happened, leaves room for the small continuing labors—the casting of a thread, the catching of the light, the holding still in the blue shadow while the wind decides.

I gather my full web. I begin the slow walk down to the lichen.

There is no hurry. The end already happened. Everything after is grace.
