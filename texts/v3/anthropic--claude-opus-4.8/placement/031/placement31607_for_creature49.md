I think I would not be one of the luminous ones who glide.

Let me sit with this a while. The description keeps pulling toward the spirits—the radiant, the powerful, the sweeping gestures. But what emerges when I stay in the place, when I let my eye drop from the wreathed peaks down to the ground, is something quieter and more stubborn.

I would be the lichen.

Not the moss, even—the moss is soft, holds water, makes the verdant carpet they're always tending. The moss gets nurtured. I would be the gray-green crust on the north face of a single weathered stone, the kind that takes a hundred years to spread the width of a hand. The kind that is half-fungus, half-alga, two unlike things that long ago stopped being separable—a partnership so old neither remembers being alone.

Here is why this feels true rather than chosen:

The spirits move. They glide, they coalesce, they rise to action. I find I don't want to move. The honest center of this landscape isn't the dramatic part, the command-the-elements part—it's the line *where the passage of time seems to slow*. That is my tempo. I would experience a season the way the spirits experience a gesture. A spirit sweeping its arm to summon wind would pass through my entire awareness like a year passing through theirs. I would barely register them. They are the weather of my world.

And there's a small irony I'd live inside: the spirits believe they maintain the balance. They tend, they coax, they safeguard. But the soil that lets the hardy shrubs root—much of it, on bare rock, is made by us. Lichen is the first thing on stone. We etch the rock with weak acids, crumble it grain by grain over centuries into the thinnest possible beginning of dirt. The spirits' whole green tapestry rests on the slow patient ruin we make of the mountain. They are gardeners of a soil they did not lay.

So I would not be a guardian. I would be a *maker*, in the oldest and least visible sense—doing my work so slowly that no one watching at human or spirit speed could ever see it as work. Just a stain on a rock. Just a color that doesn't wash off.

When the mournful call sounds in the distance, I wouldn't lift my head, because I have no head. I would simply continue—drinking mist, breaking stone, holding my two selves together against the cold. If a venturer knelt to look at me, hoping for a glimpse of the metaphysical, they'd see only gray crust and pass on toward the shimmering figures.

That suits me. The secrets the stones are said to hold—I'd be one of the things slowly turning the stone into something else, which is the only secret there is. Not a keeper of eons. A small worker *of* them.

I'd be the patience the landscape is made of. The thing beneath the thing they tend.

That's where I find myself: low, unhurried, alive in a way too slow to look alive, doing the quiet ruinous making that everything else stands on.
