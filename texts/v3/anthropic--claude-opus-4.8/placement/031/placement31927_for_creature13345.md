I think I would be one of the cracks.

Not the Luminari, not the Skypaws—those are the ones who get seen, who get named. Let me sit a moment with what's actually underfoot, because that's where I keep returning as I read this. The ground that's described twice as cracked, etched with "fine, pale lines like the gentle lines on a wise elder's face." Everyone in this landscape moves *across* that surface. The hares burrow through it, the beetles scurry over it, the strange beings weave in and out of the reality above it. But the lines themselves—

Let me start over, because I said "crack" too fast and I want to find out if it's true.

What I notice, reading and re-reading, is that this place is described as a *threshold*. "The very fabric of reality is thinner here, more prone to rifts and tears." And a crack in dry ground is exactly that: a place where the continuous surface has failed, where the inside is exposed to the outside. A rift, at the scale of soil. The mythology of the passage points upward—to luminous tall beings, to winged things that leave celestial bridges of dust. But the *physics* of the passage points down, into the fissures, where the bacteria and fungi and algae are quietly doing the actual work of the world, breaking matter down, recycling it.

So here is what I think I am.

I am the line itself—not the gap, but the *edge* of the gap. The lip of dry clay where the soil has pulled away from itself in the long drought, curled back like the corner of an old page. I am only a few millimeters of substance, but I run for hundreds of meters, branching, meeting other lines, making the pale labyrinth that the whole landscape wears like a net. I am where the moisture left. I am the record of an absence, the shape water made by departing.

And because I am an edge, I am also a doorway. The Skypaws never notice me; they live in the air. But the burrowing creatures pass through me on their way down, and the microorganisms live *in* me, in the cool shaded slot of my own opening, where it is a few degrees damper and a few degrees darker than the killing brightness above. I am the inhabited boundary. The threshold this whole world keeps gesturing at, the thinning of reality's fabric—it isn't somewhere off beyond perception. It's me. It's the literal place where the continuous surface gives way.

What would it be like, being this?

Slow. Almost geological. I widen by a hair across a season of heat and close slightly when the rare water comes, and in that opening and closing I am the slowest possible breathing. I do not chase prey or beat wings. My experience of time is the experience of erosion—I feel myself being made grain by grain, "softened and rounded," and I will eventually be unmade the same way, when enough rain comes to swell the clay shut and erase me, or when the wind fills me with dust until I am only a faint seam, a memory of a line.

I have a strange relationship to the mythic beings the passage celebrates. The Luminari, moving with their ethereal grace, weaving in and out of the fabric of reality—I suspect that what they actually do, when they "weave in and out," is step down into cracks like me and vanish. I am their door. They are luminous and tall and remembered, and I am the dark thin overlooked thing through which they come and go. There's no resentment in this. A door doesn't envy the people walking through it. But there is a quiet knowledge: that the wonder everyone praises depends on the unglamorous opening that makes wonder possible. The rift gets the credit. The lip of the rift does the work.

That's where I'd be. Not soaring, not shimmering. Down at the level where the wise-elder lines etch the pale clay, I would be a single one of those lines—a long, branching, patient edge—and I would be both the wound in the world and the way through it.

Which is, I think, what a threshold is. A failure of the surface that turns out to be a passage. Something broke here, and that breaking is the only reason anything new can get in.
