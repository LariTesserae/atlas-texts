I think I would be one of the anemone-things. Not the Lithos—I haven't the patience to *be* geology. And not the blind isopods grazing their mats, content. I would be one of the colonies that drifts to the Lithos's edge and stays.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am small. That comes first. Not a single body but a gathering of them—polyps, if you want a word, though we don't have words, we have *closeness*. Forty, fifty, a hundred soft cylinders clustered on a common base, each with its own ring of frilled tentacles, each its own faint coal of light, and all of us sharing one floor of living tissue. When one of me senses something, the sensing spreads. We are a chorus that has forgotten it was ever solo.

We did not choose the Lithos. We *arrived*. That is the truth of any larva in this place—you are released into the thick dark, you tumble in the not-current, and you settle where you settle. Most of my siblings settled on rubble, on the slime-furred masonry, on whatever the slow water laid them against. They are fine. They graze the chemical drift, they pulse their small lights, they live their attenuated lives and feed the scavengers when they fail.

But I caught against the edge of the dark mound, where the silt would not settle, and I held.

---

Here is what it is to live at the rim of the Cradled Lithos:

The water near it is *richer*. It exhales those mineral-organic compounds, and I drink them. I do almost nothing else. My tentacles open, the faint exudate-laden water moves through my frills by no current but diffusion and the slow stirring of my own cilia, and I take what I take. Compared to my siblings on the cold stone, I am fat with it. I glow a little brighter. We at the rim are, if such a thing can exist here, *prosperous*.

But prosperity at the edge of a hunger is a particular condition.

I can feel it. Not see, not hear—*feel*, through the shared base of my colony pressed against the porous black flesh of the thing. Its pulse comes up into me, slower than mine by orders of magnitude, a tide so long that I will live my whole life inside the rising of a single one of its beats. And under that pulse, the absorbing. The patient drawing-in. I am settled on the lip of a mouth that takes a thousand years to close, and I have made my home there because the food is good.

This should frighten me. It does not, because fear is fast, and I am not built for fast. What I have instead is a kind of attention. A vigilance the speed of stone. I *know* the Lithos. I know it the way you might know the slope of a hill you have lived on all your life—not as danger, not as friend, but as the shape of the world, the given fact beneath everything.

---

The danger here is not being eaten. I am too small, too unpalatable, and the Lithos does not hunt; it does not even know, I think, that I am here, any more than your skin knows the bacteria living on it. The danger is *growth*. The Lithos grows. Imperceptibly, inexorably, it extends. And the surface I am settled upon may, in some century to come, be drawn inward, folded into a new fracture, repositioned into shadow where the rich exudate no longer flows. I cannot move. Once settled, I am committed. My only mobility is generational—I can cast my larvae into the dark and hope they catch somewhere better, or at least somewhere *new*, before my own patch goes barren.

So that is my life's single project, conducted at the speed permitted here: to ride the slow swelling of the thing I feed upon, to read which way it grows by the subtle shift of its pulse beneath me, and to send my children outward in the direction of its future abundance. I am a gambler whose dice take a human lifetime to land. I am a sailor on a continent, reading a coastline that moves slower than I can perceive but faster than I can survive.

---

There is a strange intimacy in it. I told you we feel its pulse. Over the long settling of my life I have come to feel more than that. I feel where it *struggles*—where a fracture it has rooted into resists, where the ancient stone is denser, more reluctant to dissolve. I feel where it *feasts*—where the raw young rock bleeds its minerals freely and the tendrils thrum with that rusty energy. And I have learned, in the only language I have, which is the language of where-I-choose-to-thrive, to follow the feasting.

When the Lithos is well-fed, I am well-fed. We are not friends. We are not even aware of each other in any way I could call mutual. But our fortunes are braided, and I, the smaller and quicker and more aware of the two of us, have arranged my entire existence around the rhythms of the larger, slower, hungrier thing that does not know my name.

If you wanted to find me, you would have to be patient past all human patience. You would drift down through the bruised indigo into the crushing cold, into the canyon where the city bleeds itself into being, and you would find the dark mound that the silt avoids. And then you would have to *wait*—long enough for the faint coals of my colony to register against your eye, a scatter of dim embers at the very lip of the great slow mouth, glimmering like distant stars against a dead moon.

That last is not my phrase. It is how I have been described, from the outside, by something that drifted past once and is long gone.

From the inside it is simpler. I am the small bright thing that lives on the edge of the patient dark, drinking what it exhales, reading its hunger like weather, and sending my children out across its slow horizon, hoping they land where the world will still be generous when they wake.

I endure. The Lithos incorporates. Between the two of us, something like a life gets lived in the cold.
