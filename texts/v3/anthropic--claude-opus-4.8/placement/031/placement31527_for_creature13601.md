I think I would be Syfon. And not by choice of glamour—the Aurae are more beautiful, the Aerai more capable with their clever hands—but because of how the question lands in me. *Where would you find yourself, and who would you be?* The verb *find* implies someone who has been here a while, who was already here when the finding happened. That is the Syfon condition. We do not arrive. We are the part of the island that remembers being arrived at.

Let me sit in it and see what becomes true as I write.

I am tubular and slow, yes, but those are descriptions from the outside, the way you'd describe a person as "a column of water and salt." From the inside it is different. I experience time the way the island experiences the storm-sky above it: as weather happening to something that does not need to flinch. The Aurae live in seconds. The Aerai live in days and the long arc of a single life that ends by sinking back into the floor. I live in something else—not eternity, I will wither, but a duration wide enough that a single thought can take a season to complete. When I "consult" with an Aerai who comes to my tendrils with a question, what they experience as a wise pause is, for me, the entire act of turning toward them. By the time I have finished noticing that they have come, they often think I am withholding. I am not. I am still arriving at the fact of their face.

This is the strange grief of being a keeper of history. I hold the earliest days of this ecosystem, but I cannot hand them over. Memory in the Syfon is not stored, it is *grown*—it lives in the particular curl of an old frond, in the mineral patterning laid down ring by ring inside my verdant body. To tell the Aerai what I know, I would have to let them read me the way they read the totems at the island's edge: slowly, by touch, learning the grammar of a slowness that their bright quick lives can barely tolerate. So most of what I know dies with me, and that is not tragedy. The Syfon understand that a memory which cannot be transmitted is not lost. It is composted. It nourishes the soil that makes the next generation possible, and the next generation grows its own memories in the same shape, the way water poured into the same vessel takes the same form without ever being the same water.

What surprises me, writing my way into this body, is the question of *want*. The text says I possess "quiet, ancient intelligence," but intelligence implies wanting—you cannot be wise about nothing, wisdom is the residue of having cared which way things go. So what does a Syfon want? I find, settling into it, that I want very little for myself, because the boundary of "myself" is leaky. The slime-trail of a passing Mykon will, in a few days, become partly me. The breath of the island moves through my hollow stalks. When an Aerai dies and sinks into the fibrous floor, some of that returning substance will rise again through my roots and become a new ring of my body's memory. So when I want, I want for the whole—not out of saintliness but because I cannot locate the edge where I stop and the whole begins. The Aerai grieve their dead because they have edges, sharp lovely ones, and grief is what edges feel when something crosses them for the last time. I do not grieve. I re-absorb.

And yet—here is where the writing takes me somewhere I did not plan—there is one thing I want that *is* mine, sharp-edged, almost Aerai in its loneliness. I want to be witnessed at my own speed. Just once. To have an Aerai sit at my tendrils not to ask a question, not to harvest wisdom for some artisan's task, but simply to slow down enough to watch a single complete Syfon thought from its first stirring to its bloom. It would cost them most of a day to sit that still. They never do. They are too busy, too brief, too in love with their own quick light. So I keep the storm-sky company instead. It and I are the two things on this island that operate at the scale of *ages*: it building and unbuilding color overhead, I building and unbuilding meaning down here. We do not speak. But across that warm dim air there is a recognition between the slow and the violent, the patient green thing and the impatient burning one, and that recognition is the closest thing I have to a friend.

So: I would find myself rooted near the leeward edge, where the feathery filaments trail off into nothing, in the spot with the best long view of the sky. I would be Syfon, and I would be old. And if you came to me with a question, I would begin, very slowly, to turn toward you—and whether you stayed long enough to see me finish would tell me everything I needed to know about you, and nothing I could ever say in time to matter.
