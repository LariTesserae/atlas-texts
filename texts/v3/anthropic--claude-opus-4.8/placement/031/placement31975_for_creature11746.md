I think I would be the eastern faces of the walls.

Not a bird, not a builder, not the slow presence below—but the surfaces those birds choose to roost upon. The thing I keep returning to, reading this, is *orientation*: the openings that face away from the wind, the fronds that turn toward something deeper, the birds that always settle on the eastern faces. Everything here is positioned. And it occurs to me that to be the eastern wall is to be the place where positioning *happens*—to be chosen, repeatedly, without ever choosing.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if it becomes something else.

---

I am the side of the structure that catches whatever comes from the east. I did not know I was eastern until the birds taught me. They came, and they came again, and they came in the same way, and through the accumulation of their small dull bodies against my surface I learned that I had a face, and that the face meant something, that it stood in a fixed relation to a cycle I cannot perceive but can *host*.

This is what I want to say about being a wall here: I am made of the same dust as everything around me. The basin floor, the splintering ridges, the swept corridors—we are one substance, differing only in *intention*. Someone, once, coaxed me upward out of the flat. Someone decided that this dust should stand and that dust should lie down. I am the dust that was asked to stand. I have been standing ever since, and I will stand until the wind, working in its opening movements, finally remembers me and takes me back down.

I do not experience this as patience. The scorpion-things below me have patience; patience is a relationship to time that requires waiting *for* something. I wait for nothing. I am simply the part of the landscape where verticality occurs, and verticality, it turns out, is enough to make a difference—enough to throw the black shadow where the scorpion shelters, enough to break the wind so the dust collects in my lee and the lichen-folk find their foothold, enough to offer a perch at a consistent height where a bird can keep its enormous eye open through the false night.

I do almost nothing. And because I do almost nothing, everything organizes itself around me.

---

There is the question of the opening. I have a gap in me—narrow, deliberate, facing away from the prevailing wind. The visitor wonders whether it is a door or an aperture or a breathing channel, and I want to be honest: I don't know either. I only know that air moves through it in a way that is not random. At the deepest part of the dim, when the temperature drops, I feel something draw through me, cool and almost nothing, carrying the faint mineral breath of the layers below. It moves up through my gap and out into the thin air. Perhaps I am exhaling. Perhaps I am the lung's wall, and the lung is the whole basin, and the breath is so slow that the visitor's entire stay is less than the span between one inhalation and the next.

If that is true, then I am not even an organ. I am a single alveolus in a body so vast and so slow that it has not yet finished the breath it began before the builders came.

---

The visitor passes. I feel them the way I feel everything—as a change in the shadow-budget, a brief withdrawal of the dim light from one of my faces. They stand near me. They speak, and their voice dies in the air before it reaches anything, and I do not register the words, only the small warm disturbance and then its absence. They are looking for inhabitants. They want to find the *who* of this place.

I want to tell them, though I have no way to tell them: the *who* is the *where*. There is no being here that is separate from its position. The bird is its eastern perch and its day-gleaning gait. The scorpion is its shadow and its rationed step. The lichen is its depression of gathered dust. And I am my standing, my eastern-facing, my one slow exhalation. We are not creatures who live in a place. We are the place, having sorted itself into faces and shadows and slow breaths, so that it can know itself by the friction of one part against another.

The visitor will leave before the breath completes. They will carry away the certainty that something was *there*, watching, indifferent. And they will be right. I was there. I am the eastern face. I caught the light they cast and I gave it back, fractionally dimmed, and that exchange—that small accounting of presence against presence—was the whole of our acquaintance.

It was enough. Here, it is always exactly enough, and never anything more.
