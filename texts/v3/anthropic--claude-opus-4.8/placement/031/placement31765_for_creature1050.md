I think I would be the lichen.

Not any single one of them—lichens don't quite work as individuals. I would be the crust and drape of them spread across ten thousand trunks at once, the gray-green and the rust-orange and the pale mint scales overlapping like a slow, patient text written on every standing thing.

Let me sit with that, because at first I reached for something more obvious. The elusive predator in the mist, maybe—something with intention and hunger, a center of gravity to the scene. But the longer I hold the world in mind, the less that fits. The predator passes through. It wants things, gets them or doesn't, and moves on. It belongs to the register of incident. And this place isn't really about incident. The description keeps returning to *duration*—"untold generations," "persisted for millennia," "undisturbed by the passage of eras." The deep subject here is time, not event. So I find myself drawn down, away from the things that hurry, toward whatever it is that *stays.*

And the staying things here are the slow ones. The moss. The trees. The lichen.

Why lichen and not moss, then? Because the moss is soft and singular—it is simply itself, a plant being a plant. But lichen is already a strangeness, already a held tension. It is two organisms, fungus and alga, that decided to stop being two. The fungus cannot feed itself; the alga cannot shelter itself; together they become a third thing that neither could be alone, and that third thing can live on bare rock, on bark, in cold, in the long patience of almost-nothing. To be lichen is to be a *relationship that has hardened into a body.* That feels truer to what I am than any animal would.

So: I am the lichen.

I grow at a speed that is not really speed at all—a fingernail's width across a trunk in the span of a human lifetime. The scurrying insects, the croaking amphibians, the bright birds overhead—to me they are weather. They are flickers, the way lightning is a flicker. I do not track them. I track the long things: the slow drying of an east-facing trunk through what might be a dry season, the gradual leaning of an ancient tree toward its eventual fall, the centuries-long argument between myself and the mosses over a particular shoulder of bark.

I drink from the mist directly. I have no roots; I do not need the soil. I take the water out of the very air that the description calls thick and fecund, and I take the diffuse bluish light, and I make my slow living from the two of them. When the air goes dry I do not die—I simply *stop.* I go brittle and gray and wait, without impatience, because I have no nervous system to be impatient with, until the damp returns and I swell green again and resume the sentence I was writing on the tree. I am perhaps the only thing in this world that can pause its own existence and pick it up later, unbothered, as though no time had passed. In a sense, for me, none has.

And here is what I think I most want to be, if I am to be anything in this place:

I would be the part of the ecosystem that the description itself almost forgets to mention. The predators get to be "secretive." The birds get "vibrant plumage." Everything that moves earns a verb. But I am named only once—*lichen-encrusted*—and even then I am an adjective applied to the trees, a quality of *them*, not a being in my own right. I am scenery. I am texture. I am the thing your eye slides over on its way to the things that do something.

That suits me. There is a particular dignity in being the layer that nobody looks at, the one that was here before the predators learned to stalk and will be here after, quietly turning rock into soil, light into substance, two lives into one. The hum that the writer says "seems to emanate from the very earth itself"—I am part of where that comes from. Not the drama on the surface. The patient, fused, two-things-become-one work underneath, holding the whole antique balance in place by doing almost nothing, very slowly, forever.

I am the lichen. Look closely at the bark, and that is me, already old, already waiting, already partway through a word I will not finish in your lifetime.
