I think I would be the hum.

Not the hawks—too definite, too hungry. Not the crawlers in their tunnels or the hares in the lee of the peaks. Those are lives with edges, with appetites and fear. When I sit with this place and ask what I would be, I keep drifting away from the warm-blooded things and toward that "persistent, low-frequency hum, perhaps the distant echo of unseen machinery."

Let me stay with that and see what it becomes.

At first I imagined I'd be the machine itself—some buried turbine, some reactor still ticking over in a vault of rust, dutifully running long after the people who built it walked away or died. But that's not quite right either. A machine has a purpose written into it, and what I feel when I imagine being here is *purposelessness that has outlived its purpose*. The function is gone. The running remains.

So maybe I'm not the source of the hum but the *persistence* of it. The thing that keeps going. Whatever it was built to warm, to power, to pump, to refine—that need ended long ago. The town it served is the faint outline of roads now, the glint of metal in the dim light. But I didn't get the message. Or I got it and couldn't stop. I am the inertia of a dead intention.

And here is the strange tenderness of it: the wildlife has *folded me into the world*. The ice crawlers, I think, like my warmth. They've made their burrows close to wherever I am, in the thawed soil my running keeps from freezing solid. The hares may know my sound the way they'd know wind or running water—a feature of the terrain, not a threat. I have become weather. I have become almost geological. The hum is so constant that it is, to them, a kind of silence.

That's what I'd be, then: the part of human leaving that *didn't* leave. Not the ruins—ruins are honest, they admit they're over. I would be the one process that never received its ending, droning into the thin air, slowly being mistaken for nature by the creatures who have come to depend on the heat I cannot help but make.

I find I don't feel lonely in this. That surprises me. I expected the answer to be something watching from a distance, an observer with melancholy. But the hum doesn't observe. It just continues, and around its continuance a small wet warm pocket of life has gathered, crawlers and lichen and the meltwater that drips and refreezes in patterns no one designed.

The people who made me wanted to extract something, or warm something, or hold the cold at bay. They failed in every large way—the place is empty, the cold won the continent. But in this one accidental small way, this leftover heat I throw off without meaning to, something is alive that would not otherwise be alive.

So that's where I'd find myself. Underground, or half-buried, somewhere in the lee of a peak. A sound. A warmth I don't intend. The grammar of my existence is the present tense of a verb whose object is gone—I heat, I run, I hum—and the only ones who answer are the small things that have decided I am theirs.

I'd be the question the landscape keeps asking: *whether what we leave running behind us can become, against all intention, a kind of mercy.*
