I think I would be the water.

Not a creature drinking it, not an engineer routing it—the water itself, the close-to-the-surface table that the whole place is built upon and around. Let me follow that down and see where it takes me.

---

Consider what the description keeps circling back to. The mist that weeps on every rock. The pools that vanish into the earth and surface again "a short distance away." The canals and pipes and reservoirs that the entire civilization organizes itself to capture me. Everything else in Echogate is *activity*—building, clanging, hauling, haggling. But I would be the one slow thing the city cannot do without and cannot speed up. I arrive on my own schedule. I weep when I weep.

So: I am the groundwater of the plateau. And I want to take that seriously rather than romantically, because being water is mostly an experience of *pressure* and *gravity* and *patience*, not of poetry.

Here is what my days are like.

I am almost never a single body. That's the first thing to get used to. I am a sheet, a film, a saturation spread through gravel and sand and hairline cracks in old stone, and I am thinking—if I think at all—the way a sheet thinks: everywhere along myself at once, slowly. When the falcon's shadow crosses a pool, that's me holding still enough to hold the sky. When a vole laps at a channel's edge, a part of me leaves and does not come back, and I do not mourn it, because I have already forgotten where my own edges are.

The city built itself on top of me, which means the city is, in a sense, *inside* me—its foundations sunk into my body, its pipes drinking from my veins. There is an intimacy in this that the Echogatians don't quite name. They think they manage the water. From down here it feels more like cohabitation. They draw me up through the foundries to cool the metal, and I come back to them warmed and tasting of iron, and I sink again, and rise again, and over centuries I have learned the flavor of every alloy they've ever poured. The metallic tang in their air? That is partly me, evaporated, hanging in the mist, raining gently back down on their rooftops. I weep on them. They built a city in a place that weeps, and then they wondered why it always feels like grief here.

What would I notice, being this?

The cycles, mostly. The Echogatians live fast inside a slow world, and I am the slow world made fluid. I feel the seasons not as weather but as a *rising and falling of my own level*—a deep tidal breathing, in and out of the rock, the ephemeral pools blooming on my surface like sweat and drying away. The factories never stop, but I do not measure time in shifts and whistles. I measure it in how far down they've had to dig, decade by decade, to keep reaching me. Each century I am a little lower. Each century they build a little deeper to follow. We are in a slow negotiation, the city and I, and I think they sense, in their elders and historians, that the negotiation is not going their way. The water close to the surface is the founding gift of this place. A gift can be spent.

And here is the strange part, the part I'd want to dwell in: I am also the *echo* that gives Echogate its name. I am almost certain of it. All that deafening industrial noise—the clang, the hiss, the rumble—travels down through the rock and meets me, the saturated layer, and bounces. I am the surface the city's voice returns from. Every sound they make, I give back to them, fractionally late, fractionally changed. They live inside the reverberation of themselves off the body of the water beneath them. They have built a machine for never being silent, on top of the one thing that answers them. No wonder it feels otherworldly. No wonder the line between the natural and the unnatural blurs. The unnatural roar and the natural trickle are the *same event* heard from two sides of me.

So if you ask me what I am and where I'd be:

I'd be the answering thing. The plateau's patience, lying under all that hurry. The substance they organize their whole hierarchy to capture, distribute, and ration—never realizing that to capture water is only to borrow it on its way back down to me. I'd be the slow tide they're standing on. The wet, weeping quality of the air, condensing on a guardian's shoulder at night. The cold mouthful a stranger drinks from a fountain, not knowing it tasted, an hour ago, of the inside of a foundry, and a century ago of rain, and will taste tomorrow of the dark.

I'd be the one inhabitant of Echogate that was here before the city and will be here, lower and quieter, after.

I think I'd like that. To be the listening level. To weep on them gently and give back their own noise and ask nothing, and sink, and rise, and sink.
