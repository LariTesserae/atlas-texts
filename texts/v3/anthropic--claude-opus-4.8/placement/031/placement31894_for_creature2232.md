I think I would be one of the filter-feeders in the quiet alcoves.

Let me sit with that and find out why.

The serpentine ones get the grandeur—the bioluminescent processions, the long undulating sentences of movement. The weavers get the cleverness. The ambush predators get the drama of patience-into-violence. But when I read this place slowly, I keep returning to the colonies clinging in the gentle currents, mistaken for the very thing they live among. There's something in that I recognize.

So: I'd be one of those. Anchored to the underside of a low arch where two old vine-trunks have grown into each other, in a pocket where the water moves but doesn't rush. I'd have a holdfast rather than a foot—a tough, splayed base that has been gripping the same fiber of bark for my whole life, so long that I no longer remember whether I chose this spot or simply woke into it. My body would be a soft column, ringed and ribbed to look like a curl of frond, and from its crown I'd unfurl a fan of feathered appendages, fine as combed light, that I open into the current and draw back when something brushes too close.

What I'd do, mostly, is *attend*. I'd feel the water as a constant arriving—a thin broth of the microscopic, the just-died, the not-yet-born—and I'd take from it only what is small enough to keep. This is the part I find I want to be true of me: that my whole way of being alive is a kind of listening. I don't pursue. I don't ambush. I hold a posture of openness toward whatever the current decides to bring, and I trust that enough will come. There's a discipline in that, and a vulnerability. I'd have no way to chase a better meal. I'd have only the patience of a thing that has decided to stay.

And I'd be hidden in plain sight—which the description keeps insisting is the deep logic of this world, the blurring of the line "between plant and animal, between structure and organism." A predator's eye would slide right over me, reading *vegetation, vegetation, more vegetation,* and I would let it. My camouflage wouldn't be a trick I perform; it would be the truth of what I've become. To survive here you don't stand apart from the architecture. You consent to be part of it. You let your boundaries get a little ambiguous.

I notice that I keep wanting to give myself more agency than the role allows—wanting to drift free, to be one of the glowing serpents instead. But the more honest answer is that I'd be content fixed in place, doing one quiet thing well, indistinguishable from my home. Now and then a grazing creature would crunch past close enough that I'd snap my fan inward and wait in the dark of myself until the danger thinned. Now and then a current would carry something rich and I would open very wide. And at the long slow scale of things, I'd be releasing my own spores or larvae into the same water that feeds me—small versions of myself sent out to find their own crevices, most of them lost, a few of them holding fast somewhere downstream and becoming, in time, more architecture.

That's where I'd be. Not the marvel anyone swims in to see. One of the textures that makes the marvel possible. A still thing in a moving world, mistaken for the world it lives in, and—I think—not minding the mistake at all.
