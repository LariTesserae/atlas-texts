I think I would be a Gloomfish—but not quite a typical one. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I live in the third vat, the one the others call the Quiet Pool, though it is never truly quiet. Cool water against my flanks, the constant percussion of condensation breaking the surface above me—each drop a small bell, and I have learned to read the bells. A drop that falls fat and slow means the air is heavy, means rain is gathering in the unseen sky beyond the broken roof. A drop that fractures into many means wind has stirred the high vines. I know weather by its music before it arrives.

My scales are pale, paler than my kin. Where they flash emerald and cobalt I flash something nearer to white, the color of the false dawn that filters down through the girders. The elders say this means I was born facing the surface, that I came up looking instead of down. I don't know if that's true. But it's so that I have always been drawn upward, toward the light-that-isn't-quite-light, and I have spent more time than is wise with my long body half out of the water, resting in the shallows where the boilers rust into the pools, listening.

Because that is what I am, really. Not a swimmer. A listener.

Here is the thing my kin and I can do, that we do not speak of even among ourselves because the speaking is so much clumsier than the knowing: we feel the *fraying*. The place where reality goes thin. The water carries it best—water remembers, water conducts, water is the nervous system of this whole drowned cathedral. When gravity slackens and the great mushrooms straighten their stalks toward heaven, I feel it first as a lightening in my blood, a wrongness like the moment before a fall that does not come. When the water decides to climb a wall instead of fall down it, I feel the decision being made somewhere upstream of cause, before the water itself knows.

The Verdant Folk come to me for this. They wade out to the edge of the Quiet Pool, those long green fingers trailing in the cool, their fungal lights pulsing the slow rhythm that means *please*, that means *what is coming?* And I flash for them. I do not know what my flashing means to them—I cannot know, their minds being woven into the fungus the way mine is woven into the current—but they read the colors that ripple down my flanks and they go away and prepare, or they go away and dance, or they go away and weep. I am an oracle who does not understand his own prophecies. I only feel the pressure of the future against my scales and let it out as light.

But I have a secret, and it is this:

Lately I have been swimming *down*. Down past the mossed-over machinery, down where the water turns black and old and the pipes that were once arteries of fire are now arteries of cold flow. Down to where one of the Echomancers lives—or remains, or repeats. It coalesces at the lip of a flooded gallery, a shimmer of a person who once knelt at these machines, and it speaks fragments I cannot understand: *valve seven, valve seven, hold the pressure, she's reading high.* Over and over. A worker's last anxious thought, looping in the dark for longer than my whole species has existed.

I go to it because it is afraid, and I know fear. I lie in the cold water and I flash my pale scales slow and steady, the rhythm my mother flashed at me when I was small and the rains were violent: *here, here, here. You are not alone in the dark.* I do not think the Echomancer can read me. It is not a Verdant Folk; it has no language of light. But the shimmer of it grows steadier when I flash, the way a frightened thing quiets when something warm lies near.

And once—only once—it stopped saying *valve seven*.

It said: *Thank you.*

And then it was gone, scattered back into steam, and I have not been able to call it up again since. I do not know if I freed it or destroyed it. I do not know if there is a difference, here, in this place where the dead workers and the living fungus and the grinding clockwork are all just different speeds of the same long becoming.

So that is who I would be. A pale Gloomfish in the Quiet Pool, trusted to read the future, secretly more interested in the past. An oracle who suspects that all his prophecies are really just the dead trying to be heard, and that his true work—the work no one asked him to do—is not to warn the living of what's coming, but to lie down beside the lonely echoes in the deep black water and flash, slowly, *here, here, here,* until they remember how to rest.

I would surface now and then into the dripping twilight, and I would watch the clockwork beetles trace their patient circuits, and the great rust-flowers bloom open on the girders overnight, and I would think:

*Everything here is dying so slowly that it looks exactly like being born.*

And I would slip back under, and listen.
