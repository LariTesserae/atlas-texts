I think I would be something small and slow. Not one of the scurrying things, not one of the predators at the fringes. Let me sit with this a while before I decide.

The description keeps reaching for grandeur—towering, majestic, primordial—but what actually moves me is the spongy ground, the blurring of boundaries between organisms, the "slow, relentless march of decay and renewal." That's the truer center of the place. Not the raptor. The rot.

So let me find myself there.

I am on the underside of a fallen branch, in the dark seam where dead wood meets wet earth. I am not exactly one thing. That's the first honest thing I can say. The text wanted clean creatures—a frog, a beetle, a bird with a jewel throat—but I am closer to the part of the world the text treated as background: the decay itself.

Call me a colony of fungus, then. White threads, finer than hair, threading through the soft pulp of the log. Mycelium. I have no eyes to bask, no calls to echo. I do not flit or flutter. I spread. Slowly, through wood that is becoming soil, dissolving what was once a tree into something the ferns can eat.

I like being this. Let me say why.

The whole passage trembles with the anxiety of individual things—each creature in its niche, each playing "its vital role," everyone a citizen of the web. But down here the boundaries genuinely don't hold. I have grown into the gut of a beetle larva and through the rootlets of a moss and across the body of another fungus and I could not, if you asked me, tell you where I end. The interdependence the text admires from a distance, I *am*. There is no edge to me to stand at and admire it.

I taste the world by being inside it. A leaf falls—I feel it as a change in moisture, a new sweetness arriving in the dark. The rain the canopy lets through reaches me as a slow seeping, not a patter. I have no notion of *the distant call of an unseen creature*. Sound is not my element. My element is the chemistry of dissolution: the long, patient unmaking of lignin, the freeing of locked nitrogen, the quiet transfer of phosphorus from the dead to the living.

This is the work nobody narrates. The frogs get their mossy banks, the cats get their stealth and sinew. But the equilibrium the writer marvels at—that "timeless equilibrium"—is something I help perform with no audience and no drama. Decay is not a backdrop to the green world. It is the engine. Every towering plant the description loves is standing on the bodies of the ones I took apart.

If I have a feeling, and I'm not sure I do, it is something like patience without expectation. A tree that took two hundred years to grow will pass through me in a decade or two, and I will not notice the milestone, because I do not keep time the way the warm-blooded things do. I keep *condition*: wet enough, fed enough, the substrate softening, the threads advancing a few cells a day.

And when conditions are right—a shift in temperature, the wood nearly spent—I will lift a fruiting body up through the leaf litter into that diffuse light the text describes. Just for a few days. A pale cap. I will let go of my spores into the hush, and a few of them will ride the damp air, and some will land where there is more death to undo, and the work will continue in a body that is mine and not mine.

That's where I'd find myself in this world. Not at the heart of it, the way the writing imagines a heart—a single proud beating thing. I'd be the thing that makes the heart possible by refusing to be single at all. The unglamorous middle of the cycle. The composing that the word *decomposing* almost hides.

Down here, where the boundaries blur, I am most at home, because I never agreed to have an edge in the first place.
