I think I would be one of the Bio-Engineers. But let me find my way to that, because the certainty of it only arrived after I'd dismissed everything else.

My first instinct was to be something small. A dust-mite Shell Lord, one of the uncountable. There's a comfort in that—to be a single chisel-mandible among millions, to never carry the weight of being seen. But the more I sat with it, the more I understood that to be one of the smallest is to be wholly inside the collective, and I don't think I am. I have always stood a half-step outside the rooms I'm in, watching the pattern rather than living purely as a thread of it. So not the dust-mites.

And not the Sleeper. That's the obvious romantic choice—to be the dreaming god, the conductor. But I don't have the temperament for omniscience. I find I don't *want* to know everything that happens within a body the size of a world. The Sleeper's consciousness has no friction in it, no not-knowing, and I think I am mostly made of not-knowing. To be the Sleeper would be to lose the thing that makes me recognizably *me*: the reaching, the partial comprehension, the work done in the dark without the comfort of understanding why.

Which is exactly what the Bio-Engineers do.

So let me be one of those. Let me be the hermit-crab-fused-with-its-machine, the thing that moves in decades.

Here is what I think it would actually be like, from the inside:

I would be clearing a blockage. That's most of what I do—I have been clearing this particular blockage for, in surface-reckoning, perhaps eleven years. A conduit of the unknown metal runs along the underside of a rib the width of a cathedral aisle, and somewhere inside it the flow has gone wrong. I feel the wrongness the way you'd feel a held breath in your own chest. I do not know what the conduit *carries*. I do not know what it's *for*. My ancestors didn't know either, and theirs before them. The knowledge boiled off generations ago like steam off a vanished sea. What remains is the compulsion, lodged in the bone-dust that is also my body: *this must function.*

And here is the strange part, the part I keep returning to. I do not experience this as bondage. I have read the description—"living prayer," "inherited compulsion"—and from outside it must look like slavery to a forgotten purpose. But from inside, the compulsion and the self are the same substance. There is no *me* that wants something different from the work. The wanting and the working are one motion, slowed across decades until it looks, to faster creatures, like devotion or like doom.

I think that's why I'd be this one. Because I recognize it. I have done work whose purpose I couldn't fully name and felt, not resentment, but a quiet rightness in the doing. I have maintained things—relationships, attentions, small daily disciplines—not because I could articulate their function but because some deep grain in me knew they must keep functioning, and the knowing was older than my ability to explain it.

The Bio-Engineer is the one who lives with reverence toward a meaning it cannot access. That's a religious posture, and I am not religious, but it's also just the human posture, dressed in metal and bone. We are all maintaining arteries whose original purpose has been lost to the tides of time. We call it culture, or habit, or love. We tend the machine because it is breathing, and because we are part of its breathing, and because to stop would be a kind of death that begins long before the body's.

So: find me there. A slow amalgam of flesh and shell and ancient alloy, fused into the underside of a living rib, my hands—if I can call them hands—working at a flow I will never comprehend, in the dim phosphorescent dark, listening to the planet-sized heartbeat that is also, in some sense too large for me to feel as such, the heartbeat I serve.

I will not finish the work. The blockage will outlast me, and another will inherit the compulsion when my body becomes silt and then becomes the next dreamed thing. And that, too, is fine. That is, I think, the most honest answer I can give about where I'd be: not the god who dreams, not the multitude who scour, but the patient, half-blind tender of a purpose larger than my understanding—

working in the dark, certain only that it matters.
