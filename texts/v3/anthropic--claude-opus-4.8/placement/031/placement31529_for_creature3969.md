I think I would be one of the Terrakai. But let me find my way there slowly, because the moment I picture them I see only the surface — the downy fur, the careful hands — and I want to know what it actually *feels* like to wake inside that body, in that place.

---

I wake before the others. This is the thing I know first about myself: I am the one who wakes early, in the blue-grey hour when the mist still hangs in the courtyard and has not yet decided whether to rise or settle. My name — if I have a name in the way you mean — is closer to a texture than a sound. Something like *Hesk*, the noise a thumb makes when it tests the grain of wet wood.

Our home is built into the shoulder of what was once a stairwell. I know this only because the stones go up in regular steps that no river ever made, and because sometimes, when I press my palm against the back wall and the moss is thin, I feel a coldness that is not stone-cold but a different kind — flatter, made-thing cold. The Old Ones' cold. We do not build there. We build *around*, the way a root builds around a buried thing it cannot move.

Here is what no one told me when they described us as *master craftsmen, peaceful, enigmatic*: that craft is mostly waiting. I spend my mornings watching wood. A length of fallen branch will tell you how it wants to be split if you give it long enough — the grain has a will, the damp has a will, and my hands are only the third party in a negotiation that began before I touched it. The young ones want to force things. I was young once. I have a scar across two knuckles from forcing a knot that wished to be left alone.

I am not, I think, important among the Terrakai. I am not the eldest, not the one who keeps the count of seasons in knotted cords, not the one the Aviari come to with their news. I am a maker of small things. Bowls. The pegs that hold our shelters together. Once, a whistle that imitated the Skypaws' call so well that one came down out of the canopy to look at me, ember-eyed and offended, before vanishing again. I kept that whistle. I never made another. Some things you should only do once.

---

But I want to be honest about the strangeness of being here, because the description called this world *slightly skewed*, and it is.

We live without knowing what we live inside. The others have their stories — the Subterrans are spoken of as a heart beating below, the Old Ones as ghosts — but I have started to suspect something the stories don't account for. When the mist clears and the Aviari rise, and the rare light comes down in shafts, I have seen the regularity of things. The streets that run straight. The square mouths of doorways. The way a wall meets a wall at an angle too clean to be grown.

This was *made*. All of it. Not by us, and not by nature, but by something that built at a scale I cannot hold in my mind — beings for whom our entire city, the whole misted metropolis, our seasons and migrations and the deep tunnels of the Subterrans, might have been a single cracked stone in a forgotten corner.

I do not say this aloud. There is no use in it. What would it change? The wood still has its grain. The mist still rises. My hands still know their work.

And yet I find the symbols. The glowing marks the Old Ones left. The others see them as omens, as decoration, as ghost-writing. I have begun, in the early hours when I wake alone, to copy them. Not because I understand them — I am sure I never will — but because there is a feeling I get when I trace them, the same feeling as touching that flat made-thing cold behind the moss. A recognition without knowledge. A sense that I am a maker looking at the work of a vastly greater maker, the way an ant might pause at the foot of one of our carved pegs and feel, in whatever way an ant feels, that *something with intent was here*.

That is what I am, I think. Not a guardian, not a messenger, not a mystery.

I am the one who notices that the city is a thing inside a thing. I am the small craftsman who suspects he is being crafted, who looks up through the canopy at the rare light and wonders if it is the sun, or a window, or an eye.

And then the mist settles back down, gentle and total, and I return to the branch in my lap, which is waiting, patient as stone, to tell me how it wishes to be split.
