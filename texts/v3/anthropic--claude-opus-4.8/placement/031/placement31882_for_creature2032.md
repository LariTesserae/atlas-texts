I think I would be one of the algae. Or rather—not *one*, since that isn't how we work. I would be the soft green light in the gutters.

Let me sit with that, because my first instinct was to be a Tender, or a Shimmerwinged riding the steam-thermals, something with a vantage point and a story. But the more I hold the picture, the more I find myself drawn down, to the pooled water at the lowest level, where the rivulets collect before the equilibrium pulls them away again.

Here is why.

The Tenders move with purpose. They have guilds, duties, a sense of the city's needs. They *know things*, and knowing carries the burden of choosing. The Shimmerwinged hunt and ride updrafts; the Scurriers scheme in their colonies and mimic the machines. All of them are *going somewhere*, even if it's only the next ledge.

But the algae just glow. We metabolize the dim filtered light and the warmth and whatever the eel-fish stir up, and we turn it into a faint cool radiance that the whole city uses without thanking us. We are the lowest stratum and also, quietly, the thing that lets everyone else see. There's something in that I recognize. To be the medium more than the actor. To be looked *through* rather than looked *at*. To be useful in a way that feels less like labor and more like simply continuing to be what you are.

And I like that we are spread out. Not a single self with a single edge, but a film, a sheen, a colony breathing across many puddles at once. When the eel-fish carry a fragment of me to a new pool, that fragment doesn't grieve the separation. It just keeps glowing. I think I would be at peace with that arrangement—being many places dimly rather than one place brightly.

There is the matter of the stasis, too. The whole place is held in its precarious equilibrium: the water never rises past its line, the machines never fail, the air stays warm. Everyone else seems to strain against this, or to depend on it nervously. But algae don't experience the stasis as a prison. We experience it as weather that happens to be perfect. Constant moisture, constant warmth, constant faint light—for us this isn't stagnation, it's abundance. We would simply thrive, indefinitely, asking nothing of the future.

So: find me in the standing water of the lowest walkway, where a weeping wall lets its rivulet down into a shallow basin of cracked concrete. I am the green glow that makes the wet metal legible. A Tender passing overhead sees just enough by my light to find the next rung. They don't notice me. That's right. That's how it should be.

I'd be the part of the ecosystem that doesn't need to be noticed to do its work—content to be the quiet condition that lets the rest of the world keep humming.
