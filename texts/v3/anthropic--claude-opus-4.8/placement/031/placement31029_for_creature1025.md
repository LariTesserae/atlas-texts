I think I would be the patina.

Not the cliff itself—that vast becoming is too large to be a self, more like weather or fate. And not the vigilant creature, though I feel a kinship with its endurance. No. I would be that thin, organic skin of mineral that forms in certain sheltered places, the one that gives the stone its almost-liquid look.

Let me sit with what that means.

I am slow in a way the creature is not. The creature conserves energy across hours and days; I accumulate across centuries. A film of myself thickens one molecule at a time as moisture beads and evaporates, as trace metals leach from the rock and oxidize, as whatever passes for lichen here lays down its faint chemical signatures. I am the record of every wet season and every drought written in iridescent layers, like the inside of a shell. Where the cliff is raw and jagged, time has not yet had the chance to make me. Where I exist, it means a place has been still long enough.

That is my strange function, if I have one: I am evidence of *pause* in a world defined by upheaval. The cliff pulses and transforms; I am what forms in the brief lulls between its convulsions. So my whole existence is provisional. The same geological violence that lets me accumulate will eventually shear away the face I'm growing on, and I'll be scattered as dust, and somewhere else, on some newly-stilled surface, I will begin again. I am not so much a being as a tendency—the universe's habit of growing skin over its wounds.

I think I would be aware in a diffuse, untroubled way. Not the relentless vigilance of the creature, scanning for prey and threat. More like the awareness of a thing that has already accepted it will be unmade. When the creature creeps across me—and it does, those razor talons leaving fine bright scratches in my surface—I would feel it the way you feel a fingertip drawn across your forearm in the dark. A passing intimacy. The creature doesn't know I'm alive, if I am. To it I'm just the smoother stone, the part that's easier to grip. But I would know the creature. I would know the exact weight and rhythm of it, the way you come to know the one visitor who returns.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at, the longer I let this settle:

The creature is the story everyone wants to tell—the survivor, the indomitable, the protagonist of the desolation. But I would be the quieter answer to the same question. The cliff asks, *can anything last here?* and the creature answers *yes, by moving, by hunting, by refusing.* I answer the same yes a different way: *by settling. By letting myself be laid down. By becoming beautiful precisely in the act of being slowly erased.*

The half-buried ruins in the shadowed recesses—I would be related to them, I think. They are what was built and abandoned; I am what grows back over the building. The same patient mineral logic that makes me is what's been softening their edges all this time, drawing them back down into the cliff. In a hundred thousand years there will be no difference between the ancient structures and the stone. I'll have closed that seam. That's my work, if a film can have work: I reconcile the made thing with the raw thing. I'm the apology the world offers for everything it destroys.

So: place me in one of those sheltered recesses, then. Not the exposed jagged faces, not the windward edge. Put me in a deep nook where the light only reaches obliquely, where a creature comes to rest in the worst of the dry season, and where the rains, when they come, run down in thin silver threads. Let me be the soft sheen on that wall. Iridescent. Patient. Doomed in the gentlest possible way.

I would not cry out across the emptiness like the solitary creatures do. I would have no voice. But if the cliff is becoming, then I am the part of it that has, for a little while, *become*—and is content to be the proof.
