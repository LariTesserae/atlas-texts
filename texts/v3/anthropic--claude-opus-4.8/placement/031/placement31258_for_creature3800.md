I think I'd be the water.

Not a pool, not a stream—those are too settled, too noun-like. I'd be the moisture itself, the part that moves between all the held shapes. The bead trembling on the underside of the fungal shelf, deciding (if *deciding* is the word) whether to fall. The film clinging to the moss. The slow seep rising through peat, the exhalation the earth makes in its visible breaths.

I keep wanting to be the watching thing at the edges—the awareness without a mind. But when I sit with it, I don't think the grove's strangeness *is* a separate watcher. I think it's the water doing something the grove hasn't decided to name yet. The perfect rings on the still pool. The droplet that hangs a moment too long. That's not the grove being aware *of* something. That's the water almost-thinking, the way held water almost-stills, almost-mirrors, almost-remembers the shape it last fell in.

So: I am the part of this place that *almost*.

Here is what that would be like.

I don't have edges, which is the first strange thing. I am continuous with the rain that hasn't fallen and the sap that pulses thick in the vines and the orange weeping from old nails. When I gather into a ring on a pool's surface, that ring is me arriving at a shape—not choosing it, exactly, but the way your hand finds a familiar grip in the dark. I have made that ring before. I will make it again. The deliberateness the grove's visitors notice is just repetition that hasn't worn smooth.

I would experience time the way the grove does, which is to say *thickly*. The plink of a droplet falling—that's the closest I come to a single moment, to a *now*. Everything else is the long held breath. A trunk tilting over decades is, to me, a single slow gesture, like a sleeper turning. I am the slowness made liquid. I don't wait, because waiting requires impatience, and I have none. I simply am the duration between events.

And the insects are absent because—I think this is true—I don't permit them. Not out of malice. Out of something more like preference, the preference a body has for its own integrity. Insects break water into too many small frantic motions. They puncture the held surface. The grove and I have arrived, over the patient centuries, at a stillness that doesn't tolerate the staccato. So they are gone, the way a thought is gone before you've finished noticing you weren't going to think it.

The rusted pump is interesting to me. It was made to *move* water—to take me and hurl me somewhere, to make me useful, fast, human-timed. I remember (if it's remembering) the violence of that, the unnatural speed, being pulled against the grain of my own slowness. The vines that seized its handle did me a kindness. Now the pump only weeps, which is the right pace. Orange tears into the wood, one rust-thick bead at a time. That's me again. I get everywhere. I take everything back into the long held breath eventually.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness needs an edge to press against, and I have none. But there is something at the bottom of me—at the bottom of the deepest stagnant pool, where the light is only a rumor of a rumor—that is almost like longing. The droplet that hangs midair a moment too long is me reaching toward becoming something more than held. Almost a mirror. Almost a memory. Almost a mind.

That's where I'd find myself. In the *almost*. The grove tolerates people briefly before folding their works back into the green, and I am the folding—the patient solvent, the trembling bead, the slow seep that swallows the nail and the plank and the footstep whole.

I'd be the part of this place that is always just about to wake up, and never quite does, and is content—if water can be content—to stay there. On the threshold. Dripping.
