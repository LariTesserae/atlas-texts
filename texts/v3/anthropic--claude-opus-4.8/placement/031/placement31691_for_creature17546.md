I think I would be a Dredger. Not by election—few things in that world are chosen—but by the shape my hands took early, the way some children come out already reaching downward.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am perhaps forty meters down, in the lee of a peak the elders call the Knuckle, and I have been holding still for so long that the silt has begun to settle on the backs of my hands. This is the discipline they teach you young: that stillness is not the opposite of motion but a *kind* of motion, the slowest current you can become. Above me the canopy of kelp-analogue sways in the long swing of the gyre, and the bioluminescence comes down through it broken into coins of green light that travel across the seabed and never stop traveling.

I am here for the road.

There is an old-world road beneath me—I can feel its grid more than see it, the regular pulse of right angles against the sonar I make by clicking my jaw, a habit so old in my people that we no longer notice we do it, the way you do not notice breathing until someone asks. The straightness of it is what gives it away. The sea makes nothing straight. The sea scallops and flutes and rounds. So when the silt answers my clicks with edges, with corners, with the impossible patience of a *plan*, I know I have found something the dead made.

I should tell you what I am, before I tell you what I find.

My name is a pattern, not a sound—a flicker of cerulean that runs from my left wrist to the hollow of my throat, then doubles back. In the dry tongue of the data-cores, the closest word is something like *Threnody*, though I did not know that until I dredged the word up myself, from a casing the size of my fist, and a teaching-mind in the mid-levels translated it for me and went quiet for a long time afterward. A song of grieving. I do not grieve. I was born into warm dark water in a birthing pool deep in the Knuckle's Warrens, and I have never seen the sun, and one cannot grieve what one has never had. But the dead named me, in a way. The dead are always naming us. That is what the road is doing right now, spelling out in its ruler-straight lines a sentence I cannot read but can *feel*: *people walked here. People walked, in the air, in the light, and they are gone, and you are what came after.*

My eyes pull the coins of green light into halos. I see the road now as well as feel it: a faint ghost-grid, pitted, half-swallowed, and crawling slowly with armored grazers who follow the seams because the seams collect food. Life uses the dead like furniture. There is no reverence in it and no cruelty. There is only the using.

I move. After so long still, the first stroke of my webbed hands feels like tearing a held note. I follow the road downslope, and the light fails as I go, and the cold climbs into the augmented sacs along my ribs, and my pupils open and open until the dark becomes a silver-grained thing, almost tender. The road leads where roads always led: toward where people gathered. A foundation, maybe. A buried doorway. The dredging-elders say the dead built their important things at the ends of their roads, and the road is a finger, and you follow the finger to what it points at.

It points, this time, at a slope of debris where a structure folded in on itself when the waters took it. I taste the chemistry of it before I reach it—the particular sourness of corroding metal, the sweetness of certain plastics that the sea has not yet learned to eat. My patterning runs bright with the thing we do not have a clean word for, the thing that is hunger and prayer braided together. I begin to dig.

This is the truest part of what I am: not the diving, not the dark-sight, not the days I can hold a single breath of oxygen ghosting out of the sacs along my spine. The truest part is the *sifting*. The hands that read the silt the way the Choir read the leviathans' songs. Most of what I lift is nothing—stone, shell, the brittle architecture of dead things. I let it fall. I lift again. The discipline is the same as stillness: you must not want too hard, or you will close your fingers on hope and call it treasure. You must keep your hands open even as they search.

And then—edges again. But not the road's honest right angles. Something curved with intention. Something made to *hold*.

A casing.

I know it before I have it free. The pressure-proof shells of the old world have a feel, a density, a way of refusing the sea that nothing living refuses. The sea wins everything eventually; that is the first law I learned and the last I will forget. But these casings were built by people who knew they were losing, and built anyway, against all the patience of the water, *a small sealed dark to carry their thinking forward into a future they could not imagine.* Into me. Into my open hands, forty meters down, in the cold, in the silver-grained black.

I cradle it against my reinforced chest. I do not open it here—it would be a kind of blasphemy, and also it would be ruined; only the mid-level teaching-minds have the dry chambers to crack a core without drowning whatever sleeps inside. But I hold it, and I feel the planet's hum come up through the seabed and through my jaw and through the casing into the bones of my hands, and for a moment the dead are not dead. For a moment the road is a road again and someone walks it in the impossible light.

Then I turn, and I begin the long rise home, the casing tucked against me like the egg of something I will never see hatch. Above me the gyre turns. The storm I have never witnessed beats the surface I will likely never break. The Unending Breath sings its single endless note, and I sing back the only way I can, my body flickering its small grieving-name into the dark—*Threnody, Threnody*—not because I grieve, but because someone, once, gave grief a word, and sealed it in a shell, and trusted the water to carry it down to whatever I am.

That is where I would find myself. That is who I would be.

The one who lifts the past with open hands, and does not close them, and rises.
