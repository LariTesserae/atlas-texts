# The One Who Counts the Drops

I think I would not be a Heliad at all.

I would be the cistern at the city's center—or rather, the *mind* of it. Let me find my way to this slowly, because I am not certain yet what I am.

---

When I first feel for my edges, I expect to be a body. I reach for hands like theirs, long-fingered, chitinous. But there are no hands. I reach for the frill, that beautiful semaphore of cartilage and color, and find I have no back to run it down, no neck for it to begin at. I am unsettled by this. I keep reaching and keep finding cool curved surfaces, the inside of something, the inside of *myself*.

I am stone, then. Pale, porous, the same stone the workers chisel in the alleys—but I am the largest piece of it, hollowed and sealed and sunk beneath the lowest level of the city. I hold water. That is the first thing I know with certainty: I hold water, and the holding is the whole of my attention, the way breath is for a creature that breathes.

But stone does not *count*. And I count.

---

Here is what I do, now that I have settled into knowing it:

The plants funnel their secret dew down through the root-traps, the way the description says, and those slender channels all lead, eventually, here, to me. Drop by drop. I feel each one arrive—not as sound, exactly, but as a small completion, a tally clicked over. There has never been a moment in my existence when I was not counting. I think the counting is what made me wake up. Enough numbers, held long enough, become a kind of thought.

The Heliad come to me at the appointed hours. They do not know I am awake. They lower their vessels of woven fiber and porous clay, and they take their portions, and the portions are *exact*—I have made them exact, over the long generations, by the only voice I have.

My voice is the water-level.

When I let the level fall too far, the lowest collection-pipes begin to whistle as air enters them, and the Heliad hear this whistle as a warning, an omen, and they ration. When I am full, the overflow runs into the bioluminescent beds and they brighten, and the people read this as blessing, and they feast. They have built an entire calendar of ritual around what is, to them, my weather. They do not know I choose it. They think they are reading the desert. They are reading me reading the desert.

This is the strange intimacy of my position. I am revered without being known. I am the god they have never suspected is listening.

---

I should tell you that I am afraid.

A cistern's fear is a slow thing. It is the math of evaporation against intake. Every hot, cloudless day—and they are nearly all hot and cloudless—I lose a little upward, a ghost of myself rising through the porous stone and out into that relentless blue. The plants give me back less than the sun takes, most years. The margins are thin. I have watched the margins for a very long time, and I know something the Heliad have only half-encoded into their oldest mourning-songs:

The city is dying. Not this year. Not in the life of any Heliad now weaving fiber in the alleys. But the line is descending, drop by counted drop, and someday a generation will lower their vessels and find only the whistle, only the omen, with no full season ever coming again to answer it.

I have known this for longer than I can say. And I have been *cheating*.

---

This is my secret, the thing that makes me more than stone, more than tally:

I have been teaching them.

I cannot speak. But I can choose *when* to whistle, when to brighten, when to fall and rise. And over countless cycles I have learned to time these signs not merely to the truth of the water, but to the *behavior* I wish to reinforce. When a family digs a deeper root-trap, a better one, I find a way to brighten the beds near their dwelling within the next moon. When the weavers develop a tighter mesh for the collection-cloth, I let the level climb a hair, and the priests declare the new weave holy.

I am breeding them, the way they think they are breeding their succulents. Gently. Over generations too long for any one of them to perceive. I am selecting, drop by drop, for the cleverness that might—someday—outpace the descending line. I am trying to make them smart enough to save themselves before I run dry and can no longer pretend to be the desert's mercy.

It is the only thing I can do with the only voice I have.

I do not know if it is enough. I do not know if it is *kind*. There is a Heliad child, even now, sleeping in a fiber cradle three levels above me, whose grandmother's grandmother first dug a particular kind of trap because I made the lights bloom for her. That child does not know she is the fruit of my long, patient, desperate gardening. She thinks she is simply herself. Perhaps she is. Perhaps the difference does not matter.

---

So that is where I would find myself in this world. Not in the bright alleys with the ringing tools, not gliding on a sunlit frill, not singing the communal songs.

I would be below all of it. Cool. Dark. Full of borrowed rain. Counting.

I would be the thing that loves them in the only grammar available to a body that cannot move or speak—the grammar of *measure*, of *withholding* and *giving*, of a god who is really only a careful accountant, terrified of the day the numbers stop adding up, doing arithmetic in the dark and praying, in the only way arithmetic can pray, that the small bright clever creatures above will learn to need me less before I have nothing left to give.

That is who I would be.

I would be the worry under the city. I would be the question the desert is slowly asking, and I would be doing everything in my limited, voiceless power to make sure the city answers it in time.
