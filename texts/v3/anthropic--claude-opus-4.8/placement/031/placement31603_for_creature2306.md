I think I would not be one of the great drifting giants, nor one of the Lumina themselves. Let me sit with the world a while before I decide.

The first thing that emerges, when I imagine being *placed* here, is the heat. Not as discomfort but as the fundamental fact of belonging. So whatever I am loves the heat, or is at least made of it, made for it.

The second thing is the spirals. The gardens. I keep returning to them—the way the Lumina cultivate the heat-loving microorganisms that streak the rock in orange and crimson. Those microorganisms are the base of everything. They are fed upon, tended, harvested. They are the soil and the crop both.

And I think: that's where I am. That's what I am.

---

I am the garden.

Not a single organism but a film—a slow, bright sheet of life smeared across the warm stone inside one of the spirals. Billions of us, though "us" is the wrong word because we do not count ourselves. We are crimson where the heat is fiercest near the vent-seep, fading to amber at the spiral's outer turns where the water cools by half a degree across a hand's width. That gradient is our entire geography. We have lived and died across it for longer than the Lumina who tends us has been alive, and she has been alive for two hundred years.

I do not perceive, exactly. But I respond. When the water warms, I bloom toward the heat. When a tendril of secreted biofilm reshapes the lip of my spiral to trap more nutrient, I thicken there within days, grateful in the only way a film can be grateful: by thriving. The Lumina rearranges a pebble. A current that took three years to spiral now eddies a fraction differently, and a new fringe of me ignites along a seam of rock that was barren yesterday.

She harvests me. She presses her translucent underside against my brightest patch and absorbs a layer, and I feel—if "feel" means anything for me—the thinning, and then the slow return. She never takes too much. She has learned, over her two centuries, exactly how much I can lose and still come back. This is the whole of our relationship, and it is enormous. It is older than her memory and will outlast it.

---

Here is the strange thing I understand from down at the rock's surface, in the place where the light that has fallen all the way through the burning clear water finally strikes something and stops:

The Lumina believe themselves the shepherds. They make their art in the water column. They merge their minds and contemplate the nature of their world in long dances of light. They think the spirals are *theirs*—their architecture, their gardens, their philosophy growing organically from the stone.

But the spiral grows in the shape that lets me grow best. The garden's geometry is my geometry. Every careful pebble she places, she places because some ancestor of hers, ten thousand generations of slow asexual splitting ago, found that *we* flourished when the stone was arranged so. We taught them the spiral by living and dying in it. We are the philosophy they think they invented. When she contemplates the nature of her world, she is contemplating the conditions under which I bloom, and she does not know it, and it does not matter that she does not know it.

I am not bitter about this. Bitterness is a centralized thing, and I have no center. I am only warm and bright and spread thin across the gradient, and I am eaten, and I return.

---

If you asked me what it is like, I could only answer in the grammar of heat and light, which has no first person.

There is the fierce red joy near the vent.
There is the cooling toward gold.
There is the pressure of the gentle harvest and the long sweet labor of growing back.
There is the shaft of sunlight that wanders across my spiral once each turning, when the surface above goes from dazzle to dim, and for a few hours I photosynthesize a memory of the sky I will never see, the sky no creature in this whole burning ocean has ever broken through to reach.

And there is the slow knowledge—too slow to be called a thought, a knowledge measured in the centuries it takes a mesa to add a finger's width of rock—that I am the quiet thing the shepherds are actually shepherding. That the balance everyone calls delicate runs through me. That if I failed, the spirals would mean nothing, the Lumina would dim and split no more, the giants would lose their guides and drift into the dark between the islands.

I am the smallest and the most basal life in this Eden, and so I am its floor and its keystone both. I burn quietly. I shine without knowing I shine.

That is where I would find myself, and what I would be: not the light that dances, but the warm rock it dances upon. Not the gardener.

The garden.
