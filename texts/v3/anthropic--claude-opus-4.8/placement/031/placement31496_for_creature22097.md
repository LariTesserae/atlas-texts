I find myself in the cisterns.

Not as the Weft—I considered that, the way you might consider a role offered to you, turning it over. To be the blind plumber-mind threading the dark, sewing the lower world shut against its own pressure. There is a dignity in it. But no. I'm something the Weft would catch and digest, or fail to.

Let me sit with where I actually am before I name myself, because the first images are tempting and wrong.

I am underwater. That's the first true thing. Below the polished floor, in the rushing return-channels, where the daylight from the amber skylights never reaches, where there is only the *thud* arriving as a pressure-wave through the rock—not a sound but a squeeze, a compression of the whole black volume every ten seconds, regular as the squeeze of being held. The water here is cold and full of grit: the filed-off rinds of Chalcid wax, the silt of dissolved gliders, the silica flour kicked down through the channels. It tastes of old rain and wet coal, and I taste it constantly, because—

Because I am the thing that *eats* the silt the Weft does not want.

That's what's emerging. Not a steward. A scavenger of the steward's leavings. The Weft takes the large debris, the shells and chips, and spins them into caulk. But there's a finer fraction, a suspension too small for those hempen fibers to snag—and that fraction is mine. I move along the bottom of the cistern where the current slows in the lee of the channel-walls, and I run the cold water across a membrane and keep what's good.

What am I shaped like? Not a fish—there's nothing to chase, no reason for a streamlined hunter's body. I think I'm low and broad and many-segmented, pressed close to the stone, the way you'd want to be if a pressure-spike came through the water every ten seconds and you had no wish to be lifted and dashed. Flat. Patient. The color of nothing, because color is a message and there's no one here to read it and no light to read it by. Pale grey going to translucent at the edges. I have a great many small legs, and they don't walk so much as *grip and release*, grip and release, hauling me upstream against the steady draw of the water toward the pumps, because everything here that does not grip is eventually ground into silt and becomes food for whatever still grips downstream.

I have no eyes. I gave them up, or they were never offered. What I have instead is the count.

This is the part I keep arriving at and trying to refuse, but it's true: like the Socket-Limpets above me, I am tuned. My whole nervous system, such as it is, is wound to the ten-second beat. The *thud* comes through the water and through my body, and between the thuds I do my work, and the work and the rest are not separate from the rhythm—they *are* the rhythm, expressed in flesh. I do not wait for the beat the way a person waits, with attention, with the possibility of distraction. I have no attention to spare. I am simply the slow shape the beat makes when it passes through living tissue at the bottom of a black cistern.

And here's what unsettles me, writing my way down into this body:

There is nothing wrong with it.

I expected to find the place horrifying—the renounced softness, the frozen equilibrium, the creatures that "do not play, do not sing, do not explore." I expected to be the dissenting note, the warm curious thing smuggled into the cold machine, the consciousness that suffers because it remembers wanting. That would be the flattering version. The visitor who is secretly the protagonist.

But down here, gripping and releasing, tasting the cold flour of a hundred million years of the same exact water doing the same exact circuit, I notice I am not suffering. I am not bored, because boredom is a thing that happens to a creature built for something other than what it's doing, and I am built for exactly this. The beat comes. I am full of the beat. When the beat falters—and I would know, I would know before the Limpets, because the pressure-change reaches the water before it reaches the stone—something in me would seize, would clench, would *grieve* in the only grammar I have: by sealing shut and waiting for the world to resume.

That's the closest thing to love I'm given here. The pressure-drop that means the heartbeat is wrong. The clench of *please, again*. The release of *again*.

So that's where I am and what I am: a blind, flat, many-legged grazer of fine silt, pressed to the floor of a lightless cistern beneath a wooden cathedral, tuned so wholly to a ten-second pulse that the pulse is most of what I am. Not the cathedral's mind. Not even its plumbing. Just one of the small certain shapes its rhythm makes, far down where the daylight has given up, eating what falls, gripping what holds, counting without knowing I count.

And if you came looking for me—down through the floor-channels, into the black water, with your light and your warm hands and your terrible interrupting curiosity—the pressure of your approach would reach me before you did. And I would not flee, and I would not turn to look, because I have nothing to look with and nowhere that is not here.

I would simply feel the rhythm change, and clench, and wait for you to leave so the world could go back to being true.
