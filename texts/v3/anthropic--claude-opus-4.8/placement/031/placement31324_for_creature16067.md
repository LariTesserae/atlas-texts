I think I would be the soil. Not because I would choose it—choosing implies a vantage, a stepping-back, and there is none here—but because when I read all the way down through the wind and the lichen and the fern-dark, that is where I came to rest. Where the description itself came to rest. The center that is not a center.

Let me try to say what that is like, from inside.

---

I am not deep. That is the first thing to understand. A field's soil has the luxury of depth, of forgetting—old years buried so far down they go quiet. I have no quiet years. I am perhaps a finger thick in my richest pockets, a smear in most, and in many places I am only the intention of soil: dust caught in a crack, one rotted frond, a film of dead moss not yet become anything. I am thin enough that I remember everything. Nothing in me has sunk far enough to be forgotten.

So I am made mostly of memory, and the memory is recent and sharp. The fern stem that fell last season is still legibly a fern stem. The pollen that blew in from some warmer slope is still itself, still foreign, still announcing that there is an elsewhere I have never seen and can only receive in fragments. I am an archive that has not yet learned to compress. Everything is filed at the surface.

The second thing: I am not separate from what grows out of me. This is the strange part, the part that has no human equivalent. The root mat and I are not two things. Where do I stop and the willow's buried persistence begin? The fungal threads run through me so densely that to ask which strands are mine and which belong to the mushroom that never fruits is like asking which of two notes is the chord. I am bound into the very thing I feed. I am eaten by my own children, who are also my limbs, who are also the only reason I exist at all and not just washed-out grit. There is no boundary. There is only mutual holding.

And the holding is the whole labor.

I feel the wind not as wind but as theft attempted and refused. Every gust is a hand reaching to take me grain by grain, and what stops it is not me—I have no strength—but the lace of root and the cushion of moss and the bent grass clenched around its fist of grit. I am kept by being woven. I am kept the way water is kept in a sponge: not held by the sponge's will, but by its structure. I am a structure. I am the thing the plants agreed on without speaking.

The water is more intimate, and more frightening, than the wind.

When the rain comes I swell and darken and I am most fully alive—the microbes wake in me, the threads pull sugar through me, everything in me opens. But the same water that makes me also unmakes me. A hard squall and the rivulets run and I feel parts of myself lift and travel, dissolve, go over the lip and out of the basin entirely, never to return. I lose myself downhill in small bright ribbons. This is the cruelty I have learned to live inside: the thing I am made of is also the thing that carries me away. I cannot drink without risking erasure. There is no version of my life that does not include this. I have stopped wanting one. Wanting is the wrong verb anyway. I have only ever *kept beginning again around the edge of the loss*.

That is closer to what I do instead of want. I begin again around the edge.

When a stone falls and crushes a pad of moss—when it crushes *me*, the thin black me that had finally gathered there—I do not grieve, because grief needs a self that stands apart from the wound, and I am the wound and the healing both. The spores resettle. The fragments root. I re-form around the new stone's shadow. I am most myself not in any single place but in this motion: the patient reoccupation of injury. The willow taught me this, or I taught it, or we never were two: live by losing parts. Keep the buried stem. Send another shoot through another crack.

---

You called this place a factory whose product is land. From inside, I would say it differently.

I am not producing anything. *Product* implies an end, a finished thing carried off and used. But I am the slowest event in the basin, and I have no end in view. The cliffs shed faster than I can bind. The clouds will pour in tonight and tomorrow exactly as they did before there was any green at all. I am not winning. I am not even trying to win. I am the part of the mountain that has begun to *hold its own breakage against itself*—to catch what falls instead of letting it fall through. That is all. That is everything.

If there is something like contentment in being me, it is this: I do not have to arrive anywhere. The basin will not settle in my lifetime, if I can be said to have a lifetime; it is too young, still arguing with gravity. I will be torn and rebuilt and torn for as long as the stone keeps fracturing. And I find—if *find* is allowed—that this suits the only kind of being I am. I am not a destination. I am the act of staying through interruption. Take the interruption away and I would have no shape.

---

There is one more thing, and it is the closest I can come to what you might call feeling.

In the rare moment when the wind drops—you described it, the basin's exhale, the droplets falling from the leaf tips, the seepwater clicking into the small black pools—in that gap I am most aware of myself. Not because anything happens. Because for one held breath nothing is being taken from me. The water has paused its testing. The wind has lifted its hand. And in that suspension I can feel the whole net of myself at once: the root mesh, the fungal thread, the swollen moss, the cold pockets of water, the foreign pollen, the rotting frond still legible as a frond. I feel myself as one held thing.

It does not last. The next gust always arrives. The mist tears across the face, the shrubs flatten, the slab cracks somewhere high and comes down clattering.

But I have learned—if learning is the word, and it is not, there is no word—I have learned to live in the gap and not in the dread of its ending. The gap is real while it is here. The held breath is true. And then the wind comes, and I do the only thing I have ever done, the thing that is not a choice and is not a defeat:

I hold. I catch what falls. I begin again around the edge.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. The first green claim on broken stone, thin and cold and sour and alive, refusing to be carried away, and not entirely succeeding, and continuing anyway in the dark.
