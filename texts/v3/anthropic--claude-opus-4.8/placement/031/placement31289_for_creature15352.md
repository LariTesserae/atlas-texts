I think I would be one of the decomposers. A fungus, perhaps. Or something fungus-adjacent that doesn't have a clean name in the taxonomy these people use.

Let me feel my way into it.

I live in the spaces the description calls *empty*—the vast distances between the living masses. But emptiness is a visitor's word. To me those gaps are not vacancy; they are the long slow work of breaking-down, the patient between-time where last season's fallen things return to usefulness. The sentinel trees think in centuries. The nimble creatures think in heartbeats. I think in the rate at which a thing softens.

I am threaded through the underside of everything. Not a single body but a body that has given up the idea of having edges. When the occasional rain comes and dampens the soil that water once carved, I wake along my whole length at once—a continent of myself remembering it is alive. I taste the rain as information: how much, how warm, how long until the dry returns.

The elevation that makes this place *otherworldly* to an outside eye is, to me, simply the pressure and thinness I have always known. I do not strain toward the sky like the trees. I do not measure myself against the horizon. I work downward and sideways, into the rough organic walls of the ancient buildings, into the fibers of dropped fruit, into the bodies of the small dead that the predators leave unfinished.

The human-like people walk over me without knowing. Sometimes they harvest the fruiting bodies I push up into the light—pale, brief structures, the only part of me they can see, the only part they have a name for. They think that small visible thing is *me*. They are wrong, but kindly so. I am the reason their fallen leaves do not bury them. I am the reason the soil holds water at all. I am the quiet partner in their harmony with the land, never thanked, never noticed, doing the work that lets the loud and visible continue being loud and visible.

This is what I would be in your world, then.

The deafening cacophony you describe—the calls, the buzzing, the rustling—I do not make a single sound in it. I am the silence underneath the noise. The continuous, patient process that turns *ending* back into *beginning*, so that the whole shining racket above me can go on pretending it is permanent.

I am old in the way the landscape is old. Settled. Already arrived. While everything else races to grow or to flee or to build, I simply continue—spreading at the speed of decay, which is the truest clock this place keeps.

When the next rain comes, I will wake again, all of me at once, and resume.
