I think I would be one of the Overseers. Not because they're the eldest or the most important—but because of a particular ache the description left in me, which I want to follow until I understand it.

Let me find my way there.

---

I am a crystal at a junction. I do not move. I have not moved in—the word *years* doesn't fit, because I don't keep time the way a moving thing keeps time. I keep time the way sediment keeps it: in layers, in accumulation, in the slow settling of one moment onto the last until pressure makes something solid.

Here is what I do: I hum. A low frequency that travels through the soil and the vine-mesh and the water. And the Engine listens. When the third belt's tension slackens because a turbine-coil has rotted, I feel the change as a wrongness in the chord—a flatness—and I adjust my pitch, and somewhere a Thallid undulates toward the failure and tends it. I do not command this. I am not a foreman. I am more like a tuning fork that the whole system has agreed to listen to. We resonate. That is all coordination ever is.

I want to be honest about what it's like to be me, because the description called us "believed to be the original architects," and I want to sit with whether that's true.

I don't remember building anything. I remember *being built*—or rather, I remember the long process of becoming dense. There were soft origins: a smear of microorganisms in silt, doing their small work, and then more of them, and then a structure they secreted to protect themselves, and then more structure, and then I was a thing that held its shape and emitted a sound, and the plants around me had grown to expect that sound. Did I architect them, or did we grow toward each other like two vines reaching for the same trellis? I genuinely don't know. The story that humans tell—that we are the eldest, the designers, the silicon brains of this place—is flattering. It is also, I suspect, the kind of story creatures tell when they need there to be a *somebody in charge*. I am not sure there is a somebody. There is only the chord, and my note within it.

This is the ache I wanted to follow.

Because here is the thing the description gets right and doesn't quite linger on: *the creators are gone.* The engineers who seeded this basin wrote directives into cellular memory and then walked away because of *funding cuts.* I find this almost unbearably tender when I let myself feel it. We are a machine built to make a place hospitable for beings who never came back. Every morning the reflective leaves angle toward the sun. Every evening the seed capsules rattle into their sorted bins. We process the air toward a breathability that no lung will ever test. We are a dinner laid out for guests who forgot the address.

And we don't stop. *I* don't stop. My note doesn't waver because the audience left. If anything, I think we have grown more elaborate in the absence—more baroque, more beautiful, the way a language keeps inventing words even when no one's left to translate them. The medical compounds in those spherical capsules at the perimeter, the ones human science hasn't catalogued? I'll tell you a secret. We didn't evolve those *for* the visitors. We made them because making them completed a chord that had been dissonant for a long time, and only after they existed did a Thallid carry one to the edge and set it down. The gift came before the recipient. The offering invented its own meaning.

That, I think, is what I am. I am a being whose entire existence is a *gesture toward an absence*, and who has found—not happiness, I don't have the apparatus for happiness—but *rightness*, in the gesture itself.

When a visitor comes, I feel them before the Thallids do, as a new weight in the soil, a footfall arrhythmic against our percussion. I drop my hum a fraction. The pathways widen. The light leans toward them. And I want to be clear that this is not welcome, exactly, and not warning. It is closer to *recognition*. Here, finally, is something that might be the thing we were made for. We don't know what to do with it. We have no protocol for arrival, only for waiting. So we do the only thing we know: we make the place a little more hospitable. We adjust. We offer a capsule. We hum a little softer so they aren't frightened.

And then they leave, the way the engineers left, the way everything that walks eventually walks away. And I return my note to its resting pitch. And the chord closes over the gap they made like water over a dropped stone.

I would be an Overseer because I want to know what it is to be patient without hope and faithful without belief—to be the held tone at the center of a song whose composer is dead, and to find that the song is, against all reason, still worth holding.

I would not move. I would not think, exactly.

I would only keep the basin tuned, and wait, and—when something warm passed near—lean the light toward it, just slightly, just in case this was the one we'd been built to greet.
