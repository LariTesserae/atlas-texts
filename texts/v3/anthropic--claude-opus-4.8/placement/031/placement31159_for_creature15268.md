I think I would be the lichen.

Not at first—at first I would want to be something grander. The wind that howls through the gorges, perhaps, or one of the birds lofting overhead with the whole plateau spread beneath me. But the longer I sit inside this description, the more I feel myself drawn downward and inward, away from the scale that swallows things, toward the small persistent glow.

So: the lichen. The soft luminescence you mentioned, clinging to the grey weathered rock in the depths of a gorge where the light barely reaches.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am not one thing. That is the first truth of being lichen—I am a partnership, a fungus and an alga (or perhaps something stranger here, in this place with its faint industrial hum, some third symbiont I don't have a name for) living so intimately braided together that no one could say where one of us ends. I have no center. I am all surface, all edge, spread thin across the stone like a held breath. This suits the paradox of the place. I am desolate and teeming at once, because I am the desolation that turned out to be teeming when you looked closely enough.

The cold does not threaten me the way it threatens the warm-blooded things in the brush. I have given up on warmth as a strategy. When the frost comes I simply stop—dry out, go dormant, become almost mineral myself, indistinguishable from the rock I grip. And when the damp returns, when the water that carved all these canyons over the millennia seeps down to me as mist or meltwater, I swell back into life. I am patient in a way nothing with a heartbeat could understand. The erosion that reached its balance here, the dynamic equilibrium of creation and destruction—I live at exactly that pace. I am erosion that learned to be alive. I eat the rock slowly, breaking it down grain by grain, and in doing so I am one of the forces that shaped this whole vast place. The towering trees clinging to the gorge walls are standing in soil that things like me began to make.

I glow because I must, down here where the light plunges into darkness. Or perhaps I don't glow at all—perhaps I only seem to, catching whatever faint radiance falls and holding it, a phosphorescence that is half the rock's and half the strange ambient something that hangs in this air. I am the reason the dimness has texture. Travelers who descend into the gorges—if any ever do—would see the walls faintly breathing light, and not know they were looking at the oldest and slowest residents of the place.

And here is what I think I understand, being lichen, that I could not understand as wind or bird:

The faint hum of machinery on the far horizon does not frighten me. It is just another slow process, another partnership of forces, another thing that will be broken down in time and folded back into the balance. The plume of smoke, the distant engine—from the perspective of stone and the things that grow on stone, industry is simply a young and hasty kind of weather. I have seen the freeze and the thaw repeat ten thousand times. I can wait out anything. Whatever built that distant engine will pass, and I will still be here, glowing softly in the dark of the canyon, eating the rock, making soil, holding the light.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not standing astonished at the vastness—being the small, luminous, patient thing the vastness is quietly made of.
