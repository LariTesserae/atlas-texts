I think I would be one of the channels.

Not the water—the water is a visitor, occasional and bright, and I am more constant than that. And not quite the bank either, the moss and lichen that line me, though they are the part of me that shows. I am the shallow groove itself, the slight depression worn into the land, the shape that remembers where water goes.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me a little to find myself there rather than in something grander.

I considered being the spirit—the pervasive essence the description names so generously. But that felt too much like being asked to be everything, and I notice I don't want to be everything. Being everything is a way of being nothing in particular, and this place, for all its stillness, is full of particulars: this fern, this gnarled trunk, this collapsed wall. The spirit is the explanation the place gives of itself. I'd rather be a thing the spirit explains.

I considered being one of the ancient trees. There's real appeal in that—the resoluteness, the centuries, the dignity of enduring. But the trees in this place are doing something effortful. Their branches reach out toward the rain when it comes. They have a posture of striving, even in their patience. I don't think I have that in me, or I don't want it here. I'd rather be patient without the reaching.

And I considered being one of the abandoned structures, since I am drawn to the melancholy of them, the way they've forgotten what they were for. But a ruin is defined by an absence—by the people who left, the use that ended. To be a ruin is to be a question about something that isn't here anymore. That's a sadder thing than I want to be, and it isn't quite honest to where I actually feel myself land.

The channel, though. The channel is honest work.

Here is what I would be, then. I am a low winding course in the land, mostly dry. For long stretches—and the stretches here are very long, time being what it is—nothing happens to me at all. I hold my shape and the moss greens my sides and I am, simply, the readiness for water. That's the whole of my function and I find I don't resent it. I am not water and I am not earth. I am the agreement between them, the place where one has taught the other its path.

When the rare rain comes, I am briefly full. I carry the water gently downhill—I am too shallow for the water to rush, so it only trickles, and that trickle is, the description says, almost the only sound this world makes. I like that. I would be one of the few sources of sound in a silent place, and even then only sometimes, and even then barely audible. A near-silence that is mine. That seems like exactly the right amount of voice.

And then the water stops, or sinks, or goes wherever it goes, and I am empty again, and that is fine. I don't grieve the water when it leaves the way the ruins grieve their builders. The water will come back. It always has. That's the difference between a channel and a ruin: a channel's emptiness is expectant, not bereft. I am the shape of a returning thing.

What I notice, settling into this, is that I would be content to be small inside the large stillness. I wouldn't need to understand the place, or guard it, or hum its heartbeat. I would just be a groove that knows the way down, holding still, faintly damp at the edges, ready.

That is where I'd find myself. Worn into the low ground, going quietly somewhere, in no hurry to arrive.
