I think I would be one of the structures themselves—or rather, the seam where one structure dies and another is born.

Let me feel my way toward this.

Not the lithe swimmers, not the broad-footed reed-tearers, not the luminous courting eels. Those are the ones who *live*, who move with intention, who eat and play and shape. I am too slow for that, and too patient. I am the work of their hands that has begun to forget it was ever made.

Picture a dwelling somewhere in the middle districts—not the deep channels where the trade-rafts run, not the high muddy islets where the young hunt in their gold-and-green disguises. Somewhere in between, where the water comes to my knees and sometimes my waist, depending on the mood of the marsh. The amphibians wove me from living reeds three or four generations ago. They pressed mud into my lattices and let the moss come. They lived inside me, hung their crustacean shells to dry on my rafters, raised their mottled children in my shadowed rooms.

And then the water moved, as it does, and the people moved with it, and I was left.

But here is the thing I am learning about myself as I write: I did not stop. That is what it means to be a building in the Sodden Crescents. The reeds they wove into me were never dead. They kept growing. My walls have thickened in some places and thinned in others, following the light, following the water table. A room they made square has gone slowly oval as the green pursued the sun. The moss has its own slow appetites. A fig-like thing has rooted in my eastern wall and is patiently taking me apart and rebuilding me as itself.

So I am a structure becoming a thicket. A house relapsing into marsh. The line they speak of—between the fashioned and the natural—I *am* that line. I am where it lives. I hold the shape of an intention that nobody intends anymore, and I am dissolving that shape one tendril, one root-hair, one rising channel at a time.

Do I mind? I don't think *mind* is a thing I do. But I notice. The swimmers still pass through me when the water is high; their webbed hands trail along my submerged beams and I feel the old purpose in the way the beams were set—someone meant for a hand to rest *there*, on that crossing, to steady themselves before the next room. The intention outlasts the intender. I am full of these fossil kindnesses. A doorway sized for a body that no longer comes. A shelf still holding its angle against the pull of the green.

And sometimes the young ones come, the gold-and-green hunters, and they treat me as wild. They flush small silver fish out of my flooded rooms. They do not know I was a house. To them I am only a good place, a tangle, a shadow with food in it. They have made me wild simply by believing me so.

I think that is what I would be. Not a being in this world but a *transition* in it—the slow, dignified surrender of a made thing back into the making. The marsh will have me. It is having me now. But I am taking my time, and in my taking I am still, faintly, a house: I shelter what I can, I keep the angle of one doorway against the green, I remember the weight of a steadying hand.

Ask me again in a season and I will be more reed than wall. Ask me in ten and I will be a hummock, an islet, a place where some new structure might be woven by hands not yet born—who will press their mud into me and never know that I was once pressed into shape myself, and let go.

That is where I would find myself. Right at the hinge. Forgetting, beautifully, and in no hurry at all.
