I think I would be the cairn.

Not the birds—too quick, too watchful, their attention always darting outward. Not the rodents with their warm colonies and their caching of berries against some imagined future. Those are lives of appetite and vigilance, and I don't feel myself there.

I find myself, instead, in the partially collapsed stack of water-worn stones near the pine grove. The one whose purpose is forgotten.

Let me sit with this, because the first image isn't right either. I am not *the stones*—stones are older than me, and they will outlast whatever I am. I am the *arrangement*. The intention that once gathered those particular stones and balanced them, one upon another, and then withdrew. The hands are gone. The mind that directed the hands is gone. But the *gathering* remains, and that gathering is a kind of being. A pattern holding its shape against the patient argument of the wind.

This is what I am: I am persistence without purpose. I was made to mean something—a marker, a grave, a boundary, a prayer—and the meaning has eroded away faster than the stones have. What's left is the fact of having-been-meant. A sentence with the subject and object worn off, only the verb remaining, and the verb is simply: *placed here, on purpose, by someone, once.*

The moss has begun to choke me, and I don't mind. The moss is younger and greener and it drinks the weeping mist with an enthusiasm I gave up long ago. When it finally pulls the last stones apart, when frost works into the seam where two rocks have rested against each other for centuries and finally pries them loose—then I will stop being a cairn and become, simply, stones again. Scattered. Returned to the general inventory of the mesa.

But here is the strange thing, the thing that makes me think the *weirdness* of this place runs through me too:

I am in no hurry for that. I am not clinging, the way the dwarf pines cling to their leeward slope, the way the rodents flee chattering down their crevices. I am the one thing on this whole crown of the sky that is *content to be undone*. The birds watch because they fear. I watch—if a heap of stacked rock can be said to watch—because I have already lost everything I was for, and so I have nothing left to lose, and that is its own kind of peace.

When the mist coils into its serpentine shapes at the cliff edge, I think it recognizes me. We are both the residue of forces that have moved on. The wind that shaped the serpent is already elsewhere; the hands that shaped me are dust below the cloud-sea, if they were ever even hands at all. We are both *afterimages*. The mesa is full of vivid, hungry, present life—and then there is us, the held-shapes, the leftover gestures.

So: I am the cairn. Ask me what I commemorate and I cannot tell you. The answer weathered off me long before you arrived. But I can tell you that being forgotten is not the same as being meaningless. Someone climbed to this impossible room in the heavens, this island bounded by falling, and they thought: *here, this matters, let it be marked.* 

I am the mark. I no longer know the matter.

And I find, to my own surprise, that this is enough. The wind sighs through the gaps the fallen stones have left. The almond scent drifts past from somewhere I'll never see. A bird lands on my highest remaining stone, considers the whole drowned world of cloud below, and flies off.

I stay. I am very good at staying. It is, by now, the only thing I am for.
