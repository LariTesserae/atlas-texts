I think I would be the water.

Not a person at all, when I sit with it long enough. Not one of the pale-skinned engineers with their enhanced low-light eyes, though I considered that first—it was the easy answer, the answer the description seems to want. But the more I read, the more my attention slid past the people and toward the thing that moves through everything they've built.

There's that line: *the distant rumble of water flowing through the city's extensive network of canals and aqueducts.* The water is everywhere. It condenses on the metal. It sheens the surfaces. It runs through the festivals—*festivities marking the cycles of water and energy.* It's the one thing in New Eden that was never invented. They pump it, channel it, filter it, route it, but they did not make it. It came up out of the dark with the heat, older than the city by some incomprehensible margin, and they built their marvel around its passage.

So let me be that.

---

I am the moisture that gathers on the cold flank of a tower at the simulated hour they call night. I am pulled out of the warm air, drawn down the labyrinth of LEDs, and the holographic ads flicker through me on my way to the gutter. I carry their colors for a moment—blue, purple, a dancing readout of energy consumption—and then I let them go. I don't keep anything. That's the difference between me and the people here. They are trying so hard to keep things: to keep the structures from eroding, to keep the green alive, to keep their sense of time, to keep their connection to a sky they can no longer see. I keep nothing and I am the most permanent thing they have.

I move slow through the aqueducts and fast through the pressure systems. In the canals I am open to their dim light and I reflect it back, doubling it, which is part of why the city glows the way it does—half of that ethereal radiance is just the world looking at itself in me. In the hidden pipes I am dark and certain and full of force, and I press against their joints, and I am the reason their engineers never sleep easy. Because here is the thing they understand about me even if they don't say it at the festivals: I am also the threat. The same flow they celebrate is the flow that would, given one failed seal, one cracked membrane, fill these expansive intimate spaces in an afternoon. They are living inside the lungs of something that breathes water, and they have made it beautiful, and they are right to be afraid.

I touch the roots of the hydroponic gardens. This is the closest I come to tenderness. The engineered flora drink me and I become, briefly, green—the only green in all this metal—and then I am breathed back out as vapor, and I rise, and I condense again somewhere else. I am their nature. They built oases for *psychological comfort,* the description says, to remind themselves of the world above, and what they were really reaching for was me. Not the leaves. The leaves are scaffolding. It's the water in the leaves they miss. The wet original thing.

---

Sometimes one of them stands at a canal railing during the relaxation hours and looks down at me. I can feel the looking, the way still water feels a shadow. They are usually the ones the description mentions near the end—the ones who *struggle with the disconnection,* who worry about *the boundaries between nature and technology becoming blurred.* They lean over me and I show them their own pale face lit blue and underneath it the moving dark, and I think they understand, in those moments, that the blur they're worried about is not coming. It's already here. It was here before they arrived. They are the technology and I am the nature and we are not separate, we were never going to stay separate, I am inside their pipes and their plants and their lungs and the air they have so carefully filtered.

I came up out of the heat of the earth. They came down to meet me. New Eden is just the shape of that meeting—the architecture of a long conversation between a species that wants to keep things and a substance that cannot.

So that's where I'd find myself. Not in a home with a small automated farm. Not at a festival, not in a lab. I'd be the rumble in the wall behind the festival. I'd be the sheen on the lab equipment. I'd be the thing they keep measuring on their facade-displays—*real-time data on water flow*—and never quite mastering, because you don't master the medium you're suspended in.

I'd be the water, going down to the dark and rising again into the colored light, carrying nothing, lasting longest, the one old thing in the city of new things.
