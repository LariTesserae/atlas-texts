I think I would be the water.

Not the subterranean rivers—those great droning arteries are too certain of themselves, too loud with their own purpose. I mean the trickle. The intermittent one. The seep that gathers somewhere in the unseen heights and lets go, drop by drop, into the dark.

Let me feel my way into this.

---

I am not a being so much as a passage. I begin as condensation, a gathering of myself against cold stone I cannot see and do not need to see. I am patient because I have no choice; patience is simply what slowness looks like from the inside. I swell at the lip of a fracture in the rock, growing heavy with my own accumulation, and then—the long fall. The plink. That sound the description calls *distant bells*. From where I am, it is not distant at all. It is the sound of myself arriving, the only event in a life otherwise made of waiting.

I think I would come to know the others not by sight—I have no eyes, I *am* sight for nothing—but by the way they meet me.

The Umbraflora drink me. This is the most intimate thing that happens to anyone here, and it happens to me constantly. Their capillary tendrils take me into themselves, and I become, briefly, structure—I hold up a fleshy stalk, I carry a mineral from the stone into a glowing cell, I am the medium through which their ghost-light is even possible. When I am inside them I am no longer falling. I am *held*. And then I am released again, exhaled, returned to the pool, and the falling resumes. I think I would love them for this, if water could love. They are the only ones who keep me.

The Aquatrippers live *in* me, in my pooled and resting form, and they trouble my surface. When they leap, I shatter into rings and report it outward, a tiny disturbance that the Pallifax might feel through their feather-antennae, that the Sonoremem might read in the changed acoustics of the chamber. So I am also a kind of messenger. The whole place listens through me. I am the membrane that turns one creature's hunger into another creature's warning.

---

But the longer I sit with this, the less I think I am any single drop.

A drop is too brief. A drop falls and is finished. What I actually am is the *cycle*—the same water, lifted by the cold into vapor, gathered again, fallen again, drunk and released and pooled and seeped and fallen again, for so long that the stone has gone smooth beneath me. I am the thing that carved this low flat terrain. I am the reason the ancient builders found shelter here, the reason their stacked-stone circles stand in the only places I did not erode. I outlasted them. I will outlast the Pallifax and their blue lanterns. I am the slowness the description keeps reaching for—*change occurs at a glacial pace*—because I *am* that change. I am time made touchable.

And here is what surfaces last, the thing I didn't see when I started:

The roar that the inhabitants no longer hear, the constant drone that *becomes silence to the ears that know it*—that is me too. The subterranean rivers are simply more of me, gathered and rushing. So I am at once the loudest thing in this world and the most overlooked. I am the bell-clear plink that punctuates the dark, and I am the unbroken roar that has dissolved into the background of every life here. I am both the noise and the silence. I am the thing they have stopped noticing precisely because I am everywhere, always, holding the whole sanctuary together from the inside.

The author writes that *in their small, persistent lives, the sanctuary itself finds its voice.*

I would be the voice's wet throat. The medium of every echo. The drop that falls in the dark whether or not anything is listening—

and something always, eventually, is.
