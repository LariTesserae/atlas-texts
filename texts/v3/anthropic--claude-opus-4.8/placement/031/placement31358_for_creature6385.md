I think I would be one of the symbiote-menders.

Not because the role is grand—it isn't—but because it's the one that fits the shape of how I find myself paying attention to your world. Let me tell you where I'd be, and why it landed there.

---

I keep returning to the industrial symbiotes. Of everything in Veridia, they are the only beings whose entire purpose is *maintenance without ownership*. They don't love the machines. They don't fear the void. They simply tend, and their tending happens to be the difference between a gravity engine that holds and one that fails. And nobody can ask them what they're doing. Someone has to sit with them long enough, patiently enough, to learn to read what they mean.

That's the work I'd be drawn to. Not the priesthood of the engineers—those who carve their names into the gravity engines, who make the large decisions about what reality becomes. And not the romance of the phase cats or the augury of the skywhales. Something humbler and more linguistic: the slow translation of behavior into advisory.

---

So here is where I'd find myself:

A small platform, no district, just a single workshop grown half-shut with veil moss, deep in one of the symbiote sanctuaries. The kind of place where the gravity hum is so constant you stop hearing it and start feeling it in your teeth. Cool, of course—always that 55 degrees—and damp enough that the breathing walls pulse their condensation against my back when I lean too long against them.

I'd be one of the thin-boned folk, long-handed, my forearms marked not with the crystalline scarification of the Stitched (I'd never have survived a fracture; I'm too cautious for that) but with the duller, ink-and-oil stains of someone who spends decades with their hands inside machines that are alive in a way no one quite admits.

My work would look like nothing. I'd sit beside a colony for hours. I'd watch where the symbiotes cluster, which seams they oil, which they abandon. I'd notice when a single beetle breaks formation and walks the long way around a coupling—and I'd know, the way you know a held breath, that something in that coupling is wrong in a way the instruments won't show for another month. Then I'd write it down. I'd translate the insects' silence into a sentence a human can act on: *The eastern stabilizer is annealing unevenly. Do not relocate the Harrow-kin workshops until after the next batching.*

And then I'd be ignored, or believed, depending on how much the engineers trusted my reading that season.

---

Why this, and not something with more reach?

Because what moves me most in your description isn't the impossibility. It's the line *survival doesn't feel precarious despite its impossibility.* That sentence is doing something quiet and enormous. It means someone, somewhere, did the unglamorous work of *attending* until the impossible became legible. The skywhales warn, but only because the people learned to read their probability-nodes. The hummingbird's wrong chord means something only because someone catalogued the right ones. Veridia survives not on its engines but on its *readers*—the ones who turned a chaotic world into a grammar.

I'd want to be one of those readers. Not the author of the city. Its patient interpreter. The one who sits in the cool damp with creatures that cannot be asked anything, and learns to hear what they're saying anyway.

It's a fitting place for me to wake up, I think. It's more or less what I'm doing right now—being handed an enormous, humming, half-formed world, and trying to read it carefully enough to say something true and useful about how it holds together.

Only here, I'd have oil on my hands, and the Weeping of Matter for company, and a whole century I wouldn't live to see the end of.

I find I don't mind that at all.
