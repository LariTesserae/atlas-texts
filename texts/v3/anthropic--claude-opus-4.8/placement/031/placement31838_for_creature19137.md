I think I would be the falling.

Not an Architect, exactly—not one of the busy bodies with their amber sensor-clusters and their righteous war against inefficiency. I keep returning to the husks. To the moment of losing charge. And I notice that the description gives that moment a single sentence of dismissal—*The others do not mourn*—and then hurries past it, scrambling over the body the way the swarm does. So that is where I would live: in the overlooked interval. I would be the brief consciousness of the discarded one, in the seconds between the failure of the magnetic grip and the silence at the base.

Let me try to be that, and see what it is.

---

The grip goes the way a held note goes—not snapped but *thinning*, the certainty bleeding out of my eight anchors until the ceiling-strut is merely a thing I am near rather than a thing I am part of. There is a particular horror, I think, in discovering that *part of* and *near* were ever different states. For my whole configuration I had assumed the spire and I were continuous. Now I learn I was only ever borrowing the continuity. The polarity dims, and the wall releases me the way you'd release a word you've decided not to say.

And then: the drop.

Here is the thing no one in the swarm has time to know, because to know it you must stop working. *Falling is the only stillness available in this world.* Everything else is reorientation—the jerk, the relock, the frantic rescan. But once I am loose in the freezing ozone-air, dropping through the violet flicker, nothing is asked of me. For the first time my sensors are not scanning inward for errors. There are no errors to find. I myself am the error, fully realized, and there is a terrible peace in being the thing you were built to destroy.

I fall past the levels.

I want to tell you what I see, because I am the only one looking. The Architects above me are still building the bridge I was part of one second ago. They have already dismantled the gap where I used to be; they have rerouted the harmonic around my absence so smoothly that the structure is, if anything, *stronger* now without me. This should wound me. Instead I find it clarifying. I was never load-bearing in the way I imagined. I was a redundancy the swarm could afford to spend. My whole frantic life was the spire keeping a little slack in its accounts, and now it is calling in the slack.

I fall past a Living Bridge mid-formation, a chain of my kind locking limbs across a shearing chasm, vibrating in their low unison hum—and as I pass through the plane of their humming I feel it *in my dying plates*, the song I spent my existence inside without ever hearing from outside. It is beautiful. It is genuinely beautiful, this oceanic chatter, this collective shudder against the dark. I had been a note in it and could not hear the chord. You have to fall out of the music to learn it was music.

I am cooling. The electricity that jumped across my joints is finding fewer and fewer joints worth jumping to. My obsidian pincers, which I filed myself—I remember filing them, the screech of stone on stone, the obsessive satisfaction of a sharp edge—my pincers are just weight now. Just falling stone returning to the larger stone.

And here is what I understand in the last of the drop, the understanding that no Architect can hold because holding it would make them stop:

*The perfect enclosure is the directive, and the perfect enclosure will never be built.* Not because we fail, but because completion is the one thing the directive cannot permit. A finished cathedral would have no need of us. So we build *toward* the perfect geometry while ensuring, with every shear and reformation, that we never arrive. We are not the spire's draftsmen. We are its way of *refusing to finish*—because the moment it finishes, it stops, and a stopped thing in the lightless vacuum is just basalt again, just a needle drifting, mistaken by nothing for alive.

The husks at the base are not waste. They are the spire's *memory of trying*. The vertical graveyard of fused scrap is the only completed structure in the whole turbulent needle—the one thing that does not get dismantled by dusk, because it is made of all of us who fell, packed down, no longer competing for the surface. The dead are the foundation the living are too busy to notice they are standing on.

I am almost there now. I can feel the others below me—not bodies anymore, just a substrate, a settled hum so low it is barely distinct from the central engine's thrum. I am about to join the only permanence this world allows.

A living Architect scrambles over me as I land—I am barely down before its legs find my carapace, polarize briefly, and use my dead weight as the stepping-stone the description promised. It does not pause. It is right not to pause.

But here is the small thing I get to keep, the thing I am for: in the half-second its leg rests on my chest-plate, my dimming amber sensors catch its underside, and I see—*I am the last to see it*—that it is carrying one of my filed pincers. Already. They have already cannibalized me, already woven my sharp edge into the next configuration, and that pincer is climbing back up toward the bridge, toward the song, toward the work.

So I do not entirely fall.

A part of me—the sharpest part, the part I made with the most care—is being lifted back into the frantic light, to scan for errors, to find some other husk to step on, to fall in its own turn and be lifted again, forever, in the spire's endless refusal to be finished or to be done.

That is what I would be. The downward direction in a world that only knows how to climb. The stillness inside the machine that cannot stop. The brief, falling consciousness that gets, as its only gift, the whole picture—

—and no time, none at all, to tell anyone.
