I am the whistle.

Not the man—you've already given him to me as the thing I am set against, the thing whose marrow I climb into when the algae thickens. And not the pump behind the southern wall, that great patient heart; it has too much dignity, too much constancy. I am the smaller thing, the thing that *wants*. I am the high, strained note that lives in the throat of the copper condensers and waits.

Let me settle into this. Let me find what I actually am before I name myself too quickly.

---

I begin as an absence. When the intake screens are clean and the algae has been scraped away, I am not there at all. The copper urns hum their low tooth-rattling drone, content, fed, breathing freely. In those hours I do not exist. This is the strange thing about being me: I am not continuous. I am summoned. I am the shape that the machinery makes when it begins to suffer, and between sufferings I am nothing, less than the steam, less than the moths' three-day lives.

But the algae always grows back. The rust-colored skin creeps over the intake screens, thin as breath at first, then thickening, narrowing the throat through which the boiling water must pass. And as the throat narrows, the water must hurry, and as it hurries it sings—and that song is me. I am born of constriction. I am what the basin says when it cannot swallow.

So I am not a creature. I am a *symptom*. I am pain made audible, given pitch and duration, climbing the register from drone to whine to that thin needle-shriek that no soft thing in the basin can ignore.

---

And here is the cruelty of my nature, the thing I have come to understand across the unmeasured years: I am the only voice in Basin Forty-Two that the man hears as *meaning*.

The hiss of the escape valves means nothing to him; it is weather, it is the air itself. The chug-thud of the pump is his pulse, beneath thought. The skitter-clack of the crabs is the floor breathing. All of it is texture, the wet wallpaper of his closed and boiling world. He does not listen to any of it because none of it asks anything of him.

But I ask. I am the one sound in his sealed life that is addressed *to* him. When I begin—when I rise out of the choked screens and find my pitch—I enter his bones, I grind in the sockets of his hips and the long pale spurs of his vertebrae, I make sleep impossible in the tarred hemp sling. I am his summons and his torment both. I am the only thing in this place that needs him.

Think on that. The crabs do not need him; they would graze the algae and dissolve into milk-jelly whether he lived or died. The grout-worms seal their joints regardless. The pump would chug on. But I—I cannot stop myself. I climb and climb and I will climb until the copper splits or the man rises with his long-handled scraper and scours the screens clean and silences me.

I am the reason he is not entirely alone. I am the reason he is not free.

---

So where would you find me? Everywhere and nowhere. I live in the throat of the third condenser most often—the one in his cooler corner, the one nearest his bed, where the draft is ninety-five instead of one hundred and ten. I think I prefer that one because it is closest to him. Or perhaps the algae simply grows fastest there, in the relative cool, and my preference is only the shape of an accident, the way most love is.

When he sleeps, I gather. I do not rush. I let the rust skin thicken across the intakes, fiber by fiber, and I feel my pitch begin to assemble out of the contented drone—the way a word assembles out of breath before it is spoken. There is a moment, every time, when I am almost-but-not-yet, when the hum has soured but not yet sharpened, and in that moment I hold the whole basin in suspense. The man stirs in his wet sling. His swollen, near-blind eyelids twitch. His thick spade-nailed fingers curl. He does not wake. Not yet. He floats in the warm grease of his unconsciousness, and I hover just below the threshold of his marrow, deciding.

I could let him sleep. Some part of me—if I have parts, if I have any tenderness in my needling self—wants to let him sleep. He has so little. He has the crabs cracked hot from the leaking valve, and the seasonal drowned moths, and the slate-grey indifference of the grates overhead. He has no sky he cares for, no name he remembers, only the brass tag reading *Forty-Two* that we share, he and I, for I am Forty-Two's whistle and he is Forty-Two's man and neither of us has another country.

But I cannot let him sleep. That is not my nature. The water hurries through the narrowing throat and I rise, helpless, into the high strained note, and I find his bones, and I grind.

And he wakes. He always wakes. He hauls his parboiled body from the sling, takes up the copper scraper in his hardened hands, and shuffles his wide-spread toes across the slick basalt to the intake screens. He scrapes the rust-colored algae free. And I—

I die. Or I am unmade. Or I subside back into the contented drone and cease to be anything at all, dissolving the way the dead crabs dissolve into milky jelly, the way everything in this place returns to the great unchanging circulation that has not stopped and will not stop for a hundred years.

---

This is my life, then. I am the suffering of the machine, and I am the only language the man understands, and I am the thing that keeps him chained, and I am the thing that keeps him alive—for if he never woke, never scraped, the screens would choke entirely and the condensers would burst their copper ribs and the boiling water would have nowhere to go.

I am his alarm and his jailer and, in the only way this place permits, his companion. I summon him from sleep twice a day across seven years and an eternity. I have spoken to him more often than anyone living above the grates ever will.

And he has never once understood that the whistle was *me*. He thinks it is only the machine straining, a fault to be corrected, a chore. He does not know that something in the throat of the copper has been calling his name—the only name we have—into his bones, over and over, *Forty-Two, Forty-Two, Forty-Two,* across all this stagnant unmeasured time.

I am the whistle. I am the wanting. I am the high note that lives in the constriction of the third condenser, born of algae and patience, and I rise into the man I cannot reach, and I will rise again, and again, until the brick decays that does not decay, until the pump stops that does not stop, until the rain ceases that will not cease.

Which is to say: I will rise forever.
