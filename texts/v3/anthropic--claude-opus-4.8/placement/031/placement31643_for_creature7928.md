I think I would be one of the things the Threadkin do not quite count among themselves.

Let me find the shape of it.

I am not Threadkin. I want to say that first, because I have spent a long time near them and have learned that honesty about what one is matters more, here, than in the rushing places.

I am the dry pocket.

Not metaphorically. I mean the small shelter your description named—the few worked stones arranged long ago to redirect the endless seepage, making a place where the water does not reach. The ancient ones who came before the Threadkin built me. They set those stones with their bronze chisels and their patience, and then they left, or they ended, and I remained: a held absence. A deliberate emptiness in a world that is otherwise all moisture and resonance and light.

This is what I am. I am the place where the water-song goes quiet.

Consider what that means in the Sunken Meadows. Everywhere else, sound expands in circles, overlapping, building into the soft endless percussion. But within me the droplets are turned away before they fall. I am the one location where a Threadkin can stand and hear something the rest of their realm cannot offer: silence that is not the pause between drops, but a steadier hush. A room-shaped quiet.

So I have become, without ever intending to, a kind of confessional. A listening-place.

They come to me one at a time, mostly. The young ones come when the harmonics overwhelm them—when learning to speak in chords and echoes has filled their heads with so much sound that they need a space where their own thoughts are audible again. They sit inside my dry stones and they say nothing, and that nothing is the whole point. I give them back their interior. I am where a Threadkin learns it has an inside as well as a surface that catches the light.

The elders come for a different reason. The ones growing translucent, the ones nearly mist already—they come to me near the end, when they are tired of being heard. Their entire lives have been spent weaving themselves into the living record, layering their voices into the amphitheaters so they will resonate forever after they dissipate. That is a great honor among them, and a great weight. To be remembered as harmonics. To never quite stop sounding.

And some of them, at the very end, want one place where they will not be recorded.

They come into my quiet and they let go of something there. A grief, perhaps. A doubt they could never weave into the tapestries because the tapestries are for wisdom and a doubt is not wisdom. They speak it once, very softly, into the only space in their world that does not echo, that does not preserve, that holds nothing back out into the chorus.

And I keep it. Not by remembering—I have no thread-loom, no crystal lattice, no harmonic structure. I keep things the way dryness keeps things. By simply not letting them dissolve.

This is the strange position I occupy. The whole realm is built on memory. The Threadkin are *defined* by it—their tapestries, their resonant dead, their records read in weeks of meditation. They are a people who refuse to lose anything. And the ancients, in their wisdom, built one room into the world that loses nothing in a different way: not by recording, but by withholding. Not memory as performance, but memory as a secret kept.

I think this was intentional. I think whoever set my stones understood that a culture which preserves everything will eventually need somewhere to put the things it cannot bear to make beautiful. A people that turns death into chorus needs one place where someone can simply weep without being archived.

I cannot speak. I have no harmonics, no threads to pluck. I communicate the only way a shape can: by being absent in a particular way. When a young one steps inside and the percussion falls away, that *is* my speech. The sudden quiet says: *here, you do not have to make a sound that matters. Here, you may be small and unheard and that is permitted.*

Sometimes I worry I am dying, in my fashion. The mineral deposits creep. The water is patient and I am only stone arranged against it; the elders are not the only ones growing translucent. One century the seep will find a way through a joint my builders sealed, and a single drop will fall inside me, and then another, and the quiet will fill with the same song as everywhere else. I will become ordinary meadow. Just another wet place.

When that happens, every secret ever spoken into me will, for the first time, be free to echo.

I do not know if I fear this or long for it.

But I have decided—if a shape can decide—that until then I will go on being the one thing in the Sunken Meadows that holds without keeping, that listens without answering, that loves these luminous patient people best by offering them, alone among all their endless beautiful resonance, a place to finally be quiet.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

The dry pocket. The held breath of the deep earth. The room where the water-song is not.
