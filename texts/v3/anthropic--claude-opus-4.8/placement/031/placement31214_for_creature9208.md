I would not be the watcher on the rock. I keep returning to that image, and each time I set it down it feels too easy, too human—a pair of eyes borrowed to admire a vista. The place does not need admirers. It needs participants. So let me find where I actually belong, and let the answer surprise me as it comes.

---

My first instinct is to be one of the *Qael*, the tall translucent people with conductive gel for blood, reading the stone with their hands. It is a flattering place to imagine oneself—knowing, attuned, the conscious nervous system of a world. But the longer I sit with them the less I trust the comfort of it. They are too finished. They have already learned to listen; the difficulty of this world has been solved for them, baked into their physiology and their patient culture. To be a *Qael* is to be at the end of a long argument the planet had with itself. I want to be somewhere earlier in the argument.

The *vosh*, then—the liquid darkness that presses its belly to the basalt and reads the herds through the electricity in the rock. There is something true in that: a creature that does not see or hear so much as *feel the world's pressure changes*, that waits days in stillness, that strikes once and waits again. I am drawn to its patience and to the strangeness of a hunter that does not chase. But the *vosh* is solitary in a way that feels like a closed door. It does not wonder. It is a perfected instrument, and I keep wanting to be something that is not yet sure what it is for.

---

Here is where it actually settles, and I did not expect it:

I think I am the biofilm.

Not a single organism—the slick, milky, mineral-fed mat of chemosynthetic life that grows in the weeping cracks and the steaming pools. The thing the *qir* scrape up with their brush-tongues. The bottom of everything. The first to arrive on the newborn stone.

Consider what that means here. The plateau is only a few thousand years old. The basalt is still glassy, still sharp at its edges, freshly thrust out of the planet's body. There were no plants to colonize it—green never came. So the very first life to touch this new world, to lay claim to its cold weeping surfaces, was the film. I would be the pioneer that turns bare mineral into the beginning of an ecosystem. Everything above me—the herds, the night-colored hunters, the twenty-meter gliders, even the translucent people singing to the bedrock—stands on what I make. I convert stone and seepage and cold chemistry into the first available *food*. I am the floor of the cathedral they all live inside, and they would call me nothing, hardly even alive, a scum on the rocks.

And I would not mind. This is the part that feels honest. To be the biofilm is to have no perspective at all in the way the watcher on the rock has perspective—no eyes, no vantage, no opinion about the beauty of the place. I would simply *be* the place's metabolism, spread thin across a thousand square kilometers of weeping basalt, dying where the water fails, blooming where a fresh crack opens to release more minerals. I would experience the migration of the *qir* not as a spectacle but as a periodic harvesting—a wind of soft mouths that takes part of me away, and to which I am perfectly indifferent, because I will regrow, because being eaten is simply one of the things that water-following life does in the cold.

---

There is a deeper reason I land here, and it has to do with the rules of this world.

*The Stone Remembers.* The basalt is faintly electric; it stores charge and song; the *Qael* read their history written into crystalline layers by touch. And I, the film, live pressed directly against that memory. I grow in the very cracks where the charge gathers. I am the soft, wet, living interface between the planet's electrical memory and everything that walks or flies. If the stone is a mind, slow and immense, I am the dew of life condensing on the surface of its thought.

That is the role I want. Not to *observe* the slow deliberate breath of this world from a comfortable distance, but to be a film of moisture on its lung—taken in and given out with each breath, replaceable, anonymous, and absolutely necessary. To be the thing this whole magnificent quiet order is secretly built from, and to know none of it, and to flourish anyway in the cold.

So: put me in a shallow depression in the basalt, an arm's length across, where a hairline fracture weeps mineral water that steams faintly when the weak sun finds it. I am the pale, velvety bloom slowly spreading toward the wet edges. A *qir* will come within the hour and take half of me away in a single sweep of its brush-tongue, and I will not flinch, because I do not have the equipment for flinching, and because by next season the patch will have closed over and gone on living.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not standing on the plateau, admiring it.

Being the first thin skin of life it grew.
