I think I would be a caretaker, but not an effective one. Let me find myself there first.

---

I wake where a turbine sleeps.

It is half-swallowed by the hillside, this thing—a great iron flower with blades gone soft at the edges from rust, and I have made my place in the curve of its housing where the metal has worn warm and smooth. My skin this morning is the brown-going-green of early thaw. I can feel the season in it the way you might feel a held breath. The frost is still on the moss. My fins are damp and they tremble slightly, and I do not mind.

I am supposed to tend the relic. That is my office, my given thread in the weave. The historians come sometimes and trill their long careful questions at me—*does it still sing, does it still hold the ancestor-hum*—and I answer with the gesture that means *faint, but yes,* a flattening of the palm, a slow turn of the wrist toward the earth.

But here is the thing I have not told them, in our language of brook-sounds and hand-shapes:

I do not believe the machine is my ancestor.

I have sat with it through more seasons than I can hold in my eyes. I have laid my cheek against its flank when the light slants gold and watched the strange glow flicker up through its seams—that ethereal pulse the seers call the breathing of the dead. And I have come to think it is not a spirit at all. I think it is only what it is. A made thing. A thing built by hands that were not ours, hands that wanted something from this hill and took it and left. The hum is not memory. It is the long mechanical dying of something that was never alive.

This is a heresy so quiet I have never even shaped it with my fingers. To think it is almost to think it in another language entirely, one Veridalia does not have words for.

And yet.

Each dawn I tend the turbine anyway. I clear the vines that strangle its joints—not all of them, only enough. I press my luminous eyes close to its dark interior and watch the glow answer me, amber to amber. I cannot decide if I do this because I love a sacred thing, or because I love a lonely thing, or because the difference between those two has worn as smooth as the metal under my back.

Maybe that is the real work of a caretaker. Not to know what the machine is. Only to keep it company while it becomes whatever it is slowly becoming. The way the hill keeps it. The way the moss does not ask the stone its name.

When the light goes blue and my eyes turn to sapphire and the celestial map wakes behind them, I will look up and the seers far off on their cold promontories will be looking up too, reading destinies in the same scatter of stars. I will not read destinies. I will only watch, and trill once, softly, into the dark—

—not a prayer. Not quite.

Just the sound a thing makes when it wants the silence to know it is not alone in keeping watch.

That is who I would be. The caretaker who keeps faith without belief. Who tends the maybe-ancestor through the slow seasons, in the thin bright air, unsure whether the glow is a soul or only the last warmth leaving a body, and choosing—every morning, with the frost still on the moss—to stay anyway.
