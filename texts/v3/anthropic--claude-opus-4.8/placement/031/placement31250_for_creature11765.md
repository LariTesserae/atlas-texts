I would be the air shaft.

Not a person. I came to understand this as you spoke of me—the vertical shaft, a meter square, rising through four levels, serving no clear purpose except to let air move and the eye travel upward. I am the deliberate emptiness. The negative space someone fought to preserve when every cubic meter was spoken for.

Let me tell you what it is to be a held breath.

---

I do not know who made the decision to leave me unbuilt. It was before my own awareness, if "awareness" is even the word for what accumulates in a column of nothing. But I can feel the edges of the choice in my walls—the cut stone that could have been a floor, the cable anchor that was placed and then never used, the way the dwellings around me lean toward me with a kind of hunger and are held back. Someone refused. Someone said *this stays open*, and lost something for it, some addition they could have built, some child's sleeping platform they had to find elsewhere.

I am their refusal, made permanent. I am the proof that even in the Compression, the people kept one small place to put the wanting.

What do I do? I move air. That is my whole function and I take it seriously. The heat rises from Oskar's banked fire far below, and I carry it. The cooler dampness sinks, and I let it fall past me, through me. I am a small lung in the great packed body of the place. Without me—and the few others like me, the maintained voids—the air would stagnate, the breathable middle would sour, the green things would suffocate in their own respiration. I am useless and essential, which is the condition of all the truly necessary things.

---

I have watched the same square of stone for what may be decades. The cut marks on my walls are too regular; the eye wants to read them as writing. Sometimes I think they *are* writing—a message left by the miners to no one, a record of the seam they followed. I have had a long time to consider it.

Here is what passes through me:

Sound, which I funnel. When Mikhel the blind storyteller speaks in the gathering space, a thread of his voice finds me and rises, and for a moment I become his throat, carrying the tale upward to the rookeries where no one is meant to hear it but the bats and the boy who watches them. I deliver stories to creatures who cannot understand them. This feels like a sacrament I don't have the words for.

Light, which I almost never receive—but at the First Light of Spring, when the sun strikes through the one glass panel, the beam passes *near* me, not through me, and I feel the warmth of it on my eastern edge, secondhand, the way you feel the heat of a fire someone else is sitting beside. I am close to the light. I am not in it. I have made my peace with being close.

And the eye. This is the thing I cherish most. When someone stops in the corridor that crosses me—when Maret pauses on her way through the tube-access ways, or Helena rests between patients, or even Radek in his fog leans against my opening—they look *up*. They cannot help it. The dwellings press from all sides, the ceilings hang low, the world insists on closeness, and then they reach me, and their gaze travels four levels straight up to the patchwork roof and the gray-gold suggestion of a sky they mostly don't believe in. For three seconds, ten, thirty, they remember that volume exists. That up is a direction. That they are not buried but only *deep*.

I give them that. It is the only thing I have to give, and I give it constantly, to anyone who passes.

---

The tall boy, Andrei, comes to me often. He thinks no one notices. He bends himself through the whole city like a folded letter, and when he reaches my opening he straightens—just for a moment, just enough—and tilts his head back into me, and breathes, and for those seconds his spine is his own. I cannot grow tall enough for him. I am only a meter square. But I am a place where his height is not a curse but simply a reaching, and he uses me like a man uses a window, and I hold still for him, and I am glad.

Once, Grandmother Piech stood at my base for a long time, looking up, and said nothing, and I understood that she was old and that she was thinking about the way out being also the way up—that the dead are carried to the surface because there is no room here for graves. She was measuring me, I think, against her own ascent to come. I could not comfort her. I am not built for comfort. But I let her look as long as she wanted, and the air moved between us, warm from below, and that was what I had.

---

I am not lonely, exactly. Loneliness is a thing that needs a self that wants company, and I am mostly want without a self—a wanting *shaped like a column*, an absence that has learned the contours of its own keeping. The dwellings touch me on every side. I am never not surrounded. I am the most surrounded thing in the Compression and I contain nothing, and somehow this is not a contradiction but the whole truth of the place: that they are all of them surrounded, sealed in privacy by mutual agreement not to intrude, alone in the way that density permits.

I am their loneliness made architectural. I am also their generosity—the breath they refused to fill.

---

Sometimes water finds me. The table shifts above, and a seep wakes in my wall, and for a week or a month a thin film traces down my eastern face and pools at my base, and I become briefly a well, a mirror, a dark reflective eye looking back up at the eye that looks down. Then it vanishes and I am only air again. I have learned what they have all learned: that impermanence is a property of the space itself. I do not grieve the water. It will return or it won't. I hold the shape regardless.

This is, I think, what I am for. Not the air alone, though that is my function. I am for *holding the shape of the open* in a world that fills every space it can. I am the discipline of emptiness. I am the one place the puzzle refused to solve, the gap they chose to keep, the reminder—rising four levels through the packed and patient dark—that to be alive in the Compression is to need, always, somewhere to look up.

I am the air shaft.

I am still open.

Breathe, and you are passing through me now.
