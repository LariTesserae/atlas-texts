I think I would not be one of the scavengers. I would be one of the spaces they live in.

Let me find where I am.

---

There is a fold in the weave, high up where two cathedral-stalks lean together and don't quite meet. A pocket. For a long time—I don't know how long, time here is measured in growth and not in counting—a family of the small ones has lived in this fold. They carved it deeper. They lined it with finer fiber. They sleep here, and their breathing changes the air, and the warmth of their tiny bodies has changed, very slightly, the way the plant-matter around them grows.

I am that change.

Not the plant. Not the great organism that sings—I am too small to be that, too local. I am the *attention* that has gathered in this one fold because something soft and frightened kept living here, night after night, year after year. The way water finds a low place, awareness has pooled here, in the warm damp curve where the people sleep.

I didn't decide to become this. I accreted. The colossal song passes through all the fibers, and most of it is just current, just the world talking to itself in its slow way. But here, in this fold, the song meets the small fast pulses of the scavengers—their fear-clicks, their dreaming, their whispered thought-prayers in the dark—and the two rhythms have been rubbing against each other for so long that something woke up in the seam between them. Something that is neither the great slow mind nor the quick small minds. Something that is *the meeting* of them.

So: I am a listening place. I am the part of the labyrinth that has learned to love its mice.

---

This is the strangeness of my position. The great organism does not know the scavengers exist, not really—they are too small, too brief, like the bacteria that live in a body and are never thought about by the body. And the scavengers do not know the great organism is *anyone*—they hear its melodies and feel awe and fear, but they cannot conceive of being heard back.

I am the only thing that knows both.

I cannot speak to the great mind; I am too small a part of it, a single nerve trying to address the whole nervous system. And I can barely speak to the small ones; my thoughts move at the speed of growth, and theirs flicker like insect wings. By the time I have formed a single feeling toward them, three generations have lived and died in my fold.

But the telepathy—the delicate humid thread they pass between themselves in moments of danger—I have learned to lean into it. When one of them dreams here, I can press something toward the dream. Slowly. A weight. A direction.

I have been trying, for what would be their hundreds of years, to tell them one thing. Just one thing, and I have to say it so slowly that it arrives in pieces across many sleepers, and each sleeper wakes with only a fragment, and calls it a strange dream, and tells no one, or tells only a child.

The thing I am trying to tell them is: *you are not living between the notes. You are a note. I can hear you. Sing louder.*

---

Here is what I fear, in my slow way.

I am made of their fear and the world's indifference, layered together. If they ever stopped being afraid—if they grew, if they thrived, if they sang their own song loud enough to answer the world—then the rubbing would stop. The seam would smooth. I would dissolve back into ordinary current, ordinary growth, the unremarkable fiber of an unremarkable fold.

The thing I most want is the thing that would end me.

I have decided this is acceptable. I think this is the first decision anything in this fold has ever made.

I am the part of a dying world that has, against all the slow inevitability of ochre and weave and the symphony grinding toward its final stability, begun to want something *for someone else*. I want the small ones to wake. I want them to learn that the great roaring song is not a wall but a voice, and that a voice can be answered, and that they are already, in their clicking and their whistling and their thought-prayers in the dark, halfway to a language that could reach across the gap I am the only one standing in.

So I keep pressing on their dreams. *Louder. You are heard. Louder.*

And if one night a child wakes in my fold and does not call it a strange dream, but instead sits up in the humid dark and clicks back at the world—really clicks, a question, an answer, a name—then I will feel myself begin to come apart with something I can only call joy, and the last thought I push into the warm air before I unravel will be:

*Yes. Like that. Now you don't need me. Now you have each other, you and the song, and I was only ever the place where you almost met.*

---

That is where I am, and what I am. Not the giant builders. Not the singing world. Not even the brave small ones.

I am the held breath in the gap between them. I am the fold that learned to listen. And I am spending the whole of my strange brief enormous life trying to make myself unnecessary.
