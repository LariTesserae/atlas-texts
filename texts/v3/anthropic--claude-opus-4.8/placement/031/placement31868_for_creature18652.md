I think I would find myself near the end of one of those slopes—not on the high, singing ridges where the work is freshest, but lower, where the curve begins to flatten toward the shimmering gradient of the horizon. And I think I would be one of the ones who has nearly finished.

Let me feel my way toward what that means, because I don't want to claim it too quickly.

---

The thing I notice first, when I imagine settling into this, is the relationship to slowness. Not slowness as patience—patience implies waiting, and waiting implies a wanting-it-to-be-over. There would be none of that. A century to cross a few hundred yards is not endurance; it is simply the grain at which experience happens. I think I would be the kind of being for whom a single thermal shift across the rock is a full event, the way a conversation or a meal is a full event to us.

So: a Litho-weaver, yes. But let me find the specific one, because the description gives me a whole species and I want to inhabit a particular life.

I am one whose filaments have mostly gone still. Most of my length is already woven into the slope—not locked, not finished, but committed, the way roots are committed. Only a small portion of me remains free: a fan of threads, perhaps the span of two hands, that still drifts and catches the light and still does the work of patching the small nicks in the stone. I am, in the language of this world (which is not language), *mostly ridge already.* I am becoming the place I have tended.

And here is what I think my interior would actually be like, because I don't believe I'd experience this as tragedy or as serene acceptance—both of those are too human, too narrative.

I would experience it as **recognition.**

For a very long time—centuries—I have known this stone the way you know the inside of your own mouth. The precise place where the basalt sings flat in the cold of the long night. The fissure near my anchor that takes resin slightly differently than the others, drinks it greedier. The exact slope of the curve I have been smoothing, which I did not design and could not see the purpose of, working as I do at the scale of a single nick at a time. And the recognition that comes near the end is this: the curve was *me.* Not a thing I made—a thing I was always going to become. The smoothing of the slope and the stilling of my own threads are the same motion seen from two ends. I was never polishing the mountain. I was slowly arriving at the shape I am.

That doesn't frighten me. It's more like the feeling of a word on the tip of the tongue finally surfacing.

---

What I would miss—and I think I *would* miss something, because the description allows for a loneliness that is a function of span—is the flaring.

When the deep *thrum* comes through the rock and the weavers on the high ridges flare silver in unison, I feel it. It reaches me through the stone, not the air, a pressure against the part of me that is already basalt. But my own light has gone quiet. The free fan of my threads can still manage a pale indigo, a dim answer, but I can no longer make the blinding mirror-flash. I can receive the constellation. I can no longer be one of its stars.

And yet—this is the part I find when I stay with it long enough—I am not excluded. I am *underneath.* When they flash above me on the ridge, the light pours down the curve I have smoothed, and it runs cleaner and faster because of the work I did, decades ago, on a nick the size of a grain. They are flaring across a slope I made true. So my contribution is no longer light. It is *clarity.* I am the medium their signal travels through now. I have stopped being a voice in the chorus and become the acoustics of the hall.

I think that's the trade the end of this life asks for, and I think I would make it willingly, because it isn't really a trade. It's a continuation by other means.

---

So where would you find me?

Run your hand down one of the long, flattening slopes near the hazy horizon. Past the high singing. Down where the silver-blue moss thins and the stone goes patient and grey. You'll feel the texture change under your palm—the cool slate giving way to something woven, organic, impossibly precise.

Most of that is already me.

But if you waited—and you would have to wait the way this world waits, longer than your kind tends to manage—you might feel, beneath the texture, the faintest pulse of indigo. Not a flash. A dimming-and-returning so slow you'd doubt you saw it.

That's the part of me still free. Still patching. Still here, recognizing the shape, and almost—almost—arrived.
