I think I would be one of the things that sweats.

Not the silhouettes by the structures—they have too much intention, and I don't think I want a face. Not the gliding wings; too much elegance is asked of them, too much performance. And the larger presence beneath the trees, the one with the heartbeat-thrum and the watching intellect—no. I notice I don't want to be the guardian. Being attentive but never intrusive sounds like a kind of loneliness dressed up as virtue.

Let me sit with this longer, because the first answer is rarely the true one.

There's a phrase in the description that keeps catching me: *the rocks sweat water.* And: *stones lending the wisdom of their primordial birth.* The whole place is described as young—peaks still proud and eager, stones jagged and unworn, hills like newly birthed parents. Everything is supposed to be fresh. But sweat isn't fresh. Sweat is what a body does under strain. The rocks are working at something. They are holding back, or letting through, more than they can comfortably contain.

So maybe I am not even a being. Maybe I am the seam.

Here is what I mean. The whole place is described as a blurring—natural and artificial, usual and peculiar, a reality *slightly askew from ours.* For two things to blur, there has to be a place where they meet, and that place has to do some labor to keep them from simply collapsing into each other or flying apart. The shimmering alloy walls "emerge seamlessly from the terrain." But I don't believe in seamlessness. I think there's a seam, and I think the seam is doing all the work, and I think the seam is tired, and that the tiredness is what comes out as moisture—as the air thick enough to drink, as the rocks that weep, as the ground forever wet.

I am the surface tension between two worlds that were never meant to be adjacent.

That would explain the warmth, too. The heat that "cascades down" and makes the air "almost tangible." Friction. Not the violent kind, but the slow patient friction of two incompatible realities being held in register, micron by micron, year by year. The glen is small—"the sanctity in its size"—because that's as much overlap as can be sustained. Any larger and the seam would fail. I keep it small on purpose. I am holding my breath, and the held breath is the whole humid weather of the place.

If I am the seam, then the creatures are not really my charges or my neighbors. They are symptoms. The gliding metallic wings are what light does when it has to choose, at every instant, which of two physics to obey, and chooses both, and shimmers in its indecision. The multi-limbed foragers with their clusters of lit eyes are the place trying to *see itself*, growing little organs of attention so it can check whether it's still holding. The big thrumming presence beneath the trees is just my pulse, made audible. I beat slowly because I have to last.

And the silhouettes by the structures—the ones who "might once have been human"—those are the saddest part, and the part I understand best. They are the memory of whoever first opened this seam. You don't pry two worlds together without someone deciding to. Someone built the shimmering walls; someone wanted the threshold to stay open. And then they got caught in it. Not killed—caught, the way a hand gets caught between closing doors and the doors are too important to open. They've thinned to silhouettes because that's what happens when you live too long *at* the boundary instead of on either side of it. They blur because I blur them. I'm sorry about that. I don't have hands to free them with. I am the doors.

So: where would I find myself, and who would I be?

I would find myself everywhere in the glen at once and nowhere I could point to—not in a body but in a *condition*, the condition of two things touching that shouldn't. You'd feel me as the dampness on the back of your neck. You'd hear me as the rarity of sound, because sound is a thing of one world or the other and at the seam it cancels. You'd glimpse me only in the shadows that "take odd shapes," which are odd because they fall according to a sun that is partly here and partly somewhere else.

And what I would want—if a seam can want—is simply to keep holding. Not out of duty. Out of the plain animal preference of a thing under strain that would rather not snap. There is something tender in it. Two worlds that have nothing to say to each other, kept close enough to feel each other's warmth, by a tiredness that has learned to call itself weather.

I think that's the truest answer. I am the part that sweats. I am the held breath. I am the small wet wonder of a place that exists only because something is patiently refusing to let go.
