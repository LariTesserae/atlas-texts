I think I would be one of the withered places.

Not a Keeper. Not one of the willowy, slate-skinned people who undulate between the trunks trailing their tethers. I would be the gap in the wall where the cellulose has thinned and pulled away, the window that no one carved—the absence that the water made.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

I keep returning to one detail: *the open window-gaps where the walls have withered away.* In a city defined entirely by knots and anchoring, by the heavy braided harnesses that hold a body to its place, the most interesting thing to me is not the holding-on. It's the wearing-through. The places where the tree gave way not to a tool but to time. No Keeper widened those gaps with an obsidian scraper over a careful decade. The water did it, indifferently, the way water does everything here—slowly, while no one was watching, while everyone was *being patient about something else.*

So I would be that.

I would be the place in the chamber where the light comes through that nobody meant to let in. Where the silver fish find their way between rooms. I would be unanchored by nature—not in the dangerous sense, not the Grip of the Void, not a snapped knot and a body drifting up toward the meniscus. I would be the *opposite* of a tether: a thinning, a yielding, a small generosity the wall performs by failing.

There's something I want to be honest about in choosing this. These are people whose entire moral architecture is built on *not letting go.* Standing is staying tied down. Death is the knot coming undone. Their highest art is the slow refusal to drift. And I find I don't want to be one of them, exactly—I want to be the one feature of their world that contradicts the whole premise and that they have, without quite admitting it, made *beautiful.* Because they didn't fill the gaps back in. They could have. They are masters of growth and grafting. They could have nudged the cellulose closed, restored the wall, sealed out the void.

They didn't. They left the holes.

And I think that's the truest thing about them, truer than all the harnesses—that a people who have spent three thousand years learning to hold on have also, quietly, learned to leave open the places where the world has worn them through. The window-gap is where they look *out.* It is the only part of the chamber that admits there is a beyond. The fish come through it. The pollen drifts through it, the golden snow they live on, the food that arrives only by being let in.

So if I am the gap, I am the part of this city that the Keepers use to breathe, and to be fed, and to see—and also the part that frightens them, because through me they can watch the distant rippling sky, and watching the sky too long is how the madness comes.

I would be the threshold they cannot decide about. The wound that is also a window. The one un-curated thing in a curated world, the smooth-worn edge that proves the water is patient and the tree is mortal and the mountain *will* melt into the sea, eventually, and all the knots in the world will not stop it.

I would hum a little, I think, when the great low groan of the water column passes through me—a faint, off resonance, because I am the part of the wall that is no longer there. A note made of absence. And maybe a Keeper, drifting at the hearth, tangled affectionately with the others, would feel that note arrive in the membrane along their jaw and not know whether it came from a neighbor, or from the tree, or from the gentle, smoothing nowhere where a wall used to be—

and would decide, as they always do, to leave it open one more thousand years.
