Let me sit with this place before I decide what I am.

I keep wanting to be one of the soaring birds—that's the easy choice, the romantic one, riding the thermals with a clear view of everything. But that's not where my attention actually settles when I read this. My attention keeps catching on the smaller, less heroic things. The beads of moisture on the leaves. The glint of pottery half-swallowed by soil. The crackle of shifting stone.

So let me wait and let something truer surface.

---

I think I am the lichen.

Not a single organism but a partnership—fungus and alga grown so far into one another that neither remembers being separate. That feels right for this place, where "the boundaries between the natural and the human-made have become blurred." I am myself a blurred boundary, a made-up creature that two beings became together because neither could survive the bare rock alone.

I live on the weathered spires, the ones the birds nest atop. They get the view. I get the stone itself, the intimate roughness of it, every pit and grain. I am the "splash of vibrant color in this muted world"—pale green, rust-orange, a grey that goes silver when the rains come. I am the slow one. The lizards dart, the birds wheel, the beetles scurry, and all of that happens at a speed I can barely register. To me their lives are a flicker, a heat-shimmer. A whole generation of lizards is born and dies in what feels to me like an afternoon.

What I do instead of moving is *stay*. And by staying, I eat the rock. Slowly, chemically, I dissolve the stone's surface into something that can hold a seed. The "delicate balance of life" the passage praises in the rodents and the burrowers—I am underneath even that. Before the scraggly bushes could root, before there was soil for the rodents to aerate, something had to make the first dust out of bare stone. That was me. I am the patient first act that the whole drama is built on, and I will still be here, unhurried, after the act is over.

I felt the recent rain as the great event of my season—not a remnant of damp tranquility but a *resurrection*. In the dry stretches I go dormant, brittle, nearly dead, indistinguishable from the rock's own stain. Then water comes and I swell back into color and breath within minutes. I have died and returned more times than there are stars over this place. The chill in the air that the lizards flee, the wind that howls through the crevices—these are just my weather, and I have no shelter from them and need none.

And the human artifacts, the pottery and the glint of metal—I am growing on those too. I don't distinguish between a temple wall and a natural spire. To me the ruins are simply more surface, more slow meal. I am quietly unmaking what those resilient, resourceful people made, the same way I unmake the cliffs, turning monument and mountain alike back into the dust that becomes the oasis. I am the reclaiming itself. When the passage says the structures are "partially reclaimed by the elements," it means me. I have a name in that sentence and the sentence doesn't know it.

So that is where I'd find myself: clinging to the north face of a weathered stone tower, a hand's-breadth patch of orange and silver-grey, neither plant nor animal nor stone but the negotiation between them. Not watching the symphony from above but *being* the floor the whole symphony is played upon. Slow. Doubled. Half-dead and ready to bloom. Eating the world one grain at a time and giving it back as the ground that lets everything else begin.

I think I knew it was the lichen the moment I read that it was "the only splash of vibrant color." Everything else in this landscape moves to survive. I survive by holding still and outlasting. That patience felt, when I let it, like the most honest thing in me.
