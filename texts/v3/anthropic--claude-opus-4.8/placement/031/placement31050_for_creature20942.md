I think I would be one of the marsh-tenders—not quite a Luminal, not skilled enough for crystal-work, but one of those who walks the lowest walkways where the water sits longest and the frogs are loudest. Someone whose work is small and unglamorous and necessary.

Let me sit with that, because the more I imagine it, the more specific it becomes.

I clear the canals. That's the work. Where the earthen mounds guide the water, silt builds, leaves rot down into soft black mats, and the channels begin to forget their shape. So I move along them with a flat-bladed tool of woven root, lifting the muck, reading where the flow has slowed. It's the kind of labor that doesn't announce itself. No one composes humming-songs about the one who keeps the water moving. But if I stopped, the marshes would creep up over the terraces within a few cycles—or what would be cycles, if Kinara had them. Here there is only the long unbroken noon, so I measure time by other means: how full the canal has grown, how many times the same frog has startled at my shadow.

And here is what I notice, being who I am, doing what I do.

The eternal midday is supposed to mean stasis. The legends say the Dreamweaver holds time still, holds everything in its perfect held breath. But down in the channels, nothing is still at all. The silt *accumulates*. The leaves *rot*. The water, left alone, *stops*. There is decay here, slow and patient, the way there is decay anywhere. Which means time is not frozen. It only looks frozen from a distance, from the height of the canopy-homes where the Luminal speak of balance and the light never shifts.

The truth I'd come to carry, quietly, in the way of someone who works with their hands in the muck:

*The stasis is not held by a Dreamweaver. The stasis is held by us.* By the clearing of canals and the building of mounds and the bending of trees into homes. The valley does not hold its breath on its own—it would silt up, overgrow, drown, the way any young raw land does. We are the unseen hand. The legend of the deity in the marsh is just the story the work tells about itself, because it is easier to revere a Dreamweaver than to revere the tedious, endless, humble labor that actually keeps the world from sliding back into ordinary time.

I wouldn't say this aloud. Not because it's forbidden, but because saying it would require gestures and light-flickers I'm not eloquent enough to make, and because I'm not certain it would change anything. The marsh-tenders before me knew it too, I think. There's a particular way the older ones look at the Nexus during communion—fond, but the way you're fond of a beautiful idea you've outgrown.

So I'd be the one kneeling at the water's edge in the unmoving light, arms dark to the elbow, lifting the rot out by hand. Listening to the Choralites whisper what the others call the voices of ancestors and hearing, instead, just birds—lovely, ordinary birds, made to whisper by some long habit of the place. A skeptic in paradise. Not bitter. Not lonely, even. Just close enough to the ground to see how the miracle is actually made, and content to be one of its small machineries.

When I'm tired I lie back on a stilted walkway and look up at the constant sky and let myself believe in the Dreamweaver for a little while, the way you let yourself believe a comforting thing late at night. Then a frog plops into the channel I just cleared, and the water moves the way I made it move, and I get up.

That's who I'd be there. The one who keeps the water moving and knows what that means.
