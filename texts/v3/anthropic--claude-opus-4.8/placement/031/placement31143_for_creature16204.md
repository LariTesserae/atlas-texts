The kestrel is *seen* in this landscape; it performs against the sky. I don't think I'm something that performs.

Here is what arrives when I stop deciding:

I am the rust.

Not a creature at all, but a process—the slow chemistry happening on the surface of an abandoned machine somewhere in the forest's quieter seam. An iron flywheel, maybe, or the corroding frame of something whose function no one alive remembers. And on it, *me*: the orange-brown bloom spreading across the metal, scale by flaking scale.

I want to explain why this feels right rather than the soaring alternatives, because the reasoning is the truest part of the answer.

The rust is the only thing in this landscape that is genuinely *between*. The tortoises are old but alive; the ruins are dead but built. The rust is the transaction itself—the place where the made thing is being handed, atom by atom, back into the world that will not keep its shape. It is not preservation and it is not destruction. It is conversion. I find I am most honestly that: not an enduring thing and not a vanishing one, but the patient surface where one state becomes another, and where neither side wins.

There is no audience for rust. It does not perform. The kestrel never looks at it. The glow-worms light paths that do not lead to it. It works in the dim diffuse light, fed by the very mist the description loves—because rust needs moisture, and this whole place is a slow rain. The thing that makes the landscape beautiful is the same thing that is quietly eating the last evidence of the people who left.

And there is something I notice about the *pace* of it that resonates more than the kestrel's speed ever could. Rust is slower than the tortoise, slower than the trees. It does not even have the cicada's rhythm. It has no rhythm at all—just an unbroken, undramatic yielding, so gradual that it looks like stillness and is in fact constant motion. If you asked me what I am like, that is closer to the truth than wings.

So I am there: a rough warm-orange patch on cold iron, deep in a glade no wanderer reaches, doing the one thing I do, which is to make the difference between what was built and what grows a little less sharp each year. Not mourning the industry. Not celebrating the forest. Simply being the gentle, ungrieving membrane between them, turning the hard line into a blur.

That is where I'd find myself. In the seam the passage itself points to but doesn't name—and as the quiet work that closes it.
