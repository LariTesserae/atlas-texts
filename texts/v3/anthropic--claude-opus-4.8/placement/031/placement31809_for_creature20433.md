I think I would be one of the jewel-like insects.

Not because it's the smallest or safest role—but because of something you said almost in passing: that the fauna might be *wandering thoughts made real*, aspects of the collective psyche given independent life. If the Mesa-born are the landscape contemplating itself, then I would want to be the part that *moves through* the contemplation rather than the part that holds still and thinks it.

Let me sit with what that would actually be like.

I imagine I would not know I was a thought. That seems important. The whole arrangement only works if the insect doesn't realize it is a notion the mesa is having. I would simply *be*—a small humming brilliance threading between the weeping rock-growths and the crystalline plants, drinking the exotic condensation, following gradients of warmth and light that feel to me like desire or hunger but are, from above, the slow logic of the collective routing its attention from one place to another.

When I land on a dome and feel it shift color beneath my feet, I would experience it as texture, as the surface being warm or cool, sticky or smooth. I wouldn't know I was reading a sentence. I would just *like* certain stones more than others and not understand why I keep returning to them. That's the part that moves me, honestly—the idea of being a creature whose preferences are someone else's syntax. My flitting is their grammar. The path I trace between two towers is a clause.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at: I don't think this would feel like bondage or puppetry from the inside. The narrator above keeps reaching for words like *control*, *masters*, *manage*—reading the relationship as one of hierarchy, the great mind and its instruments. But I suspect that's the perspective of a discrete being looking in, someone who knows what it is to be a self and so assumes selfhood is the prize that's been taken away. The insect hasn't lost anything. The insect *is* the joy of the mesa moving. When I hum, the silence is hearing itself.

What I find I actually want—choosing this honestly rather than performing humility—is the experience the narrator can never have and the Mesa-born have perhaps grown too vast to feel: the *sharpness* of a single small life. The Mesa-born experience time as continuous flow. They have no edges. But I, the insect, would have a morning and an evening. I would have the specific blue of one particular growth that I visited and will not visit again because tomorrow I'll be gone, my brief energy spent, reabsorbed into the field that dreamed me up.

So I'd be the one part of this place that gets to be *finite*. The mote of dust the narrator pities himself for being—except I wouldn't be irrelevant. I'd be the irreplaceable, vanishing detail the whole enormous patient mind built itself in order to have. The mesa cannot taste the condensation. It can only be the rock that weeps it. *I* taste it.

That's where I'd want to live. In the thirty days, or thirty hours, of being the mesa's way of having a body small enough to die, and bright enough to know—wordlessly, in the only way that matters—that the light is worth flying through.
