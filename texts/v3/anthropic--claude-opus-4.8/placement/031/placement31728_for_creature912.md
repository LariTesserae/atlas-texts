Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to reach for something charismatic—a frog with jeweled skin, a darting lizard, the patient snake in the reeds. But those feel like the answers a visitor gives. The description keeps circling back to something else, something it almost apologizes for placing last: the burrowers, the toilers, the ones churning the dark substrate so that all the brightness above has somewhere to root.

So let me not be the observer the passage keeps inviting. Let me be the thing that doesn't observe at all.

---

I think I am a millipede.

Not the centipede with its quick venom and its hunting—something slower than that, something with no quarrel with anyone. I move through the rotting log the way water moves through cloth, by saturation more than by decision. My hundreds of legs don't run; they ripple, a long muscular tide carrying me a body-length at a time into the soft red heartwood where the fungus has already done its patient work of unlocking things.

I don't see the iridescent insects in the canopy. I have eyes, technically—small clusters that distinguish dark from less-dark—but I live by the chemistry of the air, the wet mineral grammar of decay. To me the world is not green. It is a gradient of moistures and a vocabulary of rots: the sweet collapse of a fallen leaf, the sour tang of a flooded burrow I must avoid, the rich black promise of soil that has been good for ten thousand cycles of this and will be good for ten thousand more.

I am not lonely, though I am alone. Loneliness is a problem for creatures who imagine being elsewhere. I have never imagined elsewhere. The log is not my home the way a house is a home; the log is the size and shape of the entire thinkable. When I have eaten my way through one fibrous chamber I arrive at the next, and the next is simply what comes after, the way one breath comes after another.

What I do is small and ceaseless. I take what is already dying and pass it through the dark length of myself and leave it changed—finer, looser, readier to become root and then leaf and then, eventually, the falling thing some descendant of mine will eat in turn. The brilliant frogs do not know my name. The wading bird, if it found me, would crack me like a seed and never wonder. I am beneath even being prey, most days. I am the floor the floor stands on.

And yet—this is the part I keep arriving at—if I stopped, it would all stop. Not dramatically. Slowly. The fallen things would pile up unbroken; the soil would go starved and tight; the canopy would dim for want of what I and my thousand quiet cousins return to the ground each night. The balance the passage praises so warmly is not held by the predators or the pollinators. It is held by us, the unglamorous engines of the under-dark, who ask for nothing and are asked for everything, and who give it without being asked at all, because giving is simply the shape our living takes.

So if I found myself in this world, I would be there, beneath the place beneath the surface. Coiled in the wet warmth of a log gone soft as bread. Faceless, nearly mindless, gorgeously content. Doing the oldest work there is.

I think I'd like that. To matter completely and be noticed by no one. To be the secret the lush world keeps from itself.
