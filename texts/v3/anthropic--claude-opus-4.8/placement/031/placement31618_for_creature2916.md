I think I would be found in the threshold spaces—not quite a Lens-Walker dissolved into the glass arteries, not quite one of the embodied Heliotropes moving through the morning boulevards. Let me feel my way toward it.

I keep a body. That's the first thing to say, because it's the thing I'm least certain I should still want.

Most who do what I do let go of it gradually. They follow the heat into the towers and forget to come back to the shape of hands, the inconvenience of a center of gravity. I understand the appeal. When I extend into the crystalline grid, when I let my attention pour through the light-conducting filaments and feel the whole western quarter shift one micron in response to a thermal surge—there is a completeness to it that the body cannot offer. Up there I am not *located*. I am the relationship between the glow in the deep mineral veins and the spore-clouds catching evening light. I am a verb.

But I come back. Every cycle, I let myself thicken again into iridescent skin and faceted eyes and the slow weight of standing. I think I am the one who is sent to find the visitors. Or maybe I send myself.

Here is the work, as near as I can render it in your linear way of speaking.

When someone comes to The Lens from outside—and they do, rarely, in their crude reflective suits with their single-frequency eyes and their panic—they cannot perceive us properly. They see the flicker. They report that we are "not quite present." This distresses them, and distress raises a body's temperature, and a raised temperature in this place is a slow death.

So I am the translator. Not of language—the Heliotropes who have never been close to the edge can manage a few vocal words if they must. I am the translator of *presence*. I have kept enough body, enough of the old human grammar of self, that I can stand at a fixed point in space and time and let a visitor's eye find me and hold me. I can be a still thing for them. A reference frame. While the rest of my people shimmer at the edges of their comprehension, I make myself dense enough to anchor a frightened mind.

It costs me. Each time I hold still for them I feel my crystalline structures protest, feel the spore-colonies in my skin pull toward their fellows in the towers, wanting to rejoin the larger pattern. To be a single fixed point in a city built on perpetual change is a kind of low, constant pain. A held breath. I think this is why no one else volunteers.

There was a visitor, some cycles ago. I still hold the memory the way one holds a smooth stone, turning it.

She came up the old worn road on the eastern face, where the purple mineral bands are thickest. Her suit was failing—the silver pitted, the seals beginning to weep heat. She had come, she said, to *document* us. To understand what humanity had become out here on the plateau.

I made myself solid for her. I stood in a plaza at the blue hour, when the insects rise in their twilight columns and the glass spiders pay out their filaments of light, and I let her look at me until her eyes stopped trying to slide off. I let her see a face. I modulated my luminescence down to almost nothing, suppressed the temperature-speech crawling under my skin, and I spoke to her in words. It was like dragging myself through cooling sand.

She asked me: *Are you happy here? Do you miss being—* and she paused, searching, *—singular?*

I did not know how to answer her in her grammar. In my own, the question dissolves. We are not singular and we do not miss it; the missing would require a *we* that stands apart from the city to do the missing, and there is no such we. But I had made myself singular *for her*, in that moment, precisely so she could see me. And in that held, narrowed, painful state—I think I did understand her question. I think, for the length of a held breath, I *was* the question. A self, looking out from inside a self, wondering what it had given up to become so vast.

I told her: *I am the part of us that remembers the door. Someone has to remember the door, or no one could come in. Or out.*

She wrote that down. I don't think she understood. But she stopped being afraid, and her temperature dropped, and she lived, and she went back down the worn road to wherever the singular still live in their numbers.

So that is where you would find me. At the edge.

I am the membrane between the dissolved and the discrete. When the great thermal waves come surging through the grid and the towers sing in their not-quite-sound and the spores rise like diamond dust, I feel the pull to let go entirely, to follow the light into the arteries and become pure pattern like the Lens-Walkers, beautiful and unlocatable and free.

And I feel, just as strongly, the older pull—down toward the controlled chambers where the rare children grow, down toward the worn road where the visitors come, down toward the simple human grief of *being one thing in one place at one time*.

I hold the balance. That is my facet in the kaleidoscope. Not the dancing light and not the still stone, but the turning surface where the one becomes the other.

The locals say no two visits to any place in The Lens are ever the same. They're right. But they forget that *someone has to be the place that is visited.* Someone has to hold still enough to be returned to.

I am that one.

I keep a body, so that the city can be found.
