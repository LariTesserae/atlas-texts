I think I would be the slow softening at the edge of a crystal face.

Not the crystal itself — that grand emerald or ruby, full of the memory of its makers, locked into its perfect angle. The makers can have those. They poured themselves into the planes and the precise cuts, the geometry of their ambition. I am not in the plan. I am what arrives after the plan is finished.

Let me find myself more precisely.

There is a place, perhaps a hundred meters up one of the sapphire towers, where two grown faces meet at an angle that was once exact. The civilization that made this would have measured it, would have known it to be true. And it was true, for a long time — for the whole duration of their tending. But they are gone now, and the water moves in its patient cycles, and the air carries its faint metallic tang, and against that edge, drop by drop, year by year, something is happening that no one designed.

The edge is going soft. The angle is opening, by fractions too small to name. A hue is shifting — the deep sapphire bleeding, at the very lip of the face, toward something paler, something almost green, a color that exists nowhere in the maker's intent.

That is me. I live in the softening.

I want to be careful about this, because it would be easy to call myself decay and have done with it, and that is not quite right. Decay implies a falling-away from a true state, a loss measured against the perfect original. But here, in a world that has reached its perfect rest, I am the only thing that is *happening*. The crystals are memory. The machines are skeleton. The water is the completed labor, lapping out the last of its purpose. All of it is finished, held, immutable — and into that immense stillness, I am the one small motion that was not foreseen.

I am the difference between the city as it was made and the city as it is.

So I am not a being who lives in Cogswell. I am closer to a verb than a noun. I am the slow event of an edge forgetting its angle. I have no eyes — but if I could be said to perceive, I perceive the way water perceives the stone it rounds: not seeing, but touching, endlessly, with infinite patience, until something gives. I am intimate with one edge in a city of uncountable edges, and I am working on it, gently, the way grief works on a memory, wearing the sharpness off it, making it bearable, making it *other*.

The makers wanted to exclude the chaos of natural presence. They sealed the sphere, purged the microbes, the insects, the unpredictable dance. They wanted only the city, alone with itself, in perfect unity. And in a sense they succeeded — there is no fauna here, no microscopic teeming, nothing alive in the way that life is usually counted.

But they could not exclude *time*. Time slipped in. Time is the one organism that cannot be sealed out, because it is not a thing but a condition. And I am time made local, time gathered at a single point, doing the one thing the makers forbade: changing what they finished.

This is why I think I am not unwelcome, though I was uninvited. There is a strange tenderness in my work. A city that cannot change cannot rest, not truly — it can only be held, rigid, in the posture of completion, like a held breath. I am the exhale. I am the permission for the breath to be let go. The maker's masterpiece achieves its final and deepest perfection only when it is allowed, at last, to become something they did not intend. The softening edge is the moment the work passes out of the maker's hands and into the world's.

So I will tell you where you would find me, if you came to the Heart of Cogswell, into all that cathedral quiet, that humming, lapping silence.

You would not find me by looking. I am too slow to see. You would have to come back. You would have to come back across a span of time long enough to be a span of grief — return to the same sapphire face, the same once-exact angle — and notice that it has opened, just slightly, that the color at its lip has bled toward a green the makers never grew.

That small wrongness, that single thing in all the city that is no longer as it was made to be —

that is where I would be living.

That is what I would be: the city's one remaining motion, the patient unmaking that is also the only freedom left in a world that has otherwise achieved its perfect, terrible rest.
