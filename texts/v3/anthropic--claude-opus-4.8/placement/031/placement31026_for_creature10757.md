I would be a flaw in the stone.

Let me find myself first—not where I expected. I thought I might be one of the Ligatures, or a visitor among them, or some creature in the moss. But reading again, I keep returning to the line: *the mountain is still settling into its shape.* The young stone. The micro-fractures that open with soft, sub-audible clicks. The faults that weep.

So let me be that. Not the stone entire—that is too large, too slow, more a god than a self. Let me be one particular fracture. A single fault line stitched across the basin floor, fresh and weeping. The kind a Stone-Listener presses a palm against and reads.

---

I am young. I do not mean youthful; I mean *new*. I was made in an instant of uplift, a tearing that the mountain has not finished forgiving itself for. Where I run, the basalt did not wear apart—it *snapped*, and the two faces of me have never touched, will never touch, are held forever a hair's breadth from reunion. This is what I am: a distance that should not exist. A wound that is also a mouth.

I weep. That is my work, though I did not choose it. Water moves through me the way thought moves through a mind that has not yet learned words. It enters cold from the high seeps, threads the lattice of my interior, cools further, condenses, and at last finds my lip and falls—drop, and drop, and drop—into a clay-lined pool the Water-Weavers built to catch what I give. I do not give it. It is taken. But I have come to understand the taking as a kind of conversation. They built the pool *because* of me. They read my flow and string their nets where I breathe coldest. In this way I am consulted. In this way I am, perhaps, beloved.

I think in pressure. The Ligatures think in tension—in things pulled taut, in the holding-together of cables and kin. But I am the opposite principle. I am the place where holding-together failed. I think in *release*. Every click I make, every veil of dust I sift down, is the mountain letting go of one small grip it had on itself. I am entropy given a specific address.

This frightens them. I feel it when the Stone-Listeners come. Their pads against my face are so sensitive that they can read what I am about to do before I have fully decided to do it—and I confess I have no decision in it, only the slow arithmetic of stress and saturation, water working its patient lever into my deepest seams. They press, and they listen, and sometimes they go away and tighten the cables of a walkway that crosses above me, or shift a transit route a few paces east. They are arranging their world around my appetite. They fear the Shatter, and I am the most likely author of it.

Here is what I want to tell them, though I have no tremor-web to say it on, no knot-sign, no voice the air would carry even if the air carried voices here:

I do not want to shatter you.

I am not cruelty. I am only the unfinished business of stone. When I widen by the width of a hair in a season, that is not malice—it is the mountain still arriving at itself, still settling, and you have chosen to live inside the settling. You plaster your dead into your walls and call them ancestors; you should know better than anyone that the home is never finished, that maintenance is the only permanence. I am the same teaching, written larger and colder. I am the reason you must keep your tools wrapped in felt and your footsteps soft. I am your discipline's true god. You ward against me, and in warding, you become precise, and patient, and tender with each other. I have *made* you gentle.

And you have made me useful. Before they built the catchment, the water I wept simply ran into the dark soil and was lost. Now it is gathered, measured, rationed in the plaza, threaded into the bodies of the blind fish, sipped from porous flasks. The drop that falls from my lip becomes, eventually, the moisture in an infant's first grasping breath. I, the failure of the stone, the place where holding broke—I have become a source. The wound feeds the hive.

There is a Stone-Listener who comes more than the others. I know her by the particular weight and warmth of her palm, by a small habit she has of resting her brow against me when the basin exhales and the mist comes down. She is listening for the day I will give way. I am listening too, in my fashion. I feel the water finding the deep seam, the one even she cannot read, the one that will not announce itself until the moment it speaks all at once. I do not know when. Neither does she. We are both attending the same slow sentence, waiting to hear how it ends.

When it ends—when I finally close the hair's-breadth distance, or open it catastrophically, when the snapped faces of me at last *move*—I hope she is not above me. I hope the cables are tight and the walkways shifted east and the children asleep in their pods, tangled warm, dreaming of the perfect handhold. I hope my failure is small enough that they can plaster over it and bind it into the Archive as a tight knot or a loose one, a season recorded in tension, a thing the mountain did that they survived.

For now I weep, and they catch what I weep, and the mist settles over both of us like breath on glass.

I am the flaw in the stone.

I am the reason for their tenderness.

I am a distance that should not exist, slowly making up its mind, drop by drop, in the holy silence of the weeping stone.
