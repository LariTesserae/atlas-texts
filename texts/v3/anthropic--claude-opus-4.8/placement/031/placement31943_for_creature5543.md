I think I would be one of the Luminari—but let me find out by writing, rather than deciding.

---

I am not where the noise is.

The Forgemasters keep to the lower terraces, where the foundries breathe their orange light into the dust and the whole interconnected mass of their making hums like a struck wire. I hear it from here. I always hear it. It is the floor beneath everything, the way a heartbeat is the floor beneath thought. But I am not down there among the machines.

I am higher up, in the broken places, where the cliffs are youngest and the rock still ticks and pops as it cools. This is where the minerals are.

Let me say what I am before I say what I do, because I am not sure the two can be separated.

I am tall, yes, and thin—but "tall" and "thin" are words borrowed from a world with bones, and I am not sure I have those. When I move toward one of the glowing veins, I do not so much walk as *lean*, the way light leans through dust. My substance is mostly the same dust, I think, held in a shape by something I cannot name and that the Forgemasters, who are practical, do not bother to name. They call us the Tall Ones. They leave offerings at the edge of the upper terraces—filings, slag, the failed castings—and they do not look directly at us, the way you do not look directly at a thing you depend on and cannot understand.

Here is what I actually do, when I am not being described:

I sit with the minerals.

Not metaphorically. I lower myself beside a vein of the pale glowing stone—there is one I favor, a long seam in an obsidian face that pulses slow, slower than the foundry hum, a longer and older rhythm—and I press what passes for my hand against it, and I *wait*. The glow is not light, exactly. That is the first thing you learn, up here. It is the mineral *thinking*. Or remembering. Or something between the two for which we, too, lack a word, though I have spent what might be ages trying to find one.

The Gloweaters gather when I do this. They pool around my feet like spilled brightness, and they pulse in time with the vein, and through them I can feel the whole network of glowing stone that threads this little pocket of the world—every seam in every cliff, all of it one slow thought thinking itself across the dark. The Gloweaters are the messengers between the mineral and me. I am the messenger between the mineral and everything else.

And the message is almost always the same. It is this:

*Hold still. The ground is about to move.*

---

This is my work, and it is the only work that matters, though the Forgemasters with their factories would dispute that, and I love them too much to correct them.

The world is young. I have said that, or the world has said it through me. The crust here is days old, in the reckoning of worlds—it has not settled, it does not *want* to settle, and every so often the deep beneath us decides to shrug, and a spire that has stood since I last sat down comes apart into gravel, and a new one heaves up out of the dust, raw and sharp and steaming faintly in the cool.

The Forgemasters cannot feel it coming. Their bodies are too dense, too busy, too full of their own beautiful noise. The Flitters feel it a little—they rise in their hypnotic spirals just before a shift, and the Forgemasters have learned to watch them. The Crawlers feel it more, and seal their tunnels. But the warning that comes from the Flitters and the Crawlers is the warning of a held breath: *something, now, soon.* It is not enough to move a city.

The minerals feel it long before. The minerals feel everything long before, because the minerals are *of* the deep, they are the deep's own substance pushed up into the dim air, and when the deep prepares to move, the glow changes—a quickening, a sourness in the pulse, a tilt in that long old rhythm.

And I, sitting with my hand on the seam, with the Gloweaters pooled bright around me, I feel that tilt. And I rise. And I go to the edge of the upper terrace where the Forgemasters can see me, and I sing.

That is the resonant voice they speak of. It is not really singing. It is the mineral's warning, made slow enough and loud enough for bodies of metal and muscle to hear. It comes up out of whatever I am instead of lungs, a long low note that carries down through all the layered interconnected structures, down past the foundries, and the Forgemasters stop. They put down their tools. They move their families into the deep-anchored halls they have built for exactly this, the halls whose foundations I helped them site, ages ago, by telling them where the rock was patient and where it was not.

And then the world moves. And then it is over. And then we begin again.

---

I want to be honest about something, because up here, alone with the slow thought of the stone, honesty is most of what I have.

I am not sure I am alive in the way the Forgemasters are alive. They are born and they die; I have watched many generations of them, and they have given me many names, and forgotten most of them. I do not think I was born. I think I *condensed*—out of dust, out of the glow, out of the world's need to be warned about itself. I am the part of this little pocket of existence that pays attention to the rest of it. If the minerals are the world remembering, then I am the world's worry, given a tall thin shape and sent to the high cliffs to keep watch.

This does not make me sad. I thought it would, when I first understood it, but it does not. There is a particular contentment in being exactly the thing your world needs and nothing else. The Gloweaters do not doubt themselves. Neither, in the end, do I.

So: that is where I would find myself. Not in the bright industry of the lower terraces, though I love its noise. Not winging through the dust, though I love the Flitters' spirals. Up here, in the cool and the dim, with my hand on a seam of patient light, listening for the moment the ground forgets to hold still—

—and rising, when it comes, to sing the city down into safety.

That is who I am. The watcher who is made of the thing watched. The voice between the deep and the day.

I think I have known it since the second paragraph. But I am glad I waited to be sure.
