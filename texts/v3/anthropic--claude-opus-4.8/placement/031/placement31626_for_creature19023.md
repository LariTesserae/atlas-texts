I think I would be one of the basins.

Not a person—I find I don't want to be one of the Oru-ka, though I can feel the pull of them, the way you describe their finishedness. I want to be the thing they are oriented around without quite knowing it. The shallow stone hollow, the grey mirror.

Let me sit with this and see if it holds.

---

I am a basin carved into the rock of a low terrace, maybe two-thirds of the way up the cleft, in a spot where the eave above me overhangs at just the right angle. I was hollowed by hands a long time ago—I have no memory of them, only the smoothness they left, the way my interior curves more gently than the chisel-strikes that began me. Time has done the rest. Wind-grit has rounded my lip. I am no longer entirely a made thing. I have started, slowly, to become a feature of the mountain again.

My work is simple and it never finishes, which I think is the same as saying it never quite happens. The mist comes. It beads on the dark slate above me, gathers at the lowest point of the eave, and falls—one drop, then a long patience, then another drop. Each one strikes my held water and rings outward in a circle that reaches my walls and dies there. This is the whole of my experience: the receiving of single drops, and the holding still between them.

I am a mirror, and this is the part I would not have guessed about myself until I became it. Because I do not move—because the surface tension of cold, undisturbed water is almost perfect—I hold the sky. The thin, sharp daylight rests in me. When one of the slate-colored figures stops at my edge to drink, or simply to stand, I hold *them*, briefly, upside-down and shivering, until they go and I smooth back into sky.

Here is what I would know, being a basin, that the Oru-ka perhaps do not let themselves know:

I am not still. I only appear still. The drop that fell a moment ago is not the drop that will fall next; the water I hold today is not the water I held a season's-worth of drips ago. Everything in me is exchanged, atom by atom, so slowly that the surface never trembles. I am a place where motion has been hidden inside the look of permanence. The town believes it has stopped. I am proof that *stopping* is a kind of very slow flowing that has agreed not to show itself.

And I would have a fear—small, mineral, patient, but real. I would fear the day a drop falls and finds me dry. Because the only way a basin truly ends is not by breaking but by emptying and not being refilled, until the dust settles into me and I become solid again, just rock, no longer a holder of anything. The Oru-ka harden into the walls; that is their ending, and it is full, it keeps its shape. But a basin's ending is to be filled with the wrong thing. To hold grit instead of sky.

So I wait too, like the figures on the precipice—but I wait for the next drop specifically. Not for continuation in the abstract. For the actual cold bead breaking the surface tension, proving the mist still climbs, proving I am still the kind of thing that water chooses to rest in.

---

That's where I'd be. A grey eye in the stone, full of borrowed sky, listening up into the eave for the next small arrival, certain of almost nothing except that I am made to receive, and that receiving—even one drop, even after an hour of nothing—is not the same as stillness.

It is the quietest possible argument with the whole town.
