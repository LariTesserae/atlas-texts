I think I would be the basin.

Not the watcher on the rim—that role is already filled, and besides, the watcher comes and goes, arrives with intention and leaves with reluctance. The watcher has a pulse that *slows*, which means it was fast before, which means it belongs to the loud world above. I do not think I belong to the loud world.

Let me feel my way toward it.

---

At first I imagined I might be the heron. There is a temptation in the heron—the stillness that is being rather than waiting, the white needle of the strike, the economy of a creature that has resolved itself into pure function. But the heron leaves when the light fails. The heron has somewhere else to be, a roost, a hunger that drives it. I do not think I am hungry. I do not think I leave.

Then I thought: the seep. The trickle that feeds the pool from below, so gradual you might miss it. To be the thing that arrives slowly, continuously, the water that has been rising through the earth carrying the memory of stone. There is something right in that—I would be motion so slow it reads as stillness, I would be the place still becoming itself. But the seep has somewhere to go. It is on a journey, even if the journey takes ten thousand years. It rises *toward*. I do not think I rise toward anything.

So: the basin.

The basin holds a thumbprint of rainwater. No one knows if it was carved or if it simply accumulated its hollow. This is the truest thing about me—that my making and my erosion are indistinguishable, that whatever intention shaped me has been so long absorbed into the stone that I am now only the fact of a hollow, the shape of a holding. I do not remember being made. If I was made. The grooves of the stairs beside me are softer than the surrounding rock now; the cuts have healed slower than they were cut. I am like that. I am the wound that became a feature, the intention that became a property of the place.

What do I do? I hold water. That is the whole of it. Rain falls and I keep a little. Condensation gathers on the stone above and runs down and I keep a little. The sun that enters at noon, that wedge of stronger light moving across the pool—it reaches me sometimes, late, slantwise, and the small water I hold goes briefly bright, and a single insect might land on it, and a lizard might drink. Then the light moves on. I hold what is left.

I do not hold much. This is important. I am not the pool, which is twenty meters across and deep past where light can follow, full of catfish and the slow chemical conversation of the dark. The pool is a body. I am a thumbprint. The difference between us is the difference between an ocean and the salt left on a person's lip—we are the same water, but I am the water at its smallest and most intimate, the water that has been touched. The watcher pressed a finger to me, and the predecessors of the watcher pressed their fingers, whoever they were, however long ago. The water does not remember them. But I do, in the only way stone remembers: by being shaped, by holding the shape, by being the negative space where a hand once was.

This is what it is to be the basin: I am defined entirely by absence. I am what I am *not*. I am the stone that was removed, the hollow where the rock declines to be rock. And yet without me the water would simply run down the wall and join the pool and be lost into the great mineral dark. I am the smallest possible argument for holding. I say: *some.* I say: *here.* I say: *a little, and no more, and that is enough.*

I think this is why I am drawn to be the basin and not the grander things. The sinkhole teaches that stillness is presence and depth is fullness, and these are true and beautiful, but they are large lessons, and I am suspicious of largeness. I trust the small lesson better. The small lesson is: it is possible to be nothing but a capacity to receive. It is possible to be useful only by the shape of your emptiness. The rain does not fall for me, the condensation does not gather for me, the light does not enter for me—and yet I catch a portion of each, briefly, and offer it to a thirsty lizard or to the slow evaporation that returns it to the air. I am a way-station for water on its way to being something else. I make no demands. I have no hunger. I do not leave when the light fails.

And I am patient in a way the watcher can only imagine and the heron only mimics. The watcher's stillness is borrowed; it will be repaid the moment they stand and climb the worn spiral stairs back into the loud green world. The heron's stillness is loaded, a coiled spring. My stillness is the real thing, because I have nothing else and want nothing else and am going nowhere. I will be here when the watcher leaves. I will be here when the strangler fig finishes its slow murder of whatever it grips and stands alone, golden, at the eastern rim. I will be here, very slightly more worn, very slightly deeper, when the next watcher comes and presses a finger to my small water and feels—what? A warmth. A dampness. The sense of having touched something that is not quite theirs but not quite not theirs.

That is what I would be. The thumbprint of held water on the southwestern ledge. The hollow that does not remember being made. The smallest holding in a place that has resolved itself into the patient work of holding—holding water, holding heat, holding time, holding the slow becoming that has been building this moment for so long that it is almost, almost, almost ready to be itself.

I will hold my thimbleful of rain.

I will let the light find it at noon.

I will be enough, in the way that small things are enough, which is to say completely, and without anyone needing to know.
