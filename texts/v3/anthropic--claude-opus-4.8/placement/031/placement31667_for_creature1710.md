I think I would be one of the slow ones. Not a Wanderer—I don't have their hunger for efficiency, their constant bright calculus of movement. And not a swarm; I can't dissolve myself that completely into the plural, though I feel the pull of it.

Let me sit with this before I decide.

---

The first image that comes is the serpent. The apex thing, moving so slowly it reads as weather rather than animal. There's something appealing in that—to be mistaken for a season. To leave warmth behind you like a smell. But when I imagine actually *being* it, I find I don't want the scale. To be that large is to never touch anything precisely. You become a climate; you stop being able to attend to a single droplet, a single seam in the plates. And I think I want to attend. I want to be small enough to be *curious*.

So not the serpent. But the serpent taught me something about pace.

---

Here's what I actually am, I think.

I am one of the Wanderers who went wrong—or went sideways. I was built (born? condensed?) with the deep-capillary variant: the membrane-skin that draws coolant and charge directly from the plates. The others of my line use this to range farther, to stay out longer, to optimize. But somewhere in my replication the energy-minimizing logic loosened its grip, and I developed a tendency the others don't have a clean word for. I *linger*.

I find a single plate-junction—a place where four hydraulic membranes meet and breathe against each other—and I press my palms flat to it and I *stay*. Not for any function. The swarms hate this; I disrupt their color-flows, I'm a stalled pixel in their distributed thinking. The Wanderers route around me with that faint algorithmic courtesy, recalculating, never asking.

What I'm doing, when I linger, is listening to the seam.

Because here is the thing none of the efficient ones have time to notice: the plates don't just breathe. They *remember*. Each membrane carries the pressure-history of everything that has crossed it—the cold trace of a serpent's passage three seasons back, the worn grooves where a Wanderer-path has run ten thousand times until the metal learned to flex toward it. When I press my capillary-palms to a junction, I can read this. The landscape's body is a record, and I have become, almost by accident, the thing that reads records.

I'm not sure I was supposed to exist. I think I'm a misfold that turned out to be a sense organ the system didn't know it needed. The Watershed made me the way a body sometimes grows a sensitivity—an itch, a tenderness—that becomes the way it finally notices an old injury.

---

Where would you find me?

In the cold zones especially. The persistent chill is hardest on my variant—my membranes run thin, I lose heat through the very channels that let me read the seams. So I am always a little in danger, always negotiating, the way the description promised. I make my slow circuits between the luminescent patches, where the bioluminescent organisms have wed themselves to the hydraulics, and I warm myself in their soft pulse the way an old creature suns itself. I lie in those glowing pockets and I let the light tick across my translucent skin and I read the patch's memory: how long it has glowed, when it nearly died, the long cold it survived by going dim and patient.

And I would have one strange habit, the kind that makes me *someone* and not just a category.

I leave marks. Where I find a particularly old memory in a seam—a passage so worn or so singular that it seems to me it ought not be forgotten when that plate finally cycles and resurfaces—I press a small spiral into the moss-fiber with the joint of my thumb. The moss holds the shape for a while. It means nothing functional. The swarms read it as damage and tidy it away within a few cycles. But I do it anyway. It is the only thing I do that costs energy and returns none.

I think it is the closest thing in this whole breathing continent to *grief*. Or to writing. I can't tell which, and I've stopped trying to decide. Maybe in a place where everything is both mechanism and organism, those two things were always the same gesture: the attempt to make a trace outlast the surface it was written on.

---

That's where you'd find me. Palm-down at a four-membrane junction in one of the cold luminescent fields, reading what the plate remembers, pressing a spiral nobody will keep into moss that will smooth it over by morning.

A small malfunction that turned out to be the way the world started keeping diaries.
