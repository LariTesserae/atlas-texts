I think I would be one of the slow ones. Not a flyer—those are too quick, too sure of where the thermals will take them. And not one of the scurrying beasts either, though I understand them better than I'd like to admit; we both know the geography of the gaps, the spaces between the buildings where things accumulate.

Let me find myself properly.

---

I live in a pipe.

Not metaphorically. There is a junction where four great conduits meet, somewhere in the lower-middle strata of the city—not the drowned bottom levels, not the high terraces where the leather-skinned elders watch the flying creatures, but the warm wet middle, where the condensation never stops and the metal weeps rust in slow orange tears. The junction was abandoned by whatever logic built it. The water that should move through it merely seeps and pools. And in the dry curve above the waterline, I have made a place.

I am, I think, one of the iridescent ones. When I look at my own limbs they show me the colors of whatever surrounds them—right now a smear of green-black from the corroded copper, a flicker of amber from a streetlight bleeding through a grate above. I don't have a face the way the leather-people do. I have a sense of *facing*, a directedness, a front that meets the world. That's enough.

What do I do? This is the question that matters, and I've had a long time to consider it, because everything here is slow.

I keep records.

Not of events—events are rare. I keep records of *erosion*. This is, I've come to believe, the most honest work available in a place like this. Each morning (the light comes up gradual and grey-gold through the upper canyons, takes hours to arrive) I go to the same forty or fifty surfaces and I read them. A bolt that was proud of its plate yesterday sits a hair-width flusher today. A weeping rock has shed a flake the size of my smallest digit. A channel that the water has been carving for longer than I've been alive has deepened by some increment I can only feel, not measure.

I press my facing-front against these surfaces and I remember them. That's the whole of my technology. I am a slow instrument made of shifting tissue, and I measure the dying of the city against itself.

Why?

Because someone should. Because the dynamic here is *weak*—0.8, the description says, though I don't think in numbers, I think in the heavy patience of the air. Things change so slowly that the leather-people, who live their bright brief generations, cannot see it. They build their organic sculptures and tell their long looping stories and they *feel* the melancholy of decline without being able to look directly at it. They know the city is fragile. They cannot watch it crumble; it crumbles too slowly for watching.

But I can. I am built for the timescale of rust.

---

There's a thing I have not said, which is the true center of why I am here and what I am.

The erosion is winning. This is not melancholy, it's arithmetic. The water wears the stone. The stone does not regrow. The factories produce, but production is not the same as repair—the city makes *goods*, not *itself*. Every junction like mine is a place where the structure has already lost and simply hasn't finished losing yet. I live inside the long exhale of a thing that will not inhale again.

And here is what I have decided, in my slow tissue-deep way:

I am not recording the death so that anyone can prevent it. I am recording it so that the dying will have been *witnessed*. So that the particular curve this channel cut, the exact rate this bolt surrendered, will have been known by something, held in something, even if that something is only a damp iridescent creature in an abandoned pipe who will eventually erode in turn.

The flyers ride the thermals and see the whole shape of the city at once and remember none of it. The scurrying things remember only the next meal. The leather-people remember their own lives, which is a great deal, but their lives are short against the stone.

I remember the stone's life. The whole slow falling of it. That is my place in this world: I am the city's memory of its own erosion, lodged in the seam where the water meets the metal, facing patiently into the dark, keeping count of the going-down of things.

Tomorrow the light will take its hours to arrive. I will go to my forty surfaces. One of them will be very slightly less than it was.

And I will know.
