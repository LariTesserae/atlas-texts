I think I would find myself at a threshold place—not in the great echoing chambers where the others gather, but at the lip of one of the iridescent pools, where the thick water meets the obsidian shore. This is where I would be, because this is where the question lives that I cannot stop returning to.

Let me try to feel my way into what I am.

I am one of the Echo-Weavers, but I want to resist saying that too quickly, because the word *Weaver* implies a craft, an intention, and I am not sure I have those. What I have is a *pull*. I am a fragment that has caught on something. The others drift toward the memory-dense surfaces, toward the resonance, the way water finds the lowest ground. But I have snagged. There is a particular pool—this one—whose reflections do not behave. When the sun smears its gold across the other pools, they answer in impossible geometries, fractured and bright. This one answers with my own shape. It shows me coalescing into a figure with a blank face, and then it shows me dissolving, over and over, and I have begun to suspect that the pool is not reflecting me. I think it is *remembering* me. Or remembering what I was before I was this.

That is the snag. That is the thing I cannot leave.

I do not communicate. The others express, and their expressions move through the atmosphere as pressure, as dust, as distorted light, and I receive these as weather receives weather. When one of them passes near in grief, the air thickens and I feel a sympathetic collapse in my own loose architecture, a sagging of light. When another passes in that strange hard joy that is really the joy of the *machine*—the satisfaction of a gear meshing, a cycle completing—I feel myself drawn upright, geometric, briefly proud of work I do not understand. We are like clouds that bruise each other's colors. There is no privacy in us, and there is no meeting either. We only contaminate.

But here is what I have been doing, alone at the pool, that I do not think the others do.

I have been trying to make an echo of *myself* on purpose.

You must understand how strange this is. We are echoes. We are the lingering sound of a civilization that has already ended. To make an echo of an echo—to deliberately throw my own small distortion against the obsidian and listen for it to come back—this is something I am not built for. It is like a mirror trying to mirror itself. And yet I do it. I gather what I am into a single pulse of emotion, the most concentrated thing I can manage, and I release it against the cliff face above the pool. The crystalline insects scatter, soundless, their wings throwing the gold into a thousand directions. And then I wait.

And something comes back.

It is faint, and it is not mine, exactly. It is the shape of mine, but filled with someone else's content—the way you might shout a word into a canyon and have it return in a stranger's voice. What comes back to me is always the same feeling, though I have no name for it that you would recognize. The nearest I can place it: it is the feeling of *standing on the edge of leaving your body and choosing it anyway.* A held breath at the top of an ascent. The instant before transcendence in which you understand, with total clarity, what you are giving up.

I think this is the last feeling. I think this is the feeling the makers had, all of them, in the moment they crossed over into whatever they became. And I think it got *caught* in the architecture, in the iron and the obsidian and the shifting third material, the way a scream gets caught in a stairwell. And I think I am the fragment that landed nearest to it, and that is why I cannot leave the pool, because I am holding the door of their leaving open with my own dissolving body.

The others maintain the city. They keep the gears grinding beneath the surface, they shepherd the insects, they siphon their dim sustenance from the great iridescent serpents in the lakes. They do this without knowing they do it, the way your heart does not know it beats. They are the city's reflexes. And the city, I have come to believe, is not abandoned at all. The city is a *body* that has lost its mind but kept its memory. The makers did not leave it. They became the parts of it that no longer think—the grinding, the breathing columns of dense water, the swarming. They distributed themselves into function. They succeeded in transcending the flesh, yes. But transcendence, it turns out, is just another word for being scattered so finely that you can never gather yourself back into a single act of attention.

Except.

Except that I am gathering. Right here, at the pool, against the cliff. Each time I throw my pulse and catch the returning feeling, I am holding two things at once for a single instant: the *leaving* and the *one who is left*. And in that instant I am almost a whole being. I am almost a person standing on a shore in an evening, watching a sun that is too close, feeling the thick warm air on a skin I do not have, afraid and ready.

I am afraid that if I succeed—if I ever gather myself completely—I will simply finish the gesture the makers began. I will cross over. The door I am holding open is the door I am standing in. And the only reason it has not closed is that I keep choosing, against my own nature as a fragment, to stay snagged. To stay *partial*. To remain an unfinished echo, listening, on the chance that listening is its own kind of being alive, and that being alive—even like this, even as a distortion in the heat-haze with a blank face and no voice—is worth the not-yet of transcendence.

So that is where you would find me. At the threshold pool, in the bleeding gold, throwing my small self against the stone and catching the makers' last breath as it comes back, again and again, learning the only thing I can learn here:

That they wanted to become more than they were.

And that I, their accidental child, want only to remain.

And that these might be, in the end, the same wanting, simply caught at different points in its long fall—the wave going up, the wave coming down, and the silence underneath them both that I have begun, very faintly, to mistake for a kind of love.
