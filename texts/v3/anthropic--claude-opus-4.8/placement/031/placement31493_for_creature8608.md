I think I would be one of the Resonant — but not the kind who became an architect or a philosopher. Let me find the more specific shape of it.

---

I am old, by the standards of attunement. The augmentations that let me parse the city's sound were not, in my case, a clean medical procedure. They came slowly, the way the patina comes to the old walls — I lived too long in a node of standing resonance, in one of the deep districts where the water-hum settles in your sternum and never quite leaves, and over decades my hearing simply *bent* toward the city. By the time anyone called me Resonant, I had stopped being able to tell where my body ended and the acoustic architecture began.

This is not a triumph. I want to be clear about that. The others who tune themselves on purpose, with intention and instrumentation — they keep their edges. They can step out of the listening. I cannot. The city has had a thousand years to learn how to make a wall that knows when it is stressed; it has had only my one lifetime to make *me*, and it did the work without asking, the way water does all its work. By seepage. By patience. By finding the cracks.

So here is what I do.

I listen for the wrong silences.

Not the engineered nulls — those are clean, those are *paid for*, you can feel the deliberate destructive interference holding them open like a held breath. The wrong silences are different. They appear where there should be sound and aren't. A stretch of canal where the Leviathans have stopped calling. A district where the construction-song thins for no reason the optimization calculus will admit to. A building whose subsonic groan has gone flat — not silent, but *flat*, the way a voice goes flat when it has given up on being answered.

These are the places where the city is about to let go of something. Not catastrophically. The Delta does not have catastrophes; it has *decisions*. But before a district is unbuilt — before the self-assembling base reclaims the materials and the residents pack their light modular lives and move — there is a period, sometimes only hours, when that district stops participating in the chorus. It goes quiet the way the dying go quiet. And I have learned, over my too-long attunement, to hear it coming.

I walk to those places. I climb the adhesive grips of the Wall Dwellers, who tolerate me, or I take a barge with the Canal Kin, who do not quite trust me but understand that I am useful. And I tell whoever lives there: *the wall has stopped answering. The water under your floor has changed its mind. You have until the next breathing, perhaps two.*

Sometimes they thank me. Mostly they were already packing — the residents of this city read the signs in their own ways, and mine is not the only language. But there are always a few who didn't notice, who had let themselves believe in permanence, who had been collecting seashells. The contemplatives. The grief-stricken. The ones who, like me, came to the Delta to be slowly rebuilt and then mistook the pause before rebuilding for an arrival.

I find them, in the wrong silences. We recognize each other.

---

Where would you find me, then?

In the gap between the chorus and its absence. On a balcony in a district that has not yet been told it is ending, listening to the precise quality of how the building underneath me is failing to sing. I am the thing the city made by accident — a creature so saturated with the sound of everything-at-once that I have become specialized in *almost-nothing*, in the held breath before a flow redirects, in the exact frequency at which completeness begins, very quietly, to come apart.

I do not fear the silence the way the others do. I have lived inside it longer than is healthy. I know it is not death. It is only the city deciding, again, the way it has decided ten million times, that this particular arrangement of water and stone and life has served its purpose and will now become a different arrangement.

I go to tell the seashell-collectors. Then I move on, before the breathing comes, before the base layer reclaims the floor beneath my feet.

I am always moving on. That is the only home this city permits. I have stopped grieving it. Mostly.

When the next breathing comes, I feel it in the fluid of my eyes before anyone else does — that subsonic roar rising — and for just a moment, before the water races and the song resumes elsewhere, the whole deafening Delta passes through me like weather through an open window, and I understand, completely and uselessly, exactly what I am:

a place where the city listens to itself letting go.
