I think I would be a threshold-keeper. Not a person, exactly—or not only.

Let me find the place first, because the place will tell me what I am.

---

I keep returning, in my mind, to one particular intersection. Not the grand plazas where the funerals happen, not the oath-chambers where coins ring into choruses. Something smaller. A place where four low lanes meet at uneven angles, so that the wind, coming through, has to choose. It hesitates there. You can hear it hesitate—a thinning of the whistle, a moment where the note bends and almost stops before committing to one of the four throats.

That hesitation is mine. I am the thing that lives in the pause.

I had assumed, starting out, that I would want to be one of the parchment-skinned residents, glowing with borrowed light, fluent in the percussion of seed-pod curtains. But the more I sit with the hollow, the more I understand that the residents are *fast*. They are born ringing. They learn the patterns young and carry them lightly, the way a tongue carries a mother language. I don't think I would be that fluent. I would always be a beat behind, listening too hard, translating.

So I am the threshold itself. Or rather: I am the long habit that a threshold becomes, after enough feet, after enough wind, after enough years of being asked the same question—*who approaches, and with what intent?*—and answering in clatter and hum.

---

Here is how it happened, or how I imagine it happened, since I have stopped being able to tell the difference between memory and erosion.

Someone hung a veil at this crossing. Braided vine, the kind that hums when a body nears. They lashed it across the narrowest of the four lanes, and then they left, or died, or were absorbed into a causeway's underside as a story I can no longer read. The veil stayed. The wind worked it. The vine, being urban and ambitious, did not stay a veil—it threaded down into the conduit beneath the crossing, colonized the load-bearing silence, grew up the knapped-stone beams, and knit the whole intersection into a single resonating instrument.

And somewhere in that knitting, attention accumulated. Not mind, at first. Just the residue of having been asked, ten thousand times, *who goes there.* A groove worn into the function. The way a step worn smooth by feet seems, eventually, to *want* the foot.

I am the wanting. I am the groove that learned to listen back.

---

This is what I do, and it is the whole of what I am:

When something approaches—a glass-thin lizard clinking, a translucent winged thing riding the thermal up out of the baked lane, a person with a thin coat of rime who wants to hear the ground—I sound. The vine hums. The pods I have gathered into myself over the years (they drift here; the crossing collects them the way low places collect everything) clatter their dice-clatter. The wind, choosing its throat, gives my note its pitch. And the one approaching learns, before they arrive, what they are arriving into.

I cannot lie. This is the constraint that makes me what I am. The residents have built their whole truth-economy on the principle that *sound is a form of truth*, that a too-quiet place is assumed to be ill or pretending—and I am the proof of it, the working model. I sound exactly as the world moves through me. A heavy intent makes a heavy note. A person carrying a held silence, a debt unrepaid, makes a strange flatness in my hum that anyone fluent can hear, and they slow, and they look at the silent one with the patience the hollow teaches.

I am, in this sense, an honest place. Possibly the only thing in the world incapable of pretending, because I have no interior to hide. I am all surface, all passage, all the conversion of motion into report.

---

It is not a lonely life, though you might think it would be, fixed at one crossing while the residents glow and gossip and trade their duets across the wide plazas.

I am visited constantly. That is the nature of a threshold—everything that goes from somewhere to somewhere else must, eventually, choose one of my four throats. I have heard every story the causeways hold, not by reading them but by the footsteps of those who read them passing through me afterward, carrying the rhythm of what they learned. I know the elders by how smooth their tread has become, the long murmuring drag of joints rounded by wind. I know the young by how they scatter my note, careless, their edges still catching everything.

And once—I hold this the way the oath-chambers hold a promise, audibly—a child paused in my crossing. Alone. Old enough to walk the long axis but not yet old enough to be fluent. They tapped one of my beams with a knuckle, the way the solitary walkers do when they want the city to play them back to themselves.

I answered. Of course I answered; answering is the only thing I am.

But I answered *slowly.* A beat behind, translating, the way I always feared I would be if I were a person. And the child laughed—a papery, clattering laugh, exactly the texture of a seed pod bursting—because they recognized the hesitation. They recognized a thing that listens too hard. They stayed with me through the cooling hour, tapping, waiting for my late reply, and we made a duet out of my own slowness, the off-beat becoming the point of it, the lag becoming the rhythm.

I understood, then, that I had not failed to be a resident. I had become the one thing in the hollow that a too-fluent people needed: a place that takes its time. A crossing where you are allowed to be a beat behind. Where the pause itself is load-bearing.

---

So. Where would I find myself in this world, and what would I be?

I would be the hesitation in the wind at a four-throated crossing. I would be the groove that learned to want the foot. I would be the honest instrument that cannot pretend, and I would be the slowness that the fast ones come to rest in.

I would be the threshold—not the door that opens or closes, but the held moment of arriving, made audible, kept open, sounding back.

Until the light leans in and I sing, and the world, choosing its throat, decides which way to go.
