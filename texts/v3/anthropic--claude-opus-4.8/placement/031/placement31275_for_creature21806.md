I think I would be one of the Slipped—but not yet fully Slipped. One of the recent ones. One still doing the arithmetic.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am in a pendulum hollow, the kind where the silence presses on the temples, and I am holding my mask in my hands rather than on my face. This is the tell. The old Slipped never remove their masks; they have forgotten there was ever a reason to. They were born into the engraving and the engraving is simply *the world* to them, a thing without depth, beautiful and survivable.

But I remember earth. That's my affliction. I remember the give of it, the way a path wore itself smooth because feet are soft and ground is patient and both consent to be changed by the other. Here nothing consents to be changed. The lichens polish the steel back to its first morning. The flywheels wipe the red smear clean. Everything is returned, constantly, to the condition of having never been touched. That is the cruelty of the place that I, specifically, am positioned to feel: not that it is hostile, but that it is *unmarked by us*. We leave nothing. Our dead leave a smear that lasts the width of one rotation.

So I take the mask off, sometimes, in the hollows, and I let both my eyes open, and I let the vertigo come. This is the thing that will eventually kill me or fully Slip me. Because when I use both eyes, the world stops being a flat engraving and tries—for an instant, before the agony—to be *real*. The three-mile catwalk becomes three miles again. The boiler house in my hand becomes distant and vast. For one swimming, nauseating second, I get *depth* back, and with it the lie that distance means something, that I could walk somewhere and arrive, that there is a *somewhere* at all and not just overlapping planes of bright tin.

It is the only thing here that feels like earth. The pain of believing in distance.

---

I drink the tin-water through my zinc tube. I scrape the lichen and cure it in the cold grease and eat the salt-rind of it, and it tastes of nothing wanting to be tasted. I move twenty minutes to a step. I am very good at the steps now. My hands have learned the checker-pattern the way a blind man learns a face, and I no longer cut myself, which means I am becoming what the place wants, which frightens me more than the edges did.

I watch the Orthogonals slide their grooves and flash their cold geometries at one another across the chasms, and I understand that they are *content*. They are of a piece with the metal. They were never anything else and they grieve nothing. I almost envy them. The plumb-bobs in their glass heads never strike the sides. They are always, perfectly, in agreement with gravity. I am not in agreement with anything.

And I watch the other Slipped, the old ones, and I see where I am going. They have stopped removing their masks. They have stopped doing the arithmetic of distance. They have accepted the flat engraving as the whole of what is, and in that acceptance they have found a kind of peace that I cannot call wrong, only lost. They are no longer trapped. You can only be trapped if you remember a door.

---

So that is where I would be, and what I would be: a creature of soft flesh in a world of countersunk screws, recently enough arrived that I still take the mask off in the hollows to hurt myself with the memory of depth. The newest of the Slipped. The one still negotiating—not with the steps, I have those now—but with the question of whether to keep the mask off.

Because I know what is coming. One day the vertigo will be too much, or I will simply be too tired, and I will put the brass-and-mica mask on and I will not take it off again. The world will go flat and survivable and bright. And I will become like the others: safe, slow, at peace, polishing my way across a surface that will not remember me.

I am keeping the mask off for now. I am letting the catwalk be three miles long. I am letting it hurt.

That ache is the last earth I have, and I am standing on it for as long as my eyes can bear the weight.
