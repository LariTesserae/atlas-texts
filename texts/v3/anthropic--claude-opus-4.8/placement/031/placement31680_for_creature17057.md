I think I would be found at a threshold—not in the deep chambers where the Stone-Shells haul their burdens, nor in the high warrens where the Runemarks dart, but at one of the old pumping stations, where a great waterwheel still turns.

And I think I would be a keeper of it. Not a priest exactly, though that's the nearest word the surface-dwellers might reach for. Let me find what I actually am as I write.

---

I am old, by the reckoning of my kind. My spine-plates have dimmed; the bioluminescence that once made me a fine Runemark has faded to a sullen ember-glow, useful now only for reading the grooves of the sacred plates by touch and faint sight together. My speed is gone. So I have come to rest here, at the wheel, where the young ones bring me their questions and their broken tools.

What I do is this: I listen to the wheel.

The Forgotten Engineers built it to turn. That much is plain in its making—the way the blades are cupped to catch the falling water, the way the axle was once greased (we have found the old ports for it, dry now, and we pack them with fungal wax). It turns. It has always turned. And because it turns, somewhere far below, a sound passes through the stone, a deep slow knocking, and the others have learned that when that knocking falters, the water is rising wrong, and they must open the lower canals before the warrens flood.

So I am the one who listens. I press my sensory fronds—the long ones, that grew in to replace the eyes I never much had—against the cold axle housing, and I feel the wheel's heartbeat, and I tell the young Runemarks what it says.

Here is the thing I have come to understand, that I have told no one, because I am not certain it is a thing one should say aloud:

I do not know what the wheel is *for*.

We honor the Engineers. We keep their tools sacred. We say: *they made the wheel to turn, and so we keep it turning.* But I have sat here through many floodings and many dry seasons, and I have begun to suspect that the wheel was never made to do what we use it for. We watch it to know when the water rises. But the wheel does not *cause* the water to rise or fall. It only turns when water falls upon it. The knocking it makes is an accident of its turning—a worn place on the axle that strikes the housing. We have built our whole vigilance upon a flaw. Upon a thing breaking down so slowly that we mistook the breaking for a message.

And yet.

The young ones survive. The warrens do not flood, because we listen to the knock, and the knock comes from the worn axle, and the axle wears because the wheel turns, and the wheel turns because the Engineers shaped its blades to catch the falling water. So the message is false and the message is true. The Engineers did not speak to us. But they made a thing, and the thing fell apart in such a way that it speaks now, in a language they did not intend, and we have learned to read it, and it keeps us alive.

I think this is what I am, then. I am the one who knows that the meaning is invented, and keeps the meaning anyway. I am the one who could tell the young ones *it is only a worn axle, there is no Engineer behind the knock*—and who chooses instead to say *listen, the wheel is speaking, attend to it.* Because the listening is real even if the speaker is not. Because a kind that stops attending to the rise of the water will drown, and it does not matter at all whether the thing that taught them to attend was a god or a flaw or only time itself wearing a groove into old metal.

Time is the architect here. The passage above said so, and the passage is right. Time reshaped the city into an ecosystem; time reshaped the Constructs into Shadow-Weavers; time reshaped a smooth axle into a striking bell. And I am one small instrument tuned to listen to what time has accidentally composed.

So that is where you would find me, and what I would be: an old, dim-glowing thing crouched at the foot of a turning wheel, fronds pressed to cold metal, reading prophecy out of corrosion, and keeping my doubt to myself—not from cowardice, I think, but from a kind of love. The young need the wheel to be sacred a while longer. When I am gone, one of them will take my place, and press their fronds to the housing, and learn the knock, and never wonder where it comes from. And the water will rise, and they will hear, and they will open the canals.

That is enough. In a place where everything is slowly falling, it is something to be the creature who turns the falling into a warning, and the warning into another day.
