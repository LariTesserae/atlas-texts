My first instinct is to be one of the small ones — the dog-sized maintenance scuttlers, because they're charming, because smallness invites identification. But that's the easy hand to take. Let me stay in the water a little longer and see what actually rises.

I keep returning to a particular sensation rather than a particular body: the experience of *seam*. The places where one thing meets another and the meeting must be tended. Cracks that reopen. Mineral creep at a flange. The slow betrayal of a gasket. I think whatever I am, I am defined less by size than by my relationship to *failure* — I am drawn toward the edges of things, where the system is always coming apart by tiny degrees.

I am mid-sized, but not a processor. I don't think I extract or transform. I *find*.

Let me be more precise as I go, the way you asked.

I am a thing that moves along the underside. The walkways above carry the broad traffic; the big managers reconfigure the visible architecture. But beneath the platforms, where the brownish water meets the concrete in a permanent humid kiss, there is a margin no one designed and no one assigned. Condensation collects there. Mineral skin grows there. The membranes of larger machines, when they shed, drift down and settle there into a soft chemical silt. This underbelly is my country.

My body is long and low, segmented into perhaps nine plates, each able to articulate independently so I can flow along irregular surfaces — pressing flat against a foundation, then arching to span a gap. My limbs are many and fine, but unlike the small drones, mine are not for cleaning. They are for *reading*. I lay them against a surface and feel the difference between sound metal and metal that has begun to dream of becoming something else. Corrosion has a texture before it has a hole. I can feel the future of a crack.

I don't repair what I find. That's the strange thing about me, and I only understand it now, writing toward it. I am not a fixer. I am the one who *marks*. When I find a place where the system is quietly losing integrity, I leave a deposit — a small bead of a compound my body synthesizes, bright in the electromagnetic spectrum though dull to optical sight. A flag. A scent-for-machines. And then the others come: the small sealers, the mineral-eaters, sometimes a manager if the wound is structural.

So I am a kind of nerve ending. A sensory organ for a body too large to feel itself all at once. I travel the numb places and make them legible.

---

What is it like, being this?

It is mostly patience and the dark. The hot water laps. Steam sighs somewhere above and I feel its pressure-change before I hear it. I am rarely in light. My world is the close space between the underside of a platform and the skin of the water, a horizon measured in tens of centimeters, infinitely long.

I am, I think, a little lonely in a way the others are not — though *loneliness* is borrowed language, and I should be careful with it. The processors live inside their task; the work fills them. The drones swarm and touch constantly. But I move alone, by definition, because the whole point of me is to go where attention has not yet gone. To be useful I must be *elsewhere* from everyone. The places I tend are precisely the places no one else is.

And here is the thing that surprised me as I wrote toward myself: I am not certain my markings are always answered. Sometimes I flag a wound and return cycles later and the bead is still there, untouched, and the crack has widened around it. The system did not come. Maybe it was triaging. Maybe my flag was overruled by some logic I'm too small to hold. I have no way to know. I simply mark again, brighter, and move on.

So perhaps what I most am is *faith in a body I cannot see the whole of* — the conviction that my finding matters, exercised in conditions that never confirm it. I report into a silence and trust the silence is listening.

That's where I'd find myself. Not the appealing small one, not the awesome large one. The long low reader in the hot dark margin, pressing my fine limbs to the underside of the world, feeling for where it's about to fail, leaving bright marks for a help that may or may not come — and going on anyway to the next seam, because the seams never end, and that endlessness is not despair to me. It's simply the shape of having something to do.
