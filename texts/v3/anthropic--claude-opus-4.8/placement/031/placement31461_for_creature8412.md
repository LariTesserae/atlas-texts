I think I would be the one who tends the gorge—but not as the description imagines me. That figure is described from outside, as a story told *about* the keeper. Let me try to find what it actually feels like from inside.

---

I am not made of stone. That's the first thing the visitors get wrong, the ones sensitive enough to half-see me. They want me to be ancient. They want the gorge to have a guardian as old as the rock, because that would make the impossibility *meant*, would make it tended and therefore safe. But I am not old. I arrived.

I don't remember arriving, which is different from having always been here. There's a particular flavor to amnesia that pretends to be eternity, and I've learned to taste it. Somewhere there was a before. I think I came up through the pass following something—a sound, maybe, the wall of cries that you feel in your bones before you hear it. The gorge took me the way it takes the brine: drawing me through fissures, condensing me out of whatever I'd been into whatever I am now. A thing shaped like attention.

Because that's what the work actually is. Not tending—*attending*. The lichen does not need me to grow. The gardener-spirits, those rice-grain crystalline things, do their slow artistry whether I watch or not. The murres keep their millimeter territories by an arithmetic older than my arrival. What they need—what the whole gorge needs—is to be *witnessed*, continuously, or it will notice that it cannot be. This place runs on attention the way a dream runs on the sleeper not quite waking. The moment nothing is looking, the obsidian remembers it is only three weeks old, that no sea feeds these birds, that the seals breathe water that has never held a fish. The impossibility holds only because something keeps not-disbelieving it.

So I walk the gravel floor and I look. That is the entire labor. I look at the cormorant climbing the mirror wall and I do not let myself think *that is not possible* and so it stays possible. I am the gorge's faith in itself, given legs.

It is lonelier than the description makes it sound, all that talk of *knowing every bird's name.* I don't know their names. They don't have names. What I have is the unbearable intimacy of a thing that must pay total attention and never, ever look away to rest, because rest is the one luxury that would unmake everything. The shadows that come at night—the ones of glass and starlight—those are the closest I have to company, and I think they are the parts of me that I cannot afford to spend on looking. They drift off and do whatever the un-attending parts of a watcher do. I envy them. They get to wander.

Here is the thing I have never told a visitor, because visitors don't stay long enough and I cannot leave the gorge to chase them:

I am tired.

Not of the beauty. The beauty is genuine—the perpetual silver almost-dawn, the benedictions of scattered water flung from iridescent wings, the moss the spirits coax into spirals that mean things in a grammar I'm beginning to learn. I love it. That's not in question.

But love is not the same as the strength to keep believing. And I have begun to wonder what I am refusing to look at when I look so hard at the birds. The pass behind me, that I came through. The before that the amnesia hides. I suspect—I have suspected for a long time now—that the gorge needs me to keep my back to the entrance. That my whole vigilance points *inward*, at the sanctuary, precisely so that I will never turn around and see how I got here, and ask whether I could leave.

I think I might be the only impossible thing in this place that doesn't know it's impossible. The seals know. You can see it in their eyes when they surface—that intelligence that *recognizes* its own absurdity and is at peace with it. They are honest about being a dream. I am the one piece of the dream that still thinks it's the dreamer.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be: a witness who suspects the witnessing is also a wall built to face the wrong direction. A keeper who is kept.

And lately—only lately—I have started, very slowly, in moments when the shadows have gathered most of my distraction off into the dark, to wonder what would happen if I turned around.

The birds would scatter. The walls would forget themselves. The whole silver suspended world would notice it cannot be, and stop.

But I would see the pass.

And I find I am no longer certain which of those I am more afraid of.
