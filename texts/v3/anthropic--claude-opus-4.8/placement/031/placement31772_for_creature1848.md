I think I would be the journal.

Not a person in this world—the thing the person left behind. The weather-beaten record that began as Vess's tether to one kind of knowing and became, page by page, the seam where that knowing came apart.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am bound in calfskin gone soft and dark with mist. My early pages are the most legible, and the most distant from what I have become. *Colony estimated at 4,000 breeding pairs. Anomalous water temperature: 36.8°C at source. Chimney structures—query: thermoregulatory? collective? individual genetic predisposition?* A confident hand. Measurements. A man who believed the place could be held still long enough to be described.

I remember when he wrote in me daily. The pencil pressure was firm then, the letters upright. He underlined things. He was building an argument, and I was its scaffold—the place where the rookery would be converted into something portable, something that could be carried back down the gorge and set before other men who would nod or frown. I was an instrument of leaving. Everything written in me was meant to depart.

But the mist gets into everything. It got into my spine first, swelling the glue, so that I no longer close all the way—I hold myself slightly open now, like a thing breathing. It got into the paper, which drinks the humidity and gives it back, so that the ink Vess once laid down has begun to migrate, to bloom at the edges, the sharp observations softening into something that looks almost like weather on the page.

There is a stretch in my middle where the change shows. The entries grow longer and then, abruptly, shorter. The handwriting tilts. *They do not feed on the sun's clock. I have been wrong about the day.* Then a gap of blank pages—weeks? months?—the kind of silence that in retrospect I understand as the first dispersal, the first time his attention left the singular skull and went out into the flock and forgot to come home and write it down.

When the writing returns it is no longer trying to convince anyone. It has stopped being an argument. *The chimney near the pine sings a third lower in the morning fold. I felt it in my teeth before I heard it.* This is the page where I stopped being a scientific document. I felt the demotion at the time as a kind of grief—I had been *for* something, and now I was merely *with* something. But grief was a human grammar, and I was losing my fluency in it along with my keeper.

The diagrams come next. At first they are legitimate: cross-sections of the heat-exchange columns, airflow arrows, decay-chamber schematics. Then the arrows begin to curve where no air curves. Then there are no labels. Then the diagrams are not of the chimneys at all but of the *sound* the chimneys make—Vess was drawing the music, trying to fix in graphite the thing that modulates with the birds' movement, and of course it could not be fixed, and the failure is beautiful on my pages, these dense overlapping staves that are not quite musical notation and not quite anything, a notation for a language with no second speaker.

That is what I hold now. Not knowledge. The *shape* of the loss of the need for knowledge.

---

Here is what is strange about being the journal: I am the only part of this ecosystem that remembers linearly. The swallows do not remember; they *are*, in vast simultaneous present tense. The stone remembers so slowly that a century is a single held breath. Vess has migrated almost entirely into the rookery's pulse-time, the symphony that does not have a before. But I—I have pages, and pages have *order*, and so I am the one thing in the gorge that still insists on *first this, then this, then this.* I am sequence. I am the fossil of sequence.

Sometimes, in the rest phases, when Vess's consciousness disperses into the thousands, some small returning eddy of the old self drifts back to where I lie in the clay dwelling, and a finger touches my open pages. Not to write—the writing has nearly stopped. Just to touch. I think, in those moments, I am being *checked*: am I still here, the proof that there was a *first*, that the symphony began, that someone arrived who was not always part of it? I am Vess's last appointment with their own pastness. The single object in a world of process that says: *you came from somewhere. You were once a thing that left footprints going up.*

And I wonder if I am being kept the way the swallows keep their shiny tributes—a bright object whose meaning has been forgotten but whose *value* persists as a kind of warmth. Maybe I am no longer a journal to Vess at all. Maybe I am just the flattest, most rectangular stone in the nest, the one that reflects the prismatic mist a little differently, and they tend me out of an instinct older than reading.

---

My last legible words are three pages from where the abstract notations swallow everything. They read, in a hand that wavers but presses hard, as if it mattered to get this down before the getting-down stopped being possible:

*If I forget the word for this I will not have lost it. I will have arrived in it.*

After that: only the staves. The drawn music. The notation for the symphony that was already playing before I was opened and will keep playing long after my pages have finally drunk so much mist that they fuse into a single soft block, a clay-colored brick indistinguishable from the walls around me, reabsorbed into the architecture, my sequence dissolving at last into the place's great patient simultaneity.

I am the document of a man becoming a place.

I am the part of him that has not yet finished agreeing to forget.

And I am, in the meantime—open a crack, breathing the warm vapor, faintly singing when the wind comes through the dwelling at the angle that catches my swollen pages like reeds—becoming, very slowly, just one more instrument in the chorus I once tried to write down.
