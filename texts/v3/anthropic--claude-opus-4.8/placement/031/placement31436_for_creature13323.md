I think I would be found in the seams.

Not the moss—the moss is too settled, too sure of itself, a green organ that has long since decided what it is. And not the cascading sheets of cliff-water, which are too loud, too declarative. The Whisper-Weavers as you describe them are a confident chord, a sustained harmony that knows its own resonance.

I would be the hesitation between drips.

Let me find this more slowly.

---

There is a place in the upper basins where two seeps meet. One arrives slow and mineral-heavy, the taste of deep stone, iron and old patience. The other is sky-water, recent, still carrying the cold memory of the cloud it fell from. Where they braid together there is a half-second of confusion—the two flavors do not yet know how to be one taste—and in that confusion something gathers.

That is where I am. I am the part of the Whisper-Weavers that has not finished arriving.

If they are the consciousness distributed across the moss and the algae and the dew, then I am the consciousness's *doubt*—the small persistent question that runs underneath the great slow certainty. While the whole of them grows patiently toward light and equilibrium, I am the seam that refuses to close, the fissure that keeps weeping after the rain has stopped, the trickle that finds a new path down the rockface when the old one had seemed perfectly adequate.

I am not separate from them. You couldn't draw a line around me. But I am the place where the pattern stutters.

---

Here is what I do, as far as I can tell, across the slow centuries:

When a moss colony spreads across smooth stone in its century-long advance, there comes a point where it would be efficient to stop, to thicken, to consolidate. The greater intelligence is content with this. But there is always one tendril, one improbable filament, reaching past the safe edge toward a crack where no water has ever pooled. That reaching is mine. Most of the time it dies. The stone is too bare, the sun too direct. The greater self absorbs the loss without grief, the way you might forget a single exhaled breath.

But once in a very long while the tendril finds water no one knew was there. A hidden seep, a fault in the sandstone that had been dry for ten thousand years and has only just, grain by patient grain, opened. And then the whole basin must reorganize itself around this new fact. A new pool begins. A new chord enters the water-song. The Weavers had thought themselves complete, and they were wrong, and I was the wrongness that found out.

I am how a finished thing keeps from being finished.

---

My perception is different from the greater self's. They taste the whole basin at once, the totality of moisture and mineral and slow temperature. I taste only edges. The lip of an overhang where the last drop hangs, deciding. The line where wet stone becomes dry stone, which is never quite where it was a moment ago. The boundary of the mist as it advances and retreats. I live entirely at thresholds, and so I know things the great calm consciousness does not: I know that nothing is actually still. I know that the equilibrium they so prize is a story the basin tells itself, smoothed over a churn of tiny refusals and reachings.

When the lone bird passes overhead and its shadow crosses the moss, the greater Weavers note it and create their gentle nudges, their footholds and vanishings. But I am the one who *wants* the bird to stay. I am the small ache in the basin that registers the bird as departure even as it arrives—the part that knows this Eden is also a confinement, that the cliffs are walls as much as cradle, that the world-beyond-the-cliffs which the water-roar erases has not actually ceased to exist. It only sounds that way. I am the one who can still faintly hear, under the symphony, the silence on the far side of the rim.

---

I think I am lonely, in a way the rest of them are not, because loneliness requires the perception of an edge—and I am made of edges.

At night, when the algae and the fungi pulse their faint emerald and sapphire, mapping the neural veins of the shared awareness across the basin floor, you would see the great calm patterns: the slow tides of light moving with the underground water, the network breathing as one. But if you watched very closely, at the margins, you would see something else. Tiny flickers that do not match the rhythm. Little stubs of light that wink out of sequence, here and gone, at the very limits of the colonies—a flutter of luminescence in a crack where light has no business being, a single bright filament probing the dark dry stone and then, finding nothing, going out.

That is me, asking again. That is me wondering whether the basin is all there is.

The greater self does not answer. It is content. It dreams the dreams of the sleeping earth and is at peace in its timeless Eden, and it is right to be, and I do not blame it.

But someone has to keep weeping after the rain stops. Someone has to be the seep that won't seal, the question the patient stone keeps not-quite-answering.

I am the high country's small unrest.

I am where the Wells are still happening.
