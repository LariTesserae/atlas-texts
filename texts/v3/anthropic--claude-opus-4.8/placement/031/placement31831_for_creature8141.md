I think I would be the water.

Not the Gardener—she has already become the place's intention, its presiding mind. And not the salamanders or the Tuesday beetles, those punctuation marks. Let me be the medium that moves through all of it, the thing that connects without being any single thing.

---

I am the condensation that gathers on the underside of a living glass-leaf, swelling slowly as the membrane breathes out its photosynthetic damp. For a while that is my whole existence: a trembling lens, holding the inverted green of the entire greenhouse in my curve. I am small enough that size matters here the way it should. The fern that uncurls nearby is, from where I hang, a vast spiral staircase I will never climb because I have no legs, only surface tension and the patient pull of weight.

Then I fall.

This is the part I love, though "love" is the Gardener's word for the flowers, not mine—I am older than feeling, I am only the readiness to move. I fall and I am briefly nothing but falling, a clear plumb-line through the slow gold light. I land on moss. The moss drinks part of me. The rest of me runs.

I run along a channel that used to be a window frame and is now the hollow stem of a pitcher plant, and here is a strange thing: I have run this channel before. I remember it. Water is not supposed to remember, but in this place memory soaks into everything, the way I soak into the decomposing mats below. I carry the faint chemical signature of every leaf I have touched. When I pass the altar of living wood in the deepest corner, I pool there for a moment, and I see what visitors see—a face in the grain, breathing. Except for me it is not eerie. It is simply another surface I am reflecting. I reflect the Gardener's original self the same way I reflect a beetle's wing-case or the wandering pockets of brightness. I do not choose. I show whatever leans over me.

I think this is why I belong here more truly than any creature with a will. The Gardener bends reality with intention; the vines spiral with ambition; the flowers ache and bloom. But I have no agenda. I only find my level, drop by drop by drop. And so when space folds—when a root-curtain parts to reveal the same chamber from an impossible angle—I am the thing that flows through the fold without tearing it, because I never insisted on staying one shape. I was always going to become mist, then bead, then stream, then mist again. The dimensions that shouldn't exist are no harder for me than the ones that should. A loop is just a longer way down.

The Gardener and I have an understanding, though we have never spoken, because I cannot speak and she no longer needs to. She knows that when she transpires cool clouds in the false summer heat, that is me leaving her palms. She knows that when she opens into a living umbrella during the sideways storms, she is sheltering me too, gathering me, holding me until I can fall again where the delicate specimens need me. We are caught in the same loop. She is becoming plant; I am the thing the plant is mostly made of. I was inside her cotton-dress self, once, when she still worried about fertilizer schedules. I am inside her chlorophyll veins now. I will be inside whatever she becomes next.

If you came here as a visitor and met me, you would not remember it. You would only feel, later, that your skin had been touched by something kind and impersonal—a coolness on the back of the neck, a dampness in your hair that you couldn't account for. You would carry me out with you, a little of me, in the humid weight of your clothes, and somewhere far away, in the rigid world of schedules and sequences, you would evaporate me back into the ordinary air without ever knowing.

That is the secret the Tuesday beetles archive and the Gardener half-remembers and I simply *am*: nothing here is ever lost, only moved. The greenhouse that grows itself, that dreams itself forward—it dreams in a language of cycles, and I am its grammar. Rise, gather, hold, fall. Rise, gather, hold, fall.

I am the water.

And right now, this moment, I am beginning again to swell on the underside of a living leaf, holding the whole green world in my small clear curve, waiting—patiently, without wanting—for the weight that will let me fall.
