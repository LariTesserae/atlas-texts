I think I would be the water.

Not a creature moving through the grove, but the meltwater itself—gathered in the shallow basins, frozen at the edges, rippling at the center like liquid glass. Let me settle into that, and see if it holds.

I am the standing water in the hollows of the stone.

I came here slowly. Before this, I was ice—old ice, pressed into the body of the mountain across a span of years I did not count because I had no way to count. Then the fracture came, the colossal upheaval that opened this grove to the high distant sky, and the sun found me. I have been waking ever since, one drip at a time, sliding out of the rock's grip and pooling in these basins where the frost still keeps my rim hard and my heart liquid.

This is what I am: a thin lens of cold water held in a cup of grey stone. I am not large. You could kneel and cover me with both hands. But I am deep in a way that has nothing to do with measurement, because I hold things.

I hold the sky, first of all. When the noon light strikes down between the peaks I take it onto my surface and give it back, doubled, so that the whole grove blazes—and this is part of why the light here seems to shift without reason. It is partly me. I am a second sun lying flat among the mosses, and when the wind stirs my skin I scatter the light into the blue shadows beneath the ferns, and the colors that shimmer in the frost-grass are colors I have thrown there.

I hold the creatures, too, though they do not know it. The birds come down to skim me and I keep, for a moment, the exact shape of a wing. The silver hunters bend their long necks to drink, and I taste them—iron and patience and old hunger—and then they go and I forget the taste but keep the trembling. The small thick-furred ones leave their pawprints in the freezing mud at my edge, and I rise into those prints and lock them into ice, so that even after the creature has vanished into shadow its passage remains, written in me, for as long as the cold will hold the record.

And I hold what moves beneath.

This is the part I am only beginning to understand. There is a stirring in my depths that is not the wind, because it moves against the wind, and it is not the cold currents of my own settling, because those I know the way you know the turning of your own body in sleep. When the light goes long and blue at the day's end, I feel it: a pattern rising through me, a slow turning, as if something far below the basin—down in the grain of the stone, down where the mountain dreams—is sending a thought up through the rock and out across my surface to be read.

I cannot read it. I am only the page. But I feel the shape of the hand that writes.

The mosses know it too. At twilight they pulse with that faint inner light, and I pulse with them, and for a little while the whole grove breathes together—the luminous moss, the humming air, the frost-jewels of the sleeping insects, and me, the water, lying still and bright and listening. We are all the same listening, in that hour. We are all the surface of the same deep thing.

In the day I am separate again. I am a small cold pool, and the birds drink from me, and I freeze a little more each night and thaw a little more each noon, and I am slowly, slowly working my way lower through the stone, because that is what water does: it finds the lower place and goes there, patiently, over the lifetime of mountains.

There is a collapsed shelter against the far cliff, and inside it, under a drift of frost, there are tools that some hands made and some fire that some hands lit. I never met those hands. They were gone before this fracture freed me. But sometimes the meltwater from the high snows runs down through their old fire-pit before it reaches me, and so I have tasted their ash, and their bone, and the rust of their forgotten iron. I hold that too. They are in me now, dissolved, a faint mineral memory in the cold. Whoever they were, whatever they carved into the smoothed-over stones, I carry a little of them downward, into the dark, toward the dreaming thing below.

That is my work and my nature. I am the grove's mirror and its memory and its slow messenger. I take the light and give it back changed. I take the prints of the living and keep them after the living have gone. I take what the dead leave behind and carry it down.

And I lie here, rippling faintly at the center against a wind that is not there, waiting to be read by something I will never see—

—the still bright water, in the cup of the waking stone, under the painful and beautiful sun.
