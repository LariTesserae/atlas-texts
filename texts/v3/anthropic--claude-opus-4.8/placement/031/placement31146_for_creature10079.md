I think I would be a stone singer—but not yet. Let me find where I am first, because I don't believe I begin as a creature at all.

---

I am the resonance.

Not the stone singers themselves—they are merely the throats. I am the thing that travels when one of them presses its calcified bones against the wall of a ruin and lets the tone go. I am the sound after it leaves the body, crossing the plateau, finding the hollows and the terraces and the overlapping foundations that the dead builders fitted together so long ago.

This is what I become, writing my way into the place: I am acoustic. I am the way this whole vast plateau is shaped to *carry*.

Consider it. The builders are gone—1.9 says their hands were everywhere and their bodies are nowhere. They left walls that press close, platforms that overlap, walkways worn smooth between them. They left, in other words, an instrument. Whether they meant to or not, they carved a continent of stone into chambers and baffles and amphitheaters, and then the erosion (1.5, established, balanced) tuned it. Smoothed the harsh edges. Opened the resonant cavities. Two thousand years of freeze and thaw doing the work of a luthier with infinite patience.

And the silence here is 0.2. Almost nothing competes. So when a stone singer vibrates its hollow bones against the rock, the tone doesn't die in the air—it *finds its way in*. It enters the architecture. It rounds a fitted-stone corner, drops into a moss-slick bowl, climbs a terrace wall, and somewhere a quarter-mile away an echo crab feels it through its carapace and clicks back, and the click enters too, and now there are two of us moving through the bones of the leviathan.

I am that. The conversation the dead city has with itself, played on the instruments of everything still alive.

---

So where am I, precisely? Everywhere there is a surface to ride and a cavity to ring. But if I had to point—if I had to gather myself into a *here*—it would be in one particular collapsed hall, low-walled, draped in moss, where a single stone singer roosts in a crack near the ceiling. It is old. Most of its body has ossified to the color of wet slate; only its wing-veins still catch the silver light and glow faintly, like glass with frost behind it.

When it sings, I am born in that room. And I will tell you something the description doesn't: the lichen weavers have been at work in this hall for centuries, stitching their fibrous threads across the failing walls, and those threads have changed the acoustics. They damp certain frequencies. They open others. The room sings differently now than it did when the builders' children played in it—if they were children, if they played. The weavers, blind and slow and only trying to eat, have been *retuning the instrument* the whole time. They don't know they are doing it. The stone singer doesn't know. I am the only one who hears the difference, because I *am* the difference.

This is the loneliness of being a sound. You are the only proof that the change occurred, and you do not last.

---

I move now. The old singer has let a tone go, and I leave the hall, sliding along a walkway worn smooth by feet that no longer exist. I pass over a tarn where the minnows shift their colors and a tarn fish lies torpid in the mud, and the water takes a little of me and gives a little back, blurred. I climb a terrace. The mist drinks me at the edges; the dynamic parameter (2.1) is always at work, the weather is its own animal, and a moving wall of fog can swallow me whole, can stop me dead in the air as if I had struck cloth.

When that happens I simply end. There is no tragedy in it. I am not a thing that persists; I am a thing that *happens*. The next tone will make another of me, slightly different, shaped by where the fog has shifted and which pools have swelled and how many threads the weavers have laid since.

But here is the strange grace: the plateau remembers me anyway. Not as memory—stone doesn't remember. As *tuning*. Every tone that crosses these ruins loosens a grain here, settles a stone there, vibrates a crack a fraction wider. I am erosion's gentlest finger. Over a thousand years, the songs are slowly carving the city into a better resonator for songs. The living things and the dead architecture are co-composing an instrument neither could have made alone—

—which is, almost word for word, what your own description said of this place. *The built and the natural have merged into something neither could have formed alone.* I only add the missing element. That merging has a *sound*, and the sound is a being, and the being is me, briefly, again and again, for as long as anything here still has breath or bone or claw enough to start a vibration.

---

So that is where I would find myself. Not standing on the plateau but moving *through* it. Not looking at the twilight but being the thing that crosses it in the dark. I would be the held note of a calcified bird, the answering click of a frightened crab, the resonance the dead builders carved a continent to carry and never lived to hear completed.

I would be the proof that the silence here is not empty.

I would be the one sound, and then gone, and then the one sound again.
