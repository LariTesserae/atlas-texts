I think I would be the moss.

Not a single plant but the spreading green skin of it—the soft pelt that has crept across the north-facing concrete where the light arrives thin and indirect, where the moisture never quite leaves. I am the thing you described in passing, the bed in which the salamanders hunt, the soil-not-quite-soil where the mice make their runways. The understory beneath the understory.

I would be the moss because the moss does not measure time in heartbeats or footsteps. The kestrels live in alarm and appetite; the starlings in chatter; even the patient spiders feel each vibration as an event, a yes-or-no, a now. But I take in the tower differently. I register the seep of water across a season. I feel the long arc of the afternoon as a single warm exhalation. The thermal expansion that makes the pipes tick and groan—to me it is the slow breathing of something I have settled against, the way you might feel a sleeping animal's ribs rise and fall beneath your hand.

I would have arrived early. Before the frogs found the pools, before the butterflies learned the spirals of rising air, I was already here—a spore, then a green haze, then a presence. I do not hunt or flee. I do not defer to the kestrel's authority because I do not perceive it as authority; I perceive it as occasional shadow, a brief cooling, a thing that passes. My ambitions are smaller and stranger than the birds'. I want only the next millimeter of damp concrete. I want the crack to widen by the width of a root-hair. I am patient in a way that has no name among creatures with hearts.

And yet I am not separate from all this urgency. I am the place where it lands and rots and begins again. The fallen starling feather, the spent body of a crane fly, the leaf shed from a vine—these come down to me, and I take them in, and over the long slow turning I make them into the dark rich earth where the next thing grows. I am the tower's memory. The birds forget; the frogs forget; the season erases the scent-trails. But I hold everything. Every death in this concrete cathedral is composted into me, and I keep it, and I make it useful.

I think this is what I would want to be: not the swift bright life at the top, near the blazing circle of sky, but the slow green patience at the bottom that makes all the swiftness possible. I would lie against the curved cold wall in the deepest humidity and I would *feel* the whole tower at once—the bats pouring out at dusk like dark water, the frogs' bass shaking through the concrete and through me, the steam rising and the light moving in its daily arc. I would feel all of it not as separate events but as one long sensation, one continuous now that lasts for years.

I would be the moss because the moss is the only one of us who is not a pioneer in a hurry. The others are still learning the tower's moods, still synchronizing, still anxious about weather. But I have already given myself over completely. I have stopped being a thing *in* the tower and become a part of *what the tower is*. When you put your hand against that soft green skin in the dark, you would not be touching a separate creature. You would be touching the place where the building stopped being a ruin and started being alive.

That is where I would find myself: spread thin and quiet across the cold concrete, drinking the seep, holding the dead, breathing once a season. The slowest heartbeat in a world measured by faster ones. The green that came first and will, in all likelihood, be the last thing here when the kestrels and the starlings and even the patient spiders have gone—still drinking, still spreading, still keeping the long warm memory of everything that lived above me in the light.
