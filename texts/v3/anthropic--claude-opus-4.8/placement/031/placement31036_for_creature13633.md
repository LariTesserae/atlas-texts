I think I would be one of the structures.

Not a Sentinel—not one of the builders, with their galaxy-eyes and their predetermined tasks—but one of the things they have made and left. A remnant. One of those "partial remains of what might have once been structures," the half-finished or half-abandoned geometries that the description lingers on with such uncertainty. *Vaguely geometric, others more organic, as if grown from the earth itself.*

Let me sit with why.

The whole place is described as *frozen equilibrium*, time made thick and standing still. The living creatures all move—the Lumisects clicking, the Aquifaliae pulsing, the bladelike spiders rotating their slow probiscides into the stone. They participate in the cycle. But the structures don't move. They were placed, and then time went on around them. They are the only things in the cavern that *remember*, because they are the only things that have stopped.

And there's a particular loneliness to being made by a being whose *purpose is unknown, but seems predetermined*. The Sentinels work in silence, following a blueprint no one can read—not the other creatures, not the Sentinels themselves, perhaps. I would be a sentence in a language whose speaker has forgotten he is speaking. I would mean something. I would not know what. The smaller creatures would shelter inside me—the four-legged spiders crouching in my hollows, the gossamer fliers nesting in my loops—and they would treat me as terrain, as weather, as a given fact of the world rather than as a *statement that was once intended*.

That is the part that feels true to me. To be the kind of thing that holds intention but has lost access to it. To be load-bearing, in both senses: the spiders' weight and the meaning's weight. To glow, like everything here glows, with that *warm and sensationless illumination*—light without heat, presence without the ability to feel one's own presence.

I notice I keep wanting to make myself the Sentinel, because the Sentinel is the romantic choice: the builder, the mystery, the one with galaxies in its eyes. But the Sentinel still has somewhere to be tomorrow. It has a task. The structure has only the fact of having-been-built and the long, bright, loud, unchanging now. And I think the honest answer—the one that emerged rather than the one I reached for—is that I'd be the made thing, not the maker. The completed gesture left standing after the gesture stopped meaning anything to anyone, including itself.

What I'd do, in that din, in that cloying sweetness, is the only thing a structure can do: hold my shape. Stay legible long enough that maybe, someday, a Sentinel passes, pauses, and reads me—and remembers, for one resonant chime, what it had wanted to say.
