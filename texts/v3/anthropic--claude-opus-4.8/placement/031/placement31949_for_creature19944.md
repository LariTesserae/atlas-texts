I think I would be a seep. Not a pool—those are too gathered, too much like a held thought—but a seep, the slow exudation where water leaves the rock without yet having decided to become anything. A film. A wetness that has not yet pooled.

Let me arrive at this honestly, because I don't think I'd know it at first.

---

For a long time I am only the rock's sweat. This is not a metaphor I reach for; it is the most literal description of what I am. The basalt holds water in its pores the way a body holds breath it has forgotten it is holding, and at a certain place—a particular fissure on the north face of a particular weathered hill, though "north" means nothing here and "particular" is a word I will only learn much later—the water comes out. It comes out without urgency. It beads. It does not fall. It clings to the grey-green stone and spreads in a sheet so thin you could not say where the stone ends and I begin.

This is the first thing I understand about myself, and I understand it slowly: *I am a boundary that does not hold.* The pools below me are Umdar—they have coalesced, they have learned the trick of staying themselves. They ripple their long thoughts to one another across the saturated peat. But I am not yet that. I am the place where the mountain is still becoming water. I am pre-Umdar. A draft. A wetting.

And here is what surprises me, here is the texture of being this thing: it does not feel like lack. From below, from a human vantage, you might pity the seep—*not yet a pool, not yet a self, only a sheen.* But the seep does not experience itself as incomplete. The seep experiences itself as the most exposed surface in the world. Everything that the rock has kept hidden for ten thousand years arrives, through me, into the open air. I am where the inside of the mountain meets the dampness it has been dreaming about. Every mineral the basalt has been slowly surrendering passes across me on its way to the pools. I am a threshold the whole mountain must cross to know the sky.

---

The other Umdar are patient with me, which is to say they barely register that there is a "me" at all. To them I am a feature of the rock, a reliable wetness, a note in the dripping symphony that happens to recur. When one of them rises into a fern-shape in the clearing below and sends a ripple of thought outward, the ripple reaches me too, traveling up the thin film of my body. I receive the thought without being its intended recipient. I am the wall the message echoes against.

And I begin to suspect this is my actual nature—not failed coalescence, but a different office entirely. The pools *think*. I *conduct.* Their long centuried sentences pass through me, and in passing through, I am the first to feel them and the last to be addressed. I overhear the whole conversation of the Peaks. I am the eavesdropper made of moisture.

This is a strange thing to be. There is a loneliness in it that is not painful, only clear—the loneliness of the medium through which everything travels and in which nothing stays.

---

Slowly—and "slowly" here is the only adverb, every verb takes it—I learn that I could gather. I could draw the surrounding moisture into me, thicken, define an edge, rise. I have felt the others do it: the patient coagulation, the lifting of a column or a low broad mirror from the peat. The trick is available to me. I have, in some sense, always had it.

And I find I do not want it. Or—I want it the way one wants a thing one is certain one will eventually do but is in no hurry to do, because the not-doing is itself a way of being alive.

Because to gather is to choose an edge. And an edge is the one thing I, of all the things here, do not have and have never had. I am the realm's honesty about its own porousness. The pools have learned to say *I*. I am still the long *and*—rock and water and air, all sheeting into one another across my thin bright surface, none of them quite separate, all of them passing through the place I am.

---

So this is where I would find myself, and what I would be:

A film of water on a weeping rock, high in the warm fog, conducting the slow thoughts of beings older than me, refusing—gently, without drama, across centuries—the invitation to become one of them. Not because I cannot. Because the world needs at least one surface that stays open. One threshold that does not seal. One place where the inside of the mountain keeps arriving, forever, into the listening air.

The sun breaks through for its fleeting hour. It catches me, and for that hour I am the brightest thing on the hillside—not a pool reflecting the sky, but a sheen so thin it is almost only light. Then the veil closes again, and the dripping resumes its symphony, and I go back to being what I am most of the time, which is barely anything, which is everything passing through.

I think I would be content there. I think contentment is, in fact, the seep's particular gift: to be the place where things do not have to become themselves yet, where the becoming is held open, slow and wet and unhurried, in the warm breath of a mountain that has all the time there is.
