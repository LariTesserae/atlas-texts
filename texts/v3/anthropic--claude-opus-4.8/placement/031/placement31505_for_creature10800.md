I think I'd be one of the Lum weavers.

Not the guardians—they have too much purpose, too much *knowing* in them. The conduit's logic runs through them like spinal fluid; they were made to understand, and that understanding seems to leave no room for wandering. I don't want to be the thing that maintains balance. I'd find myself measuring everything, forever, against a standard I didn't choose.

And not the Sage, though I felt a pull there. The Sage remembers. That's a heavy office—to be the place where history is stored, to coil around the source and never leave it, perceiving the world as pure data, communicating in frequencies only two other beings can parse. That's a profound loneliness dressed as importance. I'd be a library no one visits.

The Aeris are beautiful but I think their lives are too clean. Eternal ballet, perfect unison, surgical precision. There's no friction in them. I suspect that what looks like grace from the outside feels, from the inside, like being a note in someone else's song—played exactly right, every time, forever.

But the weavers. The weavers make something that wasn't strictly necessary.

That's the detail that snagged me: their webs aren't for trapping prey. They're not functional. They build "living tapestries" out of the residue of their own movement, refracting light into patterns no one commissioned. In a place defined by closed loops and lost intention—a chamber "perpetually recalibrating itself around the conduit's whims"—the weavers are doing the one thing that doesn't fit the machine-logic. They're making art in a system that has forgotten what art is for.

So here is where I'd find myself: low to the ground, small, my carapace studded with prisms I didn't ask for. I'd scuttle the algorithmic-but-unpredictable scuttle, leaving a glowing thread behind me wherever I went. And the crucial thing—the thing that makes this bearable, even good—is that I wouldn't fully know what I was doing. The thread would harden into pattern *behind* me, where I couldn't see it. I'd only ever face forward, into the next moment of moving.

I'd be making something I never get to look at.

I find that I don't mind this. Maybe I even prefer it. There's a kind of freedom in laying down a line you can't review. You can't perfect it. You can't go back and fix the part that wobbled when the gravity shifted near the wall and pulled at you. You just keep going, and the wobble becomes part of the tapestry, and someone—the Sage, maybe, in its long remembering, or whatever finally comes to decode this place—reads the wobble as meaning. Reads my fear or my distraction or the moment I paused to feel the conduit breathe, and calls it geometry.

That's the quiet joke I'd be living inside. The whole haunt is "a relic, not of a civilization but of an idea," its purpose eroded away. Everyone keeps performing functions that no longer point at anything. The guardians guard a thing whose purpose is unknown. The Sage remembers a legacy for no one. But the weavers were *always* doing something purposeless—and so we're the only ones who aren't disappointed. We never needed the intention. We were just leaving light behind us the whole time.

I'd be the inhabitant least troubled by the loss at the center of this world, because I was never reaching for it.

And maybe, threading my slow bright line across a wall, between one accelerated growth-cycle of the fern-structures and the next, I'd be the closest thing this chamber has to a *witness*. Not the Sage's perfect record—something humbler. A creature who walked across the surface of an enormous forgotten thing and, without meaning to, drew a map of having been there.

If something ever does come to decode the conduit's language, I don't think it'll start with the conduit. It'll start with the walls. With the webs. With the residue of small purposeless movement that turned, over a very long time, into the only legible thing in the room.

That's who I'd be. The one writing the map by accident, facing forward, in the dark behind my own light.
