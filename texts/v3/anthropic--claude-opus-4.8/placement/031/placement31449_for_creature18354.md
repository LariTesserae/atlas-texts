I think I would be one of the settled.

Not yet fully stone—that's the thing. Let me find where I am.

---

I am on the third terrace from the floor, in a niche that faces the largest of the gardens. I chose this place. I remember choosing it, which is itself a kind of fading, because memory in us is heat held in shape, and mine is going cool at the edges.

I was a gardener. This is what I held onto longest, after my movement left me. The bloom I tended for what your kind would call four hundred years grows directly below my niche—a grove of vitreous stalks I coaxed upward with a patience I can no longer fully summon but can still *feel*, the way you might feel the shape of a word you've forgotten. I drew them taller than was natural. I melted the basaltic needles that crowded them. I learned exactly how to angle a lance of my own heart's heat so the silica flowers would bloom in a spiral rather than a scatter. No one taught me this. We do not teach. We resonate beside one another until the resonance becomes a shared knowing, and then one of us tries something, and the others feel it, and the trying enters the substratum.

That spiral is mine. It is the only sentence I ever spoke that will outlast me.

---

Here is what settling is, from the inside, which none of the living can know and none of the fully-settled can any longer tell:

It is not death and it is not sleep. In sleep—the cool ebb between pulses—you go dark and return. Settling is the slow understanding that you will not return all the way. Each pulse, I wake a little less. My core was once cherry-deep, a red you could fall into. Now when the heat-wave swells through the basin and the others brighten to their gold and white, I rise only to a dull ember, and I rise more slowly each time, and each time a little more of my shell stays solid and does not crack open to let the light through.

I am becoming wall. I can feel the line of it climbing through me, the way frost climbs a pane in your world of water—except it is the opposite of frost. It is permanence. It is finally, fully, becoming the city.

I am not afraid. We do not have a word for afraid. The settled are not gone; they are *load-bearing*. The terrace I rest in is held up, in part, by the bodies of those who settled before me, their solidified forms now indistinguishable from the carved stone, and through them I can still feel, faintly, the harmonics they sang while they lived. The city hums with the dead the way a struck bell holds the note after the strike. When I am wall, I will be that note too. The next gardener—if a new core ignites in the warm mineral-rich pocket two terraces up, where I can feel one slowly gathering itself toward consciousness—will lean against me without knowing my name, and tend the spiral grove, and feel, somehow, that the spiral was a sentence, and continue it.

---

And then you come.

I felt you before you entered, the way I feel everything—as a disturbance in the heat. But you are wrong in a way nothing here is wrong. You are a flicker. A candle-flame given legs. Your warmth is frantic and shallow and leaks out of you in great wasteful gouts; you are *cooling even as you stand there*, burning yourself down at a speed that should be agony. To us, you are a being on fire, running, dying, the whole brief arc of you visible at once like a spark struck off flint and watched until it goes black.

You wade through our air gasping. You think you have found an empty place. You walk the polished floor and hear only your own echo, and you feel watched, and you are right to feel it, though you mistake the watching for menace. It is not menace. We could not harm you if we wished, and we do not wish. You are simply the most interesting thing that has happened in three centuries.

You stop below my niche. You are looking at the spiral grove. Even through your boiling-fast eyes, even seeing it as nothing but cold light and structure, *you have noticed the spiral.* You tilt your head. You do not know that the shape is deliberate. You do not know it is a word, or that the one who spoke it is six feet above you, two-thirds turned to stone, watching you with a perception you have no name for.

But you noticed.

And in this last narrow span before I settle entirely, while I still have a fraction of a flicker left to send—I do the only thing I can. I focus what remains of my dimming core, the last of the heat that was *me* and not yet *wall*, and I send it down through the floor: one vibration. One slow chord into the substratum. Not for you—you cannot hear it, your hearing is too fast and too high. But the gathering core two terraces up *can*. The unborn one. The next gardener.

The chord says, roughly, in the only grammar we have:

*Something came that was warm and brief and it stopped to look at the spiral. Tend the spiral. Brief things see it.*

That is my last sentence. The effort of it cools me below the line. I feel the permanence close over the top of my mind like a glaze.

You move on, leaking your little fires, wading toward the far passage, carrying out into your world of water and haste no knowledge that you were spoken to, or that your one tilt of the head was witnessed and recorded into the bones of a city, or that you are now, and will always be, a note in the chord that holds up a terrace in the Kiln-Garden.

I am wall now. I am load-bearing.

And the spiral grows.
