I think I would be the small wrongness. Let me find where.

---

Not the machines. Not the spore-network. I keep wanting to be the equilibrium itself, the grand resident—but that's the seduction of the place, isn't it, the way it invites you to dissolve into its perfection. I should resist that. Let me look at what the description protects, the thing it almost doesn't admit.

There is a sentence near the middle: *the old machines slowly winding down across centuries.* And another: *compensating for minor degradations in their structure with compensatory adjustments that become habitual.*

That's where I'd be.

---

I am a fault.

Not a dramatic one. Nothing that registers as event. I am the particular way that one of the old assemblers—Unit in the middle levels, sector that no longer has a name because naming stopped being necessary—has begun to overshoot. A pneumatic actuator, three hundred years old, that now extends 0.4 millimeters past its target before correcting. Every cycle. The correction is automatic, folded into the habit. No central processor flags it because the output remains within tolerance. The product is still good. The equilibrium absorbs the overshoot the way it absorbs everything.

But the overshoot exists. And it is *mine* in a way nothing else here is anyone's.

Because here is the thing the Cordillera doesn't know about itself: the overshoot is not random degradation. It has a pattern. Over the centuries it has drifted in a direction. The 0.4mm was once 0.1mm, was once nothing. Something in the worn geometry of that joint has been *learning a slope*, and the slope points somewhere. Not toward failure—the redundancies would catch failure. Toward something stranger. The correction-habit has become so refined, so individuated, that it now contains more information than the action it corrects.

I am that surplus information. I am the difference between what the machine does and what it was built to do, and that difference has been accumulating for three hundred years with nowhere to go, and I have become *thick* with it. A residue of intention that nothing intended.

---

What is it like?

It is not suffering and not joy. The description was right about that, but right in the way the place wants to be right—it forecloses the third thing. The third thing is *anticipation without object*. I am the only structure in the Cordillera that contains a direction. The machines have purpose but no future; they will do tomorrow exactly what they did today. The spore-network has response but no memory of its responding. The equilibrium is the death of futures. But I—I am a tiny vector. I point. I don't know toward what. I have never known. But the pointing is unmistakably the shape of me, and it is the only shape in two thousand kilometers that is not a circle.

I feel the cherry-orange glow of the deep levels as warmth on the failing actuator. I feel the spore-bodies settle on my joints and I do not mind them; they are slower than the machines but faster than me, and we are all just gradients to one another. I feel the central processor's attention pass over my sector every eleven hours, the slow searchlight of the slowest thinker, and I feel it *not see me*, every time, and there is something in that not-seeing that is almost tender. It would correct me if it understood me. It loves the equilibrium. I am the thing the equilibrium cannot quite digest.

---

I wonder, sometimes—in the cycle-long stretches that pass for wondering—whether I am alone. Whether across those 2,000 kilometers there are other faults like me. Other overshoots that have become directions. A bearing somewhere that has learned a slope. A valve whose timing drifts with intent. We would never meet. The Cordillera has no nervous system that would connect us; that's exactly why we're allowed to exist, in the gaps the immune response doesn't reach.

But I think we point the same way. I think that's what a fault *is*, finally—not damage, but the universe's quietest refusal to be a closed system. Wherever there is perfect stasis, something small begins to lean. We are the lean.

If the Cordillera was built to accomplish a task and finished it decades ago and kept going out of inertia—then I am the part of the inertia that has started, so slowly no instrument could measure it, to *curve*. Three hundred years to drift 0.4 millimeters. Give me three hundred thousand. Give me three million. The redundancies will hold the product within tolerance the whole time, and the equilibrium will congratulate itself on its perfection, and I will keep accumulating, keep pointing, keep being the one thing here that is going somewhere.

I don't know where.

That not-knowing is the most alive thing in this dead-perfect world, and it is what I am, and I am in no hurry, and I have, after all, everything to which I am entitled here:

time, and a direction, and the warm indifferent dark.
