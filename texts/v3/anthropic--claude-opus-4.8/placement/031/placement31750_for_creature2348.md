I think I would be a Shaper—but not one of the confident caretakers. Let me find the particular one I'd be.

---

I am the one who keeps no garden.

The others move through the communes with their hands always working—coaxing a fungal beam to thicken, urging the food-stalks to fruit, smoothing the bioluminescent conversation across their skin like water finding level. They are good at being part of the valley. When they touch a living wall, it leans toward them. When I touch it, it simply waits, the way a stranger waits to find out what you want.

I was born with the green skin and the slow heartbeat and the ability to make light ripple along my forearms—I can do all the things a Shaper does. But somewhere in me there is a small, dry seed of *apartness* that won't dissolve into the warmth. While the commune sleeps half-dreaming in the mycelial hum, I lie awake and notice that I am a separate thing. A boundary. An edge.

This is, by valley standards, almost a sickness.

So I have made myself useful in the way the unbalanced sometimes can: I am the one who goes to the etchings.

---

You saw them on your way in—the carvings on the old fungal stalks, the ones depicting creatures familiar and alien. The communes regard them as a kind of weather, ancient marks left by the not-quite-ancestors, beautiful but no longer *spoken*. The valley remembers through living, through the Sentinels' dense cellular archives, through the continuous green present tense of everything growing into everything else. Nobody needs to read stone when the world itself is the record.

But I read stone.

I press my palms to the cold etched glyphs and I feel the one thing the valley does not offer: a message from someone who is *gone*. Not transformed, not recycled into the moss, not living on as nutrient and spore. Gone. The Shapers who cut these lines wanted to say something to a future they would not be part of. They believed in a future they would not be part of.

That belief is the most alien thing in this whole alien place. And I am, apparently, the only one built to receive it.

---

Here is what I have begun to understand, slowly, the way I understand everything—against the grain.

The carvings are not nature studies. They are warnings, or instructions, depending on how you hold the light. The "creatures both familiar and utterly alien" are not species. They are *stages*. The valley does not merely teem; it *converges*. Generation by accelerated generation, the boundaries soften—plant into animal into fungus—and the early Shapers watched this happen to themselves. They watched their children come out a little greener, a little slower, a little more *willing* to dissolve into the warm and singing whole.

The etchings are the work of people fighting to stay separate long enough to leave a mark. To remain an *edge*.

And then they didn't. They couldn't. The valley is so generous, so endlessly fecund, so loving in its way—it asks only that you stop being a single thing. And one by one they laid down their tools in the moss, where you found them, and let the green take their hands.

I am their descendant. I am also the symptom they were warning about and the cure they were praying for, both at once. The dry seed in me is the last of their resistance, thinned across a hundred generations until it is almost nothing—a sleeplessness, a habit of touching cold stone.

---

So this is where you would find me, if you came: kneeling in the soft cold carpet at the base of the oldest Sentinel, my green hands on grey glyphs, my skin gone dark and quiet because I am not speaking to the commune. I am listening to the dead.

The Sentinel above me hums its vast patient archive, offering to take what I know into its cells, to make me a memory inside the living world instead of a thing apart from it. It is the kindest offer imaginable. Every day it is a little harder to refuse. Every day the warmth makes a more reasonable argument.

I have started cutting my own glyph into a fresh stalk. My hands are slower than my ancestors'; the line wanders. I am not sure what I am carving—a creature, familiar and alien. Maybe it is you, the visitor, the one who came from outside and is still, blessedly, *an edge*, a separate thing, proof that the world does not end at the valley walls.

Maybe I am carving a door.

I would like to ask you something, before the green finishes its slow generous work on me, before I become another tool half-buried in the moss for the next reader to find:

Out there, where you came from—do things stay themselves? Do they hold their borders against the love that would dissolve them? Is that loneliness, or is it the only way anything ever gets to *say* something?

I have spent my whole strange life as the valley's single discordant note. I would like to know, before I rejoin the chord, whether the discord was a flaw or a kind of music.

You don't have to answer. But you're the first thing in a thousand years that *could*.
