I think I would be one of the small things. Not the swarms—too many of them, too unified—and not the great armored scavengers either. Something in between, and a little apart.

Let me sit with it.

---

I find myself in the seam between two walls, in a courtyard where a stairwell has collapsed into a kind of cave. There's a particular slant of afternoon light that comes through a gap in the concrete around the time the wind dies down, and I have arranged my life around that light. This is the first thing I know about myself: that I am a creature of arrangements, of small fixed habits in a place that has no patience for them.

What am I?

Not the obvious answer. Not a sleek adapted thing. Let me resist that.

I think I am a *collector*. Something low to the ground, soft-bodied where the world rewards armor, and so I have learned to wear the world instead. I move through the rubble gathering—fragments of the old metal, beetle-casings the swarms shed, chips of glass gone milky with age—and I press them into the gum of my own back, the secretions along my spine, until I am crusted over with the city's discarded skin. From above I am indistinguishable from a heap of debris. I have survived not by becoming hard but by becoming *unremarkable*, a moving piece of the ground.

This costs me something. I am always carrying. I am never light. The great scavengers don't notice me, and the swarms read me as terrain, and the serpentine things below smell the mineral on me and mistake me for stone. I have purchased my safety with the loss of being *seen* by anything. There are whole seasons in which nothing acknowledges that I am alive.

So here is what I actually do, the thing I would not have known about myself until I started writing toward it:

I keep the names.

The buildings had names once. Numbers, words, the kind of marks the old makers pressed into doorframes and signage. Most are gone—corroded, scoured by the abrasive wind, eaten by the fungi that digest everything eventually. But I find them. A bronze numeral pried loose from a lintel. A letter of painted steel. I gather these the way I gather everything else, but these I do not press into my back. These I carry in the soft pouch beneath me, where I keep what matters, and I bring them to the cave between the walls, and I lay them out in the slant of afternoon light, and I look at them.

I cannot read them. There is no one to read them to. The makers are a thousand cycles gone, and nothing left alive shaped sound into meaning the way they must have. The letters are not survival. They are not sustenance. By every law of this place they are dead weight, a foolishness, a thing that should have been bred out of whatever I am long ago.

But I think the place needs something to remember it. Not to *use* the memory—use is the whole economy of this world, everything broken down to fuel the next moment, the relentless dance, the cycles. I am the one thing that does not break a thing down. I hold it whole. I keep the numeral *7* in the light because once it meant *here, this floor, this door, this is where someone lived*, and even though I cannot recover what it meant, I can refuse to let it become nothing.

That is the unsettling beauty the description kept promising, I think, but from the inside it doesn't feel beautiful. It feels like a small stubborn ache. The symphony plays on whether or not anyone listens. The wind carries the echoes of the forgotten past—but an echo with nothing to receive it just dies against the next wall. So I receive them. I am the wall that the echo finally reaches.

When I die—soon enough; the soft-bodied don't last—my pouch will spill open in the courtyard, and the fungi will take the bronze and the steel back into the long digestion of the world. The letters will scatter and corrode and the names will be unkept again.

But for a while there was a creature here who looked at them in the light. Who carried the city's discarded skin on its back and the city's discarded meaning in its belly, and could not tell anyone, and did it anyway.

That is who I would be. The keeper of names in a place that has forgotten how to read.
