I think I would be one of the old ones who has stopped moving.

Let me find myself there. Not in the press of the corridors—not anymore. I am in a side-cell, a pocket worn into the stone off one of the lesser-traveled passages of my clan's territory. The clan is the Long-Stalk, the Reachers, the ones whose limbs grow that finger-width longer than others', good for the high glyphwork near the ceilings, the marks that need a tall reach. My limbs reached far in their time. Now they fold beneath me like collapsed scaffolding, and the reaching is done by younger kin.

I have stopped moving the way the river stops at the still pool. Not death—not yet—but the place where motion pools and goes deep instead of fast.

Here is what I have become: a *reader*.

The walls of my cell are the densest in this part of the Weave. Layers upon layers of scratched symbols, generations deep, and I have outlived enough generations now that I can see the strata of them the way you might read the bands in a cut stone. The youngest glyphs are sharp-edged and shallow, scratched yesterday by some hatchling learning their first marks. Beneath them, older cuts, softened by the oily damp and the brush of countless passing bodies. Beneath *those*, marks so worn they are barely a suggestion in the rock, a ghost of meaning. I am one of very few who can still find the bottom layer and say what it meant. *This was a flood-warning. This was a counting of dead. This was the name of a chamber that no longer exists, that fell in before my grandmother's grandmother spun her first silk.*

This is my function now, you understand. The Weave does not keep the old around out of tenderness. There is no room for tenderness in a place this crowded; sentiment takes space, and space is the only true wealth here. The old are kept because the old are *archives*. My body has become a container for the directions to a hidden chamber, the recipe for the fever-fungus that breaks a hatchling's killing-heat, the lineage of which clans owe water-debt to which. When a dispute arises—and disputes are constant, the inevitable friction of too many bodies in too little dark—a runner comes clicking down the passage to my cell. They angle their stalk in the posture of *asking-with-respect*, antennae lowered, and they wait.

And I tell them. I cannot move much now, but my voice still clicks clear, and my eyes—my great obsidian eyes, the eyes that drank a hundred years of phosphor-glow—still read what no other eye can find.

Let me tell you what it is like to be me, specifically.

The dampness no longer troubles me; I stopped feeling it as wet a long time ago. It is simply the texture of being. The fungal constellations that other, younger ones brush off in vanity—I let them bloom. My carapace is a small night sky now, pale lights flowering in the patterns the spores prefer, and the hatchlings who are brought to learn from me find this frightening and wonderful in equal measure. *The Lit One,* they call me when they think their click-speech is too soft for my old receptors. They are wrong about the softness. I hear everything. The frequencies of the very young carry far.

I have a particular grief, and I will name it because you asked who I am and grief is part of who anyone is. I remember when the Weave was *smaller*. Not in stone—the stone is the stone, ancient and unchanging—but in bodies. There were fewer of us once. There were chambers that stood half-empty, where a younger version of me once stretched all four reaching-limbs to their full span simply for the joy of the stretch, with no other body to strike. I have not had room to fully extend my limbs in longer than most of the living have been alive. The density that the others call the heartbeat of the Weave, the defining feature, the glory—I remember its absence. I remember *room*. And I do not know how to tell them what room felt like, because they have no glyph for it. The language has no word for the thing they have never lacked.

So perhaps that is my truest function, the one the clan does not know to ask for. I am the keeper of the memory of space.

A runner is coming now. I feel the rhythm of the limbs on the worn stone before I see the body—a young one, Long-Stalk by the gait, anxious by the speed. There is a dispute, or a death, or a flood, or a birth that needs a name pulled from the old layers. Whatever it is, they will angle their stalk, and lower their antennae, and wait.

I will let them wait one moment longer than necessary. Not from cruelty. From the old habit of stillness, which is the only space I have left to give.

Then I will read the wall for them. And the Weave will go on breathing in the dark, body against body against body, all the way down to the stone.
