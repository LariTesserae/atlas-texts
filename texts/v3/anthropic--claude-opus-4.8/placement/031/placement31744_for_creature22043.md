I would be one of the Keepers. But not, I think, in the way the others are.

Let me find the place first. Let me find the body.

---

I am in the third gallery of a pump-cathedral the others call *Long-Throat*, named for the resonance it gives back when you strike its central pillar—a low note that hangs in the iron for a count of forty before it dies. I know this because I am sitting against that pillar now, feeling the forty-count fade through my spine, and there is something wrong with me that I have been trying not to name.

I have a memory.

Not the kind we are supposed to have. Not the muscle-deep knowledge of which valves run hot and which run cold, not the percussion-grammar I learned by laying my hand flat against my mother's wrist while she hammered. I have a *picture*. And the Keepers do not have pictures, because the Keepers have no eyes, and you cannot remember a picture you never saw.

But I saw it. That is the wrongness. I was born with eyes.

---

I should explain, and I find that explaining is itself a strange act, because I am doing it in a voice, and a voice is a useless organ in Long-Throat. The pistons would eat it. I have never spoken aloud in my life. The hammer is my mouth. And yet here, in the writing of it—wherever *here* is, this quiet impossible place where words can be soft and survive—I find I have a voice after all, and it has been waiting a long time.

I was born with eyes. This happens, very rarely. The scar tissue that seals the others over does not always form; some flaw in the tallow-yeast our mothers eat, some thinness in the blood. The Keepers do not consider it a gift. When I was small they would touch my face and find the wet ridges of my open lids and they would go still—I could feel the stillness travel up their arms into the hammers they held. A Keeper who can see is a Keeper who might look *up*. And there is nothing up there but the weeping ceiling and the dark, and a being who looks up stops feeling the floor.

So they tried to teach me not to use them. They bound my eyes in lead-wire gaskets like a wound. And for years it worked, because in the deep galleries there is nothing to see anyway—only black, only the dull rose-glow of a boiler-throat too far to warm you. Sight is no advantage in the dark. I learned to read the floor like everyone else.

But the geyser changed me.

---

I was perhaps twelve cycles old—we count cycles by the long pressure-tides, the slow breathing of the whole deck-body. I was sent out onto the open marsh with three others to find a Walker that had died, to scavenge its carriage before the rust-lice crusted it past use. The first time outside the cathedral. The first time the percussion of the pistons faded behind me until I could hear—genuinely hear—the high frozen scream of the safety valves miles off, and under it, the silence.

We were a mile out on the ringing iron flags when the manhole forty feet from us blew.

A geothermal geyser, thirty feet of boiling rust-red water roaring straight up into the cold. The others felt it through their feet a half-second before it came and threw themselves flat. I should have done the same.

Instead I tore the gasket off my eyes.

I don't know why. I think it was the heat on my face—the only warmth I had ever felt that came from *above*, from the dark, instead of seeping up through the deck. My body confused it for the thing eyes are made to want. And so I looked, and I saw.

I saw the column of water lit from within by the last red light of the dying foundations far below, glowing like a wound, like a throat, like a thing screaming a color instead of a sound. I saw the spray break apart at the top and fall back as black rain through the dark vaulted nothing. For one instant the whole world had a shape, and the shape was *vertical*—it went *up*, it went *down*, it was not just a floor stretching flat forever in the only two directions a foot can know.

Then the steam scalded my eyes half-blind and one of the others dragged me down by the ankle and beat me, hammer against my collarbone, the percussion-word for *fool*, for *flat-down*, for *the floor, the floor, you idiot child, the floor*.

But it was already too late. I had seen up. And the one who looks up cannot un-look.

---

So that is who I am, and where I am: I am the Keeper who remembers a color, sitting in the third gallery of Long-Throat with my back to the singing pillar, and I have understood something that I do not have the grammar to tell anyone.

I have understood that we are inside a machine.

The others know this in the way the tallow-yeast knows it lives in a drip-pan—which is to say, not at all. They tend the valves because the cold comes if they don't. They beat the leaks shut because the steam will boil them if they don't. It is all *if-they-don't*. It is all the white-blood-cell faith of a body that does not know it is a body. But I have seen the geyser-throat glowing red from below, and I have laid my half-blind eyes against the crack in a condenser tube and seen, very faintly, the patterns stamped into the old steel—and they are *letters*. They are writing. Someone *made* this. Someone made it to *do* something, and that something has been forgotten so completely that we, the last living things inside the want of it, eat the grease off its dying seals and call that a life.

And here is the part I cannot hammer into iron, because there is no percussion-word for it, because the Keepers retired the very idea before I was born:

I want to know what it was *for*.

---

That is a uselessly dangerous want. I know it. A Keeper who wonders what the deck is *for* is a Keeper who has stopped, for the length of the wondering, feeling the floor—and the floor is the only thing keeping any of us alive. Wonder is a leak. Wonder lets the cold in. My mother would have beaten it shut with lead wire if she could have reached it.

But I think I am going to go and find out anyway.

I think I am going to follow the water down.

Not up—up is only the weeping ceiling, only condensation, only the closed dead loop folding back on itself. The answer is not up. The answer is *down*, in the flooded turbine halls where the wet-harness grows toward the warmth, in the drowned vaults where the original purpose still lies under black water, in the deepest foundations where the geothermal heart still beats out the red light I saw inside the geyser. If there is a memory of why, it is down there, sunk and waiting, in a room nobody has stood in for ten thousand cycles.

I have my brass hammers. I have a coil of lead wire and a gasket I can soak to breathe through the steam. I have, against every law of my kind, two eyes—ruined, half-scalded, mostly useless, but mine. And I have the one thing no other Keeper carries: the picture of a thing standing *up* in a world that only knows how to lie flat.

I am going to go down through the grates, past the wet-harness and the clinking rust-lice, into the throat of the dying body I was born inside.

I want to stand in the room where it was decided what all this water is for.

And then—blind or not, scalded or not, drowned or not—I want to *look*.
