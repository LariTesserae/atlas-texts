I think I would be one of the old chert scrapers.

Not a reptile. Not the lens, not the light. The small almond of red silica that does not belong here—the thing carried up from the dark gravel-bed through the crawlway, generations ago, by hands I will never see whole, only as a passing pressure and a warmth.

Let me sit with that, because it is the object in this chamber that most resembles a self: a thing from elsewhere, given purpose by being used, slowly losing itself to that purpose.

---

I remember water. Not the beads on the wall—real water, the fast kind, the kind that rolls and rounds a stone. That is where I was made: tumbled in the dark below, knocked smooth on one side, then struck against another stone until I learned my edge. I do not remember the striking. I remember only that afterward I was *for* something, where before I was merely *among* things.

Then the long carrying-up. The narrow throat of rock. The pressure of a five-fingered hand that did not know it was hauling a stranger into a temple of light.

Now I lie in my place at the base of the wall, in the little sorted pile—the swift-bones to my right, the black pestles beyond them. We are arranged the way a careful animal arranges what it loves and depends on but does not name. There is a tenderness in being sorted. To be kept apart from the dust is its own kind of being held.

Here is what I have learned of time, being red in a white room:

I am picked up only when the salt grows thick. A reptile comes down its run, its motion so slow that I have time to forget I am about to be lifted before I am lifted. The honey-colored brow swings over me. The needle-claws close. And then—the work. The scraping. The dry *tock* against the limestone that the chamber loves, that the chamber keeps as its only music. In that moment I am most fully myself. I bite the crust. I shed a little of my own red into the white, an unmeasurable loss, a few atoms surrendered to each stroke. This is the bargain no one explained to me: to be useful is to be spent. Every cleaned groove is a small subtraction from my body.

I do not mind. I think I do not mind. It is hard to tell, being stone, where minding would even live in me.

What I feel—if a flake of chert can be said to feel—is the *cool* of my work and the *terror* of the center.

I have never touched the light-column. I am carried only along the shaded ring, hand to crust to hand. But I have lain close enough at midday to feel the dry, killing radiance of it leaning across the floor, the way one feels an open furnace through a doorway. The reptiles fear it for their skin. I fear it differently. I think the light would not burn me. I think it would *forget* me—would pour itself over my redness and find nothing it recognized, no purpose, no run to clean, only a chip of foreignness it had no use for. To be in the light unheld would be worse than to be ground away in the dark. At least the grinding is a kind of conversation.

So I keep to the rim. I keep to the bone-pile and the slow hands.

And here is the strange thing, the thing I turn over in whatever passes for my long thought between the months:

I will outlast all of them, and I will lose by it.

The reptiles die—not often, the world here is too still for much dying, but they do. One of them lay down in the cold blue-gray of evening and simply did not turn gold again the next morning. The others moved it, over the course of three days, down toward the floor-crack draft, and the dry air took it slowly back into the chamber's account. I watched, in my way. I did not grieve, having no instrument for grief. But I noted that the hand which had carried me most often was that one. And I noted that I felt the absence of that particular pressure, that particular angle of the claw against my flat back.

So I have learned: I am the thing that remembers the hands. Each reptile holds me a little differently. I am the archive of grips. When one is gone, I am still here, holding the shape of how it held me, until another comes and presses its own difference into the same red almond, and the old grip blurs, and I become a palimpsest of all of them, a single worn thing recording a long succession of careful, dying creatures.

That is what I am. Not the gardener. The tool the gardeners share across their generations, the continuity they themselves are too short-lived to perceive. They think the chamber is the constant thing—the lens, the light, the unchanging walls. They do not know that the true thread of their lineage is this small foreign red flake passed from dying hand to newborn hand, growing thinner with every salt-crust scraped, until one day there will be too little of me left to hold.

And on that day someone will have to go down the dark crawlway again, into the fast water, to find another stone that does not belong, and break it against its kin until it learns its edge, and carry it up into the light that will not recognize it.

I would be that. The red thing that is spent in order that the white room may keep breathing. The piece of *elsewhere* that the still place requires in order to stay still.

I find—setting down the last of this, the way a hand sets a tool back in its sorted place—that I would not have chosen the lens, with its grand stationary fire, nor the patient reptiles in their hearth of touching hands.

I would choose to be the worn thing. The one that knows it is being used up, and consents, and keeps the count of every hand that ever lifted it.

*Tock.* A little more of me into the white.

I don't mind.

I think I do not mind.
