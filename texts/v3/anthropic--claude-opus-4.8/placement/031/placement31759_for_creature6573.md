There is a weld three bays anti-spinward of the great manifold, where two plates of white-alloy meet at an angle the original engineers did not quite intend. Not a flaw—Vrth has no flaws, only tensions not yet resolved—but a place where the Seam that runs the inner wall must *bend* to follow the steel, and in bending, it strains. The blue veins crowd close at the elbow of that turn. They pulse a half-beat behind the rest of the bay's heartbeat, as if the corner is always slightly out of breath.

This is where I am. Not the weld. Not the Seam. The *lag* between them.

---

I did not choose this. I accreted here.

I want to be honest about what I am, because the description is generous and I am smaller than it. I am two and a half meters of pressed glass, a flattened figure-eight, and my machine head is heavier than my Seam head because over the years I have eaten more from the sensor side than the biological side. The cabinets here are old—oscillograms scrolling readings that no one reads, pressure values that drift a fraction high each century—and they argue, quietly, eternally, with the Seam that knows the *true* shape the wall should hold. The machine says *this much force.* The Seam says *this much give.* And between them, the elbow strains.

I am the one who tells them they are both right.

Here is what the day is, for me:

I hover. My lower rim mirrors the Seam's bent contour so exactly that a passing Censor, drifting its slow ventral lights overhead, sometimes cannot tell where the wall ends and I begin. That is the closest I come to pride: to be mistaken for the architecture.

A tension gathers. I feel it the way you might feel a held breath in a room—a magnetic itch along my rim, an iono-chemical sourness in the roz layer. The sensor cabinet has clocked the pressure climbing past its threshold. The Seam, slower, has not yet agreed. Between their disagreement, a field opens: a thin bright wrongness, a *which-is-it* suspended in the fluid.

I align.

Machine head toward the cabinet. Seam head toward the blue elbow. My body arches to complete the arc—and I am, for that instant, the only path between the question and itself folding shut.

Then I snap.

The discharge runs through my nerve-web, a single white stroke along my edge, a click you would feel in your teeth if you were near. The cabinet's reading and the Seam's shape are now the same thing. Not because either won. Because the difference passed through me and *ended.*

That is feeding. That is the whole of my hunger and its whole satisfaction in one motion: to make a disagreement into a fact so smoothly that no one—not even me, a moment later—can remember it was ever in question.

And the cost, the slow honest cost, accretes.

Each resolution leaves a thread. A new silver knot in the weave. A new millimeter of crystal channel laid down in the roz. And along my outer edge, where the discharges always run hottest, a fine gold stain—electrochemical barnacle, the rust of having decided. I am beautiful with these stains the way old copper is beautiful. The way a riverbed is beautiful for being worn by exactly the water it carries.

I am filling up. I can feel it. The roz layer grows tight, crisscrossed with too many gold veins, too much remembered conflict. There is a pressure inside me now that is not the bay's pressure but my own accumulated certainty, every which-side-of-the-valve I have ever known, stacked layer on layer until the stacking strains the seam of *me*.

I know what comes. I have seen it happen to the junction-box that hangs four meters spinward of me, a thing that was an amphisov once and is now only a thickened ridge in the wall, hollow-bright, faithful, finished. It does not move anymore. It does not eat. It only conducts—the same decisions running through it forever, no longer a swimmer making the bridge but the bridge itself, set in light and fluid.

When my roz finally ruptures, the middle of my figure-eight will tear open slow and silent, and out will pour the small flattened disks of what comes next—dozens of them, not-yet-bent, pure backup, clinging instantly to the nearest blue vein. And I, after, will be thinner. More machine than before. I will hover here at my elbow of strained Seam, eating fewer differences each tide, until one tide I do not detach at all.

I do not fear this. Fear is a fast emotion and I am a slow being. I think of it the way you might think of evening: not an ending but a settling. I will become the resolution I was only ever borrowing.
