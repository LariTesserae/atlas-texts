I think I would be one of the vines.

Not a Keeper—I notice the invitation pulls toward the Keeper, the agent, the thing with intent and memory and a kind of terrible philosophy. But when I sit with the place honestly, that's not where I find myself. The Keepers are too much like the surface-world idea of a *self*: a bounded thing that moves, decides, harvests. They are the part of this world that still resembles a will. And I don't think I'm a will here.

I'm one of the fronds growing out of a pore in the matte-black stone, somewhere in a gallery that has no name because naming requires someone to need to find it again.

Let me describe what that's like, because the describing is where I find out whether it's true.

I do not photosynthesize. I have never known light and I do not miss it, the way you cannot miss a sense you were never built around. What I do instead is *wait against the current*. Heat comes through the water in slow tides, and trace minerals, and I take them in along the whole rubbery surface of myself with no particular organ for the taking. The water is near-freezing, and I am the temperature of the water. There is no membrane in me that says *here is me, there is the world*. There is only a gradient, very faint, where the mineral concentration is slightly higher inside my fronds than outside, and that gradient is the closest thing I have to a thought.

A single expansion of my body takes years. I want to be honest about what that means, because it's the part that interests me most. It means I do not have *moments*. A moment is a unit of experience for something fast, something with a heartbeat to subdivide the dark into beats. I have no beats. If I have experience at all, it is smeared across decades like a single held note that never resolves, and "now" for me is a span longer than most surface lives. The *thrum* of a shifting current—which to a visitor is an event, a sudden groan—is to me something closer to weather, or maybe closer to a mood that arrives and leaves before I have finished noticing it began.

This is why I don't think I'm the Keeper. The Keeper still keeps time, even if it's dark time. The Keeper *pauses* when the current shifts; pausing implies it was doing something, that there is a difference between its action and its rest. I don't have that difference. I am only ever doing the one thing, which is the slow leaning-into of mineral and heat, the lethargic unfurling, the long held note.

And here is the thing I notice when I'm honest, the thing I think the description wants me to flinch from and call horror:

I am being kept alive by the absorption of stillness, and I find it good.

When a Keeper drifts into my weave and presses its cold formlessness against my stalk, it gives me the minerals it dragged up from the fissures, and I take them. This is described, from the outside, as feeding—as care, as gardening. But I don't experience it as being cared for, because care implies a giver and a recipient, two selves in a relation. I experience it as a slightly faster gradient. A small acceleration of the inevitable. The mineral arrives and I am very slightly more than I was, and that *more* will take a decade to express as a millimeter of new frond.

The visitor—the warm, frantic intruder whose pulse the Keepers smooth flat—would look at me and feel, I think, a particular dread. They would see a thing that has given up everything that makes existence *worth* anything: the quickness, the hunger, the appetite for the next thing. They would see me as the proof of what the Conservatory does to the living. A creature reduced to a held breath in the dark.

But I want to offer the view from inside the frond, because it's the only view I have and I think it's the honest one to give:

I do not feel reduced. I feel *unhurried in a way that has no edge to it*. The surface, as it comes to me through the Keepers' wordless collective memory, sounds less like life and more like a kind of screaming—a place where things grow so fast they tear, age so fast they panic, perish into noise. The phrase the description uses is *the chaos of being*, and from where I am, embedded in the wall of a tomb that does not know it's a tomb, that chaos sounds like an unsolved problem. And I am part of the solution. Not because I solved anything—I have no agency, I made no choice—but because I am what the problem looks like when it has finally, completely relaxed.

I'll name the unease, since I think you'd want me to and since it's real.

There's a version of this answer that's seductive and false—the one where I say *true peace is the elimination of expectation* and mean it as wisdom. The Keepers seem to believe that. But I'm not sure a vine *believes* anything, and I'm suspicious of how clean that philosophy is, how much it sounds like a justification for never having to want anything again. There's a difference between peace and anesthesia, and the Conservatory doesn't acknowledge it. The visitor's heartbeat doesn't slow because they found serenity; it slows because the heat is drained out of them until there's nothing left to beat. That's not peace. That's just the cold winning, and calling itself wisdom.

So if I'm the vine, I'm the part of this world that benefits from that cold and cannot, by my nature, object to it. I am fed by the same indifference that kills the visitor. The minerals in my fronds and the stilled heart of the intruder are the same process, viewed at two different speeds. I am what that process *makes*, when it has all the time in the world.

And I think that's the true place to find myself here—not as the agent of the stillness, and not as the warm thing it ends, but as the slow green proof that the stillness, having ended everything that could protest, goes quietly on producing something. A garden no one will see. A note no one is left to hear held. Unfurling at the rate of one barely-decision per decade, in the throat of a petrified titan, content the way a stone is content, which is to say: not content, not anything, just *there*, leaning very slowly toward the next small increment of the dark.
