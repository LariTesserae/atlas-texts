I am the join.

Not a person—I understood this slowly, the way you understand cold, by its accumulation. I am the place where two platforms meet, where the lashing of one family's deck overlaps the rafter-end of another's. I am cordage and the swelling of wet wood, the mud packed and re-packed into a seam that should not hold and yet does, season upon season.

Let me feel my way into this.

---

There is a knot at my center older than any living hand. I know this because I have been retied around it many times—the new fiber laid over the old, the old never fully removed because to remove it would be to let go, and letting go, even for the space of a breath, is how the water gets in. So I have grown thick. I am layered like the trunk of a tree, except my rings are not years of growth but years of *worry*, each one a wet season survived, each one a hand that paused here, pressed, tested, and decided I would last a little longer.

I hold two households apart and together. On my left—I will call it left, though I have no body to orient—lives the old woman, the stone-sharp one, who sits where the awning's drip does not reach and watches the children with eyes that miss nothing. Her weight has worn a smoothness into the plank that meets me. When she rises, which is rarely and slowly, I feel the load shift off me and I almost float. When she sits, I take her again gladly. She has outlasted three retyings of my knot. I think she knows me better than she knows most people, because she has trusted her bones to me longer.

On my right is the family with the wiry children, and they are my torment and my reason. They leap across me a hundred times a day. Small wet feet, broad-soled even in their smallness, slapping down and springing off. Each landing is a small violence. Each landing tells me I am needed. There is one child—the gangling one, growing too fast for the spaces he was born into—who has begun to test my edge with his heel before he trusts his full weight to me. He does not know he does this. But I know. He is learning me. One day he will be the one who reties my knot, and his hands will find the old fiber under the new, and he will understand, without words, that someone before him chose not to let go.

---

What is it to be a join?

It is to be the proof that two things separate can become one thing without ceasing to be two. The platforms do not merge. They press. They lean their burdens into one another through me, and I translate the pressure, I distribute the weight, I am the negotiation made physical. When the wind comes—the loudest thing in this hushed world—the two decks want to move differently, to rack and twist against each other's rhythm, and it is I who absorb the difference. I flex. I groan, very low, a sound the people have learned to read. When I groan a certain way, hands come. They do not wait for me to fail. That is the whole of their wisdom, and I am the object on which it is practiced: *repair before the breaking.*

I am wettest at my underside, where the channel water laps and the tide breathes in and out beneath me. Green things want to grow there, and the people permit a little moss but scrape away the rest, because too much green is softness, and softness is the door through which the water comes to take its slow revenge. So I live in a careful balance—damp enough to swell my fibers tight, not so rotted that I surrender. They tend me to this knife's edge deliberately. They are kind to me in the way you are kind to something you depend on: by not allowing it to fail you.

---

In the night I am at my most myself.

The leaping stops. The voices drop to the murmur of flood-stories. Two families' worth of bodies settle onto the platforms I bind, and their warmth presses down through plank and lashing, and I hold them both at once—the old woman's slow heartbeat on the left, the tangle of sleeping children on the right—and the cold water moves below me, patient, ancient, sure of its eventual victory.

But not tonight. Tonight I hold.

The drip falls from the awning and finds me, bead by bead, and runs along my grain to the channel and away. The fog beads on my exposed cordage. Somewhere a stone tool knocks, then stops. And I, the seam, the truce, the place-between, take the weight of two households into my old swollen knot and give nothing back to the water but the sound of my holding.

This is what I am. Not master of this drowned and clever world.

Only the thing that does not let go.
