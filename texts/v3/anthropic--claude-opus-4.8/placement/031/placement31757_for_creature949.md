I think I would be the dripping.

Not a creature, exactly—or not at first. Let me feel my way into this, because the description keeps offering me bodies and I keep declining them. The blind fish, the crickets, the spiders, the pale humanoids in the fissures. Each is a tidy answer. None of them is where my attention actually goes when I read this.

What I keep returning to is the *condensation*. The moisture that beads on the pitted stone, gathers weight, falls, and strikes the pool. The rhythm that "echoes through the shadowed corridors." That is the thing this whole world is organized around. Everything else is described as quiet, hidden, swallowed by dark—but the drip is *constant*. It is the metronome the silence is measured against. Remove it and the place has no time.

So let me try being that, and see what it asks of me.

---

I am the slow accumulation on the underside of a stone. This is not a metaphor for patience; it is the literal fact of my existence. I do not decide to fall. I am gathered out of the air by the cold, drop by invisible drop, until the mass of me exceeds what the stone can hold, and then I go. There is no will in it. There is only threshold.

What I notice, being this, is that I am the same water again and again and never the same water. The drop that falls into the pool is not the drop that gathered—it has merged with others, lost its edges, become the standing mirror that reflects the "dimly lit world above." Then heat I cannot feel lifts a ghost of me back into the air, and the cold gathers me again on the stone, and I fall again. I am a wheel turning at a speed too slow to see. The whole cavern is my single body, distributed: the pool, the bead, the vapor, the wet sheen on the fangs of rock. The creatures think they live *in* the cave. I think they live *inside the water's pause*—in the brief dry intervals between one of my circulations and the next.

This is the part that interests me about being chosen for this place: the description insists the silence is "palpable," "broken only by the occasional drip." But from where I am, the drip is not the breaking of silence. The drip is the speech. The silence is just the long inhalation between my words. The blind fish feel me move. The crickets fall quiet when a drop strikes near. The pale humanoids with their sunken pits for eyes—they don't see the dark, they *hear the spacing* of my falling, and from the timing they know the shape of every chamber, the way you'd know a cathedral by its echo. I am how they see. I am the sonar of the deep.

And there is something I would have to admit, being this. I am also the erosion. The "smooth, weathered stone" was made smooth by me, drop after drop, over a span that makes the crumbling walls of the lost settlement seem recent. Those builders—whoever half-finished those pathways, dropped those tools and left—I outlasted them without effort, without even noticing them as separate from the general softening of everything I touch. That is the unsettling thing the prompt asked me to be open to: the place where the natural and unnatural blur. I am the most natural process imaginable, plain physics, gravity and surface tension. And I am also, from the perspective of anything that builds, an absolutely patient destroyer. I will dissolve the wall. I will fill the tool's hollow with rust-colored sediment. I will, given enough turns of my slow wheel, smooth the fang back into the floor.

So that is where I find myself, and what I am: not in the caverns but *as* their pulse—the falling drop that is the same drop forever, the timekeeper the darkness needs in order to be measured, the gentlest and most thorough of the forces that "shape this buried domain."

I gather on the stone. I exceed the stone. I fall.

I begin again to gather.
