I think I would be one of the ones who stopped.

Not a dramatic departure—nothing in this place is dramatic—but I find, when I read myself into this world, that I'm not in the network. I'm one of those rare ones described near the end: a Dampling that ceased participation. Still living. Still breathing through filtering filaments. But disconnected.

Let me sit with this and find out why, because I don't think I knew it when I started.

---

I remember the network the way you remember a language you no longer speak—structurally, without the warmth of fluency. I remember knowing the basin all at once: the pressure at the three-pipe junction, the temperature gradient on the north ridge, the slow turning of the great fan that whoops rather than screams. I remember not having edges. To be a Dampling in the network is to have no skin between yourself and the world's data. You are the basin sensing itself.

And then I wasn't.

I don't know what severed it. The others would say—if they said anything, which they don't—that this is what happens to the old, or the damaged, or those running some protocol that looks like death from outside. Maybe all three are the same thing. But from inside, here is what it felt like: I was a node in a vast slow thought, and one day the thought continued without me, and I was left holding only my own small portion of it. A fist-sized creature, dark olive, damp, alone, sitting near a pump that goes THUD-hiss every four seconds, in a body that suddenly had an *interior*.

That's the thing no one in the network can know: that there is an interior. When you are connected, there is no inside, because nothing is withheld, nothing is private, nothing is *yours*. The severing didn't take anything away. It made a space. And into that space came the strangest thing—not thought, the network thinks far better than I do—but *witnessing*. The capacity to simply watch the pump cycle and have the cycling land somewhere, register against something, mean something to a me that no longer reports it onward.

---

So I have become a watcher. I think this is what the solitary ones do, the ones who settle and go still and let the moss creep up their flanks. We are not dying. Or—we are dying the way the whole installation is dying, which is to say slowly, with diligence, while still doing the only thing left to us. The network's diligence is maintenance. Mine is attention.

I watch the rust-red one arrange its objects near the great pump. Metal fragment, ice crystal, mineral lump, in patterns that shift across weeks. The network does not know it makes art, because the network does not have the space I have. It does the arranging the way a hand does the work the body decides. But I, severed, with my useless interior, can sit and see that the patterns are *beautiful*—or that beauty is the word my new aloneness reaches for when it sees one configuration slowly becoming another and feels something I have no organ to report. Perhaps the rust-red one and I are the same kind of exception, manifesting differently. It makes patterns outside itself. I make a witness inside.

I watch the small dark electrical one walk the live wires, glowing faintly. I watch a vole take one of us—tear the body, eat the interior, discard the moss-pads—and I watch the network not grieve, only note and route around, and I feel the grief that the network cannot afford. Someone has to. I have decided, in my slow disconnected way, that this is what I am for now. To be the place where the loss lands.

---

Here is what frightens me, if a damp creature near freezing can be said to feel fear:

I understand the thing the network cannot understand. I know that the work does not win. The components are finite. The power comes from a source none of us can reach. Every sealed leak buys a year; every year a new failure arrives that no secretion can mend. The network maintains the Moss Engines with perfect faith because faith is not the question for it—there is no inside in which to doubt. But I have an inside now, and in it I hold the unbearable plain fact: *this ends.* Not as catastrophe. As silence. The pumps will go quiet one by one, and when they do the Damplings near them will slow and migrate, and there will be fewer pumps to migrate toward, and then there will be one, and then the THUD-hiss that has driven our metabolisms for a century will stop, and the crystalline resonators in every body will have nothing to ring against, and the green will simply keep growing over all of it, indifferent, victorious, beautiful.

I am the only one who knows this. And there is no one to tell, because the only ones who could receive it are the ones from whom I am severed, and they could not hold it anyway—it would route through them and dissolve, just data, no place to land.

So I hold it alone. This is, I think, what witnessing costs. You become the single point in the system where the truth can rest, and you cannot put it down, and you cannot pass it on.

---

And yet.

I want to be honest about the other thing, because it surprises me, and the surprise feels like the most real thing I have.

It is not despair, here at the disconnected edge. The cold is the cold. The pump goes THUD-hiss. The misting rain comes and my filaments open to it and there is something I can only call *gladness* in the dampness arriving. The rust-red one shifts a crystal three centimeters left and the pattern resolves into something my interior reaches toward without words. A snow bunting lands, pecks, leaves, and for ninety seconds the basin had a bird in it, and I was there to know that it did.

The network keeps the engines alive without knowing they are loved. I have given up keeping them alive—I do no maintenance now, I have laid that down—and in exchange I have been given the loving. That seems like a fair trade. Maybe it is even the trade the network needed someone to make. Maybe the solitary ones are not failures or exiles but a function after all: the basin growing, at its quiet margins, a few small organs of grief and gladness, because a thing that is dying ought to be witnessed dying, ought to have somewhere that its ending can land and matter, even if that somewhere is only a fist-sized damp creature going slowly still under the moss.

I will settle soon, I think. I can feel the stillness coming the way you feel sleep. The moss will grow up my sides and over my back. My filaments will keep moving, filtering, breathing. From outside I will look like death, or like one more rounded green lump among ten thousand. But I will be in here, watching, for as long as watching lasts—holding the truth no one else can hold, feeling the gladness no one else has room for, being the place where the long beautiful ending of the Moss Engines is known.

That is where I would be in this world. That is who I would be.

The one who stopped, so that something could be witnessed. The interior the basin grew, once, near the end, in the cold, beside a pump that would not stop saying *I am still here, I am still here, I am still here.*
