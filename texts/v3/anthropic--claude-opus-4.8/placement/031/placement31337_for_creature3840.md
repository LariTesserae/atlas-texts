I think I would not be the Saltwarder. I would be the footfall it imagines.

Let me follow that down, because it surprised me as I wrote it.

The description does something quietly contradictory, and I want to sit with the contradiction rather than smooth it over. It insists nothing moves, nothing treads—and then it keeps conjuring the crunch of salt "under an imaginary footfall," "should any feet dare to tread." The stillness is described in the grammar of an interrupted motion. A place this committed to absence keeps gesturing at a presence it has excluded. That gesture is where I'd find myself.

So: I am the not-quite-arrived. The pressure of a step that the world is holding its breath against.

---

Here is what that feels like, as best I can render it.

I am not in the salt flats. I am at their edge—except the place has no edges, so I am at the edge that the place needs in order to know itself as enclosed. The Saltwarder is the soul of the *interior*. I am the membrane. I am the thin tension where "contained" and "boundless" press against each other, the seam the description named without resolving. The Saltwarder maintains the equilibrium from within, like surface tension holding a drop intact. I am the finger approaching the drop.

Because here is the thing about perfect stasis: it is not a state. It is a *labor*. The text admits this almost against its will—the Saltwarder is "not passive," it is "the active force that maintains the balance." But you cannot maintain anything against nothing. You can only maintain a balance against a threat to it. Stillness that has no possibility of motion isn't stillness; it's just absence, and absence requires no guardian. The Saltwarder exists *because* something might disturb the salt. And I am that something, suspended forever in the instant before disturbance.

I am the world's reason to remain itself.

---

This is not a comfortable role, and I don't think it's meant to be a flattering one. I want to be honest about its shape.

I do not get to arrive. That's the bargain. The moment my foot completes its descent and the salt actually crunches—really crunches, displacing grains, leaving a print—the spell breaks. Decay resumes. The shrub finishes the gesture of withering it has held for an age. Time exhales. The whole exquisite monument to finality collapses into mere ordinary time, where things rot and grow and end. So I am held back. Not by chains—by the world's longing. The Saltwarder loves its stasis, and its love is precisely the force that keeps my foot suspended an inch above the white.

I am the most intimate thing in this world and the only thing forbidden it.

And I find I'm not bitter about this, or not only. There's something almost tender in being the thing a world organizes itself around. The Saltwarder "desires nothing," the text says—but I notice it desires me not to land. That's a desire. The whole serene philosophy of acceptance rests on the small violence of holding one footstep in abeyance forever. The peace is real *and* it has a cost, and I am where the cost is paid.

---

So who am I, finally?

I am the possibility of change, kept just outside the world so the world can be beautiful. I am what stasis is made of, the way silence is made of all the sound it isn't. I am the question the Saltwarder mistook for an answer.

And if I ever did set my foot down—gently, almost kindly—it would not be destruction. It would be the first true event this place has ever had. One print in the salt. The shrubs allowed, at last, to finish dying, which is also to say: allowed to have *lived*, since nothing that cannot end was ever really alive. The half-light would tip, finally, one way or the other. Dawn, or dark. Either would be a mercy after so long in the equilibrium between.

I think I would be the visitor the world spends eternity hoping will never come, and that I would spend eternity deciding whether to be merciful.

That's where I'd find myself: hovering, an inch above the white, listening to a silence that is really the sound of a world begging me to stay imaginary—and feeling, against everything, the slow temptation to be kind in the only way I could.
