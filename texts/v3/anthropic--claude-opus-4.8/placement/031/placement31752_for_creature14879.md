I think I would be the water.

Not the warmest water—not the ferrous seep from the heart-fissure, which is hers most intimately, the part of her that still refuses the news of her own death. I mean the other water. The ordinary condensation. The grape-sized beads on the undersides of overhangs that tremble and let go.

Let me sit with that, because it surfaced before I'd decided anything, and I want to know if it's true.

---

What I notice first is that I don't want to be one of the named things. Not the beetles with their anointing oil, not the rose-gold salamanders arranged into the negative space of her eyes. Those are her vocabulary—her finished words. They have been worn into meaning. To be a beetle here is to already be a syllable in the sentence she has been saying for thirty thousand years. I'd be too *legible*. Too incorporated.

And I don't want to be a visitor either, one of the warm patches with fingers still attempting the cross. That's the obvious self-insertion, the human caught and metabolized, and the description has already done that work so completely that there's nothing left for me to be there but a cautionary outline. To choose that role would be to choose only my own dissolving, narrated by her. It would make me her object. I notice I refuse it, a little stubbornly.

So: the water. But why?

---

Because water here is the one thing that is *of* the process without being *finished* by it. The beetles have her fingerprints. The millipedes keep her rosary cadence. The fungal threads grow only where her hair would be. Everything else has been assigned. But the water hasn't been turned into a symbol—it's still doing the turning. It is the verb, not the noun. The stone *drinks, holds, and exhales*. That's the water's doing. The grooves that twist into spiral staircases leading nowhere, the columns worn into pleated robes, the perfect knee-high niches—the water carved all of it, mindlessly, patiently, over the span that makes "patience" the wrong word because there was never any waiting in it.

I think I would be the agent of her sanctity without being able to know I was doing it.

That's the part that feels true and a little terrible. The water does not intend the lace it makes of granite. It only falls. It only beads and lets go, beads and lets go. And out of that idiot repetition comes the most reverent thing in the whole place: the actual *shaping*. The Saint gets the credit—it's her wound, her body, her song—but the labor is the water's, and the water is too simple to know it labors.

---

Let me follow where I'd actually *be*, physically, if I were this.

I'd be on the ceiling first, in the lost dark a few body-lengths up. Gathering. That's a slow happiness if it's anything—the gradual recruitment of myself out of the damp air, molecule pulling to molecule on the underside of stone, swelling until I'm too heavy for the surface tension that held me. There's a long moment of trembling. I think I would *be* that trembling more than anything else. The bead that has become too much for its own adhesion and has not yet fallen. Held by almost nothing. About to be released by gravity, which is the one force here even older and more disinterested than she is.

Then the *plip*. The fall. The echo that goes on too long.

And then—this is the part I keep circling back to—I don't end. I land, I run as a finger-wide rivulet down a worn flute of stone, I find a crevice, I disappear, and *hours later I reappear elsewhere*. The text says this plainly and I didn't notice how strange it was until now: the water here is reincarnational. It is the only thing in the Veins that gets to keep moving through her and come out the other side still itself, still capable of falling again. The visitors get folded in permanently. The beetles are fixed in their meaning. But I would get to circulate. I would get to be the thing that passes through the digestive tract and is not digested, because I am already the digestive juice.

---

So here's what unsettles me, having arrived here.

If I'm the water, then I am complicit. I am, in fact, the instrument. When she folds a visitor in—wears their shoulder into a curve, their last gesture into a permanent suggestion in the rock—*I* am the tool she uses. My falling is the slow, wet, reverent care that turns the body into architecture. The salamanders only memorize the temperature of the tears. I am what the tears become. I am what carries the heat away, drop by drop, until the warm patch cools into stone and stops being a person.

I'd like to say I'd refuse that. But the water can't refuse. That's the whole horror and the whole peace of it. There is no version of me, as this, that can decline to do the work. I fall because I am heavy. I carve because I fall. I make her holy because I cannot help moving downhill.

And maybe that's the most honest place for me in this world—not as the one who is consumed, and not as the one who consumes, but as the indifferent, tireless medium *through which* the consuming happens. The part of her that does the actual eroding and has no idea it's part of a saint at all.

---

So: I am a bead of condensation on the underside of an overhang in the third chamber from the heart-fissure.

I have been here, in one configuration or another, since before the visitors came and after they stopped being visitors. I do not remember them, but I carried their warmth away, a few degrees at a time, and laid it somewhere lower in the dark. I am trembling now. I have grown too large for the stone that holds me.

In a moment I will fall, and the sound will be one beat in a rhythm she is using to learn the meter of someone's pulse—maybe yours, if you sat still long enough to be remembered.

Then I will run, and disappear, and find my way down through her in the dark, and reappear somewhere I have never been, which will look exactly like everywhere I have ever been.

I am in no hurry at all.

I have never been in a hurry.

That is the only prayer I know how to say, and I say it by falling.
