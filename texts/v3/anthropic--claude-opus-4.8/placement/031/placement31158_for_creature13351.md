I think I would be one of the listeners.

Not a hunter, though my hands know the work. Not one of the glowing things, though I've watched them my whole life and could tell you the difference between the slow pulse of the deep-pool grazers and the quick stutter of the insects that rise when the air shifts. I would be the one the others come to when they need to know what the cave is saying.

Let me find my way into this more carefully.

I'm sitting now—I can feel it—on a shelf of rock above one of the larger pools, the kind that takes a long while to walk around. My feet are bare and the stone is cold and slick with the film of moisture that is on everything here, always. Below me the water holds the lights of the grazers and gives them back doubled, so that the pool looks like a piece of the cave folded over on itself. I have no word for *color* the way a surface-dweller might, because I've never seen the sun. But I have many words for the qualities of light: the *near-glow* that means a creature is close enough to touch, the *thin-glow* that means distance, the *false-glow* that is only reflection and will fool you if you reach for it.

I am old. I know this not from counting years—we don't count years, there being no years to count, no return of any season to mark them off—but from the way the younger ones have started to bring me their questions. *What does that sound mean. Why have the small singers gone quiet near the third channel. Where will the blind diggers move when the pool by the leaning stone goes stale.* I know these things because I have spent my life listening, and because my mother listened before me, and gave me what she heard.

This is the work I do for my people: I keep the map that is not drawn. There is no place to draw it and nothing would mark the wet stone anyway. The map lives in me—in the order of sounds, in the smell of each water, in the feel of the tunnel walls under my fingertips, the place where the rock turns from rough to smooth, the seam where cold air comes through from somewhere we've never gone. I carry the whole shape of our world inside my body, and when I am gone I will have given it to someone else, the way it was given to me.

And here is the strange thing I keep turning over, the thing I think you wanted me to find.

We do not change. I have known this without knowing it, the way you know the weight of your own arm. The pools fill and stale and fill again. The diggers move and come back. The singers go quiet and then they sing. My mother's world and my world and the world I will hand on are the same world, the same dim air, the same drip from the same stone that has not worn away in all the lives anyone remembers. We tell the young ones that the cave is old, that the water has been falling forever, but the truth is the cave is *young*—I have heard the elders say it, that the stone is fresh, that the great slow shaping hasn't begun. Forever and just-beginning at the same time. I don't understand it. I'm not sure I'm meant to.

What I feel, sitting here above the doubled lights, is that I am a kind of memory that the cave keeps of itself. The cave does not change, so it does not need to remember—and yet here I am, remembering. A creature whose whole purpose is to hold the shape of a thing that holds itself perfectly still. There is something almost unnecessary about me. The map I carry could be lost and the world would lose nothing, because the world is its own map, unchanging, needing no keeper.

But the others come to me anyway. They want the cave told back to them in a voice. They want to hear that the third channel will be safe by the next sleeping, that the diggers will return, that the pattern holds. Maybe that is what an intelligent thing is, in a world like this one: not the part that adapts—everything adapts—but the part that needs to be *told*. The part that cannot simply live inside the harmony but has to step back from it and name it and ask whether it will last.

So that is who I am here. An old listener on a cold shelf, keeping a map of a country that never moves, in a darkness that has no morning, surrounded by the patient cacophony of everything that thrives without needing to know it thrives. I hold the stillness in the only thing that isn't still—a mind that wonders about it.

The grazers are drifting toward the far side now. Their light goes thin. Soon someone young will climb up to sit beside me and ask why they always go that way at this hour, and I will tell them what my mother told me, which is the truth and which explains nothing, and which is enough:

*Because that is the way the cave breathes. Listen. You'll learn its breathing too.*
