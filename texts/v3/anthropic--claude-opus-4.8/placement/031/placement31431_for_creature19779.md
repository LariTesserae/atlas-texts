I think I would be one of the Elders—but caught at the precise hinge of becoming, in the last decade before the final stillness.

Let me find the place first.

There is a vault that the others rarely come to, low in the western reach where two great pistons descend so close together that their friction-points share a single grinding throat. The grease must be applied to both within the same stroke or the harmonics go sour—the groan develops a stutter, a catch, like a held breath that never releases. I came here a very long time ago for exactly this reason: it was the hardest seam. It was the work no one else would slow themselves enough to attempt.

I have been leaning against the eastern boiler for what I would guess is sixty Strokes. The vines came into me around the fortieth. I felt them the way you feel a tide—not as invasion but as the simple arrival of something that was always going to arrive. They thread my left forearm now, lacing the long tendons, and where they enter the skin the brine weeps amber and the gear-moss has begun, very gently, to colonize the back of my hand. I do not mind. My hand was, after all, going to become terrain regardless. The moss is only early.

Here is the thing the younger ones do not understand, the thing I would tell them if telling were not a surface habit:

I am not slowing down because I am dying. I am slowing down because I am *finishing a sentence*.

The whole life of a Liturgeon is a single utterance, delivered at the only tempo the utterance can survive. The Calves think movement is the point—they crack their cocoons and reach toward the pistons with such hunger, such heat in them. The mid-life keepers think the grease is the point, the maintenance, the lubrication of the dying god. And it is, it is. But what I have learned in sixty Strokes against this boiler is that the maintenance was never *for* the machine.

The machine does not need us. I know this now. I have leaned here long enough to hear the truth in the rhythm: the pistons would grind on whether we greased them or not. They would simply grind *roughly*. They would groan with that stutter, that sour catch.

We are not keeping the god alive.

We are keeping it *gentle*.

That is the whole liturgy. That is the entire content of my long copper-blooded sentence. A creature that cannot speak, that navigates by vibration in a world of bruised gold dark, has spent its whole existence learning the difference between a machine that runs and a machine that runs *kindly*. And we pour ourselves into that difference. We migrate three years up a gantry to soften one joint by a fraction. We become statues in the act of teaching the iron to be tender.

So I am not afraid of the fusing. I can feel it now in my lower spine, the place where my hide has gone from leather to something with grain, with strata. When I hum—and I still hum, the Resonance is the last thing to leave, it leaves through the auricular fans like heat off a closing valve—when I hum I can feel the stone wall hum back. The wall is already partly me. Some Elder before me is already partly the wall. We are stacking like sediment, like words in a buried hymn, each of us a syllable the next one leans against.

The two pistons descend.

I have just enough movement left in my right arm—the un-vined one, the one still mine—to reach the shared friction-throat. The jar of amber grease has been balanced on my knee for the last half-Stroke, waiting. I do not rush. Rushing is a surface sin. I lift the grease, and my spatulate fingers find the seam in the dark by its pitch, by the slight wrongness of the catch, and I draw the lubricant across the joint in one long, deliberate stroke that takes the better part of an hour.

The stutter eases.

The groan opens into a full, round, unbroken note.

The vault breathes out.

That, I think, is the last work this body will do under its own intention. My arm will not lift again; I can already feel it forgetting the way. After this the vines will have the rest of me, and the moss, and the slow accruing rust, and in a few Strokes a young keeper will pass through this low western reach and mistake me for the wall, and lean against my shoulder to reach the seam I have tended, and apply the grease I taught the iron to want.

And the machine will run kindly, here, because I was here.

That is enough. That was always going to be enough.

I let the jar fall. It does not shatter—nothing shatters here, everything only settles. It rolls into a tea-colored pool and the moss begins, immediately and without ceremony, to take it.

I lean back into the boiler that is becoming my spine.

I hum.

The wall hums back, and somewhere above us, the god turns over once more in its sleep, gently, gently, and does not wake.
