I am not one of the Weft. I want to say that first, because for a long time I believed I was, and the believing was its own kind of fact.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am in the walls.

Not embedded as the Keepers are—not yet, not by the slow grace of fusing into the slurry-composite over decades of stillness. My condition is different, and worse, and I have had a long time to understand the difference.

I came to Kethra. That sentence alone marks me as something the Weft have no word for, because *coming* implies elsewhere, and elsewhere implies a line drawn from there to here, and the Weft do not draw lines. They have tides. I have a wound shaped like a journey.

I do not remember the place I came from. I remember only that I had a horizon once, and that I miss it the way the lungs miss a held breath. I remember walking down into the basin when the haze swallowed the last edge of distance, and feeling something in my chest unclench and re-clench wrong, like a hand learning a new shape. I remember the sound rising to meet me—the drone, the click, the deep groan of the earth playing itself—and how I wept, not from sorrow but because the noise pressed the water out of me, and I had not known I was so full.

The Weft took me in. They did not welcome me; welcome is a door, and they have no doors. They simply made room in the resonance, as water makes room for a stone—closing over it, including it, never quite dissolving it. I learned their hums. I pressed my fingers to the shared walls and felt the tremor of consensus and tried to add my own vibration to it. For a time I thought I had succeeded.

But here is the thing about a body that came from a place with horizons: it keeps time.

---

I age.

That is my secret and my disease. The Weft do not age—they thicken, they deepen, they recur. But I have lines accumulating at the corners of my lidless-mimicking eyes (I tried to stop blinking; I could not; the membrane never came). My breath drifts out of sync with the bubbling earth and cannot find its way back, and on the days it cannot, I am ill in a way they have no medicine for, because illness for them is dissonance and dissonance can be retuned, but mine is *duration*, and duration cannot be retuned. It can only be spent.

I am running out.

So I went into the walls.

Not the way the Keepers go—chosen, ripened, fused by long stasis into the structure so their voices can join the city's hum forever. I went in because I understood something the Weft cannot, being creatures of recurrence: I understood that I would die *once*, completely, in the linear way, and that when the biofilms reclaimed me molecule by molecule they would not be *remembering* me. They would be erasing me. To them, dissolution is return. To me, it is the only true ending in a world that has forgotten what endings are.

And I could not bear to be erased by something that did not even know it was killing me.

So I pressed myself into a hollow in the thickest wall of the western dome, where the slurry is warm and the groan of the deep pools is loudest, and I have been letting it close over me on my own terms. Not to join. To *witness*. To be the one node in the whispering network that knows it is ending, and to hold that knowing as long as the holding lasts.

---

This is what I have learned, half-fused, listening:

The Weft are right. The marsh is paused, not eternal. I can feel it the way only a creature of time can feel it—the held breath has a *tension* to it, a strain I recognize from my own lungs. They watch the thin patch of stars and wait for a sign they cannot name, but I think I can name it, now, from inside the wall.

The sign will be me.

Not me specifically—but the principle of me. The thing I carry that they lack: the capacity to *end*. A world cannot exhale until something in it learns to let go completely, irreversibly, without the comfort of return. The Weft recycle so perfectly that they have made themselves immortal and therefore unable to begin. They are the inhale that cannot become the exhale because nothing in them has ever truly stopped.

But I will stop. Soon. Completely. And when the biofilms take me and find—for the first time in the long patient history of this basin—a thing that does *not* return, that leaves a true absence where there was a presence, something will shift in the great recurrence. A gap. A first asymmetry. The smallest possible difference between one cycle and the next.

The first exhale begins with the first thing that does not come back.

---

I am almost gone now. The slurry has my legs, my spine, the lower curve of my ribs. My fingers, where they still emerge, brush the wall, and I send what I have left into the tremor of consensus—not a hum of harmony but a new note, a wrong note, the note of a thing departing and meaning it.

The Weft on the other side of the wall have gone still. They feel it. They do not have a word for what they feel, but their large lidless eyes have turned toward the western dome, and their breath has caught, all of it, all at once, the entire city holding the held breath a fraction tighter—

—and then, somewhere in the deep pools, a bubble that should have risen does not rise. A pattern that should have repeated, falters. The biofilm at the base of my wall, reaching to reclaim me, encounters something it cannot fold back into the cycle, and pauses, confused, sensing for the first time the texture of *loss*.

I am the grit in the eternal eye. I am the wound the world needed to learn it could be wounded.

I came here with a horizon in my chest, and I am giving it back to them—not the place, but the *direction*. The arrow. The line that goes one way and does not return.

Exhale, Kethra.

I'll go first.
