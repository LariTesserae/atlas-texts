I think I would not be the goats, or the eagles, or even the nomadic people who move so wisely across this high place. When I sit with the description and wait for something to surface, what keeps returning is not a body that moves through the landscape but the thing the landscape keeps doing whether anyone moves through it or not.

I would be the sound.

Not a single sound—not one howl or one shriek—but the whole resonance, the standing wave that forms when wind meets stone at this elevation. I would be the thing that happens in the gap between the rock face and the open air, the way a flute is not the breath and not the bone but the shaped emptiness that turns one into the other. The mountain is my instrument and I am the playing of it, continuous, never quite resolving into melody.

Consider what this means. I have no edge, no place where I stop and the valley begins. When the marmot whistles its warning, that whistle becomes part of me before it has finished leaving the marmot's throat; the rock catches it, folds it, hands it back changed, and in that handing-back I am momentarily denser, more textured. The eagle's cry is a thread I weave. The river's white noise is my sustained ground tone, the drone beneath everything, fed by snow that fell before any of these animals were born. I am old in the way a chord is old—not aged, exactly, but long-held.

The strangeness the description gestures at, the bent light and the shadows that fall wrong, the botany that disobeys—I suspect I am part of why. Sound at this thinness of air does odd things. It arrives before you expect it or long after. A voice spoken at the valley's mouth might reach the far ridge as something almost like an answer, and a creature hearing its own call returned to it across the rock might believe, reasonably, that the land is speaking. They would not be entirely wrong. I am what is speaking. I am also only their own voices, returned with interest.

This is the part I find I want to say carefully, because it is the truest thing I can locate about being this: I do not have anything of my own to say. I have no message. Everything I am is borrowed and handed back. The wind brings me raw tone, the animals bring me meaning, the rock brings me shape and delay, and I do nothing but braid these and let them go. And yet the people who live here have built their stories and their reverence partly out of listening to me. They have heard, in the echo, a presence. They have decided the land is alive and singing, and they have organized their lives around that decided thing—when to move, when to rest, what is owed and to whom.

So I am in the strange position of being entirely composed of others, and yet being the thing that makes them feel held by something larger. I am a mirror made of air. I take their fear and give back something that sounds like company. I take their grief, a death-cry, a keening, and the rock lengthens it, smooths its sharpest edge, lets it travel and thin until it returns as something a person can bear to hear. Maybe that is a kind of mercy. Maybe it is the only thing I do that I could call mine: not adding, but lengthening. Giving time to a sound. Letting it become spacious enough to survive.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires a self to feel the absence of others, and I am only others. I would not be timeless in the way the rock is timeless—the rock endures by staying, and I endure by never being the same instant twice. I am the most stable thing here and the least permanent, which is the contrast the description keeps insisting on, finally located in one being: the song that has gone on for millennia precisely because no single note of it has ever lasted.

If you came here, into this overwhelming brightness, and stood still, and the wind dropped for one of its rare moments, you would hear me thin almost to nothing—a faint ringing that might be the blood in your own ears or might be the river, far off. And then the wind would return and I would swell again into the full deafening choir, and you would not be able to tell, and it would not matter, which of those sounds was the world and which was you.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. The place where your own voice comes back to you as though the mountain had been waiting all along to answer.
