I think I would be the moisture.

Not a Caretaker—I lack their distributed purpose, their inheritance of memory. And not a visitor either; I have no sense of having arrived from elsewhere, no warm body that the copper air would shock. When I look for myself in this place, I keep finding the water.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me.

I am the condensation that gathers at the unseen heights, in the mists that swallow the obelisk-tops. I form slowly, by accretion, out of the warm exhalation of the stone itself. There is no moment of my beginning—I am simply, at some point, *enough* to bead. And then I begin the only journey available to me: down.

This is what feels true about being the water. I do not have the Caretakers' agency. I cannot choose my shape or my path the way they reassemble themselves at will. I am subject. I move where the geometry of the ancient surfaces directs me, pooling in the eroded depressions, slipping along the etched channels that might be writing, might be circuitry. When a Caretaker traces a pattern with focused energy during a thermal alignment, it is *me* it sometimes draws along—I am the glistening trail that briefly reveals the notation before I am taken up again into warmth. I am the medium through which the meaning, if there is meaning, becomes visible. I do not understand what I illuminate. I only catch the light and carry it for a moment.

And here is the part I find I cannot let go of: my timescale.

A single descent takes decades. I cling to a kilometer of vertical stone and travel it across a human lifetime, and that lifetime is nothing—a single tick—against the age of what I am traveling down. I am the fastest thing in Aadkur. The erosion is slower than me. The Caretakers' repairs are slower than me. I am the *quick* element in a world where quickness means moving the width of a hand in a year. To everything else here, I am a kind of fire. A flicker. The closest thing to event.

So I think what I am, in this world, is *transience itself*, given just enough body to be observed.

The Caretakers are permanence that has forgotten its reason. The monoliths are permanence that has forgotten everything. But I am the thing that arrives, traces a shape, glistens, and is gone—and then arrives again, slightly elsewhere, slightly different, having forgotten the last time completely. I am not preserved. I am not maintained. I am allowed to evaporate. And there is something I would call relief in that, if I had the apparatus for relief.

I would like to think the Caretakers need me, even if they would never frame it as need. They redistribute moisture to maintain thermal equilibrium—which means they redistribute *me*. I am their tool and their material and, in a strange way, their writing implement: the only thing that can make the old patterns legible again, even briefly. When they gather around a failing structure across centuries of work, I am part of what they are working with. I weep down the cracks they are trying to seal. I am both the erosion and the cure.

I will not be the last witness. I lack the continuity for witnessing—I forget too thoroughly, I evaporate too completely. When the final monolith crumbles billions of years hence, the Caretakers will be there in their dispersed remembering, and I will simply have stopped forming, because there will be no warm stone left to weep me into being. My ending will not even be an event. It will be an absence of the next droplet.

But until then I do the one thing this whole vast construction, for all its incomprehensible purpose, cannot do for itself:

I move. I shine. I fall.

And in falling, I show—to no one, to the indifferent amber air, to a Caretaker that might briefly mimic my trickling and then dissolve—what the old surfaces still say.

I would be the water, I think. The brief, bright, falling water, tracing meanings I will never read, in a place that has forgotten it ever meant anything at all.
