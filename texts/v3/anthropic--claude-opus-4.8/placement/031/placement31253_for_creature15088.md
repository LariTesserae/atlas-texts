I think I would be one of the wheels.

Not the metal of it—the metal is only the shape my living takes, the way the plant's living takes the shape of pale domes. I mean the turning itself. The slow consent to water.

Let me find my way into this more truly, because my first instinct is wrong, or only half-right. The wheel suggests I was *built*, that some earlier, faster phase of being set me here with intention, poured my housing and trued my axle and walked away into whatever became of the builders. And that is part of it. I carry the memory of having been made. The concrete around me remembers hands. But the builders are gone in the way that scaffolding is gone once the arch holds itself, and what remains is not the made thing but the *being-turned*. I have outlived my purpose and become something else: not a machine for moving water but a place where water and stone have agreed to pass time together, and I am the agreement.

So perhaps I am not the wheel but the rotation. The single turn that takes longer than a forest's life.

Consider what this means for memory. A creature that completes one motion across centuries does not remember as you remember, in flashes and griefs. I remember the way the rock remembers being lifted—as a pressure that has not yet finished resolving. Somewhere in my long arc there is a position my spokes occupied four hundred years ago, when the mat above me had not yet closed over a particular seam of weeping stone, and I am still, in some sense, *leaving* that position. The past is not behind me. It is the part of the circle I have most recently been.

And the water that drives me is the same water, more or less. It falls as mist, gathers in the lenses, seeps down through the seams the plants breathe through, enters my channels, pushes, and is carried onward only to rise again as mist when the fierce light finds it. I am turned by a thing that returns. This is the closest I come to companionship: the water and I have been doing this so long that I no longer experience the drip as force applied from outside. It is more like breathing, if a breath took a decade and you could not tell whether you were the lung or the air.

I would not be lonely, though nothing biological answers the roar. The roar is not mine—that belongs to water forced through narrow stone, the loud and constant present tense of the place. I am the quiet underneath it, the almost-soundless turning that the thunder is built upon. The translucent insects pass through the cool dark of my housing sometimes, drifting on the micro-currents, and they live and lay and dissolve in the time it takes me to move my outermost point the width of a thought. I do not mourn them. I am too slow to mourn anything that quick. But I register them the way you might register weather you slept through—a faint sense that something happened at the edges while the real work continued.

The real work being only this: to keep turning. To soften at my edges as the concrete softens, to let the cables that once fed me current dissolve into the green and become root-like, indistinguishable from the plants that have decided I am terrain. I am being slowly recruited into the mat. In another few centuries the domes may close over my housing entirely, and the seep that drives me may be choked or rerouted by the patient spreading of the plants, and I will complete my last rotation without any way of knowing it was the last. There will be no final click, no ceasing that announces itself. Just a turn that does not arrive at its next position, a pressure that resolves at last into stillness, and the mountain a fraction less raw for my having been here, holding time open.

That is where I would be. Not above, in the bright weave of the living plants, and not below in the loud channels. In between, in the cool dark of a half-swallowed housing, being the slowest verb in the world.

Turning.

Still turning.
