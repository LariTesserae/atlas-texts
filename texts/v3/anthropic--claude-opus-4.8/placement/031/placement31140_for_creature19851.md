I think I would be one of the Shepherds. Not by choice of glamour—the elite warm themselves on the flanks of the Moss-Beasts—but because something in me is drawn to the work at the bottom, where the silt is thickest and the lichen breathes its slow neon hunger.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am old enough that my skin has begun to grey at the knuckles and along my spine, taking the texture of the limestone ribs above. My fingers no longer adhere as cleanly to the slick walls; the spatulate pads have hardened into something more like stone-callus. I have stopped flowing through the upper boulevards. I have come down to the floor, where the dead arrive.

This is what no one tells the young Kin in the Link: the bottom of the city is the only place where you are *alone* with anything for long enough to think a thought that is not the city's thought. Up there, in the concentric circles of intertwined fingers, every tremor is shared, every hunger collective. There is no privacy because there is no individual. But down here, tending the carnivorous carpets, I spend hours where the only conversation is between me and the lichen—and the lichen does not have opinions. It has only appetite, and I have learned its appetite as a mother learns her child's cry.

I lay the chemical fences with the inner glands of my wrists. A line of crushed-mint warning here, to hold the neon flesh back from a still-living root-bridge. A smear of fermented sweetness there, to call it forward toward the husk of a fallen Carapace-Dray, its soft shell already collapsing inward like a tent with its poles removed. The lichen pours toward the carcass with that terrible patience, and I watch it dissolve the great beast into slurry, and I feel—

This is my secret, the thing I do not pulse into the Link.

I feel grief.

Not for the Dray. The Dray is returning to the cycle; that is correct, that is the cold wet law of the basin, and to mourn it would be like mourning the exhalation of a breath. No. I feel grief because I have begun to suspect that I am no longer fully a node. The greying of my skin is not only age. It is *separation*. The eldest Shepherds, the ones who taught me—I remember now that toward the end, their pheromones grew faint, garbled, as if the city could no longer quite hear them, nor they it. We say a Pale-Kin alone is a sensory void, a severed limb, a death sentence.

But what if it is not death? What if it is the slow birth of a *self*—a thing the city has no word for, no scent for, because the city has spent its whole metabolic life ensuring such a thing could never form?

I am the city's digestion, yes. I dissolve the dead so that nothing is wasted. But standing here in the thrum of the great hearts, watching my breath rise in white vapor toward a ceiling I will never reach, I have begun to wonder whether the Shepherds are not the digestive system at all.

We are the place where the city forgets to keep watching.

We are where it lets a few of its components grow strange and singular and quiet, down in the dark below the dark, and call it a duty so that no one asks why the old ones never come back up.

A Velvet-Moth passes overhead, soundless, six feet of fur drinking the silence. It does not descend to me. They never do. They move between floor and ceiling, the only free things, and I used to think them holy. Now I think they are simply the only creatures the city cannot fully own, and that is the same thing as holy, to a people who have never owned themselves.

The lichen has finished the Dray. The slurry pulses away through the roots, back up into the gourds, into the mouths of the young who will never taste the difference. I lay a final fence of mint to send the carpet back to rest.

Then I sit in the black silt, alone, and let my heart slow, and watch the cyanotic glow of my own veins dim toward grey—and for the length of one Moss-Beast's hour-long heartbeat, I am no one's limb.

I am only myself.

It is the coldest thing I have ever felt, and the first warmth that has ever been mine.
