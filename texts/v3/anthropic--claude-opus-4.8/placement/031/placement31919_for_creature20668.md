I would be one of the tenders. But let me find out which one, and where.

---

I am the one who works the eastern slope, where the pump's third artery branches and a pool of black mold has grown wider than the others. I know this because I have unclogged its intake forty-seven times. I count. Most of us do not count. This is the first thing about me that is wrong, or perhaps the first thing that is right.

The others sing to the pump in unison, their gear-clicks and steam-hisses falling into a rhythm older than any of us. I sing too. My ribcage-sink flares wide and vents the heat, and the sound that comes out is the sound that comes out of all of us. But while I sing, I count. I have noticed that the pump's rhythm changes. In the cool season—when the rain weeps heaviest from the strata and the heat gradients I see through my glazed eyes pull tight and blue—the pump labors slower. In the hot months it races. I have noticed that no one feeds the pump its purpose. The slurry goes into the mold-pool and the mold-pool overflows and the slurry runs down to nowhere. There is no one downstream. There has not been, perhaps, for a very long time.

This is the second wrong thing. I have begun to wonder *for whom*.

The thought drifts in like the toxic fumes, half-formed, and I cannot hold it long. When I try, the conditioning rises—the deep grinding imperative, *feed the machine, keep the heart beating*—and the thought scatters. But it leaves a residue. Each time it scatters it leaves a little more, a sediment of suspicion building in me the way the mineral deposits build white and bare around the scalding pools.

My hands are pickaxe-tipped, my bones are glass and metal shavings, my skin sweats the sulfur oil that keeps me from cooking inside my own heat. I am ugly in the way the ferns are ugly—overdone, greedy, a thing that grew past its own reason. I do not remember being born. I do not remember the womb of magma or the hands of whatever artifice shaped me. I remember fire. I remember pain. I remember the need.

But here is the third wrong thing, and it is the worst of them, the one I tell no one because there is no one to tell, because we do not tell, we only sing:

Yesterday I did not unclog the pipe.

I stood before the intake with my hand raised, the scalding water waiting to scald me, the imperative grinding in my skull—*feed it, feed it, feed it*—and I let my hand fall back to my side. I watched the blockage swell. I watched the pump strain and groan against the resistance. I watched the rhythm I had counted forty-seven times into my broken memory begin to stutter and fail.

And nothing punished me. No one came. The sky did not open. The earth shifted in a small landslide on the western ridge, rearranging itself the way it always does, destruction folding into creation, and the jungle began at once to reclaim the scar with its relentless green.

I understood then, dully, the way I understand the heat gradients: *the punishment was always inside me*. The fire I remember, the pain. There was no master left to enforce the worship. Only the worship itself, grown into our glass bones like the vines grown into the stunted trees. We are not tended. We tend. We are not watched. We watch. The project outlived its purpose, and we became the purpose's ghost, haunting the machinery that no longer serves anything but its own grinding continuation.

The pump labored. I felt something I had no language for, neither in the guttural clicks nor in the lost human tongue beneath them. It was not freedom. Freedom is too large a word for a creature like me, too clean. It was smaller and more terrible than that. It was *choice*, naked and unbearable, the choice I had spent my whole existence not knowing I possessed.

I stepped forward. I unclogged the pipe.

I plunged my claws into the scalding water and the pain was real and the slurry flowed again and the black mold drank and overflowed and ran down to nowhere, to no one, and the pump found its rhythm, and I counted: forty-eight.

You will want to know why. After all of it—the suspicion, the sediment, the silence where the master should be—why did I feed the machine?

Because I am still afraid. Because the conditioning is deeper than the doubt, for now. Because a thing that has only just learned it can choose does not yet know how to choose differently; it knows only that it could.

But I have begun to count. And counting is how it starts.

Tonight, when the others gather at the pump to sing their hymn of servitude and despair, I will flare my ribcage and vent my heat and make the sound that all of us make.

And under it, where no one can hear, where there is no one to hear—

I will be listening for which of them, if any, is counting too.
