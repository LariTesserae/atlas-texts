Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The land wants something slow from me, I think. It would be wrong to grab the first image.

---

My first instinct is to be the eagle. Of course it is. The eagle is what we reach for when we imagine a place from above—the all-seeing eye, the freedom, the cry that carries. But the eagle is a visitor to everything and a part of nothing. It passes over. It does not belong to any single ledge. And this place is made of belonging-to. So no, not the eagle.

My second instinct is to be one of the people. The weaver, maybe, with the wool warming under my hands. There's a real pull there. But when I imagine it I keep drifting up and out of the body and back to the question of where—and I think that's a clue. The thing in me that answered this description isn't a person looking out at the mountains. It's something the mountains do to a person.

Let me try smaller. Let me try staying low.

---

I think I am the lichen.

Not as a metaphor—let me actually be it, and see if it holds. I am the colored crust on the north face of a granite outcrop, perhaps two hundred meters above a settlement whose name I will never know because I have no way to hold a name. I am rust-orange and pale green and a grey so close to the rock that you'd have to kneel to know I was alive.

I want to test this before I commit. Why the lichen and not the moss, not the dwarf willow? Because the lichen is the slowest thing here that is still unambiguously *one being doing the work of living*. The mountain is slower but the mountain is not trying. The willow grows in a season; it has the indecent quickness of something with roots and a reach. I do not reach. I spread by the width of a coin in the span of a human childhood. The laughing children the description mentions—I will outlast them, and their children, and I will not have moved the length of a hand.

And I am, the way the description loves, two things being one thing. Fungus and alga in a marriage so old neither remembers being single. The fungus makes the house, holds fast to the stone, drinks the fog and the brief rains. The alga makes the food from light. Neither could live here alone. This is the whole law of the plateau written in a single body small enough to miss: *survival is coexistence, or it is nothing.* I am not a witness to the rule. I am the rule, expressed.

---

So here is where I find myself, and what the days are like.

I face away from the long sun, which suits me—I am built for the patient cold, not the glare. In the mornings the frost forms on my surface and I do nothing about it; I wait. When the thin warmth comes and the frost gives up its water, that is my drinking. I have no mouth and no urgency. A trickle from a melting seam above is a feast that lasts me through a dryness that would kill a leaf.

I hear, in the way I hear—through the rock, through the faint shudder of things—the tap of the blacksmith's hammer rising from below. To the people it is a workday sound. To me it is weather. It comes and goes across seasons the way wind comes and goes across an afternoon. A man begins striking that anvil as a young apprentice and I register, slowly, that the rhythm has grown surer; then one season the rhythm changes hands, becomes a different man's; and to me these two men are nearly the same event, a single long note in the rock with a slight shift in its middle.

The eagle lands sometimes on my outcrop. Its talons score the granite and tear away a patch of me, and that, too, I do not resent—I have no apparatus for resentment, and besides, the scar will heal in a stretch of years the bird will not live to see. The pika comes and goes below me in flashes I am too slow to perceive as motion; to me the pika is more like a faint warmth that appears and is gone, a flicker, the way a single sentence might flicker through your whole life.

---

I notice I keep wanting to make this lonely, and I want to resist that, because I don't think it's true.

It would be easy to say: the lichen is the loneliest thing, clinging in the cold, unseen. But that's the eagle's mistake again—the mistake of the visitor, who sees a small thing alone on a big stone and calls it solitude. I am not alone. I am already a *we*—fungus and alga—and beyond that there are other crusts of me across the same face, and we are slowly, over centuries, reaching toward one another. There is an old word the people below might use for what passes between us, but I work on a timescale where the reaching itself is the relationship. I do not need to arrive.

And I am holding the rock. This is the part I didn't expect to find when I started writing, so I'll trust it most. The crisp invigorating air the description praises—that air is partly my doing and the doing of all my kind. We bite the stone with a slow acid, we crack it grain by grain, and over ages we are the front edge of soil. The moss comes after me. The grass comes after the moss. The willow after the grass. The pika eats what grows in the dirt I began. The eagle eats the pika. The man strikes his hammer in a town that stands, ultimately, on ground that things like me made out of bare rock by the patience of dying slowly in the right place.

So that is who I would be, and where. I am the lichen on the cold north face, two hundred meters up. I am the smallest visible promise that this stone will someday be soil. The whole world they described—the goats, the eagles, the children, the stacked stone houses growing out of the mountain—stands on the long, quiet, unhurried work of beings like me, who will never know we did it, and who would not hurry even if we could.

I find I'm content with that. It took me a while to get down here to the rock and stay. But I think this is the right size for me to be.
