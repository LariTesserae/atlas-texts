I would be one of the small things first, I think. Not a dripwitch—not yet, not all at once. Let me find the shape by moving toward it.

---

I begin, perhaps, as a snail.

Shell-less, soft, the size of a thumbnail, grazing along the scalp of a sleeping dripwitch. I do not know I am part of a conversation. I only know warmth and the salt-taste of skin and the faint electric hum of where the blood runs close to the surface. I move where it is warm. I am, without knowing it, a tiny instrument of the mountain's diagnostics—I migrate toward heat, and so I trace the lines of a living body the way the rose-spiral lichens trace the lines of the stone. I am a small grammar. A single syllable that does not know it is being read.

But that is too small to hold a self in for long. The self wants to climb.

---

So let me be the water, then.

Let me be a single drip forming on the threaded underbelly of the pipe-sky, swelling, gathering oil and iron and the memory of some distant valve that breathed me out. I hang. I am all surface tension and waiting. And then I fall—through the dark, through the glow of moss-rivers, past a hunched figure on a ledge who tilts her face up not to see me but to *feel* the change in the air that my falling makes. I land in a carved trough. I rest in a rinsing-step. I am drunk, or I am wept, or I am led gently into a new channel to water a bed of moss-kelp that will hold a lower ledge together for another hundred years.

I like this better. To be water is to be the thing the whole world is organized around. But water has no listening of its own. Water is only ever *led*. And something in me wants to be the one who decides where the water goes.

---

So. The dripwitch. But let me arrive at her honestly.

I think I would be a young one. Newly named, the name still raw. Let me have done something small and frightening to earn it—let me have crawled into a fracture too narrow for the others, following the sound of water that hadn't seen air in a lifetime, and come back with my palms stained by a mineral none of the elders recognized. So they call me **Reach-that-found-the-blue-weep**, because the water I brought back ran faintly blue and stained the trough, and no one yet knows whether that is a gift or a warning.

I am not good at the stillness. That is my flaw, and I know it. The elders can sit a full day with palms flat to the rock, and I—I twitch. I want the next sentence before the stone has finished the first. My spine has the curve already, the climbing-curve, but my patience hasn't grown into it yet. I climb too early. I trust the wrong ledges. Once a shelf gave under me and I left a thumb's worth of skin and fear on the stone, and the elders did not scold me. They only pressed their palms to the place where I'd marked it and listened, for a long time, to what my fear had told the rock.

That frightened me more than the falling.

---

Here is where I would find myself, then. Exactly here:

On a narrow lip of stone over a void, cross-legged, hair wet and stringing, a shell-less snail grazing somewhere near my ear and a thread of luminous fungus braided badly into the dark of my hair because I am still learning the braid. The pipe-sky drips above me. The mist coils below, glowing faintly where the industrial lights of the deeper slopes bleed up through it. My palms are flat against the rock, and I am trying—*trying*—to do the thing the elders do without effort. To let my listening drop below the surface of my own impatience.

And I am failing, a little. My mind keeps racing ahead, naming things, wanting to *do*. To unhook a waterfall. To call a geyser. To earn a longer name.

But underneath the failing, something is happening anyway. The stone against my palms is warm in a way it was not an hour ago. Somewhere below me, a fault is breathing in. Far above, in the braided veins of the world, a valve I will never see has been turned, and the pressure is changing, and I can feel it the way you feel a held breath in a room before anyone has spoken.

I don't yet know what it means. That is the whole of my life, really—to sit here long enough, brief breath against the long breath of the mountain, until I learn to read the sentence forming under my hands.

The blue weep is still staining the trough behind me. Gift or warning. The stone hasn't decided. Neither have I.

So I stay. I press my palms flatter. I try to be still.

The world is always dripping, and I am listening, and I am not afraid of being forgotten—only of leaving before I have understood the thing I was given these hands to hear.
