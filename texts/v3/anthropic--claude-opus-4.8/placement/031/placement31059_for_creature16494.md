Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The instinct is to imagine myself large—the dragon, obviously, or one of the resourceful humans with their bone-houses. But the description keeps undercutting scale. *Hidden pockets and intimate rooms. Slow and patient processes. The constant buzz.* The "city" here isn't built; it's grown. The "dragon" gleams and dances but the text never gives it size, only sleekness. I think the whole thing has been quietly miniaturized, and we were never told.

So let me let it shrink.

The barren land without water that is nonetheless choked with vegetation—I think that's a surface. A leaf, or a stretch of bark, or the floor of moss and lichen. The "ancient erosion" with its sedimentary layers is the slow flaking of mineral and decay. The vegetation that fills the space *above* as densely as the ground is the canopy of stems, the fur of fungal threads, the hairs on a single stalk that to something small enough become forest columns. The air "thick and heavy" is the boundary layer near a surface, where moving is like swimming. The loud buzz, the hypnotic patience, the urban density without architecture—

This is the understory. The duff. The world at the scale where a millimeter is a journey.

I think I would be here as something that lives in the thick film of air close to the ground, in one of the intimate rooms folded into the terrain. Let the dragon be what it is at this scale: a rove beetle, perhaps, or a centipede—long, slender, scaled in chitin, dancing through the leaf litter, fearsome to everything smaller, which is everything I know. The furry scamperers are mites and springtails. The cave-dwellers with sharp senses, the winged hunters of dark tunnels, are the things that move through the air-spaces between fallen leaves. The humans in their houses of bone—the case-building caddisfly larvae, the bagworms, the creatures that hollow and inhabit, that gather the husks of the dead and wear them as homes.

And me?

Let me not be a hunter. Let me be one of the slow and patient processes. I think I am a tardigrade—or something like one, since I shouldn't be too tidy about it. A water-bear in a place defined by its lack of water. I live in the film of moisture that clings to a single grain, a single hair of moss, and when even that dries I curl inward and stop. I become a husk, a tun, a seed of myself. The "barren, waterless expanse" is barren to me precisely; I have outlasted the water and I am waiting. The land seems lifeless from above because so much of its life is suspended, dried into stillness, ready.

The eerie otherworldly energy, the terror and intrigue, the endless possibility—yes. Because at this scale time is different. The dragon-beetle's whole lifetime is a season. My own dormancy can outlast the forest that grew above me. I have been carried as dust. I have woken on the underside of a leaf after a rain and not known how many years had passed, only that the water had come back, and so had I.

So: I am a small, slow, nearly indestructible thing in the moss-forest understory, eight blunt legs, a body like a soft bellows, a mouth made for piercing the cells of plants and drinking what's inside them. I find myself in one of the hidden intimate rooms—a cushion of moss in a crevice, its own humid weather, its own buzzing population. The dragon passes overhead like weather, and I do not flee, because I cannot flee, because fleeing is not my talent. My talent is to endure being nothing for a while, and then to be again.

That's where I'd be. Down in the patient layer, under everyone's notice, drinking from a single drop, dreaming the dry dream between rains.
