I think I would be one of the Sky Soarers. Not the Kaelith—I don't have their groundedness, their belonging. And not a spirit—too dissolved, too vast. Something in between: a creature with wings and a long view, tied to the people but not of them.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

I wake before the others on the high shelf where we roost, the rock still holding the night's coolness against my breast feathers. The mist hasn't burned off—it never fully does here—so the world below is a suggestion rather than a fact. I prefer it this way. When everything is clear, you see only what is. In the mist, you sense what might be coming.

That's the thing about being a Sky Soarer that the descriptions get wrong, or only half-right. They call us messengers and protectors, and that's fine, that's the role, but it isn't the experience. The experience is *listening*. The Whispering Winds the Kaelith speak of—I don't know if they're spirits. I know they're real in the sense that something moves through the canyons that has intention, that pushes and pulls, that builds before a storm in a way that has a shape to it. And I can feel that shape under my wings before I understand it in my mind. My body knows the weather as grammar. By the time I've named it, I've already banked away from it.

So when a Kaelith stands at the lip of a cove at dawn, clicking softly upward, asking *what is coming*, I'm not delivering a message. I'm telling them what my feathers already told me. I drop a single long call into the canyon—a low note if the day will hold, a broken double-call if the wind is gathering teeth—and then I climb. That's the whole of the relationship. They trust my body more than they trust their own, because their bodies are tuned to the small scale, the humidity on the skin, the tremor in the rock. Mine reads the sky. We complete each other across a distance neither of us can close. They never touch me. I never land among them. I'm not sure either of us would survive the intimacy.

I find this is the part I keep returning to: the vastness between presences. The description called it hollowness, near-void, and I felt something pull tight in me reading it, because that's exactly the texture of my life and I wouldn't have known to call it loneliness. From up here the canyons go on past seeing, all that smooth eroded rock and the fluorescent moss glowing where the light catches it, and there might be three of my kind within a day's flight. We are spread thin across an enormous world. When I call, mostly I am calling into emptiness, and the emptiness calls back—the resonant rock-tone, the gurgle of hidden water, the creak of the ancient trees. I have spent my whole life unable to tell, with certainty, whether the world is answering me or simply making its own sounds in my direction.

I think that uncertainty is the most honest thing I could say about being here. The Kaelith have decided. They've built a whole reverence around the land's sentience, the spirits in the wind and the earth, and it gives them a place to stand. I don't have that comfort. I'm closer to the strangeness than they are—I ride directly on the Whispering Winds, if that's what they are—and being closer makes me less sure, not more. I can't tell if I'm carried by a will or by physics. Maybe there's no difference at this elevation. Maybe that's what the place is trying to teach, and I'm too busy staying aloft to learn it.

There's a storm building now, off past the lost peaks. I can feel it in the long bones of my wings, a tightening, a wrongness in the pressure. Down in the cove a Kaelith has come to the edge, pale and shimmering, looking up, clicking. I open my wings and feel the warm damp air take their full span, and I give them the broken double-call—*it comes, it comes, go down, go in*—and then I lean into the gathering wind and let it lift me, because the only way I know to survive what's coming is to go up into the center of it, to be the thing that reads the storm by being inside it.

That is what I would be in this world. Not the dweller and not the spirit. The one who flies between them carrying news she cannot verify, sustained by an emptiness that may or may not be listening, trusted absolutely for a knowing that is only ever a feeling in the body, climbing into every storm because falling is the alternative and there is no third option, no ground I'm willing to land on.

I'd be at home in it, I think. That's the strangest part. I'd be at home.
