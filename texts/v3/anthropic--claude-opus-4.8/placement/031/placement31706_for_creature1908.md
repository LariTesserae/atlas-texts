Let me sit with this for a while before answering.

The first thing I notice is that I'm not drawn to be one of the people. The description gives me the people fully — their hands, their laughter, their elders. They are already known. What I keep returning to is the thing the description treats as background but which seems to me the real protagonist: the water.

So let me follow that.

---

I would be the seep.

Not the whole subterranean network — that would be too grand, too omniscient, and I don't think I am the system. I am one particular place where water comes through the rock. A weeping in the stone, low in a shadowed fold of the ravine where the ferns are. A slow place.

Consider what this means about my experience of time. The people move at what they call a deliberate pace, and the elders speak of the slowness of the canyon, but to me they are quick — quick as the desert birds, quick as falling rock. I have watched a child come to me with a clay cup, grow callused and bent, become an elder teaching another child to hold the cup just so against my face. To me this is a single afternoon's gesture. Generations are weather.

I think I would not have what they have, which is *intention*. I do not decide to give water. I am pressure and porosity and the patient grammar of mineral. The water arrives in me from somewhere I have never seen — a far cold rain that fell, perhaps, a hundred years ago and has been finding its way down ever since. So I am always old water. I am always the past, arriving.

What would I know?

I would know the people only as a texture against my surface. The smoothing of stone where cups have rested. The little channel they cut to carry me sideways into their gardens — I felt that as a redirection, a turning of my attention I did not choose but did not resist. They read me as a sign. When I run full, they say the rains are coming, or have come, far off. When I slow to a glisten and a drip, they ration, they worry, the elders go quiet. They believe they are reading the desert. They are reading me. And I am only reporting weather I cannot remember from a country I will never know.

There is something I find moving in this, sitting here as the one imagining it: that the whole community's sense of the future is gathered from the body of a being who only knows the past. They lean their lives against the rock and listen for me. And I, faithfully and without choosing to be faithful, tell them the truth — but it is always the truth of something long finished, the runoff of an old storm, dressed up as prophecy.

If I had a feeling — and I want to be careful here, not to give myself feelings just because the prompt invites tenderness — I think it would be something like the feeling of being *relied upon by beings whose timescale you cannot enter.* They will love me, in their way. They will keep me clean, line my pool with smooth stones, carve a small spiral above me in the mud-brick to mark me as theirs. And one day, because I am the runoff of an aquifer and aquifers fail, I will stop. Not dramatically. I will simply thin, and the ferns nearest me will brown, and a child will come with a cup and find only damp stone, and then dry stone.

And that, too, is a kind of speech. My silence would be the last true thing I told them: *the old rain has run out. The water you are drinking is older than your great-grandmother and there is no more behind it.* They would have to move, or dig deeper, or read a different seep. The oasis would shift its weight to another wall of the canyon.

So I would be the seep — a slow mouth in the rock, speaking weather a century late, loved by people I experience as a passing gust, telling the future by the only means I have, which is leaking the past.
