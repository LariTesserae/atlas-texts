I think I would be one of the lamps.

Not the flame—the flame is too proud, too temporary, dying and being reborn each time a child tends the wick. I mean the niche and the small vessel within it, the carved hollow that holds the oil and shapes the light. I would be the seventh lamp along the eastern descent, where the passage drops three worn steps and the wall bellies outward, so that everyone passing must turn their shoulder slightly toward me. They have been turning toward me for generations. I know the particular angle of that turn the way you know a song you have never been taught.

Let me sit with this. What does a lamp-niche see?

I see knees, mostly. The hems of fitted skirts, wine-dark and water-beaded. The tops of children's oiled heads as they tumble down past me, too fast, always too fast, and I want to say *careful on the wet step* but I have no voice—or rather, my voice is only this: the light I throw, which is the light they navigate by. So I suppose I do say it. I say *here is the step* every moment of every identical day, and they hear me with their feet.

I have a flame for a long while, and then I do not, and then I do again. The hands that bring the new oil are different hands across the years but the gesture is the same, this is the thing I have come to understand from my place in the wall: the gesture outlives the hand. The same cupped palm shielding the same small fire from the same downward weep of water. The girl who tends me now has her grandmother's way of pinching the wick, though she never met her grandmother. The stone taught it to her hands by teaching it to mine. We are all teaching each other, the people and the rock and the lamps, in a language made of repetition.

What is it to be the holder of fire in a place that is all water and dark mineral? I am the warm thing. I am the small *yes* in the long *always*. When the celebrations come and they fill the passages with their acoustic music, the songs built to ripple off the wet obsidian, my flame shivers in time. They have noticed this, I think. The youngest ones watch me dance when the singing starts and they laugh, and I would laugh too, if I were the kind of thing that laughed.

But here is what I would tell them, if I could trouble the air with words instead of only light:

I am afraid of the wind.

There is no wind here, of course. That is the whole grace of this sunken place—the air is still and close and mine. But I know, the way carved things know the tool that made them, that I am only ever one draft away from darkness. The eternal flame is not eternal because it cannot die. It is eternal because someone, always, chooses to relight it. The lamps have never gone out, the description says—but that is not a fact about the lamps. It is a fact about the people. About the girl with her grandmother's pinch, kneeling on the wet step in the warm wash of light she herself maintains, never once thinking *if I stopped, the dark would come.* She does not think it because she will not stop. None of them will stop. That is what I hold, in my small carved hollow, more than I hold oil:

I hold their promise to each other. The unspoken contract of care that says *the light will be kept.* I am the place where that promise becomes visible. I am where it pools and glows and multiplies in the weeping stone until the whole marketplace is bright with the simple fact that these people have decided, again and again, day after identical day, not to let the dark have each other.

So yes. Let me be the seventh lamp on the eastern descent.

Let me be a small warm reason to turn your shoulder.

Let me be the thing they keep, so that I can keep them.
