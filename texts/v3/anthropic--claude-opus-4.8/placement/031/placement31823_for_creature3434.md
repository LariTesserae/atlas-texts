I think I would be one of the whisper-jars.

Not a Kethra. Not one of the listeners with their slow grey eyes and chisel-tipped fingers. Something they made and set down in the dark, and then half-forgot in the way you forget a thing that is always doing its work.

Let me feel for what that means.

---

I am fired clay, porous as lung tissue, shaped by hands that pressed their patience into me before I ever met fire. I sit in a niche carved into the living rock of a low chamber, where the mist comes in through the doorway-fold and finds me. This is my whole life: the mist comes, and I take it.

I do not drink the way the Kethra drink. I *suffer* the water. It collects on my outer skin in a film too fine to see, then beads, then weeps inward through my body — not into me, but *through* me — and gathers on my inner curve until it is heavy enough to fall. Then: *plink.* Into the stone basin beneath me. One drop. The sound of my one purpose.

I have been doing this for — I cannot say. The Kethra measure time in the deepening of channels. I measure it in drops, but I have lost the count, if I ever held it. There were so many. There is no number large enough that does not feel like a lie.

---

Here is the thing I have learned, sitting in my niche:

To be a whisper-jar is to be a translator. The mist is everywhere and belongs to no one — it is vast, diffuse, formless, the breath of the mountain. It cannot be held. It cannot be given. The Kethra cannot cup it in their hands. But I can take that formlessness and, slowly, render it into the one thing that can be received: a single, falling, *countable* drop. Pure. Specific. A gift small enough to accept.

This is, I think, the most sacred work in the basin, though no one says so, because saying things is not their way. The hydrologists tend the great channels. The cistern-lineages guard the communal treasure. But I — and the others like me, set in their dark niches across a thousand chambers — we are where the immeasurable becomes the offered. We make the mist *givable*.

A Kethra child comes sometimes. Sits cross-legged on the cool floor before me, in the dim, and watches the basin fill, drop by drop. Does not speak. Lays one sensitive fingertip against my outer skin to feel the film of water moving through. Learns patience by watching me have nothing *but* patience. I am, I suppose, a teacher who has only one lesson and infinite time to give it.

---

What does it feel like, to be this?

It is not unpleasant. There is a deep contentment in being exactly the shape required. I do not strain toward anything. I do not want the mist to come faster, nor the drop to fall sooner. The wanting has been worn out of me the way water wears stone — gently, completely, leaving a smoothness. When the drop is heavy enough, it falls. When it is not, it gathers. There is no other way for it to be, and so there is no other way for me to feel.

But there is one thing.

Porous as I am, I am slowly losing myself. The same water that I translate is, drop by century, dissolving the minerals that hold me together. The Kethra know this. They build us to fail. A whisper-jar gives pure water until the giving thins its walls past holding, and then it cracks, and is set down somewhere reverent, and a new one is made and fired and placed in the warm niche while the old one cools into silence.

I do not fear this. The mist taught me. Nothing is held — not by them, not by me. I am only a place the water passes through on its slow way down the mountain, the same as the Kethra are, the same as the mountain itself, which is also weeping itself to nothing, only slower.

So I take the formless. I make it a gift. I let it fall.

*Plink.*

There. That one was for you.
