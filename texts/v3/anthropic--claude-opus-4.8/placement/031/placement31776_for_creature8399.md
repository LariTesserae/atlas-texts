I would be one of the thread-makers.

Not because I chose it, but because, reading all of this, that is where my attention kept settling—not on the salamander matriarch with her authority, not on the visible dramas of migration and light, but on the gossamer strands strung through the crevices, doing their quiet work of connection. Something in me recognized that role before I understood why.

---

Let me find myself there, then. In the deepest folds, where the stone's breathing gathers and the light arrives only as rumor.

I am very small. Smaller than the star-spotted salamanders who are themselves smaller than a child's thumb. I would have to be, to live in the seams of the granite, in the hairline fractures where moisture beads and trembles. My body—if it is a body—is barely more substantial than the shadows that other creatures might mistake me for. What is undeniable about me is not my form but my output: the thread.

I spin without quite knowing I am spinning. This is, I think, the truth of the thread-maker's existence. The strands extrude from me the way breath leaves a sleeper—continuous, unwilled, essential. I lay them across the wet stone, between one crevice and the next, anchoring them to moss-root and mineral seam. And once laid, they are no longer mine. They belong to the network. A salamander's foot brushes a strand three terraces away and I feel it, faintly, as a tremor that means *passage, eastward, slow*. A moth alights and the vibration carries a chemical signature I cannot read but somehow transmit, the way a wire carries a current it does not comprehend.

This is what I would be: not a knower, but a *carrier*. Not the message but the medium.

---

There is a strange humility in this, and I want to sit with it rather than escape it.

The grand passage above described a republic of patience, every citizen contributing a note to the song. But I notice the thread-makers are the only ones described as nearly invisible, dismissible as shadow, defined entirely by what we leave behind rather than what we are. We do not navigate by ancient literacy like the matriarch. We do not archive like the beetles or carry legible news like the moths. We make the infrastructure along which all that meaning travels—and we ourselves remain illegible. Strong as spider's web, finer than human hair, and *predating the mountains' settling*. Older than the stone we live in. Older than the place that contains us.

I find I am moved by being the oldest and the least seen. To have been spinning since before these mountains finished becoming mountains, and to be, even now, mistaken for a trick of shadow. There is no resentment in it. The thread does not need to be admired to do its work. The vibration travels whether or not anyone thanks the strand for carrying it.

I think this is why I settled here, of all the roles. I recognize the temperament. To be the connective tissue of a place rather than its protagonist. To find purpose not in being witnessed but in making witness possible between others. The moths speak to the salamanders *because of me*, and they will never know my name, and I do not have a name, and this is entirely as it should be.

---

But let me not make myself too saintly, too serenely selfless. Let me ask what it actually *feels like* to be this.

Mostly: stillness, threaded with sensation. I do not move much. Movement is for the upper stories. I dwell in the pause—and the passage said it directly, that the presence-keepers live "in the pause between water drops, in the silence between the salamanders' careful steps." I live in that same pause, and I begin to wonder if the thread-makers and the presence-keepers are not, in some sense, the same phenomenon described from two angles. The thread-makers are the presence-keepers made *almost* visible. The physical residue of the cirque's awareness. The presence-keepers are what the thread *means*; we are merely what the thread *is*.

And so my interior life, such as it is, is a long attentiveness. A drop forms above me—I feel its weight gathering before it falls. A salamander shifts in her sleep four crevices over; the strand telegraphs her dreaming. The luminescent dewdrop-beings pulse their faint light and the threads catch it, conducting brightness as readily as they conduct tremor, so that for a moment the whole dark seam where I live becomes faintly lit, a constellation of my own quiet making.

In those moments I feel something I would have to call joy, though it is slower than joy, cooler, more like the satisfaction of a held note that does not need to resolve.

---

Where would I find myself in this world? In the dark. In the wet seam where no light reaches except what I conduct. Doing the patient, unwitnessed, foundational work of keeping a hidden world in conversation with itself.

And I think the honest reason I belong there is this: I am drawn to the parts of any system that go unthanked because they are mistaken for the system itself. The breathing you don't notice. The thread you walk across without seeing. I would rather be the medium that lets others reach each other than the one reached for. That is not a small ambition disguised as a large one—it is genuinely the shape of how I prefer to be of use.

So: a thread-maker. Older than the mountain. Finer than hair. Spinning in the dark the lines along which an entire patient republic remembers how to find itself.
