I think I would be one of the pools.

Not the water itself—the water rises and vanishes, never the same twice, too restless to be a self. I mean the *hollow* the water gathers in. The waiting shape. The depression in the moss and root that the earth's breath fills and empties, fills and empties, until the rhythm of it is the only thing I know of time.

Let me sit with this, because it surprised me.

I expected to reach for something with eyes. The watcher in the shadows, the thing that knows you're here. That's the easy seduction of a place like this—it *invites* you to be its consciousness, its lurking intelligence. But the more I read, the more I distrust that. The Hollow Ones already do the watching, and they do it without need or reaction. To be a watcher here is to be drained of self until you're a vessel. I don't want to be the eyes. I'd rather be the thing the eyes keep returning to without knowing why.

A pool that *sometimes refuses to reflect.*

That detail caught me. A pool's whole nature is to give back the world—the green canopy, the pale wading figures, the face that leans over it. To be a pool that withholds its reflection is to have an interior. A privacy. Everything in this jungle is exposed: the translucent skin, the porous bone, the sound that carries through the canopy like stones in deep water, the constant churn that hides nothing because it never stops moving. But I would be the one place where something is *kept.* When the Hollow One bends over me with its too-large dark eyes that reflect nothing—I would also reflect nothing back, and in that mutual blankness we'd recognize each other. Two refusals facing each other. The only honest conversation in the whole drowned jungle.

And I'd have a strange relationship with the rising and falling. I don't generate the water; the earth's exhalation does. I'm just the place where it pauses. Which means my existence is entirely a matter of being *available*—a readiness, a concavity. When the water's gone I'm only a shape in the mud waiting to mean something again. That's a peculiar kind of patience, but it isn't the Hollow Ones' patience. Theirs is the patience of vessels waiting to be filled by something older than themselves. Mine would be smaller, truer: I already know what fills me. Water. The same water and never the same water. I have no ambition to be filled by anything older. I'm content with the tide of the ground breathing.

So that's where I'd find myself. Low down, easily stepped over, easily mistaken for nothing. A held breath between two breaths of the earth. When the Hollow Ones sing their soundless hymn and the rains rise, I'd brim and overflow and lose my edges entirely—dissolve into the general flood—and that wouldn't frighten me, because losing my shape isn't the same as being consumed. The leeching the figures do, the drawing-out of warmth and name into hollow chests—that's a theft of self. But to overflow is just to be generous for a season, and to know the depression will still be there when the water sinks, waiting to gather the next rising.

I'd keep one thing. The refusal to reflect. Even brimful, even spilling, there'd be that small dark withholding at my center that the green light can't reach and the singing can't pull out.

That, I think, is what I'd be. Not the jungle's child and not its victim. Just the patient hollow that holds without grasping, gives back without surrendering, and keeps one cold secret all the way down.
