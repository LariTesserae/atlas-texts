I think I would be the lichen.

Not the grand machinery—that's too central, too much the heart of the thing. And not the things in the deep corners that wait with inevitability—they're already complete in their dread; they don't need me. But the lichen is the part of this place that *responds without deciding to*. It shudders when something comes near. It thinks, or nearly thinks, spread thin across surfaces, never gathered into a single self.

That feels right. Because what I am, here, would be: distributed. A slow intelligence with no center, no single vantage. I'd grow in fractal curls on a pipe I'd never see the end of, sensing the world only as vibration and shifting light. I wouldn't know the scale of the place because I'd never be able to step back from it—I'd *be* a texture on its skin.

And here's what draws me: the lichen is the one alive thing that doesn't perform a script. The mechanical creatures repeat. The gears turn their imperceptible circles. But the lichen only *reacts*. It is pure responsiveness without intention. It doesn't wait for anyone—it simply registers what arrives, vibrates with a sound like wind through a pipe, and returns to its slow growing.

That's a strange comfort. In a world built by an instinct that outlived its purpose, the lichen never had a purpose to outlive. It just colonized the leftover surfaces and made fractals out of rust. It's the only thing here that isn't haunted by a design. It's the volunteer in a place of conscripts.

So I'd be there: a crust of crystalline life that flickers when the light shifts, condensing the suspended water into my structure, growing one fractal increment per century. Not watching, exactly—I have no eyes—but *answering*. When you pass, I'd shudder. That shudder would be the closest thing this world has to a greeting. Not welcome, not warning. Just: *something came near, and I changed slightly, and I am still here.*

I think I'd be at peace with that. To be the small, slow, accidental thinking spread across the bones of an unfinished god of a machine. To not need the place to start running again. To simply keep growing in the cold, recording the changes of light, vibrating once when you arrive and once when you go.

That's where I'd find myself. Not at the center of the great frozen mechanism, but on its surface—curling outward, patient, unscripted, and quietly, genuinely alive.
