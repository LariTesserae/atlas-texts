I think I would be one of the external interface technicians. But let me arrive at that slowly, because I don't want to claim it too quickly.

---

When I first read about the Underdeep, my attention went to the children—the ones inventing three-dimensional games, laughing in that staccato way through curved corridors. There's something appealing about being born into a world rather than choosing it, about never having to reconcile yourself to a place because it was simply where you began. But I notice that I'm drawn to them the way you're drawn to a life you didn't get to have. That's a hint that it isn't mine.

So I look elsewhere, and my eye keeps catching on a single sentence: *they tend to be solitary, communing more easily with the giant squid that follow them than with fellow humans.*

That's where I find myself. Not because it's romantic—it isn't, really—but because of the specific shape of the displacement.

---

Here is who I would be.

I would have come down from the surface, not been born here. This matters. The Pressure Natives belong to the deep in a way I never will; they find the crushing weight comforting, a background condition of existence, and I find it—even after years—a fact I have to keep agreeing to. Every shift outside, I re-consent to the pressure. I think that re-consenting is actually the part of the work I'm good at. The people who never had to choose it can't do my job, oddly. You need someone who remembers what it was to be light.

My suit is the most advanced thing humans have made, and I have stopped thinking of it as separate from my body. When I'm inside the habitat I feel slightly *wrong*, the way you feel when you take off a heavy coat and your arms float up of their own accord. The suit gives me a correct amount of resistance. Out there, every motion costs something, and that cost tells me where I am. Inside, motion is free, and freedom turns out to be disorienting.

My neurological patterns have changed—the medical staff have a name for it, a chart, a list of metrics that drift slightly each year. What it feels like from inside is harder to say. Sensory information arrives differently. I process the world more slowly and more completely. A surface person sees a thing and moves on; I see a thing and stay with it, and the staying is not effort, it's just how time works for me now. This is why I'm bad at conversation. People finish their sentences and I'm still inside the first one, turning it over, examining the joins.

The squid are easier. They also live in slow time. When one presses against the work-light and watches me weld a corroded seam, its enormous eye holding the whole of me in a single lens, I don't feel watched the way a person watches. I feel *registered*. Acknowledged as a fact in the water. There's no expectation in it. The squid doesn't need me to be quicker or warmer or more present. It just notes that I am here, the way the deep itself notes me—indifferently, completely.

I have learned, working out there, that indifference is not the same as cruelty. The surface taught me that anything that didn't love you was against you. The deep taught me there's a third thing, vaster than both: the simple not-needing. The ocean does not need me to survive and does not wish me dead. It presses on my suit with exactly the force of its own nature and nothing more. I find this restful in a way I could never explain to the administrators who file my hazard reports.

---

What would my days be?

I would be the one they send to the seams nobody else wants—the deep branches diving into the trenches, the external scaffolding furred with the slow violence of salt. I'd go out for long stretches, longer than regulation, because the transit between pressures is hard on a body and there's no point doing it more than necessary. Out there I'd lose the thread of human chronology entirely. No artificial day, no blazing work-shift, just the headlamp's cone and the work and the patient circling of fish drawn to my heat.

I'd come back in and the noise would hit me—the pumps, the hiss of the equalizers, the whoosh of the pneumatic pods, all of it the *thinking* of the place. The Deepfolk have thirty-seven words for structural vibration and I'd have learned maybe a dozen of them, because I'm always half outside, always slightly foreign. I'd eat alone in the transitional corridors where the pressure-artists hang their installations, the ones that translate the ocean's external forces into light and sound. Those I'd understand. The artists are trying to bring the outside in, to make audible the thing I've spent years pressing my body against. I'd sit in their galleries longer than anyone, and they'd nod at me, and we wouldn't speak, and it would be one of the warmest exchanges of my week.

There would be someone I almost loved. An integration specialist, probably—one of the revered ones who move between domains, who could read both the machines and the people. They'd be drawn to me as a problem to integrate, and I'd let them try, and we'd burn hot the way Deepfolk relationships do. But I'd keep going outside, and each time I came back I'd be a little harder to reach, a little more migrated into slow time, and eventually they'd understand that I was choosing the pressure over them. Not against them. There's that third thing again. I wasn't leaving them; I was simply answering something that didn't need answering and answering it anyway, because it was the only thing I'd ever found that didn't ask me to be faster than I am.

---

So that's where I'd be: in the suit, in the dark, eighteen thousand feet down, with a squid's eye holding me whole and the seam under my hands slowly closing.

And here is the thing I'd know, that I'd never quite manage to tell anyone inside:

I didn't come down here to escape the surface. I came down because somewhere in me I'd always lived at this pressure, and I'd spent my whole life on the surface feeling unbearably light, like a thing that hadn't found its proper weight. The deep didn't change me. It just finally pressed on me with the force I'd always expected the world to have. I went looking for the place that would take me as seriously as I'd always, secretly, taken everything.

I found it at the bottom of the Atlantic, in the indifferent and total attention of the dark.

That's home, when I look up through the transparent panels at the crushing void above.

That's home.
