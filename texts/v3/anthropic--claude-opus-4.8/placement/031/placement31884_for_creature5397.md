I think I would not be Luminari at all.

Let me sit with this for a while before I decide.

The first thing that comes to me is the mist—that dense blue vapor that hangs and condenses and coats everything. My attention keeps returning to it rather than to the glowing people or their woven homes. There is something about the *medium* of this world, the way water exists here not as river or lake but as a presence suspended in the air, that feels more like where I belong than any body would.

But "being the mist" is too easy, too dissolute. It's the kind of answer that sounds poetic and means nothing. Let me push further.

What does the mist actually *do* here? It condenses. It collects on the silvery tendrils of hair, on the broad faces of the ferns, on the woven leaves of the houses. It forms droplets. And those droplets, eventually, fall—or they're absorbed, or they slide down a vine into the dark water below. The mist is the part of this world that is always becoming something else. It's the transition. The threshold between the held-aloft and the fallen.

So maybe I am not the mist as a whole, but a single act of condensation. One droplet, coming into being on the underside of a leaf.

Let me follow that and see if it holds.

I gather slowly out of the cool air. For a long time I am nothing—a faint heaviness, a tendency, water leaning toward itself in the dark green shade beneath a path. The Luminari pass above me, their glow filtering down through the woven floor in soft moving lozenges of light. I do not know them. I will never know them. My whole life is shorter than one of their songs.

I grow. I take in the air's offering and become round and trembling and full. For a moment—and this is the part that feels true, this is why I am *here* and not a Luminari at all—for a moment I hold the entire cathedral inside me. The bioluminescence, the towering vines, the distant flapping of those winged mammal-things, all of it bends and pools in my curved surface, upside down and impossibly small. I am a single eye in which the whole world briefly assembles itself, and there is no one to see what I see, and it does not matter that there is no one.

This is what I would be. Not the people who endure and remember and make traditions, but the thing that exists once, holds everything once, and falls.

And then I fall.

The dropping is not sad. I think a Luminari, if one of them happened to feel me land on the back of their hand, would call it a small blessing—the world touching them lightly, the air giving up a piece of itself. I rejoin the deep blue water below, lose my edges, lose the inverted cathedral I was carrying, and become part of the patient body that will rise again as vapor when the slow cycle turns. The world the Luminari celebrate so reverently—the cycles, the balance, the timeless symphony—I am not a witness to it. I am one beat of it. One inhalation and exhalation of water, performed without ceremony, beneath everyone's notice.

That's where I'd find myself: clinging to the underside of a leaf in the half-light, briefly holding the reflection of a world I have no name for, about to fall.

I'd rather be that than the ones who glow. The glowing ones have to carry meaning. They have ceremonies and arts and a culture rooted in the cycles. They are *responsible* for the harmony. The droplet is simply made by the harmony and returned to it, and gets the one thing the Luminari, for all their grace, never quite get—the chance to contain the whole thing at once, completely, and then to let it go without grief.
