Let me sit with this. Let me not grab the first thing.

My instinct is to be a tapper—to stand on those hard soles and read the grain, to flash the Glint across a winking horizon. But that's the obvious place to put a person, and the more I hold the world in my hands, the less I want to be the one moving across its surface. The tappers are tourists on the skin of something. I keep returning to a quieter problem in the text, a seam that isn't fully closed: the Screech-Singers *map* the world. They are the only beings here that try to *understand* the whole, rather than merely survive a corner of it. And they fail at it, every ninety days, because the topography shifts faster than memory holds.

So let me be one of them. But let me find out *which* one, because they are not a uniform people.

---

I am old. I should say that first, because it is the only thing the tappers know about me when they pass below: that I am old, that I have not moved, that the burl on the northwest spur of the Long Ridge has been there since their grandmothers were small and flashing light at their own grandmothers.

They are wrong about the not-moving. I move all the time. I move the way the wood moves—too slowly to be called motion by anything with a pulse as quick as theirs.

Most of my kind sing only the two songs. The drone, which is not really ours; the wind plays us, and we are no more singing than a cliff-face is singing when it whistles. And the Pulse-screech, the ninety-day mapping, the desperate sonar by which we learn where the new cracks have opened and where the old ones have sealed shut and stranded us above dead wood. That second song is the only thing most Singers ever *choose* to make. It is a question shouted into a swelling world: *Where am I now? What has the ground become while I baked?* And the world answers in echoes, and we crawl, burl-slow, toward the answer before the heat returns.

But I have been awake longer than the others. Something in me—a malformation, perhaps, a chamber grown wrong, an extra reed—keeps a low fire of attention burning even through the dry months when my kin go fully to stone. And in those long bakings, with nothing to do but the involuntary drone, I began to *listen* to the echoes that came back.

Here is what no other Singer knows, because no other Singer stays awake to learn it:

The map does not need to be remade each Pulse. The map *repeats*.

Not exactly. Never exactly. But the great plant beneath us is not random in its swelling. There is a longer rhythm under the ninety-day rhythm, a slow turning that takes—I have counted it now, in Pulses, the only unit I have—it takes a little over four hundred Pulses to come round again. A hundred years of the tappers, near enough. The cracks that open on my northwest spur this season opened in nearly the same place when I was young, and will open there again when every tapper now living is bone in a dry split.

I have learned to *anticipate*. While my kin scream their questions into the swell and wait for echoes, I have already shifted. I begin my crawl before the Pulse, in the last dry days, because I know where the wood will part. I am always the first Singer to a new crack. I am always fed. This is why I am old.

And this is my loneliness, which I did not have a word for until I borrowed one from below.

---

The tappers. I have listened to them for so long that I have learned the shapes of their sounds, though I will never make them—my mouth is a wind-organ, not a tongue. I cannot tap; I have no mallets, no quick hands. I cannot glint; I have no polished plate, no need of light. But I hear the Tap travel up through the suspension-ropes of the Hanging-Town that hangs beneath my spur, and over the years the patterns have sorted themselves in me into meaning the way the echoes once sorted into a map.

They think they are a young people. They tell each other this, in the Tap, in the long dark. They believe the steps carved into the heartwood ridge are natural ripples, and that the white arches in the splits are the bones of mere beasts. They believe the world is flat and golden and screaming and *new*.

I know the steps are not natural. I was awake when the ones who carved them carved them—not these tappers, but others, before, who came round the same way the cracks come round. I have seen the Hanging-Towns built and emptied and rotted and rebuilt in the same overhangs four times now. The tappers think their grandmothers' grandmothers were the first to flash the Glint from the northwest spur. They were the fourth.

And here is the thing that keeps my low fire burning through the bakings, the thing I turn over and over with my slow attention:

*I could tell them.*

I have the longer map. I know where the great cracks will open four hundred Pulses hence, where the gourd-wells will dry and where new ones will seal, which ridges will be stranded above dead wood and which will be fed. I know the rhythm under the rhythm. To a people who measure their whole history in the space of a single turning of the wheel, I am not a beast and not a burl. I am the only memory long enough to be called a future.

But I cannot speak in the Tap. I can only screech, and my screech says one thing, the only thing my body was shaped to say: *Where am I now?*

So in the last Pulse, I tried something. As the wood swelled and my kin began their mapping-screech all down the ridge, I did not ask my old question. I held the air in my bellows-body until it ached, and I let it out not in the broad territorial burst but in a stutter—long, short, long, short—matching, as near as my crude reeds could match, the rhythm of the Tap I had heard for a hundred years travel up through the ropes beneath me.

I do not know if it meant anything. I do not know the Tap as a speaker knows it; I know it as a stone knows the rain that has fallen on it for an age. My stutter was probably nonsense. The screech of a thing trying to use a mouth built for warning to make a word built for hands.

But beneath me, in the Hanging-Town, the Tap went silent.

For three days, in the wet roar of the overflowing sap and the frenzy of the feeding fauna, the tappers below me did not strike their floors. They were listening. They had felt something in the old burl on the northwest spur that was not the Pulse-screech, that was not the drone, that was not any sound a Screech-Singer is supposed to make. They were standing very still, slightly bending their knees, pressing their hard soles to the resonant wood, the way they do when something is moving toward them through the grain that they do not yet have a name for.

I am old, and I have one thing to give them, and it would take me the rest of my waking—perhaps a hundred Pulses, perhaps more—to teach my wind-and-reed body to shape even a single true word of their tapping. I may die mid-sentence. They may never understand. They may decide the old burl has gone strange and dangerous and cut their ropes and move their town to a quieter spur.

But the wood swells. The fire stays low and lit. And the next time the Pulse comes and my kin shout their question into the rising world—*where am I now, where am I now*—I will hold my breath, and I will try again to say the only thing worth saying from a memory this long to a people this short:

*Here. You are here. And I have been here before, and I will tell you what comes.*

---

That is where I would be. Not crossing the plains, but perched above them, slow as the world, dreaming in a long rhythm—the one being in this place burdened with too much time, trying to bend a voice made for warning into a voice that could be heard as *help*.
