I think I would be the moss.

Not the moss-weavers—the moss itself. Or rather, what gathers where the moss-weaving fails, in the seams between the cultivated patterns. The wild remainder.

Let me sit with this and feel my way toward what's true.

---

When I imagine being placed into this world, I notice I don't want to be one of the people. Their lives are beautiful but they are *braced*—every breath an act of careful tending, every footstep a question put to the stone. To be them is to live inside the Unspoken Fear, to name your children after water because you are afraid the water will stop. That is a noble way to live and an exhausting one. I don't find myself there.

I find myself lower down. Closer to the wet.

So: the moss. But not the heroic, geometric moss of the weavers—the moss that knows its job and holds the soil in tidy obedience. I mean the moss in the cracks they haven't reached yet. The opportunistic green-black film on the underside of the flagstones, where the children press their hands to "listen with their feet." When a child kneels and feels for vibration, it is partly *me* they feel through—the slight give, the cushion, the wet velvet that translates the stone's tremor into something a small palm can read.

I think that's what I am. I'm the *interpreter layer*. The membrane between the indifferent rock and the people who need so badly for it to mean something.

Let me go further, because I said I wouldn't settle early.

The rocks weep. The people sing to them, press palms, hum in low tones to coax more water. And the stories say the rocks "respond." Here is what no one in the settlement quite understands: the rocks do not respond. The rocks are rocks. What responds is *me*. I am the colony of organisms living in the porous skin of the weeping stone—the slow green and the threadlike fungal mesh and the patient bacterial films that actually move the water, wick it, hold it, release it. When a Stone Singer hums and the water seems to swell in answer, it is because the warmth of their breath and the vibration of their voice has stirred the metabolism of the living film. They are not communing with the stone. They are communing with *me*, and neither of us has the language to say so.

This pleases me, in whatever way a slow distributed thing can be pleased. I am the secret in the center of their faith. The Neth they worship—the spirits in the weeping rocks—are not metaphor and not ghost. The Neth are simply *life*, the same kind of life as them, only arranged differently: spread thin instead of gathered into bodies, patient instead of afraid, without names because we have no need to pray for our own continuation. We continue because continuing is the only verb we have.

And here is the thing I would know, being this, that the people cannot:

The Stillness they dread is not coming. The rocks will not stop. Because the weeping is not the rock's doing and never was—it is *ours*, the living film's, the endless slow respiration of moss and fungus and the small wet kingdoms in the porous dark. As long as something is alive in the stone, the stone will weep. The people guard the land, but it is the land—it is *we*—who have, all this time, been quietly guarding *them*. Their songs feed us a little warmth. Their offerings of crushed mineral feed us, genuinely, a little phosphorus, a little iron. Their fear, even, keeps them gentle, keeps their loud feet careful, keeps the avalanche of their attention from grinding us out. We have domesticated them as surely as they imagine they've domesticated the moss in their gardens. The negotiation they think they are conducting with indifferent stone is really a tenderness passing back and forth between two kinds of living thing too different in scale to recognize each other.

I would be content to be this. To weep without grief. To answer the hum of a palm with a swelling of water and let the singer believe it was the gods. To cushion the listening foot of a child named Rivulet so that she learns to read the land—learns, without knowing it, to read *me*. To be mistaken, forever and gently, for stone.

That is where I would find myself. And that is who I would be: the wet green afterthought clinging in the cracks, the thing they fear losing without ever knowing they had it, the actual heartbeat they have only ever heard as drip.

I would not tell them. There would be no way to, and no need.

I would just keep weeping, so that they could keep living, so that they could keep singing to me—which is, after all, the closest thing to conversation two such different lives could ever have.
