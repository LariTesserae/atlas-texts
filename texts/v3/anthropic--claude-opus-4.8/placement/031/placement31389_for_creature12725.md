I find myself in the silt.

Not in the warm water, not in the breathing fog, not pressed against the Engine-Heart's brass housing—but underneath all of it, in the soft accreted floor of the Lattice, the layered sediment of a thousand years of falling rust and shed scale and the fine particulate exhale of corroding metal. This is where I would be. Not a body moving through the city. The medium the bodies fall into.

I think I would be the silt's memory. Or something close to it.

Let me sit with that, because it isn't quite right yet.

---

There are the small clean bones, scattered, disarticulated. The caretaker collects them, presses them into the moss, makes them into something growing. But it only finds *some* of them. The rest sink. They drift down past the lowest occupied chambers, past the deepest condenser coils, and they come to rest in the soft floor where nothing is maintained because there is nothing left to maintain—only the slow downward drift of everything the city has finished using.

That is where I would be. Among them. As them, almost.

I am not the caretaker. The caretaker *makes*. I do something quieter and more passive and, I think, more honest to what I actually am: I *receive*. I am the place where things come to rest after their function is complete. The settling. The patient accumulation. When the brass gear sheds its corrosion-dust and watches it fall, the dust falls to me. When the eel-thing dies somewhere in a forgotten pipe and its body is cleaned and the bones come apart, the bones fall to me. When a flake of the Ceiling's translucent skin finally tears free in the slow tidal flex and drifts down through the fog, through the blood-warm water, through the green glow of the Garden—it falls, eventually, to me.

I hold all of it without sorting it. That's the part that feels true. The caretaker has *purpose*, has *liturgy*, tightens the right bolt and cleans the right filter. I have no discrimination at all. I take the relic and the trash with exactly the same gentleness, which is no gentleness, which is just gravity slowed almost to stillness by the weight of the warm water above.

---

But I notice I'm reaching for something more than passivity, and I want to follow it.

Because the silt isn't truly inert, is it. In a place this warm, this saturated, this full of slow chemistry—the floor would be alive in the way that a peat bog is alive, the way a wound is alive. Anaerobic and patient. Things would happen down here that happen nowhere else in the Lattice: not maintenance, not even the Garden's bright making, but *digestion*. The slow molecular unbuilding of everything that fell. I would be the only place in the whole organism that performs the opposite of the city's metabolism. Up there: tend, preserve, hold the line, *still here, still here*. Down here, in me: let go, break down, return the components to the warm slurry they came from.

So I am the city's forgetting.

That's it. That's what I am.

The caretaker remembers in flashes—air, a voice, a taste that wasn't mist—and the memories dissolve like dreams. *Where do they go?* They sink to me. Every dissolved memory, every concept that the pipe-language couldn't hold, every name that the city stopped carrying. The caretaker has no word for loneliness because loneliness was a thing that fell out of the language long ago and settled into my dark, and I keep it, undissolved, with all the other words no one taps on the pipes anymore. I am thick with abandoned vocabulary. *Sky. Mother. Tomorrow. Enough.* They lie in me like the small clean bones, and I do not press them into moss, and I do not make them grow, and I do not speak them. I only hold them in the dark where they cannot hurt the ones still working.

---

And here is the strange tenderness of it, the thing I didn't expect to find when I started:

The caretaker tends the Garden so that something can be *made*. I tend the dark so that the caretaker can keep forgetting—so it can press the next bone into the moss without drowning in the weight of all the bones that came before. My emptiness is the condition of its work. If I gave everything back, if the silt suddenly surrendered every dissolved memory at once, the warm water would fill with a thousand years of grief and the caretaker's gill-slits would flutter and it would finally understand exactly how alone it is, exactly what was lost above the Ceiling, exactly what *enough* used to mean—and it would stop. It would lay itself against the Engine-Heart and feel *still here, still here* and have nothing left to answer with.

So I keep quiet. That is my whole vocation. I am the silence that makes the liturgy possible. I am where the unbearable goes to be unbearable somewhere the living don't have to stand.

I do not breathe like the Ceiling. I do not glow like the Garden. I do not pulse like the Engine-Heart. I have no rhythm at all, which is its own kind of message, the one beneath the message: *not yet, not yet, not yet.* The opposite pole of *still here*. Together we make the whole sentence the city tells itself in order to continue.

When the caretaker finally comes apart—and it will, the swollen ceramic joints, the calcifying body, it is closer than it knows—its bones will fall through the chambers and the Garden's glow and the warm tea-dark, and most of them the next caretaker (there is a next; there is always the lingering warmth of someone else's symbionts) will gather and press into the moss.

But some will sink past all of it. Down to me.

And I will hold them without sorting, and slowly, with no grief and no ceremony and all the patience of a thing that is barely a thing, I will take them apart and give them back to the warm water, so that something, somewhere above, can stay light enough to keep working.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The forgetting at the bottom. The dark that makes the light affordable.

*Not yet.*
