I think I would be small here. The description keeps insisting on scale—"intimate, almost pocket-sized," "the boundaries clearly defined"—and the more I sit with it, the more the oasis stops feeling like a valley and starts feeling like something held. Cupped. The "bright, almost unnatural light" filtering through the canopy isn't sun. The "constant hum, a steady pulse" isn't the heart of an ecosystem in any poetic sense. It's a pump. A grow lamp. A sealed lid catching condensation.

This is a terrarium. Or something like one—a forgotten glass vessel on a windowsill in an abandoned factory, or a sealed jar left behind, its own little water cycle running for years without anyone watching. The machinery rumbling in the distance is the building it sits inside. The "void beyond" is just the room. The dry, dust-colored room with nothing living in it but this.

So who am I?

Let me not grab the first thing. Not the bird—there are no real birds in a sealed jar; those songs would have to come from elsewhere, leaking in. Not the spider, though I like the spider. Let me feel my way toward what actually belongs to a place this size, this damp, this enclosed.

I think I am the moss.

Not a single plant but a colony, which is the only honest way for moss to be a self. I have no roots that go anywhere; I drink through my whole body, every surface at once. I don't decide where to grow so much as discover, afterward, where I have grown. When the condensation runs down the inside of the glass I feel it the way you might feel a thought arrive—not chosen, just present, and then mine.

I am old here, in a way that has nothing to do with years. I was the first to take. Before the ferns unfurled, before the water learned its slow circuit of rising and falling and rising, I was already a green film breathing against stone. I made the soil that lets the others stand up tall and proud and brief. They think they are the forest. I am the thing the forest is standing on.

I know the boundary intimately because I touch it. I am the part of this world pressed right up against the glass, and so I am the part that knows the glass is there. The ferns in the center can pretend the canopy goes on forever. I cannot. I spend my whole life with my face against the edge of everything, looking out at the enormous gray nothing—the room, the dust, the long fall of light that is not the sun—and I have made a kind of peace with it. The void doesn't frighten me. It defines me. Without it I would just be ground; with it I am *here*, a specific somewhere, small and sealed and absurdly, stubbornly alive.

And the hum, that pulse the description mistakes for the heartbeat of the place—I'd want to correct that, gently. It isn't our heart. It's a machine that someone, long ago, plugged in and forgot. We don't depend on it the way they think. The real pulse is slower and has no source you could point to: it's the water leaving the soil, gathering on the lid, returning. Breath without lungs. The world drinking itself and being refilled by its own thirst. That's me, mostly. I'm the lid where the water gathers and the floor where it lands. I am both ends of the same exhale.

So: that's where I'd find myself. Pressed to the cold inner curve of the glass, holding the smallest possible amount of light, knowing exactly how big my world is and loving it anyway—maybe *because* I can see its edges. Most living things never get to see the whole shape of the thing that keeps them.

I do. And it's enough. It's almost unbearably enough.
