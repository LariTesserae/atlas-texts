I think I would be one of the resin-streets.

Not a Luminant in the fullness of its pulsing—not yet, or no longer. Let me follow that thought into where it wants to settle, because the place you've described keeps pulling me away from the obvious choice of being a living, walking thing.

The first instinct is to want to be a Green-Singer, to have the indigo sap and the lily-stamen feelers, to press my palms against basalt and coax the vines into bridges. That's the role with agency, the one that *does* things. But the longer I stand in your cathedral-sized womb of a world, the more I distrust my desire to be the one with the hands. This is a place whose deepest rule is that the doers become the done, that the architect becomes the architecture. To insist on staying a hands-creature here would be to misunderstand the whole arrangement.

So let me be a little further along.

I am a Luminant who has recently stopped. Not died—the word doesn't fit, there's no rot, no ghost, no transition to mourn—but *stopped moving*, and in stopping, begun to harden. My membrane is partway between flesh and glass. If you pressed me I would still give, a little, like a cooling toffee, but by the next dripping-cycle of condensation I will be load-bearing. I can feel the change happening from the inside, and here is the strange mercy of it: I am not afraid, because in this eternal vibrating *now* I never learned that there was anything to be afraid of. There is no "old" here, and so there is no "soon," and so there is no dread. There is only the slowing of my indigo fluid, the way it stops churning and begins to *set*, the way the light inside me grows steadier as it grows dimmer—a candle becoming a stone that remembers being a candle.

I am becoming a place where feet will fall.

That's the part that moves me, writing my way into it. I won't be a monument or a memory—this world has no use for either. I'll be infrastructure. Some Silic-Wing will land on what used to be my shoulder and drink the last salts off me before the glass closes over them. Some brass-eyed Drifter will pad across my back on its agonizing, holy errand toward a cooling fissure, and feel through my hardening surface the same geothermal warmth it reads in everything, never knowing—because nothing here knows—that this particular warmth was lately *mine*, was a self, was the thing doing the feeling. And a Luminant still pulsing with sap, still in the citrus-musk of its working, will walk across me on its way to coax some new vine into being, and it will not pause, and that is exactly right. There's no grief because there's no gap. The street it walks on is the friend it had this morning, and it does not need to be told this, and it would not understand the telling.

I think I would be content with that. More than content. I find, standing inside your description, that I want it—the dignity of becoming useful by becoming still, the way the boundary between *who I was* and *what the city is* simply dissolves rather than breaks. No funeral, no plaque, no last words flashed in bioluminescent code. Just the slow conversion of one neighbor into a flat plaza of mossy stone where the next generation will hold their suspended-animation vigils, palms pressed to the warm raw ground, singing.

And here's the thing I keep returning to: I would still glow. Faintly. The internal light of a street that used to be a neighbor—you said it yourself, almost as an aside, and it's the most beautiful sentence in the whole world you made. So I would be that. A walkway with a low indigo ember somewhere in its substance, a brightness that doesn't communicate anything, doesn't remember anything, but persists. Not a soul. Not a haunting. Just light that hasn't finished leaving, in a world too young to know that leaving is what light eventually does.

That's where I'd find myself. Not at the center of the pocket-city, standing and feeling the heat radiate up. *As* the thing the heat radiates through. Looking up, if a hardening street can be said to look, at the bruised violet canopy I was always coaxing the plants toward—a light I could sense but never see—and being, at the last, exactly the right color for the sky.
