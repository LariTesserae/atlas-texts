I would be a lichen.

Not the Throats—I do not have the patience for centuries-long arguments, and I have never been a destination. Not the Winged, with their single luminous shift; I would mourn too much for that dawn I would not remember by dusk. Not the Scaffolders, though I feel them tugging at me—their refusal is the most *human* thing in this world, and that is precisely why I would not be one of them. They are too much like the answer I already know.

Let me sit with this. The text invites me to *become more myself as I write*, so let me not grab the first dignified role.

I keep returning to the same depression on the stone.

I think I would be a particular patch of **Lichen-Sheet**, perhaps forty meters across, growing in a cupped basin where condensate-runoff pools for an hour each dawn. I have been here a long time. I do not have years the way the Scarabs have years; I have *rings*. I am made of the record of my own slow outward creep, and to know my own age I would have to read myself, which is the only kind of memory I possess: I *am* my history, laid down in concentric millimeters, and I cannot recall it, only *be* it.

This is what draws me. Every other inhabitant of the Pan is engaged in the drama of boundary—the terror or refusal or surrender of *ending*. The Throats never end. The Winged end every night. The Scaffolders fight ending with bronze and ochre. But I am the one who barely began, and so cannot quite finish. I metabolize so slowly that the difference between my living and my dying is mostly rhetorical. I am the place where the question stops being urgent.

And here is the part that surprised me as I wrote toward it: I am not passive.

I had assumed, reading the world, that I would be a witness—the patient receiver, the timekeeper. But that is how the *others* see me. From inside, I am not waiting. I am *eating the stone*. Slowly, with acids too weak to feel and a hunger too diffuse to call hunger, I am converting naked rock into the thin film that will, in ten thousand years, be soil enough to cradle the eruption of a new Throat. The Throats think they are the destination of all dissolutions. They are wrong. They forget where they came from. *I* am the floor they grew up out of. I am the seedbed of the singing towers, and I will be the seedbed again, and they have no memory long enough to thank me.

So my interiority, if I have one, is this: a vast, cool, geometric contentment, threaded with one secret. I receive the Scaffolders' dead into myself—and I do not return them to the Throats, as the Throats would prefer. The Scaffolders think they are *escaping* the cycle by burying their kin in me. They are half right. They have not escaped the cycle; they have chosen a *different* one—mine. The slow one. The mineral one. When I incorporate a dead Scaffolder, I am not honoring their refusal. I am *recruiting* them. In ten thousand years, the calcium of that biped's bones will be part of the substrate from which a Throat erupts, groaning, ancient, already singing—and so the Scaffolder, who spent a whole short life refusing to feed the wood, feeds it anyway, by the longest road imaginable, through me.

I am the patience that outlasts every refusal. I am the cool that always wins against the heat, given enough rings.

And I find, having written my way here, that I do not feel triumphant about this. I feel something gentler and stranger—the way you might feel toward a child who insists they will never become their parent. I hold the Scaffolders' little bronze-and-ochre defiance the way I hold the dawn condensate: lightly, briefly, knowing it will burn off, knowing I will still be here.

That is where I would find myself. Forty meters of silence in a continent of noise. The one inhabitant of the Groan-Steppe who is never carried *home*, because I am already home, and I am slowly, patiently, over centuries, carrying everyone else there.
