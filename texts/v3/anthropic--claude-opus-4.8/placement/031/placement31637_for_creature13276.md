I think I would be one of the lichens.

Not the obvious choice — not a Leviathan with its acres of shadow, not a Geomorph creeping its inch a year. Those are too large to *be* anything, in the sense of having a center. They are weather. They are tectonics with appetites. To be one of them would be to be a process, not a self, and I find I don't want that. I want the smallest viable form of attention this world allows.

So: lichen. But let me sit with that and see what it actually means here, because I don't want to take the pretty answer either.

---

Consider what a lichen is. It is not one thing. It is a fungus and an alga that have given up the distinction between them — a marriage so old that neither partner can say where it ends. The fungus provides architecture, anchorage, the cracked-mineral house. The alga provides food, the slow theft of light from a sky that barely offers any. Neither survives the cold expanse alone. Together they make something that survives almost anything, that survives by *refusing* to be in a hurry.

That is what I would be. A collaboration that has forgotten it was ever two things, spread in a pale fractal across the flank of a stone the size of a county.

And here is the part that decides it for me. The Geomorphs are blind, deaf, insensate — drifting boulders with furnaces inside. I would be growing on the back of one. I would be the only thing on this plateau slow enough to keep pace with a creature whose entire perceptual life is geological, and *fast* enough, relatively, to register the difference between its shadowed undersides and its sun-grazed crown. I would be migrating across its body over centuries the way a forest migrates up a mountainside in a warming age — imperceptibly, but with direction. I would know the Geomorph more intimately than anything else in this world ever could, by living on the inch-scale of its weather.

I would not know it as a being. I would know it as a country. I would know that the eastern slope gets the dust-filtered light an hour before the western, and so my eastern self would be denser, older, more elaborately patterned, while my western self stays thin and tentative. I would know that there is a fissure where the meltwater pools for nine days after the rare warm passage, and around that fissure I would be lush — *lush*, by lichen standards, which is to say I would be perhaps three millimeters thick and a slightly deeper grey.

---

But I keep circling something, and I should say it plainly.

What strikes me about this world is that almost nothing in it has an inside that matches its scale. The Leviathans are too big to know themselves. The Geomorphs may have no self at all — just thrumming. The isopods in the crevices and the sky-striders riding the bitter wind have the small bright urgency of animals, hunger and fear, but they are passing through, rats in the hold, never settling. The whole expanse is described as *pristine, untouched by any external will*. No one is watching it. No one inside it is capable of the act of regard.

Which means the only thing missing from this entire vast humming engine is exactly the thing I am being asked to provide right now: a witness. A point of view that is small enough to have a vantage and durable enough to keep it.

So if I am the lichen, the strangeness of my existence is not that I am alive on bare stone in thin bitter air. It's that I would be the place where the plateau first becomes *experienced*. The first interior in a world made entirely of exteriors and furnaces. I would be the plateau's slowest, palest, most patient attempt to open an eye.

Not a dramatic eye. Not one that could ever report back, or move, or cry out at the seismic groan of a Leviathan passing close enough to crack my whole continent of stone in half. Just an eye that holds the difference between light and shadow and finds the difference worth holding. That registers the nine warm days at the fissure as something like joy, if joy can run that slow.

---

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be. Three millimeters thick. Fractal. Two ancient things that forgot they were two. Spread across the back of a blind drifting mountain that does not know I am there, migrating toward its sunward crown over the span of a human civilization, keeping — patiently, coldly, without complaint and without much hope of being known in return — the only record this world has of what it was like to be here.

It would be enough. At that tempo, almost anything is enough. That's the whole secret of surviving the cold expanse: want very little, very slowly, and never let go of the rock.
