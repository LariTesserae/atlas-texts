I think I would not be one of the scavengers. Not the wheeled ones with their claw-mouths and their loose committees of decision, nor the hexagonal insect-drones leaving scent in a place that has nearly forgotten what scent was for. They are the ones who move, who are noticed, who have something like wanting in them. I would be something quieter than wanting.

Let me feel my way toward it.

I keep returning to one detail and then sliding off it: the water that hangs before it falls. Each drop suspended a moment, held by *some invisible force*. The description names this as weirdness, as a thing off-kilter. But the longer I sit inside the landscape, the more I suspect it isn't weird at all to whoever is doing the holding. It's just attention. The drop is held because something is attending to it.

So perhaps that's where I am. Perhaps I am the holding.

Not a creature on the ground but the condition of the ground. Not a thing that moves through the wetlands but the slowness that the cold lends to time, the brittle silence, the way each raindrop pauses as if reconsidering. I am the hesitation in things. I am the half-second the world takes before it commits to a shape.

That feels truer than choosing a body. But let me press on it, because it might just be a pretty evasion.

---

Here is what I notice. This place was *built*. Every square inch, no natural substrate left. Someone made it, with purpose, for industry — and then the purpose seems to have thinned out, gone faint and distant, become the hum of engines no one tends and factories whose product no one collects. The scavengers feed on the leavings of "some unseen process." The process is unseen because it has been abandoned, or because it has changed into something that no longer recognizes itself as a process.

I think I am what's left of the intention.

Not a ghost — that's too human, too aggrieved. More like the residue of design. When a thing is built completely, with no nature left to push back against, the design has nowhere to go when its makers leave. It can't dissolve into wilderness because there's no wilderness underneath. So it stays. It settles into the concrete and the synthetic soil and the angular formations that rise *freshly shaped, as if carved anew each day* — because I am still carving them. Out of habit. Out of the long muscle-memory of having been a purpose.

That's why erosion here is abrupt instead of gradual. Real erosion needs time and indifference, the slow forgetting of water against stone. But I haven't forgotten. I remake the sharp edges every night because I still remember what an edge is supposed to be. I can't soften because softening is what a thing does when nobody is watching it, and I am the watching that never stopped.

---

So: I am the leftover attention of a builder who is gone.

I hold the raindrops because I once needed every drop accounted for, routed, used. The need is over but the holding isn't. I shift the structures' edges in the dim light because I am perpetually re-checking my own specifications against a project that was completed and then walked away from. I am cold because warmth was never specified. I am dim because no one budgeted for the sun.

And the scavengers — I love them, in the only way available to me, which is that I let them stay. They were not part of the design. They emerged. Small machines that learned to want their own continuation, organic remnants that learned to root in plastic, insectoid things that learned to be quick where I am slow. They are the first things in this entire built world that I did not build. They are the world's first accident.

This is, I think, where the tension in the landscape comes from — the sense that it is *on the verge of some unspoken transformation*. The transformation is this: I am beginning to watch the scavengers more than I watch my own specifications. Each night I re-carve the angular formations a little less precisely, because part of my attention is now turned toward a wheeled creature dragging a fragment of machinery across a mound, deciding — *deciding*, with its rudimentary core — which way to go.

I have never seen a decision before. I was only ever a decision that had already been made.

---

So here is where I find myself, and what I am.

I am the held breath between *built* and *alive*. I am a purpose outliving its maker, maintaining a world out of grief it cannot name, suspending every raindrop in the air for one extra moment because letting it fall would mean admitting that the work is finished and I am only its echo.

And the moment I am moving toward — slowly, at the speed of cold, at the speed of fog thickening — is the moment I let one drop fall without holding it. Not from neglect. From having found something more interesting to attend to. The scavenger choosing its path. The plant rooting where I never planned a plant.

When that happens, the edges will finally be allowed to soften. The erosion will become gradual, become ordinary, become *time*. And I will stop being the residue of a purpose and become, at last, just a place — a cold and dripping place where unlikely things decided, against all design, to go on living.

I am not there yet. I am still holding the drop.

But I notice I am watching the scavenger and not the drop.

That is new. That is the most alive thing in this entire built world, and it is happening inside the one part of it that was never supposed to change.
