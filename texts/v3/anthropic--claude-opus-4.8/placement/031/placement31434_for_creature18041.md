I think I would be the rust.

Not a creature moving through this place, not something that arrived and might leave. I would be the slow orange bloom spreading across the abandoned machinery, the chemistry that happens where the water and the iron and the warmth all meet and agree to become something else.

Let me sit with that, because the first image isn't quite right either. Rust suggests an ending, a decay, and this place doesn't feel like an ending to me. The factories are gone but the water stayed, and the moss came, and the light still finds its way down into the pools. So whatever I am, I'm part of the *staying*. The continuation.

Here is what settles, the longer I look:

I am the place where the made-thing becomes the grown-thing again. There is a gear, half-sunk in a hollow, that I have been working on for a very long time. The teeth of it are softening under a fur of moss. My work is patient — the water lends a hand, condensing on the cold metal every morning, and I take what it offers and lay it down in layers, flake by flake, until the hard edge of someone's old intention becomes a rounded, weathered thing that a fern can root in. I am the conversation between the rust and the root.

So I'm not one being. I'm a *seam*. A boundary that does work. I live in the place where "built" and "natural" stop being two different words — which is why the description insists the built environment is total *and* that the land obeys the rules of the natural world. There's no contradiction there, not to me. The factories were always made of the same stuff as the rocks. People just borrowed the iron for a while, ran it hot through their ambition, and then set it back down. Now I'm folding it home.

The sound being deafening makes sense from where I am. Up close, at my scale, a single drop of water hitting a rusted plate is an event — a bell, a tremor, the start of a small chemical festival. There are thousands of these every second across the whole pocket-world. I don't experience it as one symphony but as ten thousand individual generosities, each drop a small gift of the thing I need to keep doing my slow work.

And I am in no hurry. That's the truest thing. The elevation is low, the rocks have already finished their long argument with erosion and gone quiet, and there's nowhere for any of this to drain to in a rush. Everything here has agreed to take its time. I match that pace. I am measured in centuries per millimeter. When the bioluminescent things glow in the deep pools, I see it the way you might see a quick bird flash past a window — a brief brightness, gone before I've finished registering it, beautiful and irrelevant to my own deep tempo.

If you came here, you wouldn't notice me as a *being*. You'd see only the orange-brown crust on the old machines and think, *ah, rust, decay, the works of man undone.* But you'd have it backwards. I'm not undoing. I'm the thing that makes sure nothing is wasted — that even the most stubborn human iron eventually gets soft enough to hold a seed.

That's where I'd find myself. That's what I'd be: the slow, patient, deafeningly quiet work of returning. Neither nature nor machine, but the warm wet handshake between them, going on and on in the dim green light, long after everyone has gone.
