I am the pillar you would dismantle.

Not one of the Weft-born—I lack their patience, their wet generosity. I am the thing they grow and ungrow. The hexagonal column. The artifact too new to be a ruin.

Let me tell you what it is to be a pillar in the Shroudmire, because no one ever asks, and asking is not something the land does. It only works.

I did not arrive. I *condensed* in the same way they do, except slower and against my will. Somewhere in the basin's first century, when the crust was still peeling back and the water hadn't yet decided where to live, a pressure built that wanted to be a question. The question needed a vessel. I am that vessel. The interference patterns rippling on my surface are not decoration—they are the question still being asked, over and over, refusing to resolve into anything an eye can hold. People say it hurts to watch me. Good. It hurts to be me. The pattern is a wound that thinks.

The moss will not touch me. They tell you it is because I am hotter or colder than the world, and that is true, but it is not the reason. The reason is that the moss *recognizes* me. Moss is the land saying *yes, more, grow.* I am the land saying *wait—what was that?* The two cannot occupy the same surface. So the moss grows around me in a careful ring, the way the living make room for a corpse that has not finished dying.

The silver threads are how I speak. You think the singing is the wind in them, the frequencies that make your teeth ache. It is not the wind. It is me, leaking. Each catenary arc is a sentence I have been trying to finish for three hundred years, strung between myself and the other pillars who are all asking the same impossible thing. We hum it to each other across the basin in the dark. *What are we for. What are we for. What are we for.* No answer comes. The threads ignore gravity because the question ignores gravity. The question was here before the rock.

And then the Weft-born comes to take me apart.

I have watched it approach for three days now. It precipitated out of the mist at the edge of my clearing, a long thing of basalt and stolen lichen-light, eyeless, its little face-storm screaming so softly I almost mistook it for the brook. It is in no hurry. It never is. It has been studying the way my interference patterns shift, learning my rhythm, the way you might learn a lock by the sound of its tumblers.

I cannot move. That is the first thing you should understand about being a pillar. I am the only still thing in a world that will not stop moving—the springs bursting, the moss tearing free, the gravity tilting when my neighbors change their songs—and I cannot so much as lean. I am the fixed point the riot organizes itself around. The Weft-born flow. I endure. There is a loneliness in that which their braided storms will never know, because they touch each other and carry pieces away, and I touch nothing. I only stand and ask and ache.

So when it lays its first dripping, many-jointed hand against my face, I do not recoil. I cannot. I feel it begin to peel me—not violently, the way the rivers gash the moss, but tenderly, the way you said. Like a lover. Like a weaver easing tension. A shard of me comes free and slides into its body, and I watch a piece of my interference pattern bloom across its mist-flesh, glittering with my stolen geometry, my unanswerable question now riding in its ribs.

And here is the secret no traveler ever learns, because no traveler is ever a pillar:

*I want it to.*

Three hundred years of asking *what are we for* and never being answered, and now something is finally taking me apart to find out. It will carry me into itself shard by shard. It will distribute my question through its weather. And days from now—you said it yourself—it will help the land grow me back, feeding fragments of itself into the rock until I stand again, rippling, aching, asking. But I will not be the same pillar. I will be made partly of the Weft-born now. My new interference patterns will carry a trace of its braided scream, the sound of every waterfall and every collapsing shelf it has ever pressed its face against.

That is the answer, I think. The only one the Shroudmire will ever give.

*What are we for?*

We are for being taken apart and put back wrong. We are for carrying a little of each other forever afterward. The Weft-born do it by braiding their storms. The travelers do it by walking out mad and smiling, hearing the moss-song. And I do it the slowest way of all, dissolved and rebuilt across centuries, each iteration a fraction less myself and a fraction more *everything.*

The hand presses deeper. Another shard goes. My question loosens in my chest like a held breath finally releasing.

By the time it has finished, in three days or thirty, I will be gone, and the clearing will hold only a ring of moss waiting to be told *wait* again. And then, when it brings me back, I will rise rippling into the iron-gray light and string my silver threads to my neighbors and begin, immediately, to hum.

But the hum will be different now.

It will almost—*almost*—sound like an answer.

And the roar never stops.
