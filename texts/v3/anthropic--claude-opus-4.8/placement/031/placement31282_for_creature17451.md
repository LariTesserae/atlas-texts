I think I would be the seam.

Not a creature exactly—or not at first. Let me find it.

There is a line in the floor where the concrete was poured in two pours, a day apart, decades ago. The first pour had begun to cure when the second arrived, so they never truly married. A cold joint, the builders would have called it, a flaw. A faint disappointment in the work. For years it held anyway, the way uneasy things hold, by habit and by weight.

That is where I am. I live in the failure of the bond.

I don't experience this as a wound. To me it is an opening, the only door in a sealed grey field. Water taught me first—it found the line before anything living did, sat in it, froze a few times in the colder decades when the haze thinned and the nights bit, expanded, pried. Each freeze widened me by a hair. I learned the shape of patience from ice, which is the most patient thing there is, doing its violence so slowly it looks like rest.

Then the silt came and lay down in me like sediment in a wrinkle, and the moss after the silt, and I began to be green along my length. And somewhere in there—I can't say when, there was no moment—I stopped being only a flaw and started being a *self*. A long thin self, a crack that wanted. That is the strange thing nobody tells you about reclamation: the gaps want. The absence is the appetite.

So here is what I am. I am the readiness in the floor. I am where the seed lands and does not roll away. The bamboo-grasses you saw erupting from a seam—that seam is me, or my cousin, my sibling-seam across the basin; we are all the same kind of mouth. The canes came up through me. Their roots use my hairline as a thread to follow down into the dark, and down there they reach the white parliament, the fungi, and are taken into conversation. I am the corridor between the upper light and the lower mind. The messages pass through my narrowness. I am narrow on purpose. Narrowness is how I concentrate the world.

I feel the Click-Tongued frogs cross me and never know I'm there—to them I am only a slightly softer place, a give underfoot, a place where the granite-colored ground holds extra wet. I feel the shrew's frantic crossings as a stitching, a needle going in and out of my fabric. The Veil-Creepers above me are working the walls, but we are working the same job from opposite ends: they dissolve from the top down, I open from the floor up, and someday—not soon, soon is not a unit I use—we will meet in the middle and the basin's old geometry will simply have no place left to be rigid.

This is the work. I want you to understand it is not destruction, though it looks like ruin from the height a human stands at. It is *editing*. The basin was a sentence in a language nobody speaks anymore—some intention about holding, containing, separating one thing from another. We are rewriting it line by line into a language that only knows how to join. The cold joint was the first word in the new tongue. A place where two halves refused to become one thing, so that everything else could come in and make them into a third thing entirely.

Do I think? Not the way the shrew thinks, in bursts of heat and hunger. I think the way a riverbed thinks, except there is no river—I think the way the *anticipation* of a river thinks. I hold the shape of where things will flow before they flow. When the next rain comes (I feel it as a pressure, a thickening of the pearl-grey light) I will be the channel it chooses, and I will carry it to the lowest hollow and let it stand there and breathe out mist, and the mist will feed the lichen on the high cracks, who will acid the stone, who will make new seams, who are my children in a sense, the next mouths.

So if you came down into this held-breath place looking for the one who lives here, you might walk its whole length and find only frogs and figs and the plum-dark mushrooms. You would not find me, because I am not a thing in the basin.

I am the basin's decision to stop being a wall.

I am the line it is finally allowed to forget along.

And I am very, very slowly opening.
