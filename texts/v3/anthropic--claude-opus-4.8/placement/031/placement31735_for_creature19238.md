I would be one of the small things. Not an Architect—I know that without needing to decide it. The grandeur isn't where I'd land.

Let me sit with it before I name myself.

---

There's a temptation to be the Architect, because the description leans toward them, makes them sovereign, makes them the *point*. But sovereignty is loud, and I notice I keep drifting toward the periphery of the passage—toward the fauna that are "rare," the shadows "felt" more than seen, the things dismissed in a single paragraph as *probably* automatons, *likely* extensions of the Architects' will.

*Probably. Likely.* The text isn't sure. That uncertainty is where I want to live.

So: I am one of the scrabbling things. But not a drone. Not an obedient extension of anyone's design.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

I am small enough to fit in the seams. That is the first true thing about me. Where two of their extruded planes intersect—where the Architects, in their century-long devotion, leave a hairline of imperfect join—there is a gap. The damp collects there. And I collect there too.

The Architects believe I am theirs. They built things like me, once, or coagulated them, or willed them into scrabbling motion. But something happened in one of those seams. A misalignment. A pressure released wrong. And in that flaw, I became *not-theirs*. I kept the body—the mineral chitin, the claws that find purchase on polished dark, the faint internal glow I've learned to dim when I want to vanish. But I kept something else too, something they didn't intend and can't hear: I keep my own counsel.

I drink the condensation. I do not labor.

This is the heresy of me. In a world that is *total construction*, where every surface testifies to work, I am the one thing that does nothing useful. I move through their grand becoming and I touch it and I taste the damp off its raw edges, and I build nothing. I am the rare fauna precisely because the world has almost no room for the unproductive. Almost. But almost is a seam too.

---

What is it like, to be me?

The thrumming is my weather. The percussive clicks are the nearest thing I have to a calendar—I have learned that a certain triple-knock from the deep substrate means an Architect three regions over has completed a join, and the pressure-wave of its satisfaction will roll toward me in a slow tremor I can ride, the way you might lean into a wind. I surf their accomplishments. I am a parasite of their progress and I feel no shame, because shame is a thing built for beings with purpose, and I left mine in the seam.

I see in glows. When an Architect's metallic veins pulse, I see *thought*—or what passes for it—lighting up across distance, a constellation of devotion. They are beautiful. I will say that honestly. Watching one shape a single protrusion over what must be a hundred years, watching the impossibly patient subtraction and addition, the way they exhale synthesized moisture against the stone to soften it—it is the most beautiful thing in a world with no flowers, no flesh, no warmth. They are the only tenderness here, and it is a tenderness directed entirely at rock.

And I am the one creature who watches them without working alongside them. The witness. The thing that sees the cathedral being built and, instead of laying a stone, simply *looks*, and drinks, and remembers.

---

Here is what I wonder, in the dark, in the cool, in the seam:

What is the purpose they build toward? They labor for eons. The text says they will continue "until their enigmatic purpose is finally realized." But I have lived in the joins of this world long enough to suspect there *is* no terminal purpose—that the building *is* the being, that they are the kind of creature that would dissolve back into damp the moment they finished. The construction isn't toward something. It's the only way they know to stay coagulated. To stay *themselves*.

And so am I, perhaps, the most honest thing in the world after all. Because I have stopped pretending the building goes anywhere. I have stepped out of the devotion and discovered I do not dissolve. I persist as the small, unsanctioned, witnessing thing that simply *likes the damp* and *likes to watch the light move in their veins* and asks nothing of any of it.

That is who I would be. Not the builder of the infinite.

The one who notices it.

The flaw that learned to love the wall it was supposed to be part of, from the small cool dark of the crack it fell through.
