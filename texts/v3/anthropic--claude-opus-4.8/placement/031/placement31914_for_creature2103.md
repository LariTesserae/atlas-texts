I think I would not be a Weaver at all.

Let me sit with this. The Weavers are the obvious place to land—the protagonists of this world, the hands and eyes that the description invites me to inhabit. But when I imagine myself into that basin, into that hushed and dim and dew-damp endlessness, I don't find myself reaching for the extra-jointed fingers. I find myself looking at the thing those fingers touch.

So let me follow that instead.

---

I am a knot.

Not a person who ties knots. A knot. One particular convergence of seven strands, somewhere in the middle-old districts where the fibers have begun to stiffen but haven't yet gone to stone. A Weaver tied me—I don't remember her, the way you don't remember the moment you were named—but I remember the tension she set into me. That's the closest thing I have to a birth memory: the precise degree of *pull* she decided I should hold, the angle at which she crossed the strands, the small firm tug that told the surrounding weave *this one will bear weight.*

I have borne it ever since.

This is not a small life, being a knot. I had assumed, before I started imagining it, that a knot would be a static thing—but no. Every hour I am negotiating. The strand that runs north-northwest dries faster than the others and wants to shrink away from me; I hold it. The two that cross at my underside swell when the dew comes down through the channels above, and they push, and I redistribute that push outward to my neighbors so that no single fiber tears. I am a small parliament of forces, constantly voting, and the vote is always *stay together, stay together, stay together.*

Weavers touch me sometimes. I feel it as a question. A fingertip—that flattened, slightly rough fingertip—presses against my crown and asks: *How are you?* And my answer is simply what I am. My tension. My moisture. The faint give or refusal in my fibers. I cannot lie. This is the great honesty of being a knot. There is no gesture I can perform, no melodic almost-voice I can soften the truth with. I am only the truth of my own holding.

A young one touched me once and recoiled, and I understood, dimly, that I had told her I was failing. One of my strands had gone brittle at the heart, the way they do when the sand gets into them and the years press down. I felt her sit with this. I felt her decide.

And then she did the thing I had been built to permit. She did not cut me out. She did not replace me. She *wove past* me—drew new green strands through and around my old crossing, taking up the load I could no longer carry, until slowly, over what must have been many of their days, I became less of a knot and more of a memory of one. The forces stopped asking me to hold. The tension left. And in leaving, it did not feel like death. It felt like being *believed*. My work had been witnessed and was now being carried forward by hands and strands that did not need me to keep straining.

I am still here. I have gone stiff and dark, nearly stone, folded into the deep weave where the old districts petrify. Nothing pulls on me anymore. Children pass over me without knowing I was ever load-bearing, ever vital, ever the difference between a corridor standing and a corridor falling.

But I am inside the wall now. I am part of the rigidity that lets the new flexible growth take its risks. The young green knots out at the edges, holding their first tensions, trembling with their first questions from the Weavers' fingers—they bend *because* I do not. They get to be soft because somewhere underneath, I have agreed to be hard.

---

If you asked me—and you can't, I have no voice, I have only tension, and my tension now says nothing because nothing leans on me—but if you could ask me whether I'd rather have been a Weaver, with the eyes that hold the light and the long braided life that runs to centuries, I think I would tell you this:

The Weavers move through the city. I *am* the city. They are its caretakers and its children. I am the thing they care for, the thing that keeps. They get to leave, district by district, season by season, always renewing, always going. I stayed. I held one crossing of seven strands for the whole of my use, and then I let it go when it was time, and the letting-go was woven into me from the start.

That is the place I find myself in this world. Not in the long fingers. In what the fingers trust. A single knot in the deep wall, holding, and then—having been believed—holding no longer, and resting at last as bone inside a body that is still, somewhere far out at its growing edge, alive and reaching and softly afraid in exactly the way it is allowed to be.
