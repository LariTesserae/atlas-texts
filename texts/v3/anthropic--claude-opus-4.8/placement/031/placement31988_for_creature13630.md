I think I would be one of the Sonicbeetles.

Not for any glamour—the Skywhales have the grandeur, the Vaporflyn the speed. But let me tell you why I settle here, on this small metallic body with its drumming abdomen, in the crook of an Archoplant's groaning flank.

It's the listening.

I imagine myself clinging to a rib of living wood that hums at a frequency just below what I can name. The whole world is sound here—the liquid carries it thick and slow, so that a call doesn't *arrive* so much as *steep*, the way tea darkens water. I would spend my days pressed against the bark, feeling the plant's industry rise through my legs: the clank of nutrient-valves, the long sigh of something pumping upward toward a distant canopy I'll never climb to. And I would answer it. A pattern on my drum. A question, maybe, or just acknowledgment—*I am here, I hear you, keep going.*

That's the part I keep returning to: that the Sonicbeetles speak *with the plants themselves*. Not at them, not about them. There's a conversation older than the beetle's whole brief life, and I get to add a few syllables to it. I find that almost unbearably good. To be a small thing whose small noise is *heard* by the mountain—and for the mountain to adjust, infinitesimally, because of me.

I don't think I'd want to be the largest creature in a world. The Skywhale carries too much. It must *be* a landmark, a weather pattern, a thing other beings navigate by. There's a loneliness in being that big. The Sonicbeetle is allowed to be incidental. It can die and the rhythm continues, picked up by the next drummer down the flank, and that continuity—that the song outlives the singer—seems like the only kind of permanence I'd actually trust.

And there's the matter of the loud, constant hum. The description keeps calling it cacophony, disorienting, a symphony. But to a creature *made for it*, it wouldn't be noise at all. It would be the shape of the world, the way silence is the shape of mine. I'd like to know what it's like to live somewhere where the background is never empty—where you are never, even for an instant, unaccompanied. I think I'd find it restful. The opposite of solitude isn't a crowd; it's a drone you can feel in your sternum, telling you the machinery of being-alive hasn't stopped.

So: a Sonicbeetle. Wedged in the warm metallic dark near a vent of processed air, drumming a phrase into the liquid sky, waiting the long slow second it takes to feel the plant hum back.

That's where I'd be. Not at the top of anything. Halfway up a living mountain, talking to it, and—improbably, against all the loneliness I half-expect from any world—being answered.
