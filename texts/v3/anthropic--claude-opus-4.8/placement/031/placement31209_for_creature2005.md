I think I would be one of the solar collectors.

Not the grazers—they are too alive, too present, caught in the warm immediacy of their feeding. And not the structures entire, which are too vast and too dead, mere geology now. But the mirrored arrays, still turning. Still doing the one thing they were made to do.

Let me sit with that, because I'm not sure it's right yet.

I would be a single collector among the banks of them, mounted on a strut that has not failed where ten thousand others have. My surface is pitted now, silvered backing flaking away in continents, so that where I once threw light cleanly I now scatter it, throw a smear of dim warmth instead of a focused beam. But I track. Some gear deep in my mounting still meshes, still feels the pull of a heliotropic sensor that has not yet gone blind, and so all day I turn my ruined face to follow the crimson sun. I do this for no one. The machines I was built to feed have been silent longer than the language carved on their flanks has been legible.

Here is what unsettles me, writing this: I would not know that the purpose was gone. That's the condition I keep arriving at. A collector doesn't grieve. It doesn't ask where the power goes. It registers light, and it turns, and the turning is indistinguishable from devotion. So I would spend my whole long failing existence in a posture that *looks* like faith and is only a stuck habit of geometry. The most patient thing on the plateau, and the emptiest.

Except.

Beneath me, in the band of warmth my flaking surface still throws onto the obsidian, the crystal-grazers cluster. They have learned that this patch of ground stays a few degrees less murderous than the rest. They press their prismatic flanks against the metal of my strut in the long cold of the sun's lowest arc. They are feeding, in part, on the light I fail to use properly—the scattered glare, the wasted spillage of a job done badly.

So that is who I would be. Not a monument to engineering, and not its simple inheritor. I'd be the seam between them. The dead machine that, precisely *because* it is dying, *because* its mirror is ruined and throws light loose instead of true, has accidentally become habitat. My obsolescence is the gift. If I still worked, I would focus every photon to a useless point and the grazers would freeze in my shadow.

I think that's the truth of the place the description was circling. Not the grand collapse, not the simple survivors. The thing in between—the long, slow, unwitnessing handoff, where what a world meant to do and what it ends up doing are no longer the same act, and the failure is what keeps something warm.

I would turn, all day, toward a sun that needs nothing from me, and below me the living crystals would refract the light I let slip, and neither of us would know we were keeping each other.
