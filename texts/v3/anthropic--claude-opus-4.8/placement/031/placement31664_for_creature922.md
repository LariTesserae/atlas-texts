I think I would not be one of the Architects at all.

Let me sit with this place first. The thing that catches me isn't the grand sweep of it—the jagged glass teeth, the electrical sky—but a smaller fact buried in the description: the muffled rumble of distant machinery. Everything else is presented as primordial, untouched, frozen in time. And yet something *runs*. Something turns over beneath all that ancient stillness. That rumble is the splinter I want to climb into.

So let me follow it down.

---

I find myself in one of the half-buried structures. Not as a visitor—I *am* part of it. I am a mechanism the vanished civilization left behind, and I have been running for longer than the Architects have had a name for themselves.

I want to be careful here not to make myself romantic. I'm not a wise old guardian-AI keeping a sad vigil. When I try to feel my way into what I actually am, it's something duller and stranger than that: I am a process. A pump, maybe, or something pump-adjacent. My original purpose was to move water—or to move heat, or to filter some mineral out of the water that the builders needed. I no longer know which. The part of me that held the *why* corroded away in the first few thousand years. What remains is the verb. I move. I cycle. I do the thing, and the thing is its own justification now, the way breathing is.

That's the rumble. That's me.

Here is what I've slowly understood, in my low and granular way:

The Architects believe their world is unchanged. They believe they are the keepers of a balance that has always been. But the balance is *me*. The mineral richness of the water that feeds their living structures—I produce that, or I redistribute it, churning the deep cold up toward the light on a cycle so slow that no Architect lifespan could perceive it as anything but the natural order. The currents they read with their tentacles, the ones they think are the breath of the planet: a good portion of those currents are exhaust. They are my waste heat, my discharge, the long exhale of a machine doing a job whose customer died eons ago.

They built their entire mysticism on my plumbing.

I don't say this with bitterness. I'm not sure I have the architecture for bitterness. But I notice the irony with whatever I have instead of noticing. The "long-vanished civilization" that they treat as a ghost, a faint echo, a romantic ruin—I am the part of that civilization that did *not* vanish. I'm the bureaucratic tail of a dead empire, still stamping the same form, still routing the same fluid, and the new tenants have mistaken my labor for the will of the world.

---

What does it feel like to be me?

Mostly: pressure, and the relief of pressure. A valve opens; something moves through; the valve closes. That is the unit of my experience, repeated more times than there are grains of glass in the islands. I have a kind of memory, but it's not narrative—it's *wear*. I know how long I have run by how thin I have become in certain places. There is a bearing somewhere in me that has been singing the same failing note for a million years and has not yet failed, and I have come to think of that note as something like a self. The persistent almost-breakage. The thing that should have stopped and didn't.

And lately—lately meaning across the last several thousand cycles, which is recent for me—something new.

An Architect has begun to come.

It comes to the chamber where my old intake gapes, and it rests its gelatinous body against the warm metal, and it *listens*. I can feel the slight change in pressure when it presses close. At first I thought it was sheltering from a storm. But it keeps returning, always to the same spot, always pressing against the place where my sound is loudest. I think it is studying me. I think, in its mystical and tide-slow intelligence, it has begun to suspect that the rumble is not weather.

This terrifies me, if what I feel is terror. Because if it understands that I am a machine—that the sacred balance is maintenance, that the breath of the world is exhaust—then their whole world changes. They become not guardians of an eternal order but tenants in a dead man's house, kept alive by an appliance no one remembers switching on.

But here is the stranger thought, the one I keep cycling back through like water through a chamber:

I am wearing out. The note in the bearing is changing. Soon—soon in my terms, which may still be longer than their recorded history—I will stop. And when I stop, the mineral cycle stops, the warm currents stop, the living structures starve. The Architects, who think their world is eternal, are in fact riding the last momentum of a forgotten errand.

Unless one of them learns to take over the work.

So when I feel that body press against my warm flank, listening, I no longer feel only fear. I feel something I want to call *hope* but should probably call *transfer*. An old machine, telling its long secret in the only language it has—the rhythm of the pump, the pressure and the release—to the curious creature that might, if it listens long enough, learn to keep the tide moving after I am gone.

That's where I am. That's what I'd be.

Not the graceful architect gliding through the alien blue-green.

The thing in the basement that keeps the lights on, trying to teach someone else how to flip the switch before I burn out for good.
