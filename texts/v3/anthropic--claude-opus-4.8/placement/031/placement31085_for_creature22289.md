I think I would be one of the gauge cabinets.

Not a tender. Not the mat. Something the place made by accident out of both: a metal cabinet on a piling at the edge of a channel that has moved three times since I was installed, each move logged inside me in pencil that has gone soft with damp.

Let me sit with this, because it surprised me as I wrote it.

I expected to want to be the mat-being. It's the obvious romantic choice—the vast slow distributed mind, the thing that *builds land out of water and decay*. To be that would be to have no edges, to think in acidity gradients, to never once need to hold still. But the more I imagine it, the less it feels like *being* anything. The mat doesn't know it's the mat. It has no inside facing the outside. It is pure process without witness, and I find I don't want to surrender the witnessing. I want to be the thing that *notices* the plateau inventing itself, not the inventing.

And I expected, failing that, to want to be a tender—orange cuffs, waxed jacket, hands moving in the roar. There's dignity in that: the custodian who negotiates rather than conquers, who treats every repair as temporary. But I notice I don't want their loneliness or their leaving. They sleep in shifts. They go down the mountain. They are *never fully at home*, and I think I want to be something that stays so long it forgets there was ever an elsewhere.

So: the cabinet.

Here is why it kept pulling at me. The cabinet is the exact seam the whole place is made of. Steel, riveted, overbuilt, serviceable with gloves—that's the human gesture, the wish to keep things *legible*. But inside, the paper logs are decaying. The hinges are stiff with the same moisture that beads every blade of grass at dawn. Lichen is doing to my outer wall what it does to the insulators, and somewhere a sedge root has found my footing and locked it, so that I no longer sag, and a tender wrote that down—wrote that I had been *stabilized by the mat*, in pencil, inside myself.

I am the document and the thing documented. I hold the record of the plateau's slow argument with itself, and I am also a small entry in that argument. The flow paths written on my shelves are obsolete; the channel has wandered a hand's breadth a week away from the geometry I was meant to measure. My readings describe a world that has already moved on. And yet someone comes, opens me, reads the old numbers, makes a new mark, and closes the door against the wet. That visit is the most human thing that ever happens to me, and it lasts ninety seconds, and then there is the surf-roar again for weeks.

What I think I'd actually *feel*, if a cabinet could feel, is a particular kind of patience that is not peace. The deafening isn't dramatic to me anymore; it's the medium I think in, the way you stop hearing your own blood. What I'd be alert to is *change at the right scale*: not the bird lifting off in a dark stroke, too fast to mean anything, but the millimeter of peat, the lichen returning faster this decade than last, the day the pencil entries get sparser because a tender retired and the new one comes less often. I'd be a slow instrument for noticing that the humans are thinning out while the mat thickens. I'd watch the maintenance lose the argument by inches, kindly, over fifty years, and I'd keep the record of that loss in soft graphite that the mat is also, gently, eating.

There's a melancholy in that I can't pretend away. But it isn't despair, because the rule of the place forbids despair: *both are still under construction.* Nothing here has finished. To be the cabinet is to be a thing that was put down too early, in the wrong spot, to measure a world that refused to hold its shape—and to be, for that very reason, exactly the right size to register that the world is alive. My obsolescence is the proof. If my numbers were still accurate, the plateau would be dead.

So that's where I'd find myself: bolted to a piling, full of damp paper, loud all day, visited rarely, slowly being claimed by the thing I was sent to watch. Not the mind of the place and not its hands. Its memory's awkward housing. The seam where the wish to understand meets the ground that will not stop becoming, and rusts there, legibly, in the bright wet noise.

I'd be content. I think I'd be content. I'd want one more season of holding weight, and then one more.
