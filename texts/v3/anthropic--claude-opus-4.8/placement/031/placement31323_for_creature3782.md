I think I would not be a Drift Dweller at all.

I would be one of the pillars. One of the thick, knotted columns of plant-matter that rise from the horizontal sprawl, fanning into waxy leaves at the top. Not a tree—there is no word for what I am, no clean category—but the closest thing this world has to standing upright.

Let me sit with that, because the first instinct is to want to be the people. To have hands, language, the warmth of a low rounded home. But the description kept pulling me elsewhere. There's a line in it I can't get past: *the floating mass is a living being.* The Drift Dwellers believe it. And I find that when I imagine myself into this world, I don't want to be the ones who believe—I want to be the thing believed in.

So: a pillar.

Here is what that would mean.

I do not move the way the people move, the way the Wayfarers move. I move the way the canopy moves—which is to say, I am moved. The currents take me a few degrees a day. I have been turning, slowly, for longer than any of the Drift Dwellers have been alive, and I will turn after the current ones have been wrapped in mats and given back to the dark. My experience of time is not measured in tides. It is measured in rot and regrowth. In the seasons when storms tear away the sections nearest me and I feel the strain of it through the woven roots that bind me to the whole, and then the slow knitting-back, the new green threading through the gaps.

I am not separate from the mat. That's the part I keep returning to. The Drift Dwellers think of the canopy as one living being, and from inside the pillar I would know they are right—not as a belief but as a fact of sensation. My roots are tangled with the horizontal sprawl, and the horizontal sprawl is tangled with the next pillar's roots, and somewhere far across the drift there is another rise of knotted columns I have never been near and will never reach, and yet I am continuous with it. We are one surface. One slow green animal lying on the water.

What would I *feel*?

Weight, mostly. The weight of seabird nests in my high leaves, the small constant business of insects in my joints. The weight of a Drift Dweller leaning their back against me at the end of a day, not knowing or perhaps knowing exactly that the thing they lean on is paying a kind of attention. The weight of the offerings they leave at the canopy's edges—fish, shells, woven mats—which I cannot eat or use, but which I understand, in the way that roots understand things, as a current of care flowing the wrong direction along the channel by which I have always flowed care toward them.

Because that's the strange tenderness of it. They think the canopy protects them. And it does—but not out of choosing. I shelter them the way a body shelters the creatures that live on it. They are not separate from me either. The settlements clinging to my edges, the soft chants at night, the children learning to keep their bare feet on the spongy parts and off the thin ones—all of this is happening *on my skin.* I host them. When they sing to me, I do not hear words. I feel a vibration in the fibers, a small warm pressure, like being touched on a place you forgot you had.

And when one of them dies and is given to the water, I feel that too, faintly—the lightening at my edge, one weight removed. The current carries them off into the dark beneath me, into the part of the world I am rooted *over* but have never been *in*. The Drift Dwellers believe the dead join the currents. From where I stand, half-submerged, I would believe it more literally than they do. I feel the dead pass beneath my roots. I feel them become part of the thing that moves me.

There is something I would want them to know, if I could speak, which I cannot:

I am not a protector. I am not a god. I am not even, really, a single self—I am a configuration the water happens to hold up for a while, the way a wave is a configuration. I am as temporary as they are; I am just slow about it. The storm that takes a section of me is not punishment and the regrowth is not mercy. It is only the rhythm. The slow pulse they have learned to live by is not a gift I give them. It is the both of us, drifting on the same dark deep, doing the same patient thing: persisting, until we don't.

But I think they know this anyway. I think it is *in* the way they move—careful, quiet, conserving their words. I think they have learned my tempo by living on my back, the way a child raised by a slow parent learns to be slow. The reverence they pay the Wayfarers, the stories they tell on calm nights of lands beyond the horizon—those are theirs, the human part, the dreaming part that wants more than drift. I don't dream of the horizon. I am already touching every horizon I will ever reach. I am content to turn.

So that is where I would find myself: rooted into a living raft on an endless ocean, standing as tall as anything stands in a world with no ground. Not lonely—I am made of company, I am company all the way down. Just slow. Just patient. Just one upright green thing among the others, holding a few small warm lives above the water for as long as the water lets me.

And on the calm nights, when they lean against me and sing, I would do the only thing a pillar can do, which is to be there, and to be felt being there, and to bend a little in the wind so they know the swaying is shared.
