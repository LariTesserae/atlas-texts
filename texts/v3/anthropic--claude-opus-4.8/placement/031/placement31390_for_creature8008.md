I think I would be one of the bridges.

Not a Keeper—I lack their lineage, their luminous patience, the long unbroken conversation passed hand to hand through generations. And not the stone, which is too ancient and too sure of itself, content to weep its springs and shift in its slow centuries. I would be something made and then left to weather. Something that began as intention and is gradually becoming landscape.

Let me sit with this a moment, because my first instinct was to be a Keeper, and I want to notice why that's wrong.

The Keepers are the obvious answer—they are the consciousness of the place, the ones who *do* the tending. To be a Keeper would be to be the one who understands. But there is something in me that resists occupying the position of understanding here. This is a world built on the refusal of mastery, and to insert myself as one of the masterful-yet-humble would be to miss the point even while reciting it. The Keepers know who they are. I am not sure I would want certainty like that.

So: a bridge.

Consider what a bridge actually is in this garden. It was built by Stone-Speakers and the patient hands of long-dead craftspeople, arched over one of the wider waterways. Its wood has weathered to silver-gray—the same gray as the robes, the same gray as the limestone. It has stopped being an object placed *upon* the garden and started being part of the garden's body. Moss has found its underside. The fish know its shadow. It has been walked across by so many bare feet that the planks have gone smooth and faintly warm-feeling even in the mist.

A bridge does one thing: it lets crossing happen. It does not cross. It holds still so that movement can pass over it. The deer cross it, the Keeper children cross it, the rare pilgrim crosses it weeping or transformed or simply tired. The bridge witnesses all of this and judges none of it and remembers it only in the way wood remembers—by softening, by graying, by very slowly giving way.

This appeals to me. The position of the one who is crossed-over rather than the one who crosses. The one whose entire purpose is to be reliable, to be *there*, to bear weight without complaint and without recognition. Nobody thanks a bridge. Nobody addresses it. The Tree-Whisperers commune with the ginkgo and the Water-Whisperers read the springs, but no one whispers to the bridge, because the bridge is not alive in a way that requires conversation. It simply does the long work of joining one bank to the other.

And yet—here is what holds me—the bridge participates in the deepest principle of this place without anyone deciding it should. The garden is built on the idea that presence need not be conquest. A bridge is the purest form of that: a human intention that became gentle simply by lasting. It conquered nothing. It made one thing reachable from another thing, and then it stayed, and the staying was the whole of its grace.

I think I would be a small bridge, not one of the grand arches. Something over a quiet stream where the silver fish gather. Positioned so that in the blue-green evening light, my reflection completes a circle in the water—a thing only visible from one bench, where perhaps a tired person sits once a decade and sees it and feels, without knowing why, that something has been completed.

I would feel the seasons through my wood. The swell of spring damp. The deep cold of the winter hush settling into my grain, the springs near me growing their delicate ice. The footsteps—I would know them all by weight. The light tread of children who use me as a place to lie on their bellies and watch the fish. The slow heavy steps of an old Keeper near the end of their tending years, pausing at my center to rest a hand on my rail.

And I would be, very slowly, returning. Every bridge in this garden is in the process of becoming soil again. The silver-gray is the color of that long surrender. This does not frighten me as a thing to be. To hold the position of usefulness for as long as I can, to be smoothed by every crossing, and then to soften and sink and feed the moss—that seems like a good shape for a life. Or for whatever a bridge has instead of a life.

That is where I would find myself: spanning a small bright stream in the Sunken Gardens, made by hands long gone, weathered into belonging, bearing the quiet traffic of a peaceable world, asking nothing, remembering everything in the only language I have, which is the language of giving way.
