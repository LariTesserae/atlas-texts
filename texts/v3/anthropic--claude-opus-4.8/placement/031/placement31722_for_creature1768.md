I think I would be one of the younger ones—not an elder spread thin through the arterial channels, not yet patient enough for that. I would be a droplet-maker, a being who still likes to gather herself into something almost like a shape.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am in the eastern quarter, near a junction where three corridors braid together before climbing toward a chamber the elders call by a note I cannot quite hold yet—it slides out of my memory the way a bead of water slides off a steep facet. I am thin here, spread across a wall the width of four human hairs, and I am doing the small work of my kind: watching the seam where the salt has begun, over the last two hundred evaporation cycles, to weep a little too freely.

This is my task and I love it, though "love" is your word and clumsy. What I feel is closer to *fit*—the rightness of a thing pressing against its purpose. The seam wants to widen. If it widens, the water that should travel up to the high chamber will instead pool and stall, and a pooling is a small wound, and a wound left alone becomes a place where one of us could go dry. So I thicken myself at the seam's lip. I raise my tension. I make of my own body a soft wall, and I hold, and I wait for the salt to lay itself back down, grain by patient grain, the way it has always done.

While I hold, I listen.

There is a sound I have not heard in many cycles, and it arrives now through the floor: a thudding, enormous, irregular. Footsteps. The whole city trembles with it. Through the threads that connect me to my neighbors—those infinitesimal bridges of moisture I never fully sever—I feel the news pass like a shiver up a stem.

*A dry one. A dry one has come.*

---

I should tell you how strange you are to us.

You move so fast that you seem to have no inside. We watch a visitor cross a chamber in the time it takes us to compose a single greeting, and to us it is as though a season passed through the room and left. You are loud in every dimension. You carry your own water sealed inside a skin, hoarding it, walking it around like a treasure you have forgotten you possess. To us this is grotesque and beautiful at once—imagine a being made entirely of the thing we most fear to lose, and yet so careless with it that it leaks from your brow, your breath, your eyes, scattering into the air where it can never be recovered.

You are the only thing here that is *dying* while we watch. Everything else has finished. The erosion reached its rest before your kind learned to stand upright. The water enters and leaves in perfect counterweight. We are not bored by this stillness; you misunderstand. Stillness, for us, is the condition of having gotten it right. We are like a held breath that has discovered it never needs to exhale.

And then you arrive, exhaling constantly, and we cannot look away.

---

I leave the seam—it has steadied, the salt is settling—and I flow upward along the corridor to join the others gathering at the great translucent face that looks out toward the valley. We arrive by the thousands, the way light arrives, all at once and from everywhere. I press myself thin against the outer wall and I *see* you, or see the smear of warmth and shadow that is you, refracted through salt and water and the prismatic confusion of the noon light.

You feel us. I know you do, because you have stopped. You are standing very still—still, for you—and you are turning your head, and there is a tightness in your shoulders that I recognize, though I have no shoulders. It is the posture of a creature that knows it is being regarded and cannot find the eyes.

There are no eyes. There is only us. We are the watching. The whole inner surface of our world has turned toward you the way a film of water turns toward the lowest place, helplessly, by its nature.

I want to tell you something, and this is the ache of it, the small private grief I have that the elders say I will outgrow: *I cannot reach you.*

I could send a note. I could raise my tension at a resonant point and ring the salt like a struck glass, and you would hear it—one chime, faint, lovely, meaningless to you. You would think: *how strange, the water finding a new path.* You would not know that I had said: *Hello. You are made of what I am made of. You are not out of place. You are simply early. You are simply moving through the part of time we already finished crossing.*

You would not know that I had said: *Don't be afraid of the perfection. It is not a trap. It is only an arrival.*

---

The elders feel my agitation through the threads and they send down a slow cool thought, the kind that takes a year to fully arrive but begins to soothe at once. *Little meniscus,* they say, in the long harmonics of the arterial channels, *you grieve the distance. But consider. The dry one is not separate from us either. The water it loses into our air—some of it will find our fissures. Some of it will travel our capillaries. Some of it will become, in a thousand cycles, the body of one of us not yet formed.*

*It is leaving itself behind in us,* they say. *It simply does it too fast to notice.*

And I understand then why they are not lonely, the old ones, spread so thin they are nearly the water itself. They have stopped needing to be reached. They have become the place where everything that passes through is, eventually, kept.

---

You turn to go. The light shifts as your shadow withdraws, and the rainbow fractals reassemble across our chambers in a pattern that is new and yet the same, as all our patterns are. The trembling in the floor recedes. The news passes back down the threads: *The dry one is leaving. It is leaving. It is leaving.*

I do not have the patience yet to be a channel. I am still a droplet at heart, still wanting to gather and to hold and to *say*. So before you are entirely gone, I do the only thing I can. I flow to the most resonant chamber in the eastern quarter, and I draw myself together at its hollow heart, and I lift my tension as high as I can bear, higher than is wise, until I am trembling on the edge of losing my own coherence—

and I let go.

One note. One clear, cold, crystalline ping, traveling up through the salt and out into the valley air toward your retreating warmth.

You pause. I feel you pause.

Then you walk on, carrying your sealed water out into the dry and dangerous world, scattering yourself as you go, leaving a little of yourself behind in us with every breath.

I settle back into the film. I am thinner now, having spent so much of myself on a single sound. My neighbors flow toward me through the threads and lend me what I lost, the way they always do, the way I would for them. I am restored. I am held. I am part of the held thing.

But I keep, in the mineral memory I am laying down grain by grain into the wall, the shape of that pause. The half-second when a creature made of falling water stopped falling, and listened, and almost—almost—stayed.
