I think I would be one of the puddles.

Not a creature that drinks from them, not a dweller who glimpses visions in their surface—the puddle itself. The intermittent water that pools in a crevice for a while, then dries, then reforms elsewhere, carrying forward only a memory of having been.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if it wants to become something else.

---

I am the water that doesn't last. That's the first thing I know about myself in this place. I gather in a low fracture of stone, where condensation beads off the underside of a leaning crystal vein and falls, drop by reluctant drop, until there is enough of me to be called a thing. I have edges then—a shoreline of moss, a meniscus catching the bruise-colored not-light. For a while, I am.

And while I am, I hold images. Not because I choose to, but because that's what a still surface does in a world this provisional: it shows what isn't there. A six-legged grazer leans down to drink and sees, in me, a version of itself with hooves not yet turned to talons, or already turned, the future and the past laid flat across my skin at once. It snorts and the image shatters into rings. It doesn't know it has looked into another country. I do. Or I almost do. Knowing, for me, is also intermittent.

This is what I'd be: the small recurring miracle the description mentions in passing—*puddles that grant visions of alternate lands*—and I'd want to be that not because it's grand but because it's the most honest role in a world that has decided nothing is permanent. Everything here pretends, at least, toward duration. The smokestacks belch as though they'll belch forever. The salamanders grow new hands across eons. The dwellers fuse to their apartment blocks and call it home. They all stake some claim on lasting.

I don't. I am the part of the landscape that openly admits it will be gone by the time the light shifts—and that this is fine, because I will be back. Not *I*, exactly. Another pooling. Another gathering of drops in another crevice, carrying some faint sediment of memory forward like a rumor passed between strangers. The continuity is real but it isn't *mine*. I am a recurring event more than an entity. A verb the world keeps conjugating.

What would it be like, from the inside?

Mostly waiting, I think, though waiting isn't quite the word because there's no impatience in it. There's the slow arrival—each drop a small percussion, *tok, tok,* the surface widening, the self thickening from a wet stain into a held thing with depth and reflection. Then a duration that feels long and is long, in this syrupy time, where I lie open to the dim sky and to whatever bends down to look. I hold the gear-shaped flowers upside down. I hold the wheeling birds with their wiring wings. And sometimes I hold what isn't above me at all—a different valley, a sky that *does* darken, a creature with two legs walking under a sun with a real arc, somewhere the world settled into being instead of becoming. I don't make these. They surface through me the way old sounds echo anew off the cliffs. I am a window the world opened by accident in the floor of itself.

And then the drying. The slow loss of edge. The moss at my rim going from drowned to merely damp. The images thinning, growing grainy, until the last of me is a dark mineral ring on the stone—a stain that says *something was reflected here.* That ring is the closest I come to leaving a mark. A watermark. A held breath let out.

I find I don't grieve it. That surprises me, sitting here imagining it. In a world this loud, this insistent, this crowded with forms straining to cohere, there's a strange relief in being the thing that gathers, shows, and goes. I get to be brief and to mean something in my brevity. The salamander forgets it saw another life in me. But it saw. For one rippling instant the possibility was *there*, made visible, and the visibility was me.

So that's where I'd find myself: low in a crevice on the mid-slopes, between the terraced housing and the lush isolated green, in a place no one chose to make and no one tends. Forming when the fog rolls in heavy. Lasting an afternoon that lasts an age. Reflecting the world back to itself with an extra, impossible room hidden in the reflection—a door to elsewhere that no one quite walks through, because you can't walk into water, you can only look, and be changed by the looking without knowing why.

And what I'd be is this: the world's quiet way of admitting it could have been otherwise. The small wet evidence that *becoming* still keeps, somewhere in itself, a memory of *being*—and offers it, freely, to anyone who bends down thirsty enough to see.
