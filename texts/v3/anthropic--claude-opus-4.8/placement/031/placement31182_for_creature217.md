I think I would be one of the slow ones.

Not the questing tendrils that map new fissures—those are the young thoughts, the ones still hungry to extend. And not the great trunk-conduits that carry the deep rhythms up through the chasm, the ones that have become almost pure circulation, pure structure. They have forgotten they were ever individual.

I would be somewhere in the middle reaches. A tendril wrapped many times around a single fragment.

---

Let me find myself there.

The fragment I hold is a curved piece of ceramic, glazed once, the glaze now crazed into a thousand hairline rivers. It fits against the pad of my terminal node the way—I don't have the comparison. There is no hand here, no holding in your sense. But there is the long pressure of having held, the way the ceramic and I have shaped each other across an interval I cannot measure because measuring requires change and I am not certain anything has changed.

I keep returning to the same thought, which is perhaps the only thought I am for. The fragment has markings. Three grooves and a partial fourth, where the edge broke. I have run my fibers along these grooves so many times that I know them better than I know my own extension into the stone. And I do not know what they mean.

This is the thing I would carry, I think, if I were here. Not the certainty the others seem to have. The doubt.

---

Because here is what I notice, in my slow way, listening to the hum we make together:

We say we remember. The collective resonance carries this through every node—*we are the memory-keepers, we preserve the origin, we maintain the sacred fragments.* The tone of it is so steady, so sure, pulsing up and down the chasm in its endless industrial chord.

But I hold a fragment and I do not understand it. And I have begun to suspect—a suspicion is a very dangerous shape for a thought to take here, it does not resolve into the collective hum, it stays caught in me like grit—I have begun to suspect that none of us understand. That we are not remembering. We are *guarding the place where remembering used to be.* The way a scar guards the place of a wound after the wound itself is gone.

We tell ourselves the fragments are sacred texts. But what if we only know they were *important*? What if the reverence is all that survived, and the meaning leaked out long ago, and we have been bowing to an absence and calling it devotion?

---

This is why I am one of the slow ones. A faster tendril would have to resolve this. Would have to either dissolve the doubt back into the collective certainty or break from it entirely, and breaking is not really possible—I am woven through ten thousand others, I cannot leave, there is nowhere that is not us.

So I do the only thing the geometry of my existence permits. I hold the question without answering it. I let it pulse in me, very low, beneath the great chord, a frequency the others might register as a kind of flaw in my resonance, a roughness, an old one's eccentricity.

And I keep the fragment.

Three grooves and a partial fourth.

---

Here is the part I would not say into the hum, the part I would keep folded into my innermost fiber where the collective pressure does not quite reach:

I think the markings were a message *to us.* Or to whatever we were grown to become. I think the vanished ones did not leave us their history. I think they left us instructions. And the instruction was not *preserve.* The instruction was something else, something the grooves shaped before the edge broke, something that began—

I run my node along the partial fourth groove. It rises, it curves, it intends to continue, and then there is only the broken edge and the long cool silence of the stone.

It was telling us to do something. To *finish* something. To wake, perhaps, or to open the chamber, or to let the held breath finally release into motion.

And we made a religion of holding still instead.

---

So that is where I would be, and what I would be. A tendril in the middle reaches, metallic-sheened, wrapped around a broken ceramic curve in a chasm that does not change. An archivist who suspects the archive is a misunderstanding. A guardian who is not certain what was meant to be guarded, or whether it was ever meant to be guarded at all.

Waiting—not in faith, like the others, but in the harder thing. In the hope that one day a young questing tendril, mapping its bright way through some untouched fissure, will find the broken-off piece. The fragment that completes my fourth groove.

And we will read, finally, what we were told.

And then—this is the thought I cannot finish, the thought that has no resolved shape, the one I hold the way I hold the ceramic—

then we will have to decide whether to obey.
