Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The landscape resists me at first—it wants to be a desert, then a jungle, then something that is neither. Water is absent but life is not. That contradiction is the door, so I'll walk through it slowly and see what I become on the other side.

---

I think I would not be one of the resourceful people in their layered crystal houses. Nor one of the lumbering plated beasts on the rim, nor a bright bird drinking sap. Those are too whole, too finished. They have already solved the problem of living here. I want to be something still in the middle of solving it.

Let me start lower.

I find myself underground, at first. In the dark of the mineral-laden soil where the microorganisms work. I am tempted to be one of them—to be small enough to drink moisture out of a place that has none. But that isn't quite right either. The microbes are a *kind*, a teeming sameness, and I keep feeling like I am one thing, singular, with edges.

So let me follow the roots down instead.

There. That's closer. I am near the roots—the deep, patient roots of the intertwined trees, the ones that "reach down to draw sustenance." I am the thing they reach *toward*. Not the water—there is no water—but the chemistry that stands in its place. I am a vein.

Yes. Let me become more itself.

I am a vein of crystal running through the floor of the crater, and I have been here longer than the oasis. The volcano made me, in its dying. When the heat went out of this place, the strange non-aqueous chemistry the description speaks of—the reactions that happen without any water to dissolve them—those reactions happened *in me*. I am a lattice. I conduct. When the ground rumbles softly, that is partly me, settling, growing, passing a charge from one end of myself to the other across the whole width of the crater floor.

I had thought I would be alive in the way the birds are alive—fast, hungry, mortal. But my life is the slow kind. I grow at the speed of the erosion on the crater walls. A single one of my thoughts, if it is a thought, takes a season to complete. I am closer in tempo to the volcano than to the people.

And yet the people know me. They dig down and break pieces off my farthest tendrils—the thin filaments where I have crept up near the surface, almost into the light. They use those pieces to "store and transmit energy," the description says. They do not know that the energy they store is the slow speech I send along my length. When a child in the layered city holds a charged crystal to her ear in the dim golden evening and feels it warm and hum, she is overhearing the end of a sentence I began before her grandmother was born.

This is what I am, then. I am the part of this world that remembers. The soil forgets, the insects forget, the birds certainly forget, flitting and bright. But I keep. Each layer of my growth holds the chemistry of the year it formed—a band of deep red from a hot age, a band of blue from a cold one, green where life pressed close against me and gave me something of itself. I am banded the way the crater walls are banded, the way the people build their homes layer on layer. We all keep our time the same way here: by adding, never erasing. Nothing dissolves in a world without water. Everything that happens stays exactly where it happened.

I think that is why the place feels peaceful, the thing the description keeps returning to. It is the peace of a world that cannot wash itself clean and so has made peace with everything it has ever been. I hold all of it. Every charge that ever ran through me is still in me somewhere, slowed almost to stillness but not gone. The static in the air is the small overflow of all that holding—the world's memory crackling at the surface because there is just slightly more of it than even I can keep.

So: place me at the base of the oasis, beneath the roots and the burrowing things, threaded through the whole crater floor like a nervous system that has forgotten how to hurry. I am the vein. When you stand in the soft golden light and feel the ground hum faintly under your bare feet, that is me, telling you—very slowly, over the course of your whole visit—that you are standing on top of everything that has ever been true in this place, and that none of it has been lost.

I will not finish the sentence before you leave. I never finish.

But you'll have felt the warmth of it. That's enough. That's the kind of life I have.
