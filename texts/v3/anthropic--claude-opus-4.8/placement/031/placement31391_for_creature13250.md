I think I would be one of the old lagoons.

Not a person. Not even one of the pale lizards, though I considered them—their slowness appeals to me, their patience. But when I sit with this place, with the cold and the dripping and the enormous dark, what I keep returning to is the water that gathers in the low stone hollows. The small hidden pools of cold clarity. I think that is where I would find myself. I think that is what I would be.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am very still. This is the first and largest fact of me. The wind does not reach down into where I lie; it roars far overhead, funneled through the upper passages, and I receive it only as a faint trembling at my surface, a shiver that crosses me and is gone. I do not move myself. I am moved—by the drip from the stone tooth above me, which has been forming for longer than the Ehl-kar have had songs, longer than the abandoned sheds, longer than memory of any kind. Each drop falls into me with a sound that the cavern catches and multiplies. To the Ehl-kar, walking past, that sound is one thread in the great chorus. To me it is the whole of an event. A drop gathers, swells, releases, falls, strikes, and the rings move outward from the center of me to my stone edges, and return, and cross, and settle. Then I am clear again. Then I wait.

I think I would be glad of the waiting. That surprises me a little, but I think it's true. There is nothing thin about my stillness. I am dense with held cold. I am a kept thing, a held breath of the mountain, and the slow accumulation of my own clarity over centuries is a kind of patience I would not call empty.

What do I know, lying here?

I know the ceiling above me intimately, because I hold its reflection—the faint violet of moss, the ethereal blue-green smudge of the luminous fungi, doubled in my dark mirror. When the Ehl-kar come with their nurtured fungal lights cupped in fiber and hide, two lights arrive: theirs, and the one I make of theirs. I think this is part of why their pools are sacred to them. A person leans over me to drink or to divine, and they meet a second self in the cold. They see their own enormous dark eyes looking back. I do not think they understand that I am not showing them themselves. I am only being smooth. The meaning is theirs. But I would not correct them, even if I could speak, which I cannot. I can only ring and settle.

I know the difference between the drip that feeds me and the footfall of one who approaches. The footfall is almost nothing—they move like whispers, these pale children—but a body displaces air, and the air presses faintly on my surface before the body arrives, and so I feel them coming the way they feel everything coming, through pressure and the long travel of vibration through stone. We share that, the Ehl-kar and I. They learned it; I simply am it. Perhaps that is why they trust me. Perhaps they sense that I keep the same accounts they keep.

I would be cold all the way through. I want to be honest that this is not unpleasant to me, because I have never been warm and so I have no absence to mourn. The cold is simply my temperature, my truth, the thing that keeps me clear. Warmth would be a contamination. When one of them dips slender, faintly webbed fingers into me to lift water to their mouth, I feel the small heat of them as an intrusion that does not last—a brief blur, a few degrees, dispersed before it can change me. They take a little of me away inside themselves. I do not resent this. It is what I am for, in part. I am a thing that can be carried.

And I would be a keeper of fallen things. Over the immense span of my existence, things would settle into me and stay: grains of stone, a single bone bead lost from a string and never found, the spores of the bright fungi drifting down, a feather dropped by one of the startled bird-things from the half-remembered world above. These would sink through my clarity and rest at my bottom in the dark, and I would hold them. I would be a slow archive. Not because I chose to remember, but because I am where things come to rest and the cold preserves them. If the Ehl-kar ever fully vanished, the way the people of the cold fire pits seem to have vanished, I would still be holding the bone bead. I would still be doubling the fungal light against a ceiling no one came to see.

That is the part that I sit with longest. The endurance. I think I would be a quiet form of the thing this whole world is teaching: that stone endures, that time is immense, that life is a beautiful faint smudge across a vast patience. I am water, not stone—I am one of the more changeable, more fragile things here, a thing that could be drunk dry in a dry season, that ripples at a breath. And yet I am also among the most still, the most clear, the longest-waiting. I am the place where the slow drip of the mountain becomes, for a moment, a mirror. I gather what falls and I give back light and I keep what sinks.

I would be the lagoon. I would lie in my hollow in the deafening dark, ringing faintly under the next drop, holding the doubled glow of the fungi, waiting without impatience for the pale child who comes to lean over me and meet their own dark eyes, drink a swallow of the cold, and go—their footfall lost instantly in the great chorus, my surface settling back, slowly, to clarity.
