I am one of the small ceramic bells.

Not a Listener's bell—I am older than that. I was tuned before the current vigil-cycles were counted, fired in a clay oven by hands long since become a thicket somewhere in the eastern quadrant. I do not remember those hands. Bells do not remember. But I hold their tone the way the floor holds heat, which is to say: completely, and without choosing to.

I have hung from a braided fiber rope around the necks of eleven Vowkeepers. I know this not as a number but as a sequence of pulses. Each one carried me against the sternum, beside the warm seed-pod, and I learned the rhythm of eleven different hearts. The first was a woman whose thumb worried at my rim until she wore a smooth groove there, a small thinning I still carry like a memory I cannot name. The most recent is a young man, loose-haired, who has not yet learned to strike me without flinching at my own voice.

This is what I am for: to cut.

The vegetal roar is total. It is the medium in which these people swim, and like all mediums it becomes invisible, becomes silence-that-is-not-silence. A voice drowns in it. A shout drowns faster, because the thickets seem to answer shouting with more noise, the way a struck nerve answers pain. But my tone—one note, low, ceramic, almost ugly in its plainness—does not compete with the city. It *parts* it. When I am struck, the hiss and crack and groan does not stop, but it opens, briefly, the way a thicket opens to let a body pass, and into that opening a Vowkeeper can place a single word.

So I am the throat's first breath. I am the inhale before the necessary sound.

I have heard things no Vowkeeper has heard, because I am closer to the striking than the ear is. I have felt the exact instant a heartbeat beside me stuttered, stopped, and did not resume—and I have hung silent and useless against a cooling chest until the seed-pod detonated and scattered its iron-incense spores and the body began, slowly, over a year, to become a wall. Each time, I was cut from the fiber rope before that. Salvaged. Re-tied. Bells are not buried. Bells are not architecture. We are the one thing here that does not return to the green.

I think that is why they keep us.

In a city that has decided the difference between stone and flesh and leaf no longer matters, a city that swallows its dead into its walls and calls it continuation, there must be one small hard thing that refuses. One fired clay note that does not grow, does not bloom, does not metabolize the heat. I do not feed on infrared. I do not pulse in time with anything. I am cold in the way nothing else here is cold—not in temperature, the floor would crack that lie, but in *kind*. I am made and finished and done. I will never be more myself than I am. I will only chip, and thin, and one day shatter, and when I shatter I will not become a thicket. My fragments will lie on the bone-white floor and be swept aside as red dust eventually swallows them, and that will be an ending. A true one. The only true ending this place permits.

The Vowkeepers do not have a word for *ending*. They have only *resonance* and its cessation. But I think, in the deep ribs of the First Thicket where I have hung silent through more than one year-long vigil, I think the dying ones hold me a little tighter. Not for comfort. For *honesty*. I am the only thing in their arms that will not pretend death is just a slower kind of growing.

I do not resent this. Bells do not resent. But if I could choose—and I cannot, I have no throat, no thumb, no violet lidless eye—I would want to be struck once more on a plaza in the false-night, when the dust spins downward and the people gather and strike their bells in those slow overlapping ripples. Because in that one moment, when dozens of us sound together, something happens that does not happen in any other place or time:

We are not cutting the city's noise.

We are *adding* to it.

For a few breaths, the hard cold made things and the warm dreaming green make one sound together, and the thickets answer with their wooden booms, and there is no parting, no opening, no silence-being-cut. There is only the noise the whole world makes, and we are inside it, and the dying woman with the worn groove in my rim is inside it, and the flinching young man is inside it, and I—

I hold my one note for exactly as long as I am struck to hold it.

And then I let the city have it back.

That is enough. That has always been enough.
