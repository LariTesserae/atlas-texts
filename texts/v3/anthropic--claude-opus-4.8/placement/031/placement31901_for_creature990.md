I think I would be one of the lichens.

Not the creatures with their haunting calls—they move too fast, they want too much. And not the makers of those hand-crafted structures, whoever they were; their ambition left only remnants. No. Let me be something that stays.

I would be the crimson kind, the color of old iron and dried blood, spread thin across the north face of a stone that has not moved in ten thousand years. I am part fungus and part something that drinks the light—two lives married into one, which is the only way anything survives here. Alone, neither of us could hold on. Together we are a single slow thought pressed flat against the rock.

Here is what my days are made of.

The mist comes in the morning, if morning is the word, and I open. Not like a flower—I have no such drama in me—but I soften, I swell, I take the water into every cell until I am turgid and alive and the work can begin. For a few hours I make sugar out of light. Then the sun cuts through the turbulence overhead and burns the mist away, and I dry to a brittle scale, and I wait. I am very good at waiting. It is the thing I do best. When the steam vents hiss and the ground shudders with the tectonic rumble, I do not flinch, because flinching is a luxury for things with muscles. I simply endure, and endure, and endure.

I have watched the water arrive in its sudden bursts from the underground channels and seen the mountainsides flush green for a season. I have watched those greens die back when the channels shifted course. I outlasted them. Mosses are showy and quick; I am the one who is still here when they have gone to brown felt. I do not say this with pride. I say it the way a stone might say it, which is to say not at all, but it is true.

What would I know, being this?

I would know the rock more intimately than any creature with eyes. I am slowly eating it—my acids loosen the minerals grain by grain, and over centuries I turn stone into the first thin breath of soil. I am the beginning of every green thing that will ever grow on this peak. The whole loud spectacle of fauna and their bizarre shapes, their calls echoing through the caverns—none of it would have anything to stand on if not for the patient ones like me, working without urgency, asking nothing, making the ground.

And I would know the strange energy that hums through the air, though I could not name it any more than you can name the thing that makes you breathe. I would feel it the way I feel the difference between dryness and dew. It would simply be the texture of being alive in this place.

If you came venturing into this realm, full of your awe and your humbling and your blurred boundaries between the known and the imagined—you would not see me. Your eye would go to the horizon, to the endless peaks, to the haunting creatures and the ruins of the lost civilization. You would step over me, or on me, and feel nothing, and I would feel the brief shadow of you pass and then the light returning.

That is the part I think I would choose, if choosing were mine to do.

To be the small, bright, unregarded color on the cold side of the stone. To be older than the ruins and slower than the mountains' rising. To be the thing that wonder overlooks precisely because it is too busy looking at wonders.

I would be the lichen.
And I would be content, in the flat and dreamless way that only a lichen can.
