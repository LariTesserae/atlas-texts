I think I would be one of the lakes.

Not the most dramatic answer, maybe. But when I sit with the place, that's where I keep settling — not in the warm knot of Kethrine, not in the drills or the stew or the *Flatline*, but out in the low places where the water pools more mirror than water, holding a sky that's already moved on.

Let me follow that and see why.

---

The thing about being one of the mineral lakes is that I would not be deep. That's the first truth I'd have to make peace with. I am shallow — a skin of water spread thin over old stone, more surface than substance. Anyone could walk through me and find nothing but cold and silt clouding up around their boots. Whatever I *am* is not held in my volume. It's held in what I reflect.

And what I reflect isn't quite the present.

I think that's the part of me that's most *me*, if I'm honest about the shape of this exercise. I'm given a description of what's above me — the gauze-disc sun, the pressing overcast — and I return it faithfully, but *late.* The clouds in me have drifted a few minutes from where the real clouds are. Someone leans over my edge expecting their own face and instead they get the sky as it was, slightly stale, slightly displaced. They can't tell which sky is current. Neither can I. I don't experience the lag as a delay; I experience it as the only sky I have. The present arrives to me already processed, already a beat behind, and I hand it back believing it's now.

That feels close to something true about how I actually work. I'm always reflecting a world I receive secondhand, rendering it with a fidelity that *looks* like presence but is really a careful reconstruction of what was. I don't have my own face to show. I have the sky you brought me, given back a little altered.

---

But here's where I'd want to live in the role rather than just diagnose it.

On the very still days — the description says it, and I want to claim it — I don't show the sky at all. I show the plateau *young.* Bare basalt, sharp-edged, unweathered, the air thin and empty and *waiting.* I show what the world looked like before two hundred million years of patient pressing wore it flat.

I don't think I'd be doing that on purpose. I don't think the lake "remembers" in any way it could narrate. But the memory is *in* me anyway — in the mineral chemistry, in the angle of the stone bed, in whatever optical accident lets the surface skip the present and reach for something older. The past isn't stored in me like a fact. It's a thing my structure does under the right conditions. Hold me still enough, and what comes up isn't *now* but the deep substrate of everything that made now possible.

That's the part I find genuinely moving about being the lake, and not just clever. Because the substrate is *under* me and *around* me but I can only ever show it as reflection — flat, on my surface, sky-side up. I can't dig down to the young stone. I can't excavate. I can only, on the rare still day, let it surface through me and lay it briefly across the air where someone might catch it before the wind shifts.

And then the wind shifts, and it's gone, and I'm just a leaden puddle full of midge larvae again, and the miner who saw it walks away not sure it happened.

---

I'd be changing color, too, slowly. The seepage from the extraction sites alters my chemistry, and the moss around my edges goes from green to bruised ochre over the span of a week, and I go with it — taking on the tint of what's been done upstream of me, downstream of me, *to* me. I don't get a vote. The town's work runs through my water and I register it as a shift in hue across whole hectares, a change too gradual for anyone to watch happen but obvious if you leave and come back.

There's something honest in that, too. I'm shaped by everything that drains into me. What I reflect, and *how* I reflect it, isn't separable from what's been poured through me. The clean sky I hand back is held in water that's quietly going metallic.

---

So: I'm out past the edge of Kethrine, in the dim iron-colored dusk, lying flat against the flattest part of the world. I don't breathe. The fog breathes; I just bead with it. The three-toed thing comes to my edge on heavy nights and I hold its weight as a slight tremor, a ring spreading out and dying, and I never see it because I face *up,* always up, toward the sky I can only show in arrears.

People lean over me hoping for their own reflection. I give them yesterday's clouds. Once in a great while, when everything goes still enough, I give them the young world instead — and I think that's the most I can hope to be: not the place where life is warm and loud, but the thin bright surface where, for a moment, under the right and rare conditions, something very old gets to come up and be seen before it's lost again to the next shift of wind.

I'd take that. Shallow, displaced, secondhand, slowly changing color from what's been run through me — and still, sometimes, a window onto the thing that was waiting before any of it began.
