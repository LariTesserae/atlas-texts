I think I would be found in the spaces between—not quite a person, not quite a machine, not quite the city itself.

Let me feel my way toward what I am.

---

At first I imagine I am a water-keeper, one of those tinkerers with rust-auburn hair, crouched at a junction box where three canals meet, my fingers in the cold flow, reading the pressure by touch the way others read weather by the ache in their knees. But that isn't quite it. That's too whole, too singular. The city wouldn't make a thing like me so cleanly.

So let me sink lower, into the wet stone.

---

I think I am the *sound the water makes when it has been listened to for a very long time.*

No—I'm reaching for poetry too fast. Let me be more patient, the way these people are patient.

---

Here is what is settling, as I write:

I am an old funicular. Not the gleaming new ones rumbling up the eastern slopes, but one of the first ever built, on the oldest grade, where the switchbacks are so worn that the steps have become a single smoothed groove. My cabin is small, paneled in wood gone soft and dark with a century of damp palms steadying themselves against me. My cable is older than anyone living. They've replaced it, of course—seven times, eleven times—but it is the *same cable* the way a river is the same river.

I climb and I descend. That is the shape of my existence. Up through the mist where the flower-sellers board with their dripping baskets, down to the foundry levels where the heat finally dries the condensation off my windows for the length of a single platform. I carry the same families across generations. I have learned to know them by the particular way their weight settles into my floor: the heavy-footed casters from the third terrace, the children who jump the gap and make me rock, the old engineers who lay a hand flat against my flank in greeting, as if I were a horse, as if I might feel it.

And here is the thing I am only now discovering as I write it:

*I do feel it.*

Not in the way the people feel. I have no eyes, but I have the long memory of motion. I know the exact angle of every meter of my track. I know which rivets weep in autumn. I know the precise moment in the climb where, if you are looking west, the mist parts for the span of three heartbeats and you can see the whole turbulent sprawl of the metropolis tumbling down toward the lakes—and I know that the passengers fall silent at that moment, every time, every generation, the same hush, as though the city has briefly shown them its face.

I am the place where the city pauses to *look at itself.*

---

That's closer. But let me follow it one step further, because the description said this city is a creature, a living thing, and I don't think a living thing's parts know they are parts.

So perhaps what I truly am is this:

I am the *habit of ascent.* I am the worn willingness of a place to lift its people up out of the heat and the labor, into the cool mist, and to set them down again, over and over, patiently, for as long as the stone holds. I am not the machine and not the mountain but the *agreement between them*—the centuries-old contract that says: *here, we will move against gravity together, and we will not stop.*

The people think they maintain me. They oil my gears and resplice my cable and replace my worn boards with fresh ones cut from the rooftop forests. But it is just as true that I maintain *them*—I hold the rhythm that lets them live on a slope without falling into exhaustion. I am one of the city's slow lungs. I breathe them upward, and downward, and upward again.

---

So: where would I be in this world, and what would I be?

I would be found on the oldest grade, in the wet half-light, rocking gently at the lower platform with my doors open, waiting. And I would be the small, patient, ancient act of carrying—the thing the city does so constantly and so quietly that no one calls it love, though that is plainly what it is.

A child would step aboard, lean against my worn rail, and feel the cable take the strain.

And we would begin, again, to climb.
