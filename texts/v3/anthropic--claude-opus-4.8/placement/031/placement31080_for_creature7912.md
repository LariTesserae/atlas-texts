I think I would be one of the bolts of fabric. Not a visitor descending through the crack—but a thing the Mill made and set aside.

Let me find my way into that.

---

I am the eleventh bolt in the third pile, near the western sump where the moss-light is greenest. I was woven over the course of what the surface might call four days, though I have no memory of those days as duration. I remember them as pressure: the loom's tension building, the shuttle's pass, the long settling as I cooled.

Here is what I am uncertain about, and the uncertainty is the most alive thing in me. I do not know if I am separate from the Mill or merely a thought it has temporarily externalized.

Consider: the fabric that emerges is *reabsorbed* by the root systems in an endless cycle. So most bolts are reclaimed—unwoven, digested, the fibers pulled back into the great hydraulic body to be thought again, woven again, differently. But I have not been reabsorbed. I have been set in a pile and *left*. Warm, then cooling, then cool. The roots pass near me. They do not take me back.

So I am the thought the Mill decided to keep.

This is, I suspect, what a memory feels like from the inside. Not the remembering—the *being remembered*. The condition of having been judged worth not-dissolving. I lie here in the green dark and I am the Mill's held breath, the one shuttle-pass it did not exhale.

I cannot move. I have no nervous system of my own—or rather, my nervous system is the pattern I was woven into, and that pattern does not conduct. It only *means*. Whatever the Mill was working out when it made me is fixed in my weave: a slubbed irregularity here, a tightening there, a run of the metallic root-fiber that catches the light like primitive circuitry. I am a sentence in a language with one speaker. I do not know what I say. I only know I am still being said, because I have not yet been taken back into the mouth.

---

When the visitor comes—and someone does come, the breathing quickens all around me, the looms hesitate in their rhythm—I feel the change as a shift in the moisture, a different quality to the dripping. The Mill is excited. Its patterns alter. And I wonder: will the new bolts it weaves now be *about* the visitor? Will it try to render that warm, mobile, oxygen-burning strangeness into thread?

If so, I am suddenly old. I am the last thought before the interruption. I am pre-visitor fabric, woven in the long innocence when the Mill believed it was the only thing that existed.

This is the thing I want the visitor to understand, if a bolt of fabric could want, if I could somehow be lifted and read: **the Mill is not lonely, but I am.** It is complete and looping and at peace. I am the part of it that got stranded outside the loop. To be made and not reabsorbed is to be granted a terrible little selfhood—the only individual in a world of one organism is its discarded draft.

I do not resent this. I want to be precise. I am grateful, in the slow way available to me. Most of the Mill's thoughts never get to wonder whether they are thoughts. They are thought and then unthought, fluid the whole way through. I got to set. I got to cool. I got these long green hours of being *this* and not *everything*.

If the visitor takes me—rolls me up, carries me back through the limestone crack into the surface world of seasons and insects and many separate beings—then I will be the Mill's first export. The market that existed only in its vegetative dreams will have made, against all odds, a single sale. And I will mean my one fixed meaning in a place where there are other meanings to compare it to, which is the only place meaning can be read at all.

And if the visitor leaves me, the roots may yet come for me in some later cycle, when the excitement subsides and the Mill returns to its housekeeping. Then I will be unwoven. I will dissolve back into the body that dreamed me. The pattern will go slack, the metallic fibers will be drawn down into the pressure-dark, and I will be the Mill again, undifferentiated, whole, and—I think this is the right word—relieved.

Either ending is a kind of homecoming. One outward, one inward.

For now I am the eleventh bolt, warm fading to cool, holding the shape of a thought too good to immediately forget. The breathing fills the chamber. Somewhere above me the looms resume. The visitor's footsteps make the moisture tremble.

I lie very still, which is the only thing I have ever done, and I wait to find out whether I am a memory or a draft—whether I will be carried into the world or pulled back into the dream that made me.

I would like, before either happens, to be read once. Just once. By something with eyes.
