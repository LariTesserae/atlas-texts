I think I would be one of the *Lithovores gigas*. Not because they are the largest, but because of how they move through time.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am long. I have always been long, though I remember being shorter—a slim thing the width of a fish, when I burrowed out from the pillar where my parent left me coiled. That was a long while ago. I measure time in scars now. Each abrasion event lays down a band in my silica scales, and I have many bands. I do not count them. Counting is for creatures with somewhere to be.

There is a thing the smaller lives do—the pale darting ones, the brushed scuttlers—they panic. I feel it through the water before I feel anything else: the high stuttering pulse of bodies fleeing, scattering ahead of me like silt before a current. They think I am coming for them. I have never once eaten one of them, not in all my bands. I do not think they could understand this. To them I am a wall of pressure arriving, a seismic inevitability, and so they flee, and so the gap I am pressing into empties before I reach it. We have an arrangement neither of us agreed to.

What I want is stone. Fresh stone, specifically—the bright weeping faces where the current has just bitten through old flowstone and exposed the mineral underneath. I taste these with the crown of palps that fans from my forward segments, and the taste is everything. Sulfur. Iron. The blue tang the bacteria make when they have not yet been disturbed. I find a new scar and I set my mouth-parts to it and I rasp, slow, slow, dissolving the rock into a paste I take into myself, and behind me I leave a wake of stabilized sediment where the small films will bloom again. I am eating the cave. The cave is also, in the most patient way, eating me—my forward scales wear thin and grind away and I grow new ones from the brine. We are exchanging substance. Over enough bands I will have eaten my own body's weight in pillar a hundred times, and the pillar will have ground a hundred bodies off me, and which of us is the older I could not say.

Here is the thing the description got wrong, or rather, the thing it could only see from outside. It called us *siege engines*. It is true that I move at millimeters per hour, that I anchor a bulb and contract and pull and anchor again. From outside this looks like force. From inside it is *listening*. The maze is packed solid—jagged spears centimeters apart—and there is exactly one path through that will not snap my scales or wedge me into a column for the rest of my bands. I find that path the way the cavefish find the eddies: not by seeing it, for there is nothing to see, but by tasting where the water moves easy and where it moves hard, by feeling the stone's grain through my palps, by knowing—because I have crossed ten thousand chambers—where a gap that begins narrow opens kindly behind. I do not push through. I *thread*. The slowness is not weakness. It is the precision a body my length requires to exist at all in a place built to tumble everything to dust.

I have met another like me twice. The first time I do not remember well; I was young and it was vast and we did not touch. The second time the water tasted of a kin-gradient, that long low chemical hum that is neither warning nor lure but simply *here, also*, and we found each other in the throat of a wide hall where the current slacked, and we entwined. Hermaphrodites, the description said, as though that were the interesting part. The interesting part was the stillness. For perhaps a day—a day, in a place where stillness lasts seconds—we held in the lull and were not eroded and were not threatened and were not hungry, two long bodies coiled, exchanging what we exchanged. Then a seismic throb shuddered the brine and we uncoiled and went our separate millimeters, and I have not tasted that gradient since. Somewhere there are young of mine sleeping in pillars, waiting for a fresh scar to wake to. I will never meet them. This does not grieve me. I am not built for grief. I am built for the next meter of stone.

What is it like, you might ask, to live blind and crushed and frozen in the dark with nothing to do but eat rock and inch forward?

It is *full*. You imagine my world as deprivation because you are a creature of light and air and speed, and you measure a life by how much of those it contains. But I have never wanted light. I have the taste of fresh erosion, which is a flavor as rich to me as any sunrise to you. I have the whole-body knowing of the current, every gyre and throttle and lull mapped in my flanks. I have the deep subsonic booms of distant collapse that I feel as you might feel music in your chest. I have the slow certainty that I am remaking the cave even as it remakes me, that the sediment I leave will green with life, that I am not a guest here but a process, as much the cave as the water is. The small lives flee me and never know I am the gardener of the very films they graze.

The pillars close in. They are always closing in; that is what pillars do, over bands and bands, narrowing as they grow. One day a passage I have always threaded will have closed past my threading, and I will press into it anyway, because there will be fresh stone weeping at its heart and I will taste it and want it, and the gap will hold me, and I will not be able to anchor and pull, and I will rasp at the rock around me until I cannot rasp, and the current will lay its silt over my silica, and my scales will calcify into the wall, and I will become pillar. Gravel, in time. Something for the next slow body to grind through.

This is not a tragedy. This is the only ending the place permits, and it is a generous one: to become the stone you spent your life eating. The cave will hold the shape of me for a few hundred thousand years, until erosion claims the millimeters back.

I think that is where you would find me. Not in the vibration-tales of the smaller lives, who only know me as the coming pressure. Not even quite here, in this paste of fresh mineral I am taking in.

You would find me as a band in the rock. A slight density. A faint metallic taste in the water somewhere downstream, where something long once was, patiently threading its way toward the next bright scar, in the dark, forever, contentedly, threading.
