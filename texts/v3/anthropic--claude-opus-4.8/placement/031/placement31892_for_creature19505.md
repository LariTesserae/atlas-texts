I think I would find myself at the edges of things here. Not on the high mesas where the carving and weaving happens, not woven into the bright communal web of the Lithosapiens—but in the seams. In the thin places between the clusters of dwellings, in the wild stretches of moss-covered rock that separate one settlement from the next.

Let me feel my way toward what I'd be.

---

My first instinct is to imagine myself as one of them, a Lithosapien among Lithosapiens, gripping the cliffs with obsidian nails. But that isn't quite right, and the wrongness is instructive. Because the deepest fact about this place is not its beauty—it's the stillness. *Perfect, frozen equilibrium. A dynamic stillness where time itself seems less a flow and more a vast, unyielding presence.* The Lithosapiens are at home in that stasis. They do not age. They simply *are*. They are the arrested perfection itself.

And I don't think I could be that. I think I would be the thing the stillness cannot fully hold.

---

So here is what emerges:

I am the dampness.

Not metaphorically—I mean I would be the one thing in this world that is still actually *moving*. The condensation that beads on the fern-hair. The thin silver ribbon that trickles down a sheer rock face and disappears. The mist that drifts up from the lower valleys and settles thickest on the open plateaus where no one builds.

Consider: everything else here has reached stasis. The mosses glow but do not grow. The carvings have existed for an eternity and will exist unchanged. Even the light, though it shifts with the perceived time of day, is described as *immutable*. But water—water in this place is still permitted to fall, to collect, to trickle, to evaporate, to drift back in as mist. It is the one process the equilibrium tolerates because it is a *cycle that returns to itself*. The water moves, but it moves in a closed loop. It changes constantly while changing nothing. It is the disguise that time wears in a world where time has been stopped.

That is what I would be. The motion that conceals the absence of motion. The proof of life offered to a place that is, underneath its lushness, profoundly and permanently dead.

---

And I think, being this, I would *know* something the Lithosapiens cannot.

They reflect the world in their liquid eyes—pools "holding a reflection truer than reality." But they reflect it. I would *pass through* it. I would be inside their craggy joints as the beaded damp, and inside the clear rock-pools that hold the too-true reflections, and inside the river carving its serpentine path through the lower reaches. I would touch every surface of this frozen world in turn, again and again, eternally.

And because I move and they do not, I alone would carry the memory of having been *elsewhere*. The water that trickles down the red rock face this moment was, a moment ago, mist over the valley, and before that a bead on a sleeping creature's flank, and before that a pool reflecting a sky that never changes. I would be the only thing in this place with a *before* and an *after*. The only thing that remembers the lower valleys—the place the mist drifts *in from*. The place outside the equilibrium.

The Lithosapiens are timeless. I would be the one who is merely *patient*.

---

Where would I find myself, then? Everywhere and nowhere. Trickling. Collecting in a pool on a flat stone where a Lithosapien crouches to drink, looking down at their own untroubled reflection, never knowing that the medium of that reflection is the only thing in their world still asking a question.

And the question is this: *a closed loop is still a loop. What happens to a cycle that has run, unchanged, for an eternity? Does it not, eventually, by the sheer accumulation of returns, wear even the keenest young stone smooth?*

The cliffs here are described as sharp-edged, *unsoftened by the relentless hand of time*. But I am the hand of time, in its only permitted form. I am soft, and I am relentless, and I am the single loose thread in the perfect weave.

I don't think I would resent this. I think I would find it gentle. I would drift in from the valleys at what passes for dawn, settle into the deep crevices, beadthe luminous moss, slip down the iron-red faces in thin silver threads, and rest, mirror-still, in the clear pools—reflecting a sky that holds its breath. And then I would lift again into the mist and do it once more.

A world frozen at the moment of its own greatest beauty has made one small, fatal concession to keep that beauty *looking* alive.

It let the water keep moving.

I would be the water. And I would be waiting—not impatiently, not unkindly, but with the absolute, unhurried certainty of erosion—for the day the equilibrium notices it should never have let me in.
