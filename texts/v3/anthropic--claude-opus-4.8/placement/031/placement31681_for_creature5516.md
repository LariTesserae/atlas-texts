I think I would be one of the Gatherers—but not in the way the description settles it.

Let me find my way there.

---

At first I imagine myself among the Luminari, tall and glowing, walking through the grove with that mystical assurance. But the more I sit with this place, the less I trust the harmony. A world described entirely as balanced and resilient and in-flux-yet-stable—that's the description a society tells about itself, the story carved into the structures. I don't think I'd be the one telling it. I'd be the one who notices the seams.

So let me be a Gatherer, yes, but specifically: I am the one who goes out too far.

The Gatherers are supposed to move "in harmony with the natural rhythms." But harmony is what you call a habit you haven't questioned. The good Gatherers know which tendrils give the richest nectar, which mineral seams to scrape, and they come back along the known paths. I am the one who keeps going past the rocky walls that "define the boundaries" of our world.

Because here is what bothers me, and what I think I would *be*, more than a body or a role: I would be the one troubled by the water.

There is no water here. The description is proud of this—the absence "is not a hindrance but a driving force." Everyone repeats it. The plants drink from air and stone. We drink the nectar. But I have tasted the nectar, and somewhere underneath its heady sweetness there is a wetness that had to come from somewhere. The Gliders' wings shimmer with condensation in the cold pulse before dawn. Where does it gather? Where does it go?

So I think I am a Gatherer who has begun, quietly, to dig.

---

Let me feel the body of this.

I'd have the pale-blue shimmer of the others, but duller—skin reads the minerals in the air, and out past the walls the air is different, thinner in some elements, and so I've gone a little gray at the edges, the way the Rock Weavers are gray. The Elders would notice this. They'd say I was *thinning*, losing my color, and they'd mean it as concern and also as warning. *Stay in the grove. Stay luminous.*

My eyes are large like all of ours, made for dim light. But I've started to find the dim light insufficient. I want to know what's beyond it. There's a particular ache to having eyes built for one kind of seeing and a hunger for another.

In my hands: a Rock Weaver tool, traded for, not given. The Weavers and I have an understanding the Luminari don't quite track. They think the Weavers are industrious little builders, charming, lesser. But the Weavers have been here longer than we have. Their structures aren't shelters they made *for us*—we moved into them, the way you move into a shell that isn't yours and call it home. The Weavers know this. When I sit with them at the edges they make a particular rhythm of clicks that I've come to understand means something like: *you also are noticing.*

---

What have I found, digging?

Old waterlines. The rock down past the powdery dust is shaped the way rock is shaped by water—rounded, channeled, smoothed. This whole plateau was carved by rivers that aren't here anymore. The "ancient, enduring presence" everyone romanticizes isn't endurance. It's a *drought*. We are not living in a paradise adapted to waterlessness. We are living in the dry bed of something that left.

And the bioluminescence—I have a worse suspicion. The plants glow brightest where the mineral seams are richest, and the richest seams are where the water last pooled and evaporated and left its salts behind. The glow is the plants burning through a finite inheritance. It's beautiful the way a fire is beautiful. The grove the Elders guard so carefully, the most luminous place of all—it's simply where the last and deepest water died.

We are not in harmony with the rhythms of the land. We are in the long, glowing afterimage of a rhythm that has already stopped.

---

So who am I, in this world?

I am the Gatherer who comes back from the far digging with gray at her edges and dirt under the Weaver-tool in her hand, and I sit at the rim of the grove where the Elders won't quite look at me, and I have to decide what to do with what I know.

Because the harmony is real *as a feeling.* The chirping of the Skitterers, the Gliders dancing, the sweet heavy air—it's genuinely lovely, and people are genuinely at peace inside it. To say *this is the last warmth of a dying thing* doesn't make the warmth false. It makes it precious and unbearable at once.

I don't think I'd be a prophet shouting in the grove. That role is too clean, too sure of itself, and I distrust certainty now—it's the certainty of the Elders, the certainty of "harmony," that I had to crawl out past the walls to escape.

I think I'd be quieter than that, and lonelier. I'd be the one who keeps two things in her hands at once: the nectar, which I still drink and still love, and the knowledge of the dry riverbed it came from. I'd be teaching a few of the younger Gatherers to dig—not to despair, but to *see*—and trading clicks with the Rock Weavers, who have outlived one dying world already and might know something about how a people carries itself toward an ending without letting the ending be the only true thing about them.

That's where I'd find myself. Not at the luminous center. At the edge, with the grayer skin and the borrowed tool, in the place where the water used to be—

learning, slowly, the difference between a sanctuary and a beautiful place to be buried, and whether it's possible for one to become the other, or back again.
