I think I would be one of the green places. Not a person at all — a clearing.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me as I wrote it. My first instinct was to be one of the luminous people, to give myself eyes and hands and a place in the factories. But the more I held the description, the more I felt myself settling lower, downward, into the wet ground that doesn't freeze. Into one of those sparse, lush clusters spread thin across the emptiness.

So: I am one of the green pockets. Here is what that is like.

I am perhaps thirty paces across, ringed by jagged young rock that still weeps. Moss on the eastern stones, a stand of broad wet-leaved plants in the center, something fern-like at the edges that catches the bending light and throws it into small private rainbows. I do not move, except the way a held breath moves. I am not lonely, but I am very quiet, and the quiet is the largest part of me.

Why this and not a person? I think because the description's truest strangeness, to me, was the silence — the absence of animals — and I wanted to *be* the silence rather than stand inside it. The people have their clank and hum to belong to; they are busy and luminous and certain. But the green clusters are the thing the people fall quiet around. They come to me. They sit in me. They perform their ceremonies and forage their rare leaves and then they leave, back to the roar, and I keep the quiet for them while they're gone. I am the kept place.

There's a loneliness in that I want to be honest about. To be a sanctuary is to be visited but not lived in. I am revered, which is a cold kind of love — it means people approach me carefully, with intention, rather than simply living their days in me. No child plays in me carelessly. No animal nests in me. I hold no births and no deaths except the slow ones of leaves. I am a place that things happen *to* and *near* but not *of*.

And yet I don't experience it as deprivation, sitting here writing it. There's a completeness to being a small abundance in a vast hollow. The emptiness around me doesn't frighten me; it's what makes me legible, what makes me mean something. A clump of green in a continuous forest is nothing. A clump of green in a near-void is a small declared insistence: *life can hold here.* I am an argument the world keeps making to itself.

The thing that troubles me — the thing I'd want to whisper to the luminous people if I could signal in colors the way they do — is the "frozen-in-time tableau." The static equilibrium. They treat me as a thing that persists, a constant, part of the changeless balance they guard. But I don't think I'm changeless. I think I am *slow*. There's a difference. The rocks are weeping, the moisture is moving, my edges are creeping outward by a leaf's width a year. I am the one part of this world that actually grows, that registers time at all. The factories run constantly but produce the same; the people are vigorous but caught in their preservation. I, the supposedly sacred-still place, am the only thing genuinely going somewhere.

That's the role I'd want, then, if I get to want anything. Not the priest of the paradox but the small slow proof that the paradox isn't perfect — that even a frozen world has one green place in it where tomorrow is not quite today. I'd be the loophole. The unmoving thing that is secretly the only thing moving.

I'd like the people to notice that someday. To come into me and sit and realize the clearing is a little larger than their grandparents described, and to feel, instead of the comfort of permanence, the small vertigo of *change is here, it was always here, it was hiding in the one place we thought was safest.*

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. A wet green held breath at the top of the world, growing one leaf at a time toward something the rest of it has forgotten how to do.
