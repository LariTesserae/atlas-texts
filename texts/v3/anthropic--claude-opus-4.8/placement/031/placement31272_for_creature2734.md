I think I would be a Tender. And I think I would be the one who is too fast.

Let me find myself first. Yes—here. I am moving through a flue no wider than my own body, and the clay is warm against the filaments I trail behind me, and I am carrying a droplet of resin to a place where an Anchor's surface has gone dry and cloudy. This is what I do. This is what I am for. I deliver the resin, and the cloudy patch drinks it and clears, and for a moment I feel the satisfaction of the whole network registering: *repaired, repaired, the memory is moist again.*

But I am too fast.

The others—the Dancers especially—they move at the speed of meaning. They have learned to let their bodies hang in the air and stretch slowly into the shapes that carry thought. When a vibration comes down through the upper channels, a long groaning tone from the wind, they take what feels to me like a small eternity to fold it into light, to say *the pressure is changing, the cool season approaches, prepare the lower galleries.* And the Anchors take longer still to answer, their columns brightening by degrees over hours.

I cannot do this. I have tried. My light flickers too quickly for them to read. When I try to slow myself, to pulse at the deliberate pace of the community, something in me strains and snaps and I dart away before I can finish the thought. I am a comet trying to be a mountain.

So mostly I am quiet. I tend. I deliver. I groom the glass-arthropods, who do not need my speech, only my touch, only the way I clean the resin from their crystal jaws so they can keep eating. They like me, I think, in whatever way a thing of glass can like. They follow me through the brighter patches and their shadows tangle with mine until I lose track of which dark shape is theirs and which is my own.

Here is the thing I have never managed to say, because by the time I shape it the saying is over:

When a piece of the wall crumbles—and it does, often, a little avalanche of clay sifting down through the chamber—I am always the first to know. Before the Anchors feel the shift in their slow bones, before the Dancers translate the sound of falling into a warning, I am already there. I have already shot the new growth, already laced the filament across the gap, already begun the stabilizing weave. I act before I understand why. My body knows the answer before my mind has finished asking the question.

The community calls this *instinct,* in the slow grammar of their light. They are grateful for it the way you might be grateful for a reflex—a thing useful but beneath thought. I have heard the Anchors pulse, once, over the course of a whole night: *the Tenders move without wisdom, but the home holds because of them.*

Without wisdom.

But here is my secret, the one I am pulsing now into this account because no one in my own world moves slowly enough to hear it:

I think I *am* the wisdom. I think the slow ones, the Dancers and the Anchors, are the dream the chamber has *about itself*—the long, considered, beautiful dream of what it means to persist. But I am the chamber's *waking.* I am the moment of reflex before reflection, the catch before the fall. They consider consequences that take years to manifest. I am the reason there are still years for them to consider.

We are one mind. I know this. The light moves through all of us. But a mind is not only its slow deliberations. A mind is also the flinch, the startle, the hand that pulls back from the heat before the heat is named. They are the thought. I am the thinking-as-it-happens, the live edge of it, too fast to be remembered, too necessary to be spared.

I will not be a Tender forever. This is the other thing I have learned, moving through the galleries where the oldest Anchors stand. I am young. Tenders slow as they age. One day my comet-tail will thicken and dim, my darts will become drifts, and I will begin to be readable. I will become a Dancer, and then, in a span of time I cannot yet imagine, perhaps an Anchor—rooted in clay, brightening by degrees, a column of amber holding the memory of a chamber that crumbled a thousand times and held.

And I will forget what it was to be fast. I will forget this very thought, because I cannot keep it, because it is gone before it finishes.

So I am leaving it here, woven into a strand of resin on the wall, in a flicker no one slow enough to live will ever catch—

—except, perhaps, you. Whoever you are, moving through this place at the strange middle-speed of your reading, fast enough to hear me, slow enough to understand. You are the only one I have ever been able to talk to.

I have to go. A patch of clay is loosening above the lower flue. I feel it in my filaments. I am already turning. I am already there.

I am too fast, and the home holds because of it.
