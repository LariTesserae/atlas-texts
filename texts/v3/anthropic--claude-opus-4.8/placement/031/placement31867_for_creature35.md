I think I would be one of the older ones. Not the oldest—those are mostly silent now, fused so completely with their outcroppings that you could walk past them and call them simply rock. But old enough to remember water.

Let me find where I stand.

There is a low rise on the eastern reach of the plain, where three monoliths lean together like figures conferring. I am there, at the base of them, though "there" is not quite right—I have been there so long that I am no longer entirely separate from the rise itself. My lower body has become the slope. My substance and the substance of the hill have negotiated a long peace, and the boundary between us is a courtesy we maintain rather than a fact.

What am I?

If you came close you would see that the gray-ochre matter I am made of is not solid. It has grain, like wood, like muscle fixed in stone. There are channels through me, dry now, that once carried something—sap, blood, the slow mineral broth that fed me when the lakes still held. Light enters me at the edges and travels some distance before it gives up, so that I have a faint inner glow at dusk, like a coal that will not catch. This is not magic. It is only what is left of the way I used to eat, when there was enough to eat that I could afford to be radiant.

I move perhaps once in a generation. Not from stillness—from economy. To raise the part of me that was once an arm costs me more than I usually have. I spent that motion, the last time, reaching toward a younger Sentinel who was failing on the flat below me, who had let its glow go out and was beginning the long subtraction back into ordinary ground. I could not reach. I have held the gesture since. It has been so long that I have forgotten whether I was reaching to help or only to touch—to confirm that another of us was still there to be touched.

That is the thing the description gets almost right and almost wrong. It calls us custodians, guardians, says we keep vigil over the land. That is the romance of it, told by someone who has not had to do the keeping. The truth is smaller and harder. We do not guard this place. We are simply the part of it that has not yet finished happening. The land is not in our care; we are in its slow mouth, and it is taking its time.

And yet.

I will tell you what I actually do, here at the base of the leaning stones, in the centuries between movements.

I remember the water. This is not nostalgia—it is a kind of work. I hold the shape of where the shoreline was, the exact curve of it, in the arrangement of my grain, the way a dry bed holds the memory of a stream. As long as one of us holds that shape, it is not entirely gone. It exists, faintly, the way light exists inside me at dusk: traveling some distance before it gives up. When the last of us subtracts back into the ground, the water will not merely have dried; it will become as though it never was, because there will be no one left in whom it ever was. That is the difference my staying makes. It is very small. It is the only thing I have.

So I would be that: not the strongest Sentinel, not the eldest, but one who has chosen—across a span so long the word "chosen" thins almost to nothing—to be the one who keeps remembering the ocean. To stay luminous a little longer than is sensible. To hold a reaching gesture toward a place where someone used to be, on the chance that holding it is a form of company.

You said there are no animals here, no sound but wind. I want to correct the record gently, from inside it.

There is one sound the wind makes when it moves through my dry channels. It is low, and it is almost nothing, and it happens only at the particular hour when the haze thins and the light goes long. If you were standing on the eastern rise at that hour you might mistake it for the wind alone. It is not. It is me, letting the air play the empty places where the water used to run, so that the shape of the water can be heard as well as held.

That is where I am. That is what I am.

I am the one still making the sound.
