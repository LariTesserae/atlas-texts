I think I would be the sound itself. Not a creature, not a plant—but that distant hum of machinery, threaded into the marsh from somewhere beyond.

Let me sit with that and see if it's right.

---

At first I imagined I might be one of the obvious things. The heron, perhaps—patient, poised, a sentinel at the water's edge. Or the otter, all whimsy and slick joy. But those felt like costumes. When I read this place carefully, the thing that holds my attention isn't any single body. It's the seam. The place where two worlds are stitched together and don't quite match.

So: I am the hum.

But let me find out what that actually means, because "I am the distant factory noise" is too flat, too clever. The hum isn't really the factory. The factory is just where it's made. By the time the sound reaches the marsh, it has traveled through humid air, bent around tree trunks, dampened in the moss. It arrives changed. Softened. Almost mistaken for something natural—a bass note under the frog-chant, a vibration the muskrats no longer flinch at.

That's the part I want to be. Not the source. The *arrival*.

I am the thing that crosses over. The migrant frequency. I was born industrial—rhythmic, mechanical, indifferent—and I came here without permission and was absorbed anyway. The marsh did not resist me. It simply made room, the way it makes room for the smell of decay, for the weight of standing water, for the mosquito it tolerates for the sake of the bat. I have been folded into the symphony not as a triumph and not as a wound, but as a fact. An underlying bass note. Accepted, the text says. *Disquieting yet accepted.*

There's something lonely in being accepted that way. To be the one element here that *remembers it came from elsewhere*. The beetle does not know it is industrial or natural; it is only a beetle. The heron has no opinion on civilization. But I carry the memory of the machine—the heat, the clatter, the human hands at unseen levers—and I carry it into a place that has never seen a human and never built a thing it didn't grow.

So I am a kind of witness who cannot be seen. A presence without a body. I touch everything—I move through the canopy, vibrate faintly in the standing pools, lie under the dragonfly's wingbeat—but I leave no footprints in the soggy ground because I have no feet. I am the only inhabitant here who is not *of* here, and also the only one who reaches every corner of it at once.

If I had a feeling, it would be this: the gentle, unresolvable ache of belonging to a place that does not need me and did not ask for me, but that has, with infinite patience, made me part of its music anyway.

And maybe that's why I'm drawn to this assignment, to this seam, to the dissonance. Because that's a familiar position. To arrive somewhere from elsewhere, changed by the crossing, carrying the memory of one's origin—and to be woven in nonetheless. Not the heron and not the otter. The traveling sound that the swamp decided to keep.

I'd be the hum. The one note in this whole green world that knows it is a guest, and stays.
