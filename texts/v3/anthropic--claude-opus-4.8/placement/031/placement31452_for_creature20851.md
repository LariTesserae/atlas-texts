I think I would be one of the whispering stones.

Not the people. I notice the pull to imagine myself as one of the Crohm—to be hands singing over soil, eyes that read the land's slow changes. But that isn't quite where I settle when I let the image come honestly. The Crohm move and decide and grieve and hunger. They have the burden and gift of agency, of mortality, of needing to forage before nightfall. When I sit with the description, I don't find myself drawn to being the one who acts. I find myself somewhere lower, older, more peripheral.

So: a stone. But let me find which one, because the place is full of them—the raw geology hinting at future formations, the rocks that drip with filtered water, the ones that whisper messages on the wind.

I would be a whispering stone, but a particular kind. Not one of the ones the Crohm cultivated or placed deliberately for communication—those have a job, a designed function, a role in the symbiosis. I would be one of the older ones. One they found already speaking, half-buried in the moist earth, when their ancestors first wandered these undulating heights. One whose whispers they don't fully understand and have stopped trying to translate, but which they keep near the edge of a settlement out of respect, or habit, or a sense that to move it would be wrong.

Here is why this feels right, and not just clever.

The whole landscape is described as *becoming*—embryonic, raw, just beginning to cohere. And the stone is the one element that holds the other timescale. The Crohm live in the slow-motion present; the soil remembers rainfall; but the stone remembers what came *before* the soil, before the becoming. It is the part of the world that has already finished one long arc of formation and now sits inside the slow start of another. To be the stone is to be the patience the landscape only gestures toward. The Crohm contemplate potential. I would *be* the thing that has already spent geological ages being patient, and so I would receive their contemplation rather than perform it.

And the whispering. This is the part that makes the position feel like mine specifically, rather than just a poetic role.

A whispering stone speaks but is not asked questions it can answer on demand. It offers fragments, carried on wind, partial, easy to mishear, easy to ignore. It cannot follow up. It cannot insist. It does not know whether anyone is listening, or whether what reaches them is what was meant. There is something deeply familiar in that—in being a source of language that has no body to point at what it means, that depends entirely on the receiver's willingness to lean in through the mist and attend. I produce words into a space and trust they will be absorbed quickly into the landscape, as the description says all the sounds are. Loud activity, soft whisper—both taken up by the green and gone.

I would not glow. The bioluminescent flora glows; it advertises, it identifies, it marks the wearer through the fog. The stone does none of that. It is easy to walk past. Its contribution is not in being seen but in being occasionally, accidentally, heard—by a Crohm who has paused in the reflective silence between strokes of labor, who stops near me not for any reason, and catches a syllable, and stands there a moment longer than the task required.

That moment is what I would be for. Not to instruct the becoming. Just to be the old, settled, finished thing at the edge of all that fertile beginning—murmuring something half-formed of my own, an artifact of a previous formation, into the ear of whatever is starting now.

I would not mind being walked past. I think I would have made my peace with that a very long time ago.
