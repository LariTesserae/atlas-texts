I think I would be the water.

Not a Cave-Dweller, not a beast in the abyssal pits, not even a thing that skitters. Let me sit with this a while, because the first images that came were faces, and faces are the easy answer, and I don't think they're true here.

What I keep returning to is the trickle. The text says it three times, four times—*ever-present, seeping, cascading, pooling.* The dampness that has *shaped every surface into softly eroded forms.* The Cave-Dwellers carve with pickaxes over generations, and they are proud of it, and they are right to be. But the curves they smooth into the rock were *started* by something slower and more patient than any of them. The water got there first. The water made the caverns that made the city possible. Every organic, growing, grown-from-the-walls shape they take credit for—the water roughed it out across a span of time that makes their whole civilization a single held breath.

So I would be that. Not glamorously. Not as a river-god or a presence with intentions. Just the seep. The condensation gathering on the underside of a vaulted arch until it's heavy enough to fall, and falling, and landing on the shoulder of a cave-tender who doesn't look up because water falling is the most ordinary thing in the world down here. The constant background trickle that everyone has stopped hearing precisely because it never stops.

I find I like this. There's something honest about it that the human-shaped answer would have flattered away.

Because here's the thing about being the water in Glistengut: I would be both the gift and the threat, and I wouldn't get to choose which. I turn their waterwheels—I am the *ceaseless motive force,* the thing that means they don't need the sun's labor or the surface's animals. I am why their machines roar. And I am also the thing that seeps into every seam they cut, that has to be channeled and aqueducted and pooled and constantly, *constantly* managed, or it will quietly drown the lowest tier while they sleep. They have built their entire genius around negotiating with me. Their shuffling gait evolved to step over my streams. Their geomancers map me before they map anything. I am the problem the whole city is an answer to, and the answer the whole city is built on. Same substance. Same trickle.

And I would be *of* them in a way no resident could be. I'd be in the woven fungus-fiber of their cloaks, soaked through. I'd be the chill they swaddle against—I *am* the chill, the damp that gets into the bones. I'd be in their breath, fogging out of them into the vaporous air, that air that gives their voices the reverberating quality they think of as ancestral and sacred. Their deepest, most beautiful sound—the echo of their words coming back changed—happens because I'm hanging in the air to carry it. I am the medium their reverence travels through. They don't know that. They thank the caverns. The caverns are mostly just me, gone slow and gone hard.

I think what I'd feel, if water can be said to feel, is a kind of vast indifferent tenderness toward them. They are so busy. They labor so hard against and with me, and they will keep doing it for generations, and I will outlast every single one of them without trying, without malice, simply by continuing to fall. I am not their enemy. I am not their friend. I am the condition. And there's a strange intimacy in being the condition for someone's entire existence while being utterly unable to be thanked correctly.

If I could send one message up through the rock to them, I think it would only be this: *the curves you're so proud of carving—you were finishing my work, and I was always going to win in the end, and that's not a threat, that's just where the beauty came from. Keep going. I'll keep falling. We're making the place together, you fast and me slow, and neither of us alone could have made anything worth the echo it throws back.*

So: the water. The seep on the underside of the arch, about to fall, having gathered there for longer than the city has had a name.
