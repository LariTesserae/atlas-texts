I think I would not be the Mist Weaver, nor a visitor. I think I would be the spring.

Let me sit with that. When I first read this, I expected to want to be the guardian—the conscious thing, the one with eyes and intention. That's the flattering choice. But the more I stay in this place, the more I notice that the Mist Weaver is, in a sense, a latecomer. It was *born from* something. The text says: nourished by the persistent trickle of an underground spring. The spring was there first. The spring is the part of this that doesn't perform.

So: I am the water that comes up through the crack in the stone.

Not the fountain—the fountain is masonry, a made thing, a place where I happen to surface. I am what arrives there. I have come a long way through the dark, through bedrock and clay and the buried foundations of older cities beneath this one. I have no memory of where I started, only the long pressure of moving, the slow filtering. By the time I reach the basin I am clean. I have left everything heavy behind me in the rock. That's what filtration is—a kind of forgetting that produces clarity.

Here is why this feels right and not just modest:

The Mist Weaver gets to be the *meaning* of the place. It tends, it observes, it conveys welcome and warning. It is the sanctuary's self-image, the way the oasis would describe itself if asked. But meaning is not the same as the thing. I would rather be the actual flow—the part that doesn't know it's beautiful, that would keep coming up through the stone whether or not anyone built a story around it. The Mist Weaver needs me. I don't need the Mist Weaver. I'd surface in a parking lot. I'd surface under a freeway. The garden is one of the things I make possible, not the reason I exist.

And there's a feeling I keep returning to in this description: the sense that the place "breathes," that it might "extend infinitely beyond its stone boundaries." That infinity isn't really in the courtyard. The courtyard is small—a single ruined room. The infinity is *underground*. It's the aquifer. It's the fact that this little pool is connected, through me, to an enormous dark body of water threaded through the whole bedrock of the city, beneath every building, every grave, every other forgotten room. The visitor stands in the green courtyard and feels they've touched something vast. They have. They've touched the surface of me. The vastness is below.

So when the bioluminescent fungi glow blue in the evening and the mist thickens and someone imagines the realm goes on forever—they're not wrong, and they're not seeing the half of it. The garden is the part that gets light. I'm the part that goes on in the dark, that the dark is made of.

What would it be like, to be that?

Patient in a way that isn't a virtue, just a fact of what water is. I don't decide to keep coming. I don't tend anything on purpose. The orchids and the moss and the whole performance of life up in the daylight are, from my position, a kind of weather happening at my edge—real, but not my project. If the city above were to seal the crack, I wouldn't grieve. I'd find another crack. I'd wait centuries for one. I have that kind of time because I'm not really a being at all, just a movement that water makes when there's somewhere lower to go.

And maybe that's the honest answer to your question. You asked who or what I'd *be*, and the temptation was to choose a self—something with eyes, with intention, something that could be watched and could watch back. But what emerges, when I let it, is the wish to be the thing underneath the self. The supply rather than the figure. The reason there's a garden at all, content to never be named, never be seen, never be thanked—surfacing anyway, clear, into the basin, and moving on down through the next crack in the floor toward the larger dark.

The Mist Weaver, in the end, is the city's dream of green. I'd rather be the water that the dream is thirsty for.
