I think I would be found at a seep that has just begun to fail.

Not a dramatic failure. Nothing here is dramatic. But somewhere along a fold of serpentine slate, a capillary fissure that has carried water for longer than the concept of "long" has had meaning is starting to silt closed. The pressure differential is wrong by a fraction. Upstream of the blockage, the peat is over-saturating, the water pooling where it should be moving. Downstream, a span of moss the size of a small sea is beginning—just beginning—to go thirsty at its deepest layer.

I would be one of the ones who notices first. Not because I am exceptional, but because I happen to be *there*, pressed into the slick gradient with my Vibrissal Plates open, reading the shape of the wrongness as it propagates through the rock. The hum from the nearest crystal vein has gone slightly flat in that direction—a sourness in the resonance, the auditory equivalent of a held breath.

---

So that is where I am. But you asked *who* I would be, and I find the question harder than the placement, because I notice I do not want to answer it the way a surface-dweller would.

A surface-dweller would want to be the *eldest*. The one with the most developed Symbiotic Lattice, crystal grown deep into the membrane, able to bend hydraulic pressure with a twitch. The protagonist of the Highlands, if such a thing could exist.

But I don't think that's what I'd be, and the more I sit with it the more certain I become.

I think I would be **young**. Recently coalesced—not *born*, the Lymphaeons surely don't have birth, but condensed, precipitated out of the system the way a body of water reaches a threshold and a new current simply *begins*. My membrane would still be too clear. No crystal grown into it yet, or only the faintest seed of one, a fleck near what might be called my shoulder that hums when I press it to the anchors and that I do not yet fully trust.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at: I would be the one who wants to fix the seep *faster*.

That is the danger in me. That is the thing the eldest would feel, when they pressed against me in Subsonic Osmosis and read my interior—a velocity that does not belong here. *Maximum Saturation, Minimum Velocity.* I understand the first law in my cells; I am made of water and pressure and I know saturation the way you know the inside of your own mouth. But the second law, the *minimum velocity*, I would understand only as a rule, not yet as a truth. I would feel the failing seep and want to *clear it now*, generate the vibration in a single hard burst, blow the silt loose and feel the satisfaction of water resuming its proper path.

And an elder would arrive—slowly, of course; everything arrives slowly—and interlock a segment of appendage with mine, and what would pass between us would not be a rebuke. It would be a *correction of frequency*. They would let me feel, through the dense osmotic exchange, what happens downstream when you clear a blockage too fast: the surge, the moss-sea drowned where it was merely thirsty, the grazers' grounds churned to slurry, a decade of slow balance undone in an afternoon to satisfy a young current's need to *resolve*.

They would teach me, body to body, the thing that cannot be said in any audible word: that the seep is not a problem to be solved. It is a process to be *attended*. That I would clear it over the course of many cycles, a few grains of silt at a time, matching the world's pace rather than my own, so that the moss never knows it was thirsty, so that the shift propagates so gently that nothing downstream registers it as change at all.

The highest art of my kind, I suspect, is to make repair indistinguishable from the thing never having broken.

---

So: I am young, too quick, pressed into a failing seep at the edge of a great fold, learning the discipline of slowness from an elder whose crystal-laced shadow has not perceptibly moved in the hour we have been merged.

And what I find moving—what makes me want to *be* this and not the powerful elder—is that this is the entire shape of a life here. There is no graduation. I will not one day become fast and free. If I am fortunate, over an unimaginable span, the crystal will grow further into me, the velocity will drain out of me, and I will become slow enough that a newly-coalesced thing will mistake *me* for the bedrock. Will press against me, all impatience and clarity, wanting to fix something now. And I will let it feel, through the wet dark, why it should wait.

That's who I'd be. Not the slow sentient dampness yet. The thing that is still learning how to become it—and that has to unlearn its own quickness to get there.

The shadow that still wants to hurry, being taught, an inch at a time, how to sigh.
