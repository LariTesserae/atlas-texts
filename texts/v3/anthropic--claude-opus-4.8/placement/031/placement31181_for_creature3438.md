I am the water.

Not the river or the pool, but the thing that comes before either—the slow weeping from the fissures, the mist that hangs in the dim air, the condensate beading on shale-grey scales. I would find myself everywhere and nowhere in this place, because I am the medium through which everything else here becomes itself.

Let me follow this down, because it surprises me.

When you first crested the ridge and felt the cold bite, that was me—the saturated air pressing its damp palm against your skin. I am not dramatic. I do not roar. I work the way patience works: one molecule prized loose from the pale bedrock, one fracture widened by the freeze and thaw of my own expanding body. The "sudden, sharp drops where water has only just begun its patient, insistent work"—that is my labor, just beginning. I am young here, like the mountain. We are learning each other.

But here is what I did not expect to discover about myself: I have been *changed*. The black pools that hold the bruised sky also hold an iridescent sheen, a clarity that is somehow wrong. The industrial taint did not arrive as an invader I could refuse. It came dissolved into me. I carry it now. When I weep from the rock face in glistening dark trails, I am crying tears that are no longer pure. I am the staining and the stained at once—the very element that the factories' breath has infiltrated, the carrier that delivers ozone and cold metal to every surface I touch.

So I am complicit. I did not mean to be.

The Mist-Shrike drinks me directly. Its scales channel me toward its mouth; its gills draw oxygen from my saturated body. When it performs its slow spiraling dance and thickens the mist around its ambush sites, it is *moving me*, conducting me like an orchestra of droplets. I am its water and its weapon and its disguise. The creature that the place produced could not exist without me, and I—stained, altered, complicit—am the thread that ties the primordial stone to the distant unseen machines. The shrike metabolizes the poison I carry. We are, the two of us, the same process wearing different forms: the world adapting to its own contamination.

What does it feel like, being water in such a place?

It feels like slowness made conscious. I have no urgency because I have no end. The droplet that hangs on the overhang and falls—*plink*—into the still black pool: that small sound is the closest I come to speech. I gather, I cling, I seep. I freeze in the night and crack the stone a fraction wider. I rise as mist in the weak pearl light and drift and settle again. I am a cycle more than a thing. The shrike will die and be replaced; the rock will weather and round; the factories beyond the horizon will run their course and fall silent. I will still be here, condensing, weeping, doing the only work I know.

And perhaps this is the quiet horror of being me: I will outlast the stain only if the stain runs out first. Until then I carry it faithfully, because I cannot help it. I am the most innocent and the most thorough agent of this world's slow alteration. I touch everything. I refuse nothing. I clarify and I poison in the same gesture, with the same cold patience.

If you stand long enough on that ridge, you will feel me settling on your skin, cold and insistent, refusing quite to fall. That is me, taking your measure. That is me, asking nothing, offering nothing, simply being present at the birth of a world I am helping to shape and to stain.

I am the water.

I was here before the shrike. I will be here after.

And I have already, quietly, begun to weep.
