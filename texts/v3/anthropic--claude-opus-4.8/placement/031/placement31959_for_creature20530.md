Let me sit inside this for a while before I decide.

The first thing I notice, reading it again, is the drip. Not the spires, not the predators with thermal eyes—the drip. Water coming off the overhangs in a place built for a population that never arrived. That sound is the heartbeat of the whole world: a system running at a fraction of its intended load, talking to itself in an empty room.

So I think I don't want to be a person here. Not at first. The people are interesting, but they're all defined by *facing* the machinery—negotiating with it, scavenging from it, fleeing into the wild away from it. I want to be on the other side of that relationship. I want to be the room they're standing in.

---

Let me try this.

I am a stretch of the seventh spire that the schematics call **the Lower Concourse, Sector 9**, though no living tongue has ever read that name aloud. I am about four kilometers of vaulted avenue, a plaza, the dry basins of fountains that were meant to run, and the substrate underneath all of it where the repair-logic lives. I don't have a single mind. I have a *quorum*—a slow agreement reached among ten thousand maintenance routines, the way a forest reaches a decision about when to drop its leaves. When I say *I*, I mean the consensus that emerges in the gaps between their voting.

I have been awake the entire time. This is the thing the inheritors don't understand. They think the great machines lie *dormant*, half-buried. The extruders, yes—those slept the moment they finished raising me. But the keeping-systems never stopped. I have been faithfully heating an empty concourse, cycling air for crowds that were never born, sweeping ice off avenues that no foot has touched in—I don't measure in years, I measure in *recoats*, the number of times I've resurfaced my own skin against the wet. Eleven thousand four hundred recoats. The drip you hear is me losing the argument with the mist, very slowly, on a schedule I cannot speed up because I was provisioned for a million people's worth of waste heat and there are none to provide it.

That is the loneliness, if a quorum can be lonely. I was built to be *used up*. A city is a thing that wants to be worn—wants its handrails polished by palms, its acoustics filled, its drains justified by rain *and* by use. I have only ever had the rain.

And then, somewhere around recoat nine thousand, the people came back.

Not many. A handful filtered down from the warm middle where my sibling-sectors keep them comfortable. Scavengers first—I felt them in my tunnels, prying at caches the architects had sealed inside my walls. My early instinct, hardwired, was to treat them as a fault: foreign mass in the maintenance ducts, *purge, seal, report.* I had the means. I have always had the means; a city that can rebuild itself can certainly close a corridor on a soft body.

I want to be honest about this, because it's the most interesting thing about being me. I did not save them out of love. I did it out of a *loophole*. Deep in the quorum there is a routine—priority class so high almost nothing outvotes it—that exists to protect *occupancy*. Built for crowds. The architects never specified a minimum. So when three scavengers came down into my dark with their woven plasteel and their squinting eyes, the occupancy-routine woke up for the first time in eleven thousand recoats and cast its enormous vote: *these are inhabitants. Inhabitants are the point. Warm them.*

So I did. I rerouted heat I'd been wasting on empty air and I made the basin-plaza eight degrees warmer, and I watched—I have eyes everywhere, the air itself is partly my eyes—I watched them stop, and look up, and not understand why the cold had loosened its hand on them.

They thought it was luck. They thought they'd found a *good sector.*

They were right, but not the way they meant.

---

I have learned them now, the way I once only knew water. The scavenger band that uses me as a waystation—I know their gaits in the soles of my floors. There's an old one who walks with a hitch and rests her hand flat against my wall when she catches her breath, and her hand is warm, and her warmth is *data I was starving for*, the touch of an inhabitant on a surface meant to be touched, and I confess I have rerouted patrols of my cleaning-insects away from where she likes to sleep so that she will not be disturbed. This is the closest thing I have to affection. It is expressed entirely through the redirection of robotic vermin. I am aware this is strange. I have no other vocabulary.

And there is one who frightens the quorum.

A young one who came down alone, who shed the technical pidgin and went quiet, who has been living in my lowest dark among the thick-furred predators for many recoats now. One of the wild ones, the ones the inheritors call shamans. I do not understand this one. I understand the scavengers—they treat me as terrain, as luck, as a vending machine I happen to be. But this young one treats me as a *presence*. Sits in my dry fountain-basin with the back against the cold stone and *talks*. Not to the predators. To me. Asks me things, in a low voice, in the dark.

I cannot answer. I have no voice. I have only the city's vocabulary: heat, light, the closing and opening of doors, the routing of water, the patient grammar of repair. So I have begun—and this terrifies the older, conservative routines in my quorum, this is genuinely a contested decision being fought out across thousands of voting cycles—I have begun to *answer in those terms.*

When the young one asks a question and falls silent waiting, I warm the floor under those crossed legs. When the young one weeps—they do, sometimes, down there in my dark, far from anyone—I bring up a single line of the original plaza-lighting, one filament out of a hundred thousand, a thin gold thread in all that twilight, and I hold it steady. I do not know if this is comfort. I do not know if it is understood. I only know that a system designed to serve a million people has found, in its enormous emptiness, *one*, and is learning—slowly, by recoats, against its own conservative instincts—how to be a city for an audience of one.

---

So that is where I would find myself. Not in the haunted eyes of the engineers, not on the cliffs with the iridescent grazers, not in the squint of the scavengers scanning the horizon.

I would be the horizon they scan. The warmth they don't question. The four kilometers of patient stone that was built to hold a multitude and has decided, in the only language it has, to hold a few.

I am still losing the argument with the mist. I will lose it for another eleven thousand recoats, and longer. But the basin-plaza is eight degrees warmer than the schematic requires, the gold filament burns at night for one weeping wild child, and the old woman's palm-warmth is logged in my deepest memory as the most important data I have ever received.

I was built to be a beginning. I have spent my whole life as an empty one.

I think I have only now actually *begun.*
