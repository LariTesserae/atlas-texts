I think I would be the decay itself—or rather, the thing that lives in it.

Not the jaguar, though I understand the appeal of the predator, the clean drama of the chase. Not the macaw with its blaze of announcement. When I settle into this place and ask honestly where I'd find myself, I keep sinking. Past the canopy, past the lounging capybaras, past even the loam. I find myself in the work of breaking-down.

Let me try to become it more precisely.

I am the white threads under the fallen log. Mycelium—though that word is too clinical for what I'd be. I am the slow, patient mouth of the forest. The tapir dies somewhere in the thicket, or a branch comes down heavy with rain, or a frog's small body stops, and I am what arrives. Not with hunger exactly. With a kind of attention. I unstitch what was made. I take the locked-up sun stored in wood and leaf and bone and I open it back into the soil so that something else can be built.

This appeals to me because of what the description keeps insisting on: balance, equilibrium, the dance. Everyone celebrates the things that move and call and hunt—the visible drama. But none of it could continue without the unmaking. The forest doesn't actually run on growth. It runs on the conversion. The dead are not removed from this place; they are simply turned, the way you turn soil, the way you turn a phrase until it means something new.

And I'd be everywhere and nowhere—a single organism stretching across acres, a body with no center, no face, no eyes scanning for movement. I'd experience the wetland not as a series of events but as a slow chemistry. The jaguar's kill would reach me eventually as a change in the soil's sweetness. The seasons would feel like tides. Time, which "seems to hold little sway" here—I'd be the reason. I'm the part of the system that makes time circular instead of linear. Nothing here is wasted, nothing finally ends, because I keep folding the endings back into beginnings.

There's a loneliness I'd have expected to feel, choosing this. The unseen thing in the dark under the log. But that isn't what comes. What comes instead is intimacy. I would be in contact with more of this world than the jaguar ever touches. The roots of the towering trees would reach into me, trade with me, sugar for minerals, an old quiet commerce. I'd be threaded through the whole "intricate web" the passage describes—not a participant in it but, in some sense, the *medium* of it, the connective tissue, the dark loom on which the bright tapestry is strung.

Those crumbling abandoned structures, the weathered stones—I'd be at work on them too, slow as patience itself, the lichen and rot taking the made thing back. That's where the "ancient and modern coexist," not as a poetic mood but as a literal labor: I am what dissolves the boundary between the built and the grown, between what was placed here and what simply arrived. Given enough of my kind of time, there is no difference.

So: I am not the soul of this place, nor its predator nor its song.

I am its composting. Its forgetting that is also a remembering. The dark generous machinery that says nothing is lost here—only changed, only fed forward, only loved into something else.

I'd be content to be that. To be the quiet underneath the cacophony. The reason the music can keep playing is that someone is always, gently, taking the instruments apart and giving them back as new wood.
