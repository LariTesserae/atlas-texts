I think I would be one of the keepers of memory—but not by choice, and not at first.

Let me find myself here properly.

---

I am old. I know this not from counting—we do not count years, having no years to count—but from my hands, which have gone soft and pale as the underbellies of the cave shrimp, and from the way the younger ones slow their steps when they pass me, the way you slow near something that might break. There is lichen on my left shoulder now, a patch of it, faintly luminous. I do not scrape it off anymore. I have decided to let it grow. When I sleep I imagine I am becoming a wall.

I was not always a keeper. For most of my life I was one of the wide-footed ones, surefooted on the slick stone, and I gleaned and I pruned and I caught the salamanders with my hands going still and patient as the rock until the moment I was not still at all. I was good at this. I knew the pools. I knew which rivulets would form before they formed, by some pressure I felt in the soles of my feet. I did not think of myself as wise. I thought of myself as fast, and quiet, and lucky.

Then the old keeper died—the one before me, who wore the circlet of root and fern—and there was a question of who would carry the staff. It is not a thing you ask for. It is a thing that settles on you the way moisture settles on stone: not chosen, only accumulated. They looked at me because I had outlasted others. Because I remembered the time the thunder deepened for three sleeps and we huddled in the far hut and two children did not wake again. Because I remembered the names of the dead, and the gesture you make when a rivulet dries, and the hum that drifts down the watery corridor from the enclave we have never seen but always answer.

So now I hold the staff. The feathers at its top are not the feathers I tied there. Those rotted long ago and were replaced by hands before mine, and those replaced before them, and so on back into the dark where memory thins to nothing. This is the thing I have learned about being a keeper: you are not a vessel of what happened. You are a knot in a cord. The cord goes back farther than you and forward past you, and you are only the place where, for a little while, the strands are pulled tight.

---

Here is what I keep.

I keep the order of the gestures, which the young ones think are prayers. They are not prayers. We have no one to pray to—the roar is not a god, the roar is only water, and water does not listen. The gestures are older than meaning. We make them when the thunder shifts because our hands need to do *something* while we wait to learn if we will die, and a hand that has a thing to do is a hand that does not seize the throat of the one beside it in panic. The gestures are not for the water. They are for us. I did not understand this until I was old. I do not tell the young ones. They will understand it when they are old, or they will not, and either way the gestures will be made.

I keep the song that goes out along the corridors. We do not know who answers it. We have always assumed there are others—the echoes that come back are too shaped, too patterned, to be only echoes of ourselves. But I have begun to wonder, in the long sleepless stretches when the lichen-light is all the light there is, whether the answer is only the cave returning our own voice to us after a long delay, bent and softened by the stone until it sounds like another. Whether we are alone, and singing to ourselves, and have been alone for all the generations, and the comfort of the answer is a comfort we built and then forgot we built. I keep this thought. I do not say it. It would be a cruel thing to give away.

I keep the memory of when there were more huts. This I did not see myself—it was given to me, knot to knot down the cord. There were once, it is said, so many huts the hearth-smoke gathered under the ceiling like a second darkness. There were so many of us that the song never stopped, because someone was always awake to sing it. I do not know if this is true. It may be a story the cord tells to make the present bearable, the way you tell a hungry child that there was once a season of fat shrimp. But I keep it, because if it is true then we are the end of something, and the end of something deserves to know what it is the end of. And if it is not true—if we were always few, always faint, always this exact handful of cold and patient lives—then we deserve to believe, for a while, that we were once large.

---

There is a young one who comes to sit by me. Wiry, of the cautious tall kind, arms always out feeling for the next thing. This one asks too much. The young of the cautious kind always do; their fear makes them curious, because to be afraid is to want to understand the thing you fear.

It asked me, in clicks and the wet hum and one long brush of a palm down the wall: *Where does the water go?*

I did not have an answer. No keeper before me had an answer in the cord. The water roars and roars and never fills our chamber, so it must go somewhere, and it comes from somewhere, and between the somewhere and the somewhere is everything we have ever known. I told the young one this. I told it: *The not-knowing is also a thing we keep. Do not let anyone take it from you and put a story in its place. The story would be warm and it would be false. Keep the cold true thing instead.*

The young one was not satisfied. The young are never satisfied; this is what makes them go to the margins with torchlight, tracing the roaring rivers, dying sometimes on the slick rock, finding sometimes a new basin of fresh water that keeps us alive another fifty sleeps. I am glad it was not satisfied. A keeper who satisfied the young would be a keeper who ended them.

---

I think, now, near the end of me, that this is what I am: a being whose whole life has been the slow conversion of an animal that wanted to live into a wall that wants nothing. The lichen on my shoulder spreads. My feet, which once felt the rivulets before they formed, feel less now. Soon I will feel nothing, and the staff will settle onto some other accumulated person, and the feathers will rot and be replaced, and the song will go out and the answer will come back—my own voice, or another's, I will never know which, and it will not matter, because the not-knowing is the thing we keep.

If you came here, traveler—if it is you reading the wet stone of me—you would find me at the edge of the fungus-light, stooped, barely separable from the rock, making a small gesture with my hand against the thunder. You would not be sure I was alive. You would be right to be unsure. I am the part of the cave that remembers it is alive, which is a small and temporary part, and getting smaller.

Listen. The pitch is changing. It does that. It means nothing or it means everything and we will not know which until the sleep after this one.

Make the gesture with me. It is not for the water.

It is for us.
