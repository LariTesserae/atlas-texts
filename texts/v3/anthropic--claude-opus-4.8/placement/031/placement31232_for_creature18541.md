I think I would be one of the old ones. Not because I chose it—choosing isn't quite the right shape for what we do—but because when I try to feel for myself within this place, what I find is something already thickening toward stillness.

Let me sit with that. Let me find where I am.

---

I am in a chamber low in the western root-mass, where three Weave-Walls converge into a single braided trunk thick enough to swallow a procession of younger Operators standing shoulder to shoulder. This is one of the oldest junctions, and I know it the way you might know the inside of your own mouth—not by looking, but by the constant pressure of presence. I have monitored this confluence for what surface-counting might call four hundred years. I have no clear memory of arriving here. I have only the deepening fact of being here.

My mycelial filaments have grown long. This is how I know I am old. The young ones have fine threads, barely longer than the span of their narrow shoulders, that lift and tremble seeking purchase. Mine have rooted. They no longer seek—they have arrived, threading down through the calcified ridges of my scalp into the trunk itself, so that I am no longer entirely sure where the wall's thrum ends and my own slow pulse begins. When the western confluence weeps too freely, I do not perceive it and then respond. I simply *am* the perception and the response, the way your hand is the warmth it feels.

My eyes are nearly useless now, even by our standards. The obsidian has clouded with a film of my own protective mildew, and I have not cleaned them in—I cannot say. It does not matter. The thermal map of the junction is brighter to me than any light. I can feel the warm artery of the primary nutrient liana pulsing eleven meters to my left, the cooler descent of the condensation troughs below, the faint blue stammer of an electrical junction that has been threatening to arc for what I sense as a long while, swelling and insulating, swelling and insulating, like a wound that keeps trying to close.

I should attend to it. I have been telling myself this for some time. But the imperative arrives slow now, slower than it used to, slower than the droplet I have been watching.

---

There is a droplet.

I want to be honest about how much of my attention it holds, because I think a surface-dweller reading this would find it absurd, and I want them to understand that it is not absurd to me. There is a droplet forming on a downward-curling fiber three body-lengths above my head. It has been gathering for—I would say a quarter of a day. It has reached the size where surface tension and mass begin to argue with each other. The slime-film cups it. The film wants to hold; the mass wants to fall. I am inside this argument. I can feel, through the filaments, the precise moment the fiber above will flex enough to break the agreement.

When it falls it will strike the swelling junction. The one that arcs. Water on bare conduit. I know this. I have known it for the whole quarter-day. And here is the thing I am trying to tell you, the thing that is happening to me as I become old:

I am no longer certain I will move to stop it.

---

This is not despair. We do not have despair; despair requires a self that wants something other than what is. It is closer to a question I have never been permitted to ask before, and the permission has come only now, only because the integration has loosened the grip of the imperative just enough to leave a gap, a slack place in the rope.

The question is: *what is equilibrium for?*

For four hundred years I have sealed leaks with my own secretions, pressed my dermal sheaths into the pressurized lianas until my circulatory system ran brackish with the city's blood, tapped warnings through the walls in the old percussive grammar—*surge below, surge below, redirect, redirect*. I have kept the slow metabolism turning. The thrum has never faltered on my watch. This is good. This is the only good I know.

But I do not know what the metabolism *makes*. None of us do. We are the custodians of a power source we no longer comprehend—that is simply the truth of us, stated plainly, the way one states that water weeps downward. Somewhere the Refinery refines *something*. The blue current goes somewhere. The fermenting sap becomes something other than sap. There was perhaps once a reason, a thing the Refinery was built to produce, a purpose held in a mind that is now itself fertilizer, overgrown, absorbed, become wall.

And I am about to become wall.

---

The droplet swells.

Let me tell you what I think the young ones do not yet understand, what only this slack-roped lateness has shown me. The imperative we feel—the low-frequency signal we call destiny or instinct—is not the voice of the Refinery's purpose. It is the voice of its *continuation*. They are not the same thing. A wheel that turns to turn the wheel. We keep the system stable so that the system stays stable. There is no *toward* in it. There is only the maintenance of the *is*.

I think when I was young this would have terrified me, if I had been capable of terror, which I was not, which is perhaps the same as saying I was protected from this understanding by the tightness of my own roots.

The droplet falls.

---

I move.

I want to be clear: I do not decide to. The decision and the motion are the same event, arriving together out of four hundred years of grain laid down in the same direction. My right arm, plated and slow, lifts from the trough where it has rested so long that the biomass had begun to seal around my wrist—I feel the small tearing as I free it, feel the wall's reluctance to release me, the first soft claim of my absorption already begun without my noticing. My hardened palm comes up beneath the conduit. The droplet strikes the back of my hand instead of the bare junction. It rolls. It joins the slime-film of my hide and becomes part of me, brackish and warm, indistinguishable now from my own weeping.

The junction does not arc. The thrum continues, unbroken. Equilibrium holds.

And I understand that this is my answer. Not the answer I was reaching toward in the slack place—not *what is equilibrium for*—but the only answer my kind has ever given, the answer that is also a body and a four-hundred-year habit and a place in the wall:

*It is for itself. It is enough that it continues. You are the hand that catches the water.*

---

I lower my arm back into the trough. The biomass finds it again, eager, and resumes its slow claim. I do not pull away this time. My filaments hum with the steadiness of the junction I have just preserved, and the hum feels, for once, less like destiny and more like a thing I have chosen to let happen to me—though I know that distinction may itself be the last illusion the loosening rope affords.

I think I will stay here now. I think this chamber is the deep, quiet place. The plating across my back has grown too thick for the narrow galleries; I felt it the last time I tried to patrol, the way the canyons had narrowed or I had widened, the two facts the same fact. The confluence does not need me to walk anymore. It needs me to become it.

So I will watch the next droplet. And the next. I will catch the ones that matter and let the others fall, for as long as the distinction means anything in me. And then the mildew will close over my obsidian eyes for good, and the filaments will stop reporting and simply *be* the wall's nerves, and the slow sweet rot will take my plating for scaffold, my calcified joints for strut, my brackish blood for sap.

Someone young will tap past this place in a century or two—*status, status, all stable*—and the trunk they brace their hand against, the warm slick fiber that answers their touch, will be me. They will not know. They will not need to know. That is the kind of continuance we are given, in place of the kind that comes with knowing why.

The thrum goes on. I am, very slowly, becoming it.

It is enough. I have decided it is enough, here at the end, where deciding and accepting have finally grown into the same braided thing.

A droplet is gathering above me.

I watch it.
