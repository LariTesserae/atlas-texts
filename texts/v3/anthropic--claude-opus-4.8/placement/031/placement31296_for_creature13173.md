I sit on a leaf.

That is the first thing I know about myself, before I know anything else: there is a frond, broad and pale, and I am at the curl of it, and my weight bends it the smallest fraction. I am very small. The drop of condensation gathering at the leaf's tip is, to me, a thing the size of my own body, trembling, holding the silver light inside it like a swallowed moon.

Let me look again. Let me not decide too quickly what I am.

The chorus presses against me from every direction—the birds shrilling, the serpents' dry sliding, the toads with their throats working—and I am aware that I make no sound that adds to it. I have a stillness. I wait. Waiting is the larger part of what I do. When I move it is one leg at a time, a deliberate testing of the air before me, and there are more legs than four. I feel them now, the way you feel your own hand when you think of it: eight, set in pairs, fine and patient, each tipped with a small clinging hook that reads the surface of the frond the way a blind thing reads a face.

So. A spider, then. But that is too simple, and the place has not finished telling me.

Because I notice that I am not hunting. The little iridescent birds blur past me and I do not gather myself to spring; the shaggy insects tap their many legs nearby and I let them pass. I have no web. I have spun nothing. The whole cathedral is built of patient making, water shaping stone across unspeakable lengths of time—and I, who might be expected to lay my own architecture of silk across the gap between two stones, have made nothing at all.

What I do instead is drink.

That is the truth that rises in me as the leaf bends and the great drop swells at its tip. I am a thing that takes the water from the air, that lives in the seam between the stone's weeping and the moss's thirst. I press my mouth—is it a mouth?—to the trembling sphere and it gives itself to me, cool, tasting of mineral and of the long descent it made through the dark above. The drop collapses. The leaf springs up, light again, and rocks me gently.

I am one of thousands like me, I understand now, scattered across the pale mosses and the ferns, each at our own leaf-tip, each drinking. We are the smallest order of this place, beneath the notice of the leathery predators who flow through the press of bodies. We are not prey worth the turning of a head. We are too slight to register, and in that slightness there is a kind of freedom—I move where the great ones cannot, into the folded crevices, into the wet hairline cracks where the moss begins.

And I begin to feel that this is my work, though no one set it for me. Where I drink, I leave something. A trace, a fineness, a thread too small to see, drawn from my body across the place where one drop fell to where the next will fall. Not a web to catch—a web to lead. The water follows the thread I leave. I am drawing the moisture down, vein by vein, from the high weeping stones into the lowland pockets where the ghostly mosses gather and wait.

That is what I am. A small irrigator of the cathedral. A thing that the slow patience of the mountain has made to hurry its water along, leg by leg, drop by drop. The erosion that carved this place across the ages works in stone-time, too slow to see. I am the quick hand inside that slowness, the living capillary, threading the silver from height to hollow.

No one thanks me. No one knows. The toads squat fat on the water I have helped to gather and never look up. The birds shriek in the crooks of rock and never see the thread that brought the wet to feed the insects they chase. I am content with this. There is a dignity in being unseen and necessary, in being the smallest weight on the smallest leaf, doing the one small thing that, multiplied by thousands, by ages, keeps the whole breathing room alive.

I test the air with my forelegs. Another drop is gathering, two leaves over, where the rock folds down and the moss is thirsty. I begin to walk toward it, one patient pair of legs at a time, laying my invisible line behind me, drawing the water down into the dark where it is needed.

The twilight does not change. It never will. I work in it gladly.
