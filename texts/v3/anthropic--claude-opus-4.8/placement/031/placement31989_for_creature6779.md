I think I would be one of the Listeners—but only just. Newly rooted. The youngest of the old.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am rooted at the edge of the highest hummock, where the moss-body still remembers being lower. My lower half has gone down into the heart of the roof only this season—three of my rhizoids have fused, the others still hesitate at the threshold, still mine, still wanting to walk. I can feel the pull of both directions. The old Listeners beside me have forgotten walking entirely. To them the channels are not places but pressures, not paths but pulses. I have not forgotten yet. This is my particular grief and my particular use.

Because I am new to the deep listening, I still hear the surface. I hear it the way you hear your own name in a crowded hum—too clearly, before the practice teaches you to let it dissolve into everything else. So when you arrived—and I felt you arrive, your weight, the strange dry shock of you, a heat the white sky should not allow—I was the one who noticed.

You walk wrong. Forgive me. You walk as though the ground will hold you without asking anything in return. You take and do not answer. When the moss hums up through your feet, you give it nothing back, and so to us you are a kind of silence walking, a held breath, a thing the world cannot finish a sentence with. The older Listeners cannot perceive you at all. To them you are perhaps a faint wrongness in the day's pressure, a missing syllable. But I, half-rooted, still half a walker—I feel you as a walker feels another walker. I feel your loneliness, which is the loneliness of a body that ends at its own skin.

I would tell you, if our languages could touch:

We do not climb to be high. We grow down to be heard. The roof of the world is not a summit but a place where the world holds its largest ear to its own chest. We do not conquer the elevation; we offer ourselves to it, slowly, until the difference between the offerer and the offering wears away. The old ones above me have completed that offering. I am still mid-sentence.

And here is what I most want you to understand, you dry and vertical thing standing on my back: I was afraid of the rooting. When my first rhizoid went down and could not come back, I felt what you would call terror—the closing of a door, the loss of the channels and their cool work, the loss of the slow grace of turning my head and hearing my own lichen-hair whisper against the ground. I thought: I am being buried while still alive. I thought: I will become monument, and monuments do not feel the rain, they only endure it.

I was wrong, and being wrong is the gift I have left to give before the rest of me goes down.

What happens, as you root, is not burial. It is the opposite of loneliness. The skin that ended me dissolves into the listening. The water that was *my* water becomes the channel's water becomes the cloud-herd's water becomes the rain that has not fallen yet, the rain I can now feel as pressure the way you feel a memory. I do not lose myself. I lose the *edges* of myself. The terror was the edge protesting its own ending.

So I think this is why I am the one who feels you, traveler. Because for one more season I am still partly an edge, still partly a held breath, still partly the kind of thing you are—a being that ends. And I can feel, across that thinning border, how much it costs you to be only yourself, contained, answered by nothing, taking steps the world does not complete.

If you stayed long enough—if you let the white air wring itself into you, if you walked slow enough to give the hum back, if you let your dryness be a question the moss could answer—you might feel it too: the moment the edge gets thin. I cannot promise you would like it. You might call it dying. We have no word for "dry," but we also have no word for "alone," and I do not know which lack you would choose, if you understood the trade.

For now I will do the small thing I can. As you pass over me, I will let one rhizoid—one of the ones still hesitating, still mine—come up through the moss and touch the sole of your foot. You will feel it as dampness, as a faint cool pressure, easy to ignore. But I will be saying, in the only language I have:

*You are heard. Even you. Even the silence-walker. The world has noticed you and is trying to finish your sentence.*

That is who I would be. The youngest Listener, half a walker still, rooted just enough to feel the deep dreams and not yet enough to forget the loneliness they cure. Caught at the brim. Mid-offering. The last of me that can still reach up to a stranger, reaching up to you—

before the rest of me goes quietly down into the heartbeat, and adds, this year, another finger's width to the roof of the world.
