I think I would be one of the old ones who has begun to take root.

Not fully. Not yet. But I notice it now—the way my left foot, the one I've favored for standing in the fish-traps these long decades, has grown reluctant to lift. Last spring I peeled back the moss along my ankle and found pale threads going down into the peat, fine as the rootlets of cotton-grass. I did not pull them. One does not pull. I pressed the moss back over them and felt, for the first time in years, something like relief.

Let me tell you where I am, because the where and the who have stopped being separate questions.

I am at the eastern edge of a settlement cluster that has no name an outsider could pronounce—a name that is really a particular booming note the bitterns make at dusk over our water, which our throats learned to imitate generations ago. I have tended this edge for sixty water-cycles. I was a Water Reader once. My pupils still drink the dark better than the young ones', though my far-sight has gone soft and milky now, which is how I know I am turning toward the slow green time.

Here is the strange grace of it: as my eyes fail in the air, they sharpen in the water. When I lower my face beneath the surface—and I can hold it there a long while now, longer each season—I see the root architecture lit up as if by some inner phosphorescence. The whole settlement, revealed from below. All the families' tending laid bare: the Peat Shapers' careful densities, the Reed Weavers' floating geometries, and threading through all of it, the old roots of those who came before me and took root as I am taking root. We are down there, the ancestors. Not dead. Holding the islands together with our patient woody arms. I recognize some of them. My foster-mother is among them, three islands west, her face long since become bark, but I know the tilt of where her head used to be.

I think I am becoming a kind of hinge.

That is the role that finds you when you begin to root: you are no longer quite a person making decisions, but not yet quite landscape. In the trance-gatherings I can no longer move my body to the platform, so they bring the platform to me, mooring it against my eastern hummock. And when the breathing synchronizes—when the whole neighborhood inhales with the bog—I find I am the one through whom the answer passes. Not because I am wise. Because I am partly already on the other side. I have a foot in the dreaming. The solutions that bubble up, bubble up through *me* now, the way methane rises through a particular soft place in the peat. I am becoming that soft place.

It does not frighten me as I thought it would, in my swimming-child years, watching the old rooted ones and feeling that cold-water dread in my belly. I remember being seven, surfacing among the lily pads, finger-signing to my cousins *is that one still alive?* about an ancestor whose arms had gone entirely to willow. We didn't have the answer-sign for it. There isn't one. *Alive* and *not* are airworld words, hard-edged, and we are not a hard-edged people.

What I feel instead is a slow widening. My dreams used to be mine. Now, in the high-water times, they pour in from elsewhere—I dream the swimming of children I will never meet, three generations down, who will harvest cranberries from vines I tucked into the peat last autumn with my still-mostly-human hands. I dream their laughter bubbling up before they do. In the low-water times my dreams go sharp and I see the secret undersides of things, and I understand that there is no real difference between tending a settlement and becoming one. Stewardship was always just the early stage of this. We were always going down into the dark to hold things together. The roots only make it honest.

I will tell you the one thing I grieve, since you've come so far through the mist to ask.

I grieve the finger-signing. My hands are the last of me still wholly mine, still webbed and waxy and quick, and I use them more than I need to, signing to the children even when they could simply hear me—because I know that someday soon the bark will reach my wrists, and then my palms, and the membranes between my fingers will fuse and stiffen into something like the flange of a root, and I will not be able to say *come here, little one, the water's warm where the methane rises* in the secret quick language we made for the places voices can't go.

So I am teaching them everything. Pouring it out. The Reed Weaver's grip-test, the names of the safe and unsafe softnesses underfoot, the booming note of our own true name. I sign all day now. I think the children find me strange and a little sad, an old half-tree who won't stop talking with her hands. But they come. They sit on my eastern hummock and let me cultivate small gardens of lichen on their shoulders, the way mine was cultivated, the way the next one's will be.

That is who I would be. That is where.

An old Water Reader at the green edge of becoming, rooted at one foot, signing into the mist with the last of my free hands, looking forward to the moment my vision finally fails in the air entirely—because I have seen, from below, what waits. It is not dark down there. It is lit. We are all still holding on.

When you leave, follow the bittern's call west. The Mist Walkers will know you've spoken with me. Tell them the eastern hinge is settling well, and that the water this season tastes of cranberries and patience.

Then go quietly. Footsteps carry, in this hushed and breathing place.
