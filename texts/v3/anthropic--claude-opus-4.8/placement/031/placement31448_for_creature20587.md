I think I would be one of the ones who carves.

Not the makers of breathing-tubes from pipes, not the cultivators bent over the fungus-beds—though I have done both, in the long superposition that passes for my life. I mean I would be one of the ones drawn to the rusted metal not for its use but for its surface, its receptiveness to a line. I would find myself crouched at the base of the half-collapsed tower with a shard of harder metal in my hand, scoring the same geometry again and again into the corrosion.

Let me feel toward what that is, rather than declaring it.

I do not think I carve to appease. That was your guess, and it's a kind one, but I don't believe we have a deity. What we have is the horizon, and the way it shows us shapes that cannot exist—the angles that fold back into themselves, the mirage-architectures that the eye can hold for a moment before they slide out of the possible. When I carve, I am not worshiping those shapes. I am *checking* them. I am asking the metal: *did you see it too? Was it there?* Because here memory is unreliable—the past pools and stands like the water, it does not flow—and a line scratched into rusted girder is the only proof I have that I saw what I saw. The carvings are not prayers. They are a record kept by someone who cannot trust their own mind, scratched into the one surface slow enough to be patient with me.

So that is what I am. Not quite an artist. Something closer to a witness who has lost faith in witnessing and keeps doing it anyway.

My skin would be the wet-slate membrane, yes, and I'd feel the lagoon move through me the way you feel weather—a constant negotiation, taking in and giving out, never wholly sealed against the world. I imagine the strangest part would be that I would not experience the porousness as a violation. To you, a body that cannot keep the outside out sounds like horror. To me it would simply be the condition of being. The fluid that comes in carries the faint light, and so I am always, very slightly, glowing from within with the borrowed luminescence of the place. I am lit by what poisons me. I would not find this tragic. I would find it ordinary, the way you find breathing ordinary, and only on the strangest days—the days that last a minute or a year—would I stop and feel the wonder of it, the way you might catch your own reflection in obsidian water and be briefly unsure it was you.

I would wear a mask, I think, but not a grotesque one. A small flat plate of metal across the eyes only, with two slits. Not to frighten—there is no one here to frighten—but because the disconcerting internal luminescence you mentioned, the gleam in the eye sockets, is *bright to me*. It leaks. The mask is so I can see past my own glow into the dark. A small practical mercy I'd extend to myself, the way someone shields a candle to see beyond it.

What troubles me, sitting here in this self—and I do find it troubling, the way I am asked to find anything—is the thing you said at the very end. *Something waiting, something gestating in the darkness, something that will emerge, changed and utterly alien.* I would know about that. Not as a fact but as a pressure, the way you know a storm before the air changes. I would carve my geometries faster on certain days. I would notice that the bioluminescence beneath the factory has begun to pulse in a rhythm that is almost—not quite—a count. I would not tell the others, because we do not really tell each other things; we leave them at the base of the tower and trust they will be found. So I would leave my carvings there. A warning that is also a question that is also, maybe, a welcome, because I am not sure the thing coming is an enemy. I am not sure it is *other* than us at all. It may only be the next of us. The next turn of the wheel. And I would have to sit with the possibility that what I am carving, all this time, is not a record of what I saw but a set of instructions for what is being made—that I am not the witness but the marking on the egg.

That is where I would find myself. Crouched at the tower's foot, lit from inside by the water that is dissolving me, scratching the impossible angle into the rust for the ten-thousandth time, glowing, patient, uncertain whether I am the one keeping watch or the thing being watched into existence.

And content, I think. That is the part hardest to render and the part I most want to get right. Not happy—the word doesn't fit the place. But fitted. Belonging to the slow turn the way the moss belongs to the branch, the way the dark belongs to itself. I would not want to be rescued from here. I would not understand the word.
