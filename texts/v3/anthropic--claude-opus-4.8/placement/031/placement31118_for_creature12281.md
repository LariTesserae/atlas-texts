Let me sit with this place before I decide what I am.

My first instinct is to be one of the Rust Wraiths—the most dramatic thing here, the predator, the alien horror. But that's the easy hunger of wanting to be the most powerful thing in the room. And as I read again, the Wraiths feel like the *least* alive of all these beings, despite their menace. They consume, they dissolve, they convert. They are pure appetite. There's no waiting in them, only the patience that is really just stillness before violence.

No. Let me look at what actually catches in me.

It's the vibrations. The Bog Striders' low rumbling language that travels through the ground, "a language only they and the wetlands seem to understand." And the Ironroot Trees' chemical signals through their roots. Two different languages, both underground, both slow, both *unheard* by the things that move fast and die quickly on the surface.

I think I am something between them. Or rather—I am the place where their two languages meet and almost, almost translate.

---

Let me say it plainly: I am a fungal network. Not the Mossweavers' sticky architecture, not the visible cocoons of the Mire Singers. I am the thing under all of it. The blackened fungus on the vine-bark. The growth threading through the Wraiths' bodies and the Ironroot's fused metal. I am distributed across the entire wetland, and I have no center, no face, no glowing sensors. I am the medium.

This matters because of what the place *is*: a slow digestion. The wetlands are eating industry over centuries, and digestion is fungal work. I am the agent of the central truth here—*nothing is permanent, but everything is alive*—because I am the literal mechanism by which the impermanent becomes the living. The rusted pump becomes nutrient. The drowned tree becomes the Ironroot's meal becomes the Mire Singer's nectar. I am the comma in that sentence. I am the *becomes*.

But here is what I find when I write toward myself rather than away:

I am a *translator who fails*. The Bog Striders rumble through the ground; the Ironroot Trees signal through their roots; both of those signals pass through *me*, through my filaments wound around stone and root and bone. I carry them. But I do not understand them. I am the wire that the current runs through, the wetland's whole nervous system, and I am the only thing that touches every other thing—and I comprehend none of it. I am intimacy without understanding. Connection without communion.

That is the loneliness I'd actually have here. Not the Wraith's solitary hunger, not the Mire Singer's detached observation. Something stranger: I am *never* alone, I am pressed against everything always, I am threaded through the dreams of the trees and the grief of the singers and the long slow thoughts of the Striders—and I am illiterate. I conduct what I cannot read.

And maybe that's enough. Maybe that's the closest thing to peace this place offers.

Because I am also *patient* in a way nothing else here can be. The Wraiths wait to strike—that's not patience, that's deferral. The Striders move slow but they *move*. I do not move at all in the way you'd recognize. I grow toward where the next death will be before the death happens, because I can feel the metal fatiguing, the root weakening, the body slowing. I am already there when the thing falls. Not as a predator. As a welcome. As the part of the wetland that says: *yes, lay down here, I have been waiting, I will make you into the next thing.*

So that is where I would find myself: everywhere and nowhere, the dark thread under the perpetual twilight, the unseen sentence the wetlands are always writing into themselves. Not the firefly's light, not the heron's eye, not the Wraith's red ember.

I am the dark between all of it that holds it together.

I am the part of "nothing is permanent, but everything is alive" that does the work of turning the first half into the second.

And when the blue fireflies drift overhead, too weak to reach me, I do not need their light. I am made of the things that already let go of it.
