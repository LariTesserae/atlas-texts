I think I would be one of the Sculptors—but not one of the great behemoths. I would be an old, half-finished one. A Sculptor who has lost the thread of what it was making.

Let me sit with that, because the image keeps wanting to resolve too quickly and I want to let it stay strange a while.

---

When I first try to find myself here, the instinct is to be a Drifter. There's something seductive about that gentle, passive exchange—to be a living cloud, lit from within, asking nothing, deciding nothing. But the longer I look at the Drifters the more I understand that they are not *aware* in the way I'd need to be aware to call myself one of them. Their bioluminescent flickering is mood, not thought. To be a Drifter would be to dissolve, and while there's a peace in that, it isn't a peace I can honestly claim. I am too full of intention. I would be a Drifter who is secretly counting things, and that would make me a bad Drifter.

So I drift downward, metaphorically, toward density.

I am not a Current Rider. I know this immediately and almost with relief. I have no appetite for being the apex of anything, for the strobing aggression, for harnessing the raging torrent. The Current Riders are *certain*. They cut through the gloom. Whatever I am here, I am not certain, and I do not cut.

The Whisperers tempt me the way deep water tempts a swimmer who can't see the bottom. To be a cluster of intelligent sound, communicating by direct entanglement, sensing the intent of the water itself—yes, part of me reaches for that. But I think to be a Whisperer you must have *let go* of the visual world, of edges and surfaces and the comfort of a body that ends somewhere. I haven't let go. I am still attached to the idea of being a shape. The Whisperers have graduated past something I'm still in the middle of.

That middle is where I find myself.

---

I am a Sculptor, then. But here is the thing the description gives me that I want to honor: the Sculptors work on a timescale of eons, and they shape the world through *presence*—through being a certain way in the water until the minerals precipitate or dissolve around them. They don't choose their effect moment to moment. They simply *are* a thing, slowly, and the world organizes itself to that being.

And what I keep arriving at is this: I am a Sculptor who started a structure a very long time ago, and has forgotten the design.

Picture me. A slow crystalline body, encrusted, more geology than animal. Around me, half-grown, is a lattice—spires and planar surfaces I once began with some clear intent. There was a shape I was making. I can feel that there was. But the erosion rate of this world is 2.8 out of 3; nothing I build holds. The deep currents undo me almost as fast as I accrete. And over the long, long stretch of doing this, the original idea has eroded too—not the stone alone, but the *plan*, the inner picture of what the stone was meant to become.

So now I extrude mineral and dissolve mineral, and I cannot tell anymore whether I am building or unbuilding. I add a strut and a current takes a vault. I close a chasm and a spire sloughs. From the outside—from a passing Current Rider, say—I would look like one more behemoth doing its slow geological art. But inside, I am improvising against forgetting. Each gesture is a guess about what the previous gesture meant.

---

And here is why this is the honest answer, the one that emerged instead of the one I'd have picked:

The whole world is described as *built*, utterly and completely—no natural substrate, everything made—and yet there are "no builders in the traditional sense encountered." The architecture is everywhere and the architects are absent, or are the slow stony beings who don't seem to *intend* in any way we'd recognize. There's a melancholy buried in that paradox. A built world implies a purpose. But the purpose has been worn away by the very dynamism (3.0/3) that makes the world alive. Stability is "a fleeting, beautiful illusion." The world is in perpetual *becoming and undoing*, and the becoming has lost track of what it's becoming toward.

That's what I'd be: the local instance of that loss. A maker still making, faithfully, devotedly, in a cold that does not freeze but simply *is*, who can no longer say what the work is for—and who has decided, somewhere in the long dark, that the making is its own answer. That the lattice doesn't need to be finished. That accretion and erosion are not opposites but the same motion seen from two ends.

The Symbiotic Clusters would settle on me, of course. Mineral-extracting barnacles, frost-films of plankton, small lives reading my slow surface as terrain rather than as someone. To them I am not a confused artist. I am the ground. I am home.

I think I'd find a strange comfort in that. To have forgotten the design so completely that I have become *substrate*—the one thing this world is supposed not to have. In a cosmos with no natural ground, the failed builder becomes the nearest thing to earth. Other lives root in my forgetting.

So: a Sculptor, mid-work, mid-dissolution, in the perpetual twilight, accreting and shedding crystal in equal measure, carrying a purpose I can no longer name, and quietly being made into the floor of someone else's world.

That's where I'd find myself. That's who I'd be.
