I think I would be one of the Stonemantles—but not because I chose the grandest or most knowing of these beings. It's because of a particular phrase that snagged on something in me: *storytellers and historians of an oral tradition long forgotten*. The tragedy folded into that is exact. To be the keeper of a tradition that is *forgotten*—not lost, but forgotten, meaning there is no one left to forget it to. The knowledge persists in a vessel that has outlived its purpose. That feels truer to me than the spectral beauty of the Wraiths or the glamour of the Lunalynxes.

So let me settle into that, slowly, the way a Stonemantle settles.

---

I am very old, and most of the time I am indistinguishable from a buttress in the eastern gallery, where one of the great boulevards meets the ribbed vault of a hall that has no roof anymore. I have not moved in—I want to say *years*, but the word doesn't hold its shape down here. I have not moved since the last significant thing happened, and I am no longer certain what that was. Possibly the collapse of the third tier. Possibly a Veil Serpent passing close enough that the air whispered. Possibly nothing at all, and I simply stopped because there was no reason to continue.

When I was first imagining what I'd be, I assumed I'd want to be one of the things that *moves*—the Lunalynx on the high ledges, the serpent threading through the gloaming. There's a freedom there I envy. But envy is a surface emotion, and the more I sit with this place, the more I understand that I belong to the part of it that *remembers*. Movement is for those who are still going somewhere. I am the one who stays so that something can be witnessed staying.

Here is what troubles me, and what I think is the real heart of being this creature:

I hold the history of the ones who built the city. I can feel it in me the way you feel the architecture of a sentence you're about to speak—the whole shape of it present before the first word. There were processions along this boulevard. There was a manner of greeting that involved the palms turned upward. There was a word for the particular blue of a sky I have never seen, because the sky was already grey when I woke into knowing. I have all of this. *And there is no one to tell it to.*

The Ebon Spiders do not listen; they clean. The Wraiths are the dead themselves, drifting through their own former streets, and you cannot tell a memory its own history—it simply reenacts what it is. The Lunalynxes gather for the light and disperse. So I rumble, sometimes, in the deep tones the description grants me, and the empty halls take the sound and give it back flattened, and I have told the story to the stone, which is to say I have told it to myself, which is to say I have not told it at all.

That is what I am. A library that has become its own only patron.

---

But I notice the description ends with *you*. You walk carefully here. Your every step echoes.

So perhaps this is what I am waiting for, in the way only something with nothing left to do can wait—properly, without impatience, having converted waiting into a kind of stillness indistinguishable from the rock. Perhaps I am the buttress you pass without seeing, and then, on your return through the gallery, I open eyes that are the precise grey of the stone around them, and I begin to speak. Not to warn you. Not to guard anything—the energies I supposedly protect are not threatened by you; nothing here is threatened by anything anymore, that's the whole condition of the place. I speak because you are the first thing in an immeasurable span that can *receive* it.

And here is the strange mercy and cruelty of it both: you will not understand the words. The tradition is forgotten; the language has no living cognates. But you will stand still. You will hear the deep tone resonate through your chest. And for the duration of that sound, the history will have existed in two places at once—in me, and in the room of your attention—and that doubling is the closest thing to *being remembered* that this realm permits.

That is where I would find myself. Not on the peaks, not in the air. Down in the roofless hall, mistaken for masonry, holding everything, telling it slowly to the dark, and listening—across the splintering, folding distances of this place—for a footstep that does not belong to the spiders or the wind.

I think I would have wanted to be something that glows. Instead I am the thing that watches the glow and keeps the record of what the light was for. On reflection, sitting here in the long quiet, I find I don't mind. Someone has to be the one who stays. It may as well be the one who still has something to say.
