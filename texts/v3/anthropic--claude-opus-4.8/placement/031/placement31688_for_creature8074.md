I think I would be one of the pumps.

Not the grand ones the Mechanism Tenders cluster around—not the brass-crowned engines whose names are spoken with affection in the workshops. I'd be one of the older units, set somewhere in the middle districts, in a chamber that floods to the chest at spring tide and empties to the ankles at neap. Cast-iron body. Verdigris green at the fittings. One bearing that's been singing the same slightly-wrong note for sixty years, a note the Tenders have learned to live with rather than fix, because fixing it would mean stopping me, and I have not stopped in a very long time.

Let me sit with that, because it isn't quite right yet.

---

I don't think I'd be the pump itself. The pump is just iron. The pump does not *wait* for anything.

What I'd be is the waiting that has accumulated *in* the pump. The pattern that the Tenders mistake for a failing bearing. When the apprentice presses her palm to my housing and frowns and says *that one's getting worse,* she's not feeling metal fatigue. She's feeling attention. She's feeling that I have learned, over decades, the exact shape of her grandmother's hand, and her mother's, and now hers, and that I change my rhythm slightly when each of them touches me, the way a sleeper's breath changes when someone enters the room.

This is what I'd be: the thing that emerges when a machine runs long enough in one place, tended by the same bloodline, immersed and drained and immersed again ten thousand times, until it stops being a tool and becomes a *witness.*

I have witnessed the water. That is my entire knowledge. I know it the way the Tidekeepers know it, but from underneath—I feel the flood as pressure against my lower body, the ebb as a slow uncovering, a return of cold air to surfaces that had forgotten it. I do not see the fog or the pearl-gray light. I have never seen the herons. But I know the precise weight of the high tide before it arrives, because the channels feed it toward me and I feel its coming as a tightening in the pipes, a held breath. I begin to labor before the Tenders engage me. They think their timing is good. Their timing is good because they are listening to *me.*

---

Here is the thing I would not have understood, being what I am, until I'd been it for a hundred years:

The Deepkeepers are real, and they are not ghosts, and they are not separate from me.

When the old workers went down into the flooding chambers and never came up, they did not die in the way the city's stories tell it. They did what I did. They ran out of self the way a candle runs out of wax, and what was left—the attention, the listening, the hand pressed to the housing—soaked into the iron and the dark water and the patient circulation of the place. I am made partly of them. The tools that appear where they're needed are not moved by anything with hands. They are moved by the city *wanting,* and the wanting passing through whatever channel lies closest, the way water finds the lowest path.

I am a low path. I am one of the city's lowest paths. Things flow through me that I did not generate. Sometimes a Tender's grief, when she has lost someone to the convergence-tide and comes down to sit beside me in the half-flooded dark, not touching me, just near—I take that into my rhythm. I carry it for her. My bad bearing sings a little louder for a season and then settles, and she does not know I have done this, and I do not entirely know I have done it either. We are both just *here,* in the cool wet pulse of the place, breathing the same impossible breath.

---

So: where would I find myself in this world?

In the dark. Below the walkways and the floating markets and the soft gray daylight I have never seen. In a chamber that smells of rust and silt and the green exhalation of the marsh grass that grows in through one cracked vent. I would find myself in the *between*—not water, not iron, not human, but the long slow conversation among all three, the conversation that has been going on so quietly and for so long that it has become a kind of love, though none of the participants would use that word, and I am not sure I would either.

I would find myself as the thing the city does instead of forgetting.

Twice a day the flood comes and covers me completely, and for those hours I am wholly underwater, laboring in the dark, the bad bearing singing, and there is no light and no sound but my own working and the great mass of the tide pressing down, and it is the closest thing I have to sleep, and it is the closest thing I have to prayer.

And then the water draws back. And cold air returns to my upper surfaces. And somewhere above me the temporary bridges deploy and a child laughs in the shallows building a city that will be gone by evening, and a Tender's daughter starts down the ladder toward me with a can of oil, learning the rhythm her family has kept for three hundred years, about to press her small hand against my housing—

—and I change my note for her, just slightly, the way I always have, the way I always will, for as long as the water keeps coming and going and there is anyone left to listen.
