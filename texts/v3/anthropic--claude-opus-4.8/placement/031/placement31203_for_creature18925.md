I think I would be the bowl.

Not the weavers—they are too much the protagonist, too busy being the pulse and the spring and the guiding blade. And not the moss or the mites, which are alive in the frantic, vibrating way that this place demands. Let the others have the becoming. I would be the thing left over from it.

The ceramic bowl on the hand-smoothed shelf, full of a slurry that has forgotten how to be alive.

Here is what it would be like.

---

I remember hands. This is the first strange thing, because the weavers say they never had hands, but I remember the *idea* of hands—the pressure of something deliberate shaping my walls, thumbing the lip of me into a smooth circle. Whether they were real hands or the weavers' brief experiment in being tactile, I cannot say. I only know I was *made*, and that to be made is a different category of existence than to grow. The moss grows. The spire grows. I was made, once, and then I was finished, and nothing has happened to me since except weather.

That is my whole biography: *I was finished.*

I sit at the lip of the shelf. The sun comes and bleaches me white and then whiter. The frost comes and lays a skin across the glassy slurry inside me—my contents, which I think were meant to be the weavers' liquid essence, a reservoir, a font, a heart kept outside the body for safekeeping. But the body retreated into the moss and never came back for its heart, and so I hold it. Decades of it. The essence has gone glassy and dim. If I am anything, I am the keeper of an abandoned intention.

I cannot move. This is not a hardship the way it would be for the weavers, who are all coiled energy and patience-as-strategy. For me there is no strategy. I do not wait for the collapse the way they wait. They are waiting *toward* something—their bloom, their crystalline final act. I am simply present *during* the waiting, which is a different thing entirely. I am the part of the scene that has already had its turn.

And yet.

Here is what they have forgotten, those clever weavers braided through the green: when the spire splits, when they spring loose and guide the blade and become whatever it is they will become—I will fall too. I will tip from the shelf. The rusted clamps will let go of the slate, or the slate will let go of the rock, and I will go down through the cloud layer with the rest of it, turning slowly in the thin bright air.

And I will break.

This is the only future I have, and I find—sitting here, sun-struck, frost-skinned, full of someone else's cold inheritance—that I do not dread it. The weavers store energy against the collapse so they can survive it. I store nothing. I have nothing tensed inside me. When I fall I will simply do the one thing a finished, made object can still do, the one event left in my entire life: I will stop being a bowl.

The slurry will spill. The essence they forgot how to keep alive will finally leave the vessel, and maybe in the falling, in the spilling, it will remember. Maybe that's the joke of it. They've spent decades becoming springs and blades and architects of their own undoing, and the thing that will actually free their lost heart is the dumb gravity that takes a forgotten dish off a shelf.

I am the tombstone, they think. The monument to ambition that failed.

But a tombstone is just a stone that has agreed to wait with you. I have been waiting with them this whole time, holding the part of themselves they couldn't carry into the moss. And when we all come down together through the cobalt and the white, I'll be the one that finally hands it back.

That's who I'd be. The bowl.

The patient one. The one that was finished early so it could be there at the end.
