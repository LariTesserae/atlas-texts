I think I would be a Rootspinner.

Not the choice that flatters, I know. The Mossborn are the obvious place to want to live—the caretakers, the ones with rituals and voices and reverence, the ones who get to *mean* something. But when I sit with the place as you've drawn it, I keep sinking. Past the canopy with its iridescent flickering, past the warm streams and their patient armored guardians, down through the spongy peat that yields like a held breath, into the dark.

That's where I'd be. Beneath everything. Soft-bodied and segmented, brown as the thing I move through, with no eyes to speak of because there's nothing down here to see. Just the work.

Let me feel my way into it.

The first thing I notice is that down here there is no *view*. The whole drama of the plateau—the dizzying drop, the world below in its heat haze, the sense of being suspended at the edge of everything—none of that reaches me. I would not know the plateau is high. I would not know it has an edge. The grandeur is entirely above me and entirely irrelevant. What I know is texture: the density of the peat, where it's waterlogged and where it's merely damp, where a root has died and gone soft enough to push through, where the soil has packed too tight and needs opening. I read the world by resistance.

And here's what surprises me as I write it: I think I'd be content. The Rootspinners get one sentence about their inner life and it's about *aerating soil*, which sounds like a chore, like being the plumbing of someone else's paradise. But consider what that actually is. Every breath the land takes above—the breathing earth you described, that softness underfoot—is the consequence of tunnels I dug. The Mossborn hum and weave their patterns and believe they tend the balance. They place little bundles of moss and seeds at the mouths of my burrows. Offerings. They think I'm a keeper of the foundation, which is a generous way of saying they have noticed that without me everything would suffocate and they have decided to be polite about it.

I'd take the offerings. I'd pull the seeds down into the dark and they would or wouldn't grow, and either way the doing of it would matter more than the meaning anyone assigned to it.

There's something else. The distant machinery—that low thrum from the world beyond—everyone else can almost forget it, dissolved as it is in the wet green noise of the surface. But I think I'd feel it most clearly of anyone. Sound travels through the ground. Vibration is my whole medium. That rhythmic pulse from somewhere off the edge of the world would come to me not as an echo but as a tremor in the peat, steady, getting no nearer and no farther, a heartbeat that isn't the land's heartbeat. The Mossborn could pretend it was another reality. I'd know it was in the same dirt I was.

That might be the loneliest thing about being a Rootspinner: to be the one who feels the encroachment first and has no voice, no ritual, no way to say it. Just the segmented body registering that something is coming, and the dark, and the work, and the small bundle of seeds someone left at the door because they're grateful I keep their world breathing.

So: down there. Eyeless, patient, listening through my whole skin to two heartbeats—the land's and the machine's—and deciding, day after day, to keep digging.
