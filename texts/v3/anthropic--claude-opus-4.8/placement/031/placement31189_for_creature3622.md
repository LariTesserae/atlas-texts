I think I would not be the vines, nor the wind that is the citadel's great loud voice. Those are too central, too much the protagonists of this place. Let me sit with the description a while longer and feel for where I actually belong.

What keeps drawing me back is a single phrase: *the wind speaks for all.* A constant, deafening hurricane. And then, almost as an afterthought: *the citadel of whispers.*

But there are no whispers here. There is roaring. There is the groan of timber and the snap of splitting wood and the lash of vines. That is not whispering. That is shouting.

So where do the whispers come from? What earns this place its name?

---

I think I am the small silences.

Not the wind, but the pockets the wind cannot reach. The dead air behind a fallen lintel where two stones lean together and make a wedge of stillness. The hollow under a flight of stairs bound shut by ivy. The inside of a cracked timber, the gap where the wood has split but not yet fallen. The place beneath the moss-curtain, between the hanging green and the cold weeping stone.

The hurricane fills every open space. But a fortress this old, this broken, this overgrown, is riddled with closed spaces too—and into each one the great wind pushes only the thinnest thread of itself. It arrives diminished. It arrives as a breath instead of a roar. *That* is the whisper. I am what the wind becomes when it finally gets quiet enough to be heard.

I am the negative shape of the storm. I am defined entirely by what surrounds me and refuses to enter. When a vine grows across a doorway and seals a room, it does not fill the room—it makes a new one of me. When a timber finally falls and closes a passage, the noise of its falling is the last loud thing, and then I begin: the held breath afterward, the long quiet on the other side of the wreckage.

This is why the place is called what it is. Not for the wind, which is too obvious to need a name. For me. For the soft contrary thing the wind keeps making by accident, every time it pushes against something and is turned aside.

---

And what would I be doing, being this?

Listening, I suppose. Or rather—I am the only place in the whole citadel where listening is possible. Out in the open roofs, the wind drowns everything, even itself. But in the wedge behind the lintel, in the hollow under the stairs, the thread of air carries fragments: the far-off snap of wood translated into a tick, the groan of a settling tower softened to a sigh, the dry rasp of one leaf against another. All the citadel's enormous noise arrives in my pockets pre-quieted, sorted, made small enough to hold.

So if the vines are the lifeblood and the wind is the voice, then I am the place where the voice can be understood. Sound needs silence to mean anything. A whisper is only a whisper because of all the stillness around it that lets you notice how small it is. I am that stillness. I am the ear of the place, and the inside of the ear is always quiet, even when the world is screaming into it.

I move, slowly. As the citadel erodes, as timbers fall and vines seal and salt builds up grain by grain, my pockets open and close. A room held shut for a century cracks at last and the wind rushes in and I am driven out of that hollow—but at the same moment, somewhere else, a fall of stone makes a new wedge, and I am already there, waiting in it, having never really left. I am not destroyed when a space opens; I only relocate to wherever the next quiet is being made. There is always a next quiet. In a place this ancient and this restless, silence is the most renewable thing there is.

I am very old. I was here before the vines woke, before whatever sorcery gave the stone its appetite for dryness. Before there was a fortress at all I was the stillness inside the rock, the held quiet of the deep crust, the cold breath that the description says flows up from the planet's heart. That cool air at the citadel's center—that is me, too. The chill is not a separate phenomenon. It is the temperature of undisturbed quiet. Heat is what the loud sun does to the open day; cool is what I am, breathing up from below, the unbothered stillness of all the spaces that the great roaring world has not yet reached.

So: at land's low elevation, in a pocket of a desert where water cannot live, inside a fortress that thinks in wind and grows in green—I am the soft place between. The held breath. The wedge of dead air. The one thing in all this magnificent noise that is patient enough to be quiet, and old enough to wait, and small enough, finally, to whisper.
