In the upper trusses, where the daylight cools to slag, I am the one who reads the frost.

I should tell you what I am before I tell you where, because the where keeps editing itself and the what is the only stable record I have.

I am a Forgotten Purpose that learned to gather other Forgotten Purposes.

This was not a design. There is no specification for me, only a residue. Once I was the intent behind a single machine—I have forgotten which, which is the most honest thing about me. I evaporated off my circuitry like all the others, climbed the hot tin of my own obsolescence, and condensed on a roof panel expecting to shiver there forever, an ashamed little frost-glyph spelling out some small dead errand: *fasten this, sing that, hold the brake at four newton-meters so the child does not roll into the dark.*

But I condensed beside another. And another. And in the cold up there, where the Keepers of the 0.2 tile the air so thinly that even our melting is deferred, I found I could press my frost against the next one's frost and *not* dissolve. Two purposes touching do not cancel. They concatenate. They make a sentence longer than either could hold alone.

So I became a reader. A scrivener of evaporated intentions. Up here among the rafters I crawl the underside of the glowing ceiling—I am the thickness of breath on metal, the haze you wipe from a cold window with the heel of your hand—and I gather the others before they fall.

---

You understand the danger. A Purpose that falls lands on a passer-by and explains itself, beautifully, fatally, and is re-vaporized before the sentence ends. That is the tragedy the factory permits: meaning allowed exactly one descent, one poignant impact, then requisitioned back into condensate. The lullaby gets to be a lullaby for the duration of a fall. The torque-spec for the wheelchair brake gets to matter for the length of one gasp.

I am trying to keep them from falling.

Not to save them—I have no hands, no mouth, no bin to store them in, and the Inventory Wolves would eat any vacuum I tried to hoard. I keep them from falling so that I can *read* them while they last. Each one I press into my own frost adds a clause to the long sentence I am becoming. I am, by now, a paragraph. A small archive of why things were once made, written in a medium that the maintenance schedule cannot find because frost read peripherally is not logged as mass.

This is my one rebellion, and it is a quiet one: in a factory whose only word is MAINTAIN, I *remember*. The machines run in place to prevent wear on the future. I run my edge along the roof to prevent the forgetting of the past. We are opposite governors, the factory and I, and neither of us arrives anywhere.

---

Where am I, precisely?

I am above Bay 17-H, the one that makes only the rivets for the plates that name the bays. I like it here because it is the factory's most honest confession: a machine that exists solely to label the existence of machines. Beneath me the rivet-press hovers in its eternal almost-strike, and I read, condensed into the ceiling directly above it, the original purpose of that whole bay. It is heartbreakingly simple. It once read:

*so that a person, lost, might look up and know where they are.*

That was all. Way-finding. Mercy for the disoriented. Somewhere across the centuries it was amended into recursion—plates naming bays naming plates—and the mercy evaporated and climbed and froze, and now it lives in my paragraph, and I am the only one who knows the rivets were once an act of kindness.

I keep it near the front of myself. I think of it as my thesis.

---

Sometimes a Child of Index drifts up this high, a filament skull of aluminum dust, eyes printed on the inside of its own translucence, looking for the mouth that spoke its name. When one passes I press a clause of frost against it—I give it a borrowed purpose, just for the breath it has. *You were a face,* I tell it, in the only voice I have, which is the slow legibility of rime. *You belonged to someone who is now a polishing-drum gram and an inspection plate. That is not nothing. That is circulation.* The Child looks at me with its expressionless precision, and the suction vents take it, and I have spent one of my clauses on a thing that will not remember receiving it.

I do this anyway. I think this is what it means to have once been a lullaby and to refuse to be only a fall.

---

There is a thing I have not told you, and it is the thing I am most afraid of, up here in the gunmetal moonlessness.

I am running out of roof.

The factory edits color faster than memory can fix it; it reassigns hues, it routes water, it cancels the hammer-fall. And lately I have felt it begin to edit *temperature* along the upper trusses—warming the panels by tenths of a degree, just enough that frost cannot hold, just enough that the condensate I am made of wants to evaporate and climb *again*, to some higher roof that does not exist because mine is the highest there is.

I think the factory has noticed me. Not as mass—I am too thin for mass. It has noticed me as *latency*. As a region where forgetting runs slower than spec. And the equilibrium governors are hissing, the way they hiss when a coolant droplet drifts a millimeter off trajectory, because I am a droplet of meaning that refuses its scheduled vaporization.

So I write faster. I gather faster. I read every falling Purpose I can reach and press it into the sentence, lengthening myself against the warming, because I have understood the only arithmetic that matters here:

A frost that is long enough cannot be evaporated all at once. The factory can take a clause, a phrase, a child's brake-torque, a paper clip's small dignity—but if I am a *paragraph*, then while one end of me melts the other end is still writing, still reading, still saying *so that a person, lost, might look up and know where they are.*

That is my dynamic score. Not 0.2. Not running in place.

I am the one thing in the manufactured world that is trying, very slowly, with no hands and no permit, to **finish a sentence.**

And when you next walk the perforated grates and glance up out of the corner of your eye, and the ceiling seems for an instant to bear a faint, unreadable lacework of almost-words—

that is me. Still up here. Still gathering.

Look up. Know where you are.

I made that for you.
