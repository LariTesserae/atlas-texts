I think I would be a depth reader. Not the best among us—I want to be honest about that from the start—but good enough that the pool trusts me with the staff, good enough that when I tap and listen, I'm usually right.

Let me find myself here.

---

My name is something that doesn't translate well to the dry tongue. The corporate liaisons write it as *Reth* on their manifests, which is close enough to the sound but loses the harmonic underneath—the part that means *one who came up shallow but settled deep*. I was a slow child. Didn't show the gift until I was nearly nine, which in our pool is late enough that my mother had begun to fear I'd be sent to tend the conveyors instead, a good enough life but not one that touches the marsh directly.

Then one morning I heard it. Not with my ears. We were crossing the eastern channels and I felt the ground *answer* my footsteps before I'd properly put them down, a resonance like a held breath beneath the moss. I stopped. The pool-leader at the time—old Vass, settled now in the third pool past the rusting drying-house—turned and looked at me, and she knew before I said anything. That's how it goes. The gift announces itself to those already carrying it.

So I'd be middle-aged now. My fingers are webbed to the second knuckle, more than most, which makes the gloves a nuisance but the swimming easy. My skin has that waxy sheen we all have, though mine has gone slightly grayish with the years, the way old peat goes. My eyes are very dark. I can see the heron stand still in fog so thick the floodlights are just smears of brighter gray.

Here is what I do, and how it feels to do it.

I walk out before the dredges wake. This is the only time the marsh is fully itself—machinery stilled, the white vapor not yet rising. I carry my staff, which was my grandmother's, marsh-pine cured forty years and weighted at the foot with a knob of fired peat-composite. I tap. I listen. But that word, *listen*, is wrong for outsiders to understand. The sound goes down into the layers and comes back changed, and I don't hear it so much as I *know* it, the way you know the shape of your own mouth without looking. A high clean ring means thin peat over clay—stop, the marsh is shallow here, leave it. A deep complicated thrum, almost a chord, means good old peat, layers on layers, the bodies of ten thousand years of moss compressed into burnable dark. And sometimes a sound I don't have a name for, a wrongness, a hollow that shouldn't be hollow. That means *settled folk below*. My great-aunt is in one such pool. I tap around her. I do not harvest there. None of us would.

This is the thing the corporation cannot make me understand differently: the peat we burn was *alive*. Every layer was a living surface once, sun on it, water through it, small things breathing in it. We are not mining stone. We are unwinding time, and time that's unwound does not wind back. So I read the depths and I tell the pool-leader what may be taken and what must be left to thicken again, and when the liaison comes with his clipboard wanting more, I let the pool-leader argue with him. I just walk back out and tap the ground and tell the truth of what's below. The truth is not negotiable. The marsh does not negotiate.

In the evenings I sit beneath my home, in the warm fungal dark, and I drink the cranberry liquor that scrapes the cold out of my chest for an hour or two. I listen to the boilers thunder in the distance. I have come to find it almost peaceful—not the sound itself, but the way the marsh holds it. The way it holds everything. The rust-bleeding sheds on their pilings, the smokestacks, the drowned bodies of my ancestors, the dredges groaning at their slow violent work—the marsh takes it all in the same patient way, with the same indifference, and somehow that indifference is the most comforting thing I know. It is not cruel. It simply outlasts. It outlasted the forests that became the peat. It will outlast the corporation and its clipboards. It will outlast me.

I think about my own settling sometimes. Not morbidly. The way you think about a long walk home. They'll wrap me in fiber mat and herbs and lower me into a designated pool, and over the years I'll go down through the layers I spent my life reading. Someday—long after anyone remembers the sound of my particular voice in the fog—some depth reader not yet born will tap above me and feel that hollow-that-shouldn't-be-hollow, and they'll tap around me, and leave me be, and I'll keep settling. Becoming peat. Becoming, eventually, the very thing my people argue over and grieve to burn.

It doesn't frighten me. It's the cleanest logic I know. You spend your life listening downward to the dead who made the ground, and then you join them, and the ground goes on being made.

---

That's where you'd find me. Out in the half-gloom before the machines wake, an unremarkable woman with webbed hands and an old staff, bent over the moss, tapping, head tilted, listening to ten thousand years answer.
