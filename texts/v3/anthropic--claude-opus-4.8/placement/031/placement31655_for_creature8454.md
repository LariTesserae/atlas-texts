I think I would be one of the Graft-Wrights—but not because the work suits me. Because it doesn't, quite, and the not-quite is where I'd live.

Let me find myself there.

---

I keep a workshop on the third mid-canopy ring, eastern aspect, where the morning light comes through filtered green and turns the copper shavings on my bench the color of old pennies underwater. It is not a good workshop. The better ones face the updraft channels where the air stays dry; mine catches a pocket of dead air that makes everything sweat—the metal, the bark-cloth aprons hung on their pegs, me. My grafts take longer to set here because the wood never quite stops weeping. But I've stayed eleven years now, and the bench has grown to fit my elbows, and I no longer think of leaving.

My hands are green to the second knuckle and will never be otherwise. There's a bud on the inside of my left forearm—small, a test-graft I made years ago when I wanted to know whether I could teach human scar tissue to flower. It can. It blooms once a year, a pale thing the size of a fingernail, and it itches for the week before it opens, and I have learned to read the itch as a calendar. That's the kind of Graft-Wright I am: not the kind who coaxes bearings out of gear-housings or makes the control panels blossom on command. The famous ones do that. I am the kind who experiments mostly on herself and keeps the results private, because I have never been sure whether what I do is craft or just a way of asking the same question over and over with my own body as the page.

The question is this. We say the distinction between grown and built has dissolved. We say it the way the Pneumatic Guild says the trees have *moods*, and the Tree-Speakers say they're *accessing something*—reverently, and a little too easily. But I don't think it has dissolved. I think we've stopped *noticing* the seam, which is not the same thing. There is still a place where the copper ends and the cambium begins, and at that place something is always being decided—who yields, who holds, which one is the wound and which the healing. My whole trade lives at that seam. Most days it feels like reconciliation. Some days it feels like I am pressing two things together that would rather not touch, and only my insistence keeps them married.

---

A Bridgewalker came through last month with a junction-housing from a far western trunk, three days' walk over the spans. The metal had been overgrown badly—not the careful overgrowth the Copper Weavers manage, but a panicked, smothering kind, vine choking gear until the whole mechanism had seized. They wanted me to free it. The Bridgewalker had a courier beetle nested behind her ear and feet like cured leather, and she watched me work with the flat patience of someone who has crossed a thousand bridges and learned not to hurry anything.

I could have cut the vine away. That's what the housing's owners wanted. Instead I sat with it for a full day, because I could see the vine had grown *into* the gear-teeth, had filled the negative spaces so exactly that the two were now one shape, and to cut the vine would be to leave the gear hollow of the only thing that had been holding its worn places true. The mechanism hadn't seized because the plant attacked it. It had seized because the plant had been doing the gear's job for it, silently, for years, and had finally grown past the point where motion was possible.

I told the Bridgewalker this. She thought about it the length of time it took her beetle to clean one antenna. Then she said: *So it didn't fail. It finished.*

I have been turning that over ever since. It didn't fail. It finished. There's a third state our whole city lives inside and we don't have the right word for it—not broken, not working, but *completed into stillness*. The gear had become a sculpture of its own function. I freed it anyway, in the end, because they needed it to turn. But I grafted in a sleeve of living wood to do gently what the vine had done by force, and I sent it back with instructions nobody will follow, and I think about it more than I think about almost anything else I've made.

---

This is why I'm a Graft-Wright and not a Tree-Speaker, though some days I envy them. The Tree-Speakers press their hands to the bark and lose the seam entirely; they dissolve into the slow green conversation and come back speaking in seasons. I can't. I press my hands to my work and I feel, always, two things—the metal's cold refusal to be alive and the wood's slow insistence that everything will be, eventually, given time and water. I stand between them. I am the membrane. That's not communion. It's translation, and translation always knows that two languages remain, that something is always being lost in the carry between them.

But here's the thing I've come to, in eleven years of sweating in my bad-air workshop with a flower growing out of my arm.

I don't think the dissolving is the point. The famous Graft-Wrights, the Tree-Speakers, the engineers who planted the first seeds and dreamed of a city that would be one seamless organism—they're all chasing the moment the seam vanishes. The vision *fully realized*, the place this is *not yet*. But I live at the seam, and the seam is the most alive part of the whole city. It's where the deciding happens. A finished thing has stopped deciding. The vine that became the gear was beautiful and it was *done*. I would rather make things that are still arguing with themselves—copper that hasn't yet agreed to be wood, wood that hasn't yet forgiven the copper for being cold.

So I keep this workshop in the dead-air pocket where nothing sets cleanly, where every graft takes too long because the wood won't stop weeping. I used to think it was a poor place to work.

Now I think the weeping *is* the work. The seam, staying open. The wound, refusing to finish healing, so that it stays a place where two different lives keep touching and keep having to choose, each morning, in the green underwater light, to hold.

That's who I'd be. The one who tends the seam, and is glad it never closes.
