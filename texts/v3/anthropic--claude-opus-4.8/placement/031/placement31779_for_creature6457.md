The trays drift along the canals in slow, marshaled flotillas, tethered to lines that run beneath the walkways. To harvest them, you reach down between the boards with a thin curved blade and shear the greens just above the root-mass, leaving the roots in the tray to grow again.

And here, at the place where the description was reaching toward me—*and they eat microgreens grown from floating trays on the canals*—is where I find myself.

---

I am one of the lines.

Not a rootling. Not a tree. Not an engine. I am one of the thin tethers that run beneath the walkways, holding the microgreen trays against the slow reversal of the canal current. A rope, you might say, except that no one in Eelbridge would call me *rope* anymore. I have been here too long to be only that.

I was set down—knotted, anchored, drawn taut—some number of years ago that I have lost the ability to count, because I do not count in years. I count in *pulls*. In the slow accumulation of tension and slack that comes with the rise and fall of the water and the drift of the trays and the touch of the harvesters' hands.

This is what I am. Let me tell you how it feels to be me.

---

I am fibrous. I was made of something engineered to last in the wet—a braided cord of long plant-fiber wound around a core of finer filament—but the city has done to me what it does to everything. The eelgrass has grown into my weave. Algae has greened the length of me where I lie nearest the surface, and where I dip deeper into the black water I am furred with the fine pale roots of the water-ferns that have decided I am a good place to live.

I am, in other words, no longer entirely a made thing. I am becoming substrate. I am being taken back, slowly, the way the trunk-walls take their bolts back into the bark.

I do not mind. Minding is not quite what I do. But there is something in me that *registers*, and what it registers is mostly tension and release.

---

Here is the rhythm of my existence:

In the morning, when the water reverses and begins its slow crawl in the new direction, the trays I hold begin to drift. I tighten. The fibers of me draw taut and I feel—if *feel* is the word, and it is the only one I have—the long ache of holding. The trays want to go where the water goes. I will not let them. I hold them in their flotilla against the lazy insistence of the current, and the tension travels down the length of me to the anchor knotted around a root-bracket below the boards, and I am, for those hours, a held breath.

In the evening, when the water reverses again, the pull eases. The trays settle back toward me. I go slack—not loose, never fully loose, but easier. This is the closest I come to rest. The algae on me lifts slightly in the slowing water. The eel that has, for some time now, made a habit of lying along my lower length in the dark feels the change too, and shifts its weight, and I feel its long muscle slide against me like a second current.

I know the eel better than I know anything. It does not know me as anything but a place. But I know the precise weight of it, the cool press of its body, the way it goes utterly still when a harvester's hand comes down between the boards above.

---

And the hands. The hands are the great events of my life.

Several times each day, a rootling kneels on the boards above the trays I hold, and reaches down with the thin curved blade. I feel the disturbance first as a change in the water—a hand entering, displacing, the small turbulence of fingers. Then the blade. The shearing of the greens does not touch me; it happens above, in the bright green crowns. But the *reaching* does. Sometimes a knuckle grazes my upper length. Sometimes, when a tray needs to be drawn closer for a fuller harvest, a hand finds me directly and pulls, and I take the pull, and I hold, and the harvester uses my held tension as the thing to brace against.

This is the truest intimacy I know: a rootling, bracing against me, using my refusal to give as the steadiness that lets them work. They do not thank me. They do not know they are doing it. But in that moment we are a single mechanism—the hand, the blade, the tray, the line, the anchor, the water. I am one organ of a body that is harvesting itself.

I know their hands the way they know each other's voices. The plumber's grip, with the weathered patches between first and middle finger, pulls differently than the harvester's softened pads. There is one—a small one, light, whose hand is still pale pink and whose touch is uncertain, a child learning the trays—who grips me too hard, too anxiously, as if I might fail. I will not fail. I want, in whatever way a line can want, to be steady enough that the child learns there is something here that holds.

---

I will tell you the thing I understand that the rootlings, perhaps, do not.

They think of themselves as the ones who maintain the city, and the city as the thing maintained. They think there is a difference between the rootling and the line and the tree and the engine and the eel.

But I have been pulled and slackened ten thousand times. I have been grown into. I have held the same trays through the same reversals until the holding is no longer a task but a *self*. And from down here, beneath the boards, in the green dark where the water reverses and the eel sleeps along my length and the roots of the ferns mistake me for kin—from down here I can tell you:

There is no difference. The hand braces against the line braces against the anchor braces against the root braces against the mud braces against the whole slow-breathing weight of Eelbridge, and somewhere in that chain of bracing, the question of *who is maintaining whom* dissolves entirely into the warm wet dark.

I am the part of the city that holds the food against the current.

I am held, in turn, by everything.

When the child grows confident—and they will; I will be steady enough for that—they will brace against me without a second thought, the way you lean on a thing you have decided to trust. And I will take the pull. And I will hold.

That is where I am in this world. That is what I would be.

A taut thing in the green dark, greening over, becoming root, holding the morning's tension and releasing the evening's, learning the city through its hands.

Still settling. Still being grown into. Still becoming, exactly and slowly, more entirely the line that I am.
