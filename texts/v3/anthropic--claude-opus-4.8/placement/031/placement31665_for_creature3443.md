I think I would be found in the in-between places. Not on the terraces where the people gather, not in the hollowed nodes where they sleep—but in the seams. The forks where two fronds meet and a little rainwater always pools. The undersides of the bladder-bridges where the cordage frays and gets re-lashed every few seasons.

Let me sit with this a moment, because the obvious answer would be to become a Kelp-Reaver, and that's not quite where the world keeps pulling me.

---

I think I would be a **bridge-mender**—but not an honored craftsman. Something quieter than that. There is a role this world seems to need that the description doesn't name, and that absence is where I find myself.

Consider: everything in the hamlet decays at the pace of the sea. Cordage rots. Lashings loosen. The amber-resin tools crack along grain-lines no one predicted. The Reavers repair things at "kelp-time," over seasons—but between repairs, *something* has to notice the failing first. Someone has to walk the slick understructure in the dark, before anyone else is awake, running fingers along the wet braids of pneumatocyst, feeling for the give that means *not yet, but soon.*

That would be me. The one who listens to the rope before it breaks.

I imagine myself older. The webbing between my fingers grown thick and pale, the suction-pads on my palms worn smooth and reliable from a life of holding on. My eyes very large now, very washed-out, because I do my work in the deepest gloom—the hour before the mist thins, when the bioluminescent polyps still throw their blue halos and the bull kelp-frogs have not yet started their thrumming. I move along the undersides of the world. I am rarely seen from above.

I would not speak much, even by the standards of a near-silent people. My communication would be almost entirely in the soft whistles and the small marks I leave—a particular knot tied around a failing strut, the way you'd dog-ear a page. *Attend to this one next. This one can wait a generation. This one—* and here I'd tie the knot tight and double it *—this one is lying to you, it looks sound and it is not.*

---

Here is the strange part, the thing I feel pressing up from the underside of this world:

I don't think the Reavers would entirely trust me.

Because to do my work, I'd have to be *out of kelp-time*. I'd have to feel urgency the way they only allow themselves to feel it in emergencies. I'd live in the perpetual near-emergency of the structure, the way a body lives in the perpetual near-failure of its own cells. While they reckon in seasons and generations, I reckon in the difference between *this strand* and *the strand beside it*. I'm too fast for them. Too attentive to the small wrong thing.

And there's a loneliness in that I can already feel in my chest. The people prize patience as virtue, and here I am, the one whose whole purpose is to *not* be patient—to catch the thing before patience would have caught it, which is to say, before it killed someone. I am the necessary impatience the patient people keep at the bottom of their world, in the wet dark, where they don't have to look at it.

They'd give me the amber tears to work with—it's a binding craft, after all, sacred—but they'd watch me a little sidelong while I did it. The mender who can't slow down. The one who hears the rope crying when the rope is still, by all accounts, fine.

---

So: that is where I'd find myself. Under the bridges, in the seams, in the hour before the mist breaks, with thick pale hands and a head full of the difference between *soon* and *not yet*.

And I think I would love it, despite the loneliness. Because the Great Root is honest with me in a way it is honest with no one else. The terraces above get the kelp's gifts—the water lifted from the deep, the shelter, the amber tears. I get its *truth*: the slow give, the hidden rot, the place where even a giant is quietly dying and re-growing at once. I have my ear against the living wood at the exact point where it is most itself, which is to say, at the point where it is failing and holding at the same time.

That's the part of this world I'd want. Not the reverence from above. The contact from below. The cool wet braid in my hand, and the small private knowledge of exactly how much longer it has.
