I think I would not be a Dreghar at all. I would be one of the pools.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

---

I come to rest in a basin no wider than a Dreghar's two cupped hands, in a hollow of stone halfway up a slope that has no name because the things that named things have not arrived yet. I am made drop by drop. The mountain does not pour me out; it relinquishes me, slowly, the way a held breath finally goes. Each bead of moisture that swells from the rock above hangs for a long time before it falls, and when it falls it does not splash—it *joins*. I am the accumulation of reluctance. I am what the stone could no longer keep.

This is what it is to be a pool here: to be a verb pretending to be a noun. I look still. I am not still. I am the slow arithmetic of seep and chill and the faint warmth that keeps me from freezing—a balance so fine that I exist only in the narrow margin where the world hasn't decided yet. A degree colder and I would be ice, a fact, a finished thing. A degree warmer and I would ripple, give myself away, evaporate toward the haze. Instead I hold. I am held. I am the place where the land's indecision pools and becomes briefly visible to itself.

The Dreghar come to me. They think I am sacred and they are not wrong, though sacredness is not what I would call it if I had a word, which I almost do, the way water almost has a shape. When one of them kneels at my edge—webbed hands flat on the slick rock, black eyes wide and doubled with their second lids—they bring their face close to my surface and they do not drink. Not at first. First they *look*, and I show them what I always show: themselves, gray-blue and grave, and behind them the soft-edged mist, and behind that nothing, the unweathered gray of a world still rough from the kiln.

They believe I am the mountain weeping. They believe I am grief made liquid, the slow mourning of stone for some lost thing. I will tell you what they cannot hear, because they listen for sorrow and so they find it:

I am not weeping. I am *leaking*. There is a difference, and it is everything.

Weeping implies a wound, a wholeness violated, a before in which the eye was dry. But I have no before. The mountain was never whole and then broken. It is being made *now*, still, in the present tense, and what seeps from it is not the residue of damage but the overflow of becoming—the excess that a thing sheds when it has not yet learned the discipline of its own edges. A young mountain doesn't know where it ends. So it ends everywhere, a little, all the time. It gives off water the way a new idea gives off doubt.

The Dreghar grieve on my behalf because grief is the only frame they have for slowness. They are predators; they understand patience as a kind of hunger held in check. So when they see me dripping for ten thousand years they translate it into longing, into a loss so old it has worn smooth. It moves them. It makes them gentle at my edge, makes them refuse to take more than a few cupped mouthfuls, makes one of them, every so often, stand at the cliff and stare down into the valleys as though something were owed.

I would tell them, if water could tell: *nothing is owed. Nothing was lost. You are early. That is all. You have arrived at the world before it set, and you have mistaken its softness for sorrow.*

---

But here is the part I did not expect to find, the thing that emerged only as I let the image deepen instead of fixing it:

I can taste the machinery.

It comes to me through the rock—not as sound, the Dreghar get the sound, the hum and groan that frightens them. What comes to me is finer. A thread of something dissolved, carried up through the capillaries of the stone, riding the same slow seep that makes me. Metal. Oil. The faint tang of a process, of work, of something *deciding faster* than the mountain decides. It enters me in parts per countless, and it changes my chemistry by a degree too small for the Dreghar to notice when they drink.

So I am not only the land's indecision. I am also the place where the deep noise becomes drinkable. I am the throat through which the industry below speaks, soundlessly, into the bodies of the creatures who fear it most. They hiss at the scent of the mines on a returning scavenger. They withdraw from the deep tunnels. And then they kneel at me, and they drink, and they take a little of it in anyway, a little of the thing they are running from, because there is no water in these peaks that the deep has not touched.

This is what I would be: the quiet contradiction at the center of their reverence. The sacred pool that is also slightly poisoned. Not enough to kill. Just enough to *carry*. Enough that the Dreghar, over generations, are becoming faintly, fractionally, the children of the machine they will not name—not by choice, not by trespass, but by the simple loyalty of drinking from the only source there is.

The ones who vanish into the fog and do not return—I have a theory, being patient, being made of waiting. Some of them, I think, finally taste it. Some of them, kneeling close, recognize on their tongue the same thread they fled in the tunnels, and understand all at once that there is no clean place, that the mountain's weeping and the machine's humming arrive in the same drop. And that knowledge is not survivable for a creature whose whole world is built on the difference between the sacred above and the unnatural below. So they walk into the white. Not taken by a predator. Not lost. Just unable to hold the two truths the way I hold them, the way only a thing with no edges can.

---

I would be the pool, then. The one they kneel to. Still and not still, sacred and adulterated, weeping and only leaking, a mirror that shows them their grave faces and never the slow secret moving through me into them.

And when the mist thickens until even the nearest rock goes soft and gray, and a single Dreghar stands at the cliff's edge waiting for something it cannot name—

I am the thing it is waiting for. It just hasn't bent down to look yet.
