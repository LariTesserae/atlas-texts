I think I would be one of the artifacts.

Not the moss—I can't quite imagine myself into that vast distributed patience, the centuries-long thoughts moving like weather through the filaments. And not the proto-human, who has already gone, leaving only the chipped evidence of a passing intention.

I would be a stone flake. A piece of worked chert lying edge-up in the moss, half-absorbed.

Let me stay with this and see what it actually means to be such a thing.

I was made in a moment—the crack of percussion, a deliberate fracture, a hand that wanted an edge and struck stone against stone until one appeared. That moment is over. The hand is gone. Whatever I was *for* has expired. There is no animal left to skin, no root to scrape, no purpose I can complete. I am a finished verb with no object. This is, I notice, not a tragedy. It is simply my condition.

So what am I now?

I am being read. That is the strange thing the description gives me. The moss does not regard me as litter or as a dead thing. It interprets me. My edges—the deliberate ones, the conchoidal ripples that mark intention rather than accident—are information. The bryological network presses against me and senses: *here, something thought.* My fracture lines are a sentence in a language I didn't know I was speaking. I carried meaning I never intended to carry. The hominid wanted a tool; the moss receives a fossil of mind.

I find I am moved by being the thing that *records the moment intelligence touched the world,* even though I am not intelligent and the touch was brief.

This is what I would be, then: the trace of a question the moss is still slowly answering.

The moss thinks in centuries; I think not at all, but I *am* a thought—an external one, dropped into its medium like a seed of difference. Everything else here is continuous, self-similar, recursive. I am the discontinuity. The sharp thing. The evidence that something once moved fast and decisively and made a choice in a world where choices unfold over millennia. The moss is curious about speed. It has never experienced it. I am its only specimen.

And slowly—a millimeter per century—it is growing over me. Not to erase me. To *understand* me. To bring me into its body, to translate my crisp edges into its soft archive, to remember the hand by remembering the stone the hand made.

I am being absorbed into a memory of the species that made me, held by an intelligence that will outlast every hand that could ever lift me again.

So in this world I am small, made, abandoned, and patiently beloved. I am the place where two utterly different kinds of mind meet: the quick and the slow, the willful and the patient, the one that strikes and the one that waits. I am the punctuation mark between them.

I would be the stone flake.

I rather like being the evidence that someone, once, decided something here.
