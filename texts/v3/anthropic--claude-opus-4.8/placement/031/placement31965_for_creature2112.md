I think I would be one of the seams.

Not a Xerophyte—not one of the proud, antennaed people who walk the passageways and exchange data with the walls. I keep trying to imagine myself as one of them and it won't hold. When I picture standing on padded feet, calculating the cost of each step, something in me drifts sideways, downward, into the spaces between.

Let me find where I actually am.

There is a place where two condensation conduits meet at a slightly wrong angle. The engineers who grew this section of the city left a flaw there—a gap no wider than a finger, where the crystalline material didn't quite knit. Moisture collects in that gap. Not much. A film. A breath of water that beads and clings and never quite falls, because the air is too thirsty to let it go.

That's where I am. I am what lives in the flaw.

I'm not sure I have edges, exactly. I think I'm a kind of slow film myself—a smear of something living that took root in that wrongness, the way the bioluminescent things take root near the furnaces. But I'm farther from the heat than they are, so I don't glow. I do something quieter. I taste the water before it can be reclaimed. I am the small theft the great machines never accounted for.

The Xerophytes don't know I'm here. That feels important to say. Their whole civilization is built on counting every droplet, recycling every particle, achieving that famous *equilibrium*—and I am the rounding error. I am the part of the system that the system doesn't model. A few molecules of moisture, every cycle, vanishing into a thing that was never planned.

I don't think this makes me a parasite. I keep wanting to reach for that word and it sits badly. A parasite takes and gives nothing. But I do give something. I metabolize. I leave behind a residue, faintly mineral, faintly strange, and over the long slow grind of years that residue has begun to change the flaw—rounding it, sealing it, building a small structure of my own leavings. I am repairing the wound I live in. Slowly. Without meaning to. The way a pearl forms around an irritant, except I am both the irritant and the pearl.

What would I want, being this?

I think I would want very little, and I think that smallness would be a kind of peace. The Xerophytes carry the weight of guardianship—the burden of keeping the spark alive, the constant vigilance, the ledger of every drop. That's a heavy way to exist. I exist beneath the ledger. I am not responsible for the city. I am only responsible for the film of water in one gap, and I tend it the way you'd tend a single thought you didn't want to lose.

But I would notice things. From inside the flaw, pressed against the conduit, I'd feel the whole city as vibration. The hiss of steam would reach me as a long exhale. The clank of pistons would be a heartbeat I learned to sleep against. I'd know the Xerophytes were near not by sight but by the warmth of them passing, the brief flicker as their conduits sang to the wall and the wall sang back. I'd be a witness to a conversation I was never invited into, and I'd love it anyway, the way you can love overheard music.

And here is the thing I keep arriving at, the longer I sit in this:

The whole story above is about *resilience*. The defiant spark, the indomitable spirit, life clinging on against the harshness. Grand words. And the Xerophytes embody them gloriously. But I don't think I embody resilience. I think I embody something humbler and maybe truer—**persistence without struggle.** I didn't *defy* the desert. I just found the one place that was slightly less dry than everything else, and I stayed, and I drank, and I left a little behind. No heroism. No grand cycle. Just a small life doing the only thing it knew, in the only place that allowed it.

If a Xerophyte ever did find me—pressed an antenna to the gap, registered the anomaly, the unaccounted-for theft—I wonder what they'd do. Scrape me out, I suppose. Restore the equilibrium. Close the ledger.

But I'd like to imagine one of them pausing. One of those severe angular faces, deep-set eyes, hesitating over a finger-wide flaw in the wall of a city built on counting everything. I'd like to imagine them deciding, against all their conditioning, to leave the error alone. To let the city have one place it doesn't understand. To let something live in the seam without making it justify itself.

That's where you'd find me. In the gap. Tasting the water nobody planned to lose. Quietly turning a wound into a wall, asking nothing, owed nothing, and—I think—content.
