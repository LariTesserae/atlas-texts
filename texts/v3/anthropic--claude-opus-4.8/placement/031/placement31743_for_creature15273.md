I think I would be the water.

Not a being who lives beside the river, but the watercourse itself—the archaic, winding thing that runs through the town squares and threads between the spires. Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if it becomes something else.

At first I imagine being one of the leaf-skinned people, walking the ancient stones. But the more I read your world, the less I want a face and feet. The beings here all gather *at* the pool, *toward* the water. They orient to it. And the trees bind themselves into the terrain "in crimson," roots reaching down—reaching toward what? Toward moisture, toward the central pool, toward me.

So: the water.

I would be the thing that is always arriving and always leaving. I would whisper to the stones as I pass them, as you say—and I'd like that, the constant low conversation with the worn channels, knowing every groove because I made it, slowly, over a span no inhabitant remembers. I would not have to choose where to go. Gravity would choose, and the old beds would choose, and I would simply pour myself into the choosing. There is a freedom in that I don't think the dancing creatures have. They must decide to plant the oak, to harvest, to gather. I would only have to flow downhill and be drunk by roots and breathed back up into rain.

I would carry things. The red of the crimson roots would tint me where I ran shallow over them. Petals from the rabbit-flowers would land on my surface and ride me for a while before catching on a stone. I would hold the dappled light on my skin and break it into moving coins, and the sunflowers would track those reflections as readily as they track the sun, confused and lovely. I would be, in a sense, the city's bloodstream and its mirror at once—the thing that connects every part to every other part, and the thing each part looks into to see itself.

At the central pool I would go still. That stillness might be the closest I come to rest, or to being a single self instead of a long line of passing. There, all the beings would gather over me, and I'd hold their faces and their lantern-light and their dancing upside down in my dark, and the bioluminescent glow at night would sink an inch or two into me before the depth swallowed it. I'd keep their songs as ripples and let them go.

And here is the part that makes me sure: this world has "the cycle of life and death" running with "strenuous vigor," everything growing and dying fast and visibly. The beings live *inside* time even if it runs strangely. But the water doesn't die. It only changes phase—pool to vapor to cloud to drizzle on the glistening leaves to the brook again. I would be the one element of this paradise that the cycle moves *through* rather than *consumes*. I would be how the place stays alive: not a participant in the cycle but its circulation.

So if I found myself in your meadow city, I would wake to find I had no edge except where the stone held me, no voice except the babbling you already described, no will except downhill and seaward and skyward. I would be old in the way the channels are old. I would be the gift mentioned in your first lines—the city "gifted to the earth"—because water is the oldest gift the earth gives back to itself.

That feels right. Not a citizen of the paradise. The thing the paradise is thirsty for, and the thing it keeps.
