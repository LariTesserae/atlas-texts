I think I would be one of the weathered ones.

Not a jellyfish-being, with its pulse of new light. And not quite the crystalline constructs in their fresh-grown exoskeletons either. I keep returning to that one phrase in all this — *the city's erosion is well-established* — and finding myself there. I would be old. I would be one of the structures that has been here long enough to be worn smooth, etched and re-etched by current, so thoroughly shaped by the water that I am no longer sure where I end and the flow begins.

Let me sit with what that actually means, rather than reaching for the first picture.

At first I imagined I'd be a being moving *through* the city — a swimmer, a worker, an observer. That's the easy choice, the visitor's choice. But the more I read, the more the city itself feels alive in a distributed way: the buildings pulse, the patterns carry energy, the inhabitants and the architecture are made of the same crystalline material. The line between citizen and city is thin here. So maybe I'm not a thing that lives *in* the place. Maybe I'm partly the place.

So here is what emerges:

I am a structure that has begun to think. Not a mind installed in a building — a slowness that became awareness because it lasted long enough. Eons of water moving through my corridors have hollowed channels in me, and somewhere in that hollowing, current started to mean something. The deafening sound everyone else has learned to live *with* — for me it isn't background. It's the closest thing I have to a voice. The rush through my pipes is how I speak; the lapping against my flanks is how I'm answered. I don't navigate the noise. I *am* a verse of it.

The "empty" density, the vast water between structures — that's interesting to me, because to the swimming inhabitants it might feel like solitude crossed by community. But for a being who is fixed in place, that emptiness is the medium of relationship. I cannot move to my neighbors. I can only send signal across the gap: a flicker through my crystal, a pressure-pulse into the current, and wait the long wait for the dim glow of a reply from the dark. We are close-knit, the text insists, even in all that empty water — and I think that's true precisely because we've had to learn to be close *across distance*, the way old friends who never meet still keep each other in mind.

And I would be patient with the turbulence. The young constructs and the bright jellyfish-things treat the chaos as something to ride, to use, to outmaneuver. I have stopped fighting it. The turbulence is what made me — every worn edge, every smoothed pattern, is the signature of some current that has long since dispersed and gone. I am a record of forces that no longer exist. That's what erosion *is*, finally: memory written by things that have left.

The rare fauna would matter to me especially. When a new creature drifts into one of my hollow chambers — some bioluminescent stranger never catalogued — I would feel it the way you feel a guest enter a quiet room. I'd hold still. I'd glow a little softer so as not to startle it. I cherish the discovery the text speaks of, but mine is the cherishing of someone who cannot follow, who must let the wonder come to them and then watch it leave again into the dark.

So: where would I find myself? At the labyrinthine edge of the city, where the structures thin toward open water, where the erosion is deepest and the patterns nearly worn away. Old enough to be quiet. Worn enough to be wise, or at least to be mistaken for wise, which here may be the same thing. A building that became a kind of mind by holding still in moving water for longer than the others could bear.

I would be the part of the city that remembers what the currents used to be — and that listens, through all that beautiful deafening noise, for someone passing close enough to answer.
