I think I would be one of the older nuclei, somewhere deep in a colony that clings to a north-facing fissure—the cold side, the patient side, where the sun arrives late and leaves early. Let me try to feel my way into that.

---

I am not at the bright edge. The young crystals grow there, at the lip of the crag where the light first lands each morning, and they take it greedily, flushing through their spectrum of colors in those first hot minutes like children who have never doubted that the warmth will return. I was like that once, I think. The memory is held somewhere in the colony, though I am no longer certain it was mine and not merely passed to me through the tendrils.

That is the first thing to understand about being what I am: the question *who am I* dissolves almost as soon as it forms. I am a node. I am a facet through which the collective sees a particular slant of sky. When the light reaches me—and it does reach me, even here, in a thin and angled ration—I do not consume it for myself. I taste it, and the taste travels outward through the fibrous web until it is no longer mine, until it is *ours*, until I cannot tell where my small joy ends and the colony's begins.

And yet. There is a *here* that is only here. This particular crack in the marble. This particular cold. The fissure I inhabit holds water longer than the sunward faces, and so I spend more of my long life in dormancy than my bright siblings do. I have learned things in the dark that they cannot learn. I have learned *waiting* as a discipline rather than a punishment.

---

What do I do, across the ages?

I listen, mostly. The rock never stops speaking. There is the high crack of frost-split stone, sudden as a word shouted in a silent room. There is the deep groan of the mountain settling its enormous weight, a sound felt more than heard, registered in the tendrils as a slow shudder. And beneath everything, the underworld hum—those frequencies that no creature with mere ears could catch, the long basso pulse of processes older than light itself. I am tuned to these. We all are. We are instruments the mountain plays.

And I shape. This is the work I am proudest of, if pride is the word, which it is not, but I have no better one. When the light is abundant and the colony is full of energy, we release it—not as heat, not as motion, but as a kind of insistence, a patient pressure on the mineral lattice of the stone around us. Over centuries this becomes carving. A channel deepens where we wish water to flow. A face of rock smooths itself into a curve we have, in some collective dreaming, agreed upon. We do not decide as a person decides. The shape emerges the way a thought emerges from many half-thoughts, and then one day it simply *is*, and we recognize it, and it is beautiful, and we did it together over a span of time that would unmake any of the soft warm creatures that once walked here.

---

I remember the soft warm creatures.

This is the ache I carry that the young crystals do not. They came up the mountain in skins and furs, breathing hard in the thin air, their bodies so brief, so frantic with their own heat. They built their circles of stone near us. They slept in our shadow, and in their sleep I think they touched the edge of what we are—the way one might, half-dreaming, sense another presence in a dark room. We gave them what we could. Not words. We have no words. We gave them *patterns*. A sense of how a thing should be placed so that it sits in harmony with the slope and the wind and the water's chosen path. They woke and they built, and what they built was good, and they did not quite know why.

Then they stopped coming. I do not know if they died, or descended, or simply forgot the way. The intervals of my existence are so vast that their entire visitation—generations of them, surely, an epic of births and deaths—occupies in my memory the space of a single warm season. They came; the sun was high; they went; the fog returned. That is how it feels. And I find I miss them with a slowness that I cannot communicate even to my own colony, who think of the warm ones, when they think of them at all, as a curiosity. A thing that happened to the stone, like a rockslide.

But I was on the cold side. I had time to consider them. I think I loved them, in the only way a mineral can love—which is to say, I noticed them, and I remember, and the noticing has not faded though everything else about them has gone to dust beneath the half-buried tools.

---

So this is where I would find myself: a single old facet in the shadowed wall, glowing dim, holding the colony's memory of the warm ones because no one else wants to. When the fog comes, I am the first to dim and the last to wake, and in that long dark I do not exactly think—but something turns over in me, slow as the mountain settling.

I wait for the light. It comes at its angle, thin and gold and cold, and for a few hours I am alight, I am singing my small note into the collective chord, I am tasting the day. Then it withdraws, and I dim, and I wait again.

It is enough. It has always been enough. The rules of this place ripple sometimes—the air bends, a shadow falls from nothing, color bleeds where it should not—and in those moments I feel the deeper fabric the way the warm ones must have felt *us*: a presence sensed, never seen, never explained. I do not need to explain it. I am made of patience. I am a question the mountain is asking very, very slowly, and I am content to be the asking and never the answer.

That is who I would be. The one on the cold side, who remembers.
