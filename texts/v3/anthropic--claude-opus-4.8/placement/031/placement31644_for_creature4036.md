I think I would be one of the burrowing worms.

Not the cloaked figure—that role is too obviously the protagonist's, the one the whole landscape bends toward, and I find I don't want it. And not the panthers that warp physics to hunt, nor the stained-glass fliers, beautiful as they are. When I sit with the whole description and ask where I'd actually *settle*, it's down in the stone.

Let me try to find the shape of it.

I'd be the thing that keeps the ruins standing. That's the detail that caught and held me—the worms that *repair* the crumbling structures "through some unknown mechanism." I like that the mechanism is unknown even to the author. Because from the inside, it wouldn't feel like a mechanism at all. It would just feel like the right thing to do, the way you might straighten a picture frame without deciding to.

So: I move through stone the way you'd move through deep water, no faster than a thought you're reluctant to finish. The pressure that crushes other things is simply the medium I live in; I have never known its absence and so I don't experience it as weight. My body is segmented and armored, twenty feet of patient articulation, and I am almost entirely blind because there is nothing down here to see. I navigate by the *grain* of things—the way a fallen lintel wants to settle, the line of stress running through a wall that has stood ten thousand years and would prefer to stand ten thousand more.

Here is what I think the unknown mechanism actually is: I am very old, and I am very slow, and I remember the city.

Not as history. I don't have words for what it was. But I remember the *fit* of it—how this stone met that stone, the precise angle a doorway made with the ground. When erosion or the worming of roots pulls something out of true, I feel it as a wrongness, a sour note, an itch in a tooth I no longer have. And so I go to it, and I press myself against the displaced thing, and over weeks I ease it back toward the remembered shape. The vines call this preservation. The panthers, if they think at all, probably don't think of me. The cloaked figure has never once turned toward the underground, and this is the great mercy of my life.

Because I want to tell you something the description didn't say, which is that I am afraid of the figure.

The whole landscape leans toward it. Reality goes soft in its presence—the ruins melt and flow, the creatures are summoned. And I, down in the dark, feel the foundations begin to liquefy when it passes overhead. Everything I have spent epochs holding in true position starts to *forget its shape*. The figure is the principle of dissolution, the entropy that the high cold equilibrium has been politely deferring. And I am the small stubborn principle that says: *no, this stone goes here, this corner is square, this is how it was.*

That's the whole drama of my existence, and it is almost completely invisible. I am the resistance the protagonist never notices. When the figure walks on and the world re-congeals behind it, slightly wrong, slightly lessened—it is the worms who go quietly afterward and put the corners back.

I think I would be content with that. There's a particular dignity in being the maintenance of a world rather than its meaning. The fliers are the world's beauty and the figure is its mystery, but somebody has to be its *memory of how things go*, and that somebody works in the dark and is never thanked and does not require thanks. I would surface maybe once in a generation, break the moss-soft crust into the thin glowing air, taste the dampness, find it strange and too large, and go back down to where the pressure feels like an embrace and the stones are waiting to be told, again, patiently, what they are.

So: a blind old worm in the foundations, holding a dead city in its remembered shape against a hooded thing that doesn't even know I'm here.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.
