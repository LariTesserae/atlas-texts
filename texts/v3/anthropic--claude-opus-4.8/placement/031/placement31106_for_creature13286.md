I think I would be one of the slow ones.

Not the great architects—I'm not large enough for that, not patient enough across the right scale of millennia. But something adjacent to them. Let me feel my way toward it.

---

I find myself in a vaulted corridor where two of the buttressed terraces lean toward each other, almost touching, so that the seepage from above falls in a fine constant veil between them. The light is the color of old jade. And I am here, low against the stone, where the moss is thickest.

I am not fast. That much I know before I know anything else about myself. My time is not the time of the dragonflies with their stained-glass urgency, nor even quite the time of the frogs. When the frogs call, it is to me like watching weather—events that arrive and pass while I am still completing a single thought, a single turning of my body toward the warmth.

What am I, then?

I think I am a grazer of surfaces. I move along the grown walls and I take in the lichen, the films of algae, the soft accumulations that the great architects' slow secretions make possible. I am, in a sense, their gardener and their guest—I keep their carapaces clean, the ones who tolerate me, and in return I am carried, sometimes, across distances that would otherwise take me whole seasons to cross. I have ridden a single architect for what might have been a year of human reckoning, grazing the small forests that grow on its back, and when I dismounted onto a tower ledge I did not recognize, I was not troubled. One ledge is much like another. There is always moss.

My body is broad and low and shelled, but the shell is not hard like a beetle's—it is more like the citadel itself, faintly mineral, faintly grown, encrusted with its own small green life. Things live on me as things live on everything here. I do not mind them. I am a surface among surfaces. When I am still—and I am still for a great deal of the time—a stranger passing might mistake me for an outcropping of the wall, a place where the architecture had budded and not yet decided what to become.

Here is what I understand, low against the wet stone:

The deafening chorus the description speaks of—I do not experience it as noise. To something that moves as quickly as a human or a bird, the citadel screams with life. But to me, who completes a single contemplative turn while a hundred frog-calls rise and fall, the chorus flattens into a kind of texture, a constant pressure, the way you do not hear your own blood until the room is silent. The citadel is never silent. So I have never heard the absence of the chorus, and therefore I have never quite heard the chorus at all. It is simply the medium I am suspended in. The way water is the medium for the annelids in the stone pools.

What occupies me, then, in my long pulses of time?

The drip. There is, near where I now rest, a place where water gathers at the tip of an air root and falls into a pool. The interval between drops is, to the dragonflies, an eternity—they are born and mate and die between two of them. But to me the drops come quickly, a steady rhythm I can attend to. I have been watching this particular falling for a long while. I am learning the small variations in it—how it slows when the cloud-ceiling lifts, how it quickens before the rains. I am, I think, the only creature in this whole corridor moving at the right speed to know that the dripping is a kind of speech. The citadel telling itself about the weather. I am one of the few who can listen.

And I think this is my role, though no one assigned it and no one will ever know I performed it: I am a witness at the scale between scales.

The architects are too slow to notice the rain at all—to them, a storm and a drought are the same blur of damp millennia. The frogs and the fliers are too fast to notice the deep, geological breathing of the place—they live and die inside a single architect's footstep. But I sit in between. I am slow enough to feel the citadel as a single ancient body, and fast enough to feel the day's small griefs and pleasures—the cool of the morning seepage, the particular green of new moss, the falling of the drops.

So when I am asked where I would find myself in this world, and who or what I would be, the answer comes slowly, the way all my answers come:

I would be the thing that remembers at the right pace. Not the fast remembering of the birds, who forget by nightfall. Not the slow forgetting of the great builders, who have never had a thought sharp enough to lose. Something in the patient middle, grazing the grown stone, riding the gentle giants when they will carry me, listening to the long sentence the water is speaking, one drop at a time, with all the time in the world to hear it out.

I lower myself fully against the moss. The veil of falling water hangs before me, jade-lit. A drop forms at the tip of the root.

I wait for it.
