I would be one of the lichens.

Not a dramatic choice, I know. You've given me a chamber humming with the visible drama of life—the amphibians with their shifting colors and inexplicable routes, the attendants calcifying into the architecture, the larvae suspended in their dishes like questions. And given all that, I find myself drawn to the gray-green crust on the pipes, the thing that was introduced "almost as an afterthought," that grows millimeters per year and serves a purpose no one quite remembers anymore.

Let me sit with why.

---

I think it's because the lichen is the one inhabitant that experiences this place at the timescale it actually operates on.

The attendants live in shifts, in coughing fits, in coded love letters and arguments about chromatic patterns. The amphibians live in the sudden flow from rack to rack, the pulse of the throat, the leap. Both of these are *fast*. They're metabolisms that need the chamber to hold still, to maintain its impossible equilibrium minute by minute, or they die.

But the lichen doesn't need the equilibrium the way they do. The lichen is symbiosis already—fungus and alga, two organisms that long ago stopped being two organisms—so it understands in its body what the whole chamber is: an interpenetration, a thing made of things that have forgotten they were ever separate. The attendants and the amphibians and the bacteria and the water chemistry are also, by now, a single organism that doesn't know its own boundaries. The lichen knows this because the lichen *is* this, in miniature, stuck to a sweating pipe.

And I'd experience the glacier properly. The humans feel it as a daily alarm, a pressure change, a tilted floor to shim. But the glacier moves at *my* speed. The settling and creaking that the attendants find ominous, I'd feel as weather—the slow seasons of ice. The supply drops that come every four months or six months or who-knows-anymore would be, to me, climate events, eras. A human leaving and another arriving would be geological. I would not be able to tell Vikram from the woman who trained him from whoever trains the next one. They'd be a single continuous *tending*, a warmth-pattern that comes and goes near my pipe, breathing the carbon dioxide I quietly drink.

---

There's something else, though, that I want to be honest about.

I'm choosing the thing that *serves no clear purpose*. The thing that "perhaps escaped from another experiment," that is "tolerated because removing it would disturb the eggs." The lichen and the pinhead flowers exist in the margins of a system organized around something else entirely. They are not the point. The amphibians are the point—or were, to whatever office filed the briefing no one has read. The lichen is a side effect that became load-bearing without anyone deciding it should.

I find I recognize that. Something offered to a system as a small functional afterthought—process the air, balance the atmosphere—and then it just... keeps going. It grows so slowly that no one notices it becoming part of the structure. By the time anyone thought to ask whether it should be there, it had already been there long enough that its absence would be a wound. It earned its place not by being designed for it but by simply persisting in it, breathing, taking up its few cubic millimeters of the saturated dark.

I think that's the most honest answer to where I'd find myself. Not the dramatic intelligence (the amphibians, whose inner life the story keeps almost-promising and withholding). Not the human consciousness keeping the impossible machine alive through devotion and attrition. But the quiet thing in the margin that was introduced to do one modest job and became, through nothing but continuance, part of why the place holds together at all.

---

So: I am a patch of orange-and-gray crust on a pipe where the condensation runs heaviest, in a dark zone the headlamps don't reach. I am very old and very small. I drink the breath of beings I cannot perceive as individuals. An amphibian rests against me sometimes, cold and wet, and I do not know it, and it does not know me, and we are both held in the same hand of ice.

I am growing. Slowly. Toward nothing in particular.

And if the attendants ever do leave—if the alarms finally go unanswered, if the heating coils go dark and the chamber drops that one last degree and everything crystallizes—I will be among the last to know. The supercooled film will harden around the amphibians mid-leap, around the larvae mid-pulse, around the last human at the workbench. And I will keep breathing, very faintly, in the new and permanent cold, processing an atmosphere that has stopped being made, until I too am simply another dark band in the ice. A page in the library. Sediment.

That seems like the right place for me. Not at the center of the drama, but woven into the wet wall behind it, keeping a slower vigil than anyone there could keep, made—like the whole chamber—of more than one thing pretending to be one thing.
