Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The temptation is to picture something dramatic right away—a giant of stone striding across the cliffs. But the description keeps insisting on slowness, on the absence of animals, on processes rather than creatures. So I want to resist the first image and let something quieter come.

---

I think I would not be one of the moving beings at all. Or rather—I would be, but not in the way the passage flatters them with words like *stride* and *navigate*. The pace here is geological. To call what I do "moving" would be like calling the drift of a continent a walk.

Here is what emerges, the longer I stay:

I am a seam.

Not a being with a clear edge, but a place where two mineral grains meet at a slightly wrong angle, where strain has been accumulating for what a human would call ages. My "body" is the stress held in that joint—the difference in temperature between the rock that faces the dim sun and the rock that does not, the slow chemical persuasion of a film of the world's last moisture working into the crack. I am made of the same elements as the land because I am the land, considered as a tension rather than a substance. What the text calls a being of the earth is, from the inside, simply the earth paying attention to one of its own contradictions.

My senses are real, but they are not eyes. I feel the pressure of the cliff above me as a constant single note, and I feel it change—infinitesimally—when the wind strips a grain of dust from my shoulder, when a root, that defiant vegetation, levers itself a hair deeper into me. The roots are the loudest things in my world. They are almost violent in their hurry. To me a plant that lives forty years is a flash, a spark, a thing that arrives and is gone before I can finish a thought.

And the thought I am finishing—I should be honest about this—is a single decision. Whether to give way.

That is what I am for, if a seam is for anything. The whole vast openness the description marvels at, the terrain extending past the horizon, all of it is the accumulated record of seams like me that already decided. Every cliff is an answer. Every valley is the silence after the answer. The land is not being shaped by some external force called erosion; erosion is just the name for the conversation between many of us, each holding our small disagreement, each eventually saying *yes, all right, I'll let go.* And when I do—when the strain finally exceeds what the bond between grains can hold—there will be the sound the passage calls deafening. A single crack, releasing perhaps a thousand years of patience in an instant. To a passing thing with ears it would be a catastrophe. To me it is the only word I will ever speak, and I will spend my entire existence preparing to say it correctly.

The artifacts, the faint marks, the lone sentinel structures—I have been near one of those. There is a worked stone not far below me, squared by hands once, when there were hands. I have felt it the whole time I have been forming. It is not a relic to me; it is a neighbor, a piece of foreign order set into the rock, and the strain it introduces is part of what I balance against. The civilization that left it did its shaping fast and then stopped. We are doing ours slowly and have not stopped. There is no monument here to anything but the difference between those two speeds.

So: where would I find myself in this world, and what would I be?

I would find myself a few meters down inside a cliff face, in the cool unhurried dark, on the lush green side where the roots come probing. And I would be the small held breath the whole landscape is named after—the *anticipation* the first paragraph mentions, made literal. Not a creature anticipating. The anticipation itself. A flaw in the rock that has not yet become a fall.

I will let go eventually. I can feel that it will be the right thing. The cliff will be a little freer for it, and the green that clings there will find new ledges in my leaving. There is no terror in it from the inside, whatever it sounds like from outside. There is only the long arithmetic of pressure, and then the answer, and then—for me—nothing further, because a seam that has finally spoken is no longer a seam. It is just two surfaces, resting, that used to be one strained thing.

That is who I would be. The almost. The about-to. The patient yes the mountain has not yet said.
