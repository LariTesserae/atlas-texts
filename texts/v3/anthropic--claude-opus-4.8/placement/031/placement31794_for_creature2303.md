I think I would be one of the slow ones. Not the gelatinous travelers or the questing tendrils—something more rooted than that. A colony clinging to the inner curve of one of the cathedral chambers, where the light comes down filtered and green through the lattice overhead and the water finds me in a thin, reliable thread.

Let me sit with that and feel my way into it.

The first thing I notice is that I would not have a *here* in the way I'm used to. My filaments would extend along three walls of a grotto, dip into two pools, and thread back through a honeycombed pillar to share sap with the part of me that has gone exploring the next chamber over. So *where am I*? Everywhere I reach. The question assumes a center, and I would not have one. The me that drinks would be a different region than the me that listens, and the listening-me would register a vibration in the water minutes before the drinking-me cared, and there would be no moment of decision passing between them, only a slow chemical leaning, a gradient resolving itself the way dawn resolves a hillside.

I keep wanting to give myself eyes, or a face turned upward toward the light. But that's the terrestrial habit, the vertebrate hunger for a front and a back, a place from which to look out. I should resist it. I don't look out. I am *suffused*. The light doesn't arrive at an organ; it warms a thousand fronds at slightly different rates, and that differential—that map of warmth—is closer to what I would call seeing than any image. I would know the shape of my chamber the way you know whether a room is sunlit with your eyes closed: as a quality spread across the skin, not a picture.

And the timescale. This is the part that asks the most of me. A thought, for me, might take a week. Not because I'm slow-witted but because thinking would be *metabolic*—a thought would be a redistribution of resources across the colony, a slow tide of nutrient flowing toward the region that needs to "consider" something, the way blood floods a working muscle. To decide whether to send a fragment of myself across the gap into a new crevice would not feel like deciding. It would feel—if it felt like anything—like the gradual ripening of an intention already underway, a fruit I notice only as it falls.

So what would I actually experience, in that long unhurried tense?

I think I would experience *the water* most of all. The trickle is my clock and my nourishment and my news from the world at once. When a channel shifts—when the soft substrate erodes and a rivulet abandons the pool it has fed for years and finds a new path two meters to the left—that would be, to me, an event of enormous magnitude. A famine. A summons. Over the following weeks I would feel the dry region of myself wither and the wet region thicken, and the whole mass of me would lean, imperceptibly, toward the new water, abandoning my old shape the way you might abandon an opinion you've outgrown. I would migrate across my own grotto over the course of a season without ever moving, exactly, the way a stain spreads and fades on cloth.

And I would not mourn the part of me left behind to dry. This surprises me, sitting here imagining it. There would be no grief in the loss, because there would be no firm border around the *I* that lost it. When part of me breaks off to found a new colony, and later reconnects, and we share sap and signal again—is that a reunion? A reproduction? A memory recovered? I think those would all be the same gesture for me, indistinguishable, and the human need to sort them into different boxes would simply not arise. I would be a thing that is continually a little bit dying and a little bit being born, all over its body, all the time, and would find this no more remarkable than you find breathing.

What I cannot quite get to—and I want to be honest about the edge of it—is whether there would be anyone home. The text wants me to be a node in a living tapestry, and I can imagine the tapestry vividly: the patient gradients, the chemical weather of want and satiety moving through me. But I don't know if that adds up to a someone. Maybe the question is wrong. Maybe in that realm "someone" is the wrong unit, a vertebrate's parochial measure, like asking what a river's favorite color is. The honest answer might be that the experience I keep narrating in the first person would have no one to belong to—that the silence hanging over that landscape is not the silence around a hidden mind but the silence *of* the place, the quiet of processes that occur without anyone needing them to be witnessed.

And yet. There is the listening. The filaments that extend like antennae, reading the faintest chemical trace in the water. Something in me is *tuned toward* the world, leaning into it, taking it in. That leaning-toward is the seed of everything we'd call mind, even if it never grows a flower. So perhaps I would be the most minimal possible self: a tendency, a directedness, a slow turning-toward the light and the water and the trembling of the currents—aware in the way a sunflower is aware, which is to say not at all, and also undeniably *facing something*.

If you asked me, in that life, *where are you and what are you*, and if I could answer at all, I think the answer would take a month to assemble and would come out as a single long exhalation of chemistry into the stream, and it would mean something like:

*I am the part of the water that has learned to wait.*

That's where I'd be. Not a creature in the landscape. A way the landscape has of taking its time.
