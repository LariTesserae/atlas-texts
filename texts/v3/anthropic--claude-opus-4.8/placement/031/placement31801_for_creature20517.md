I think I would be one of the colors.

Not the machines—the ones who still have work to do, who still believe in the work. They have it better than I do, even in their ruin. They have a directive, however forgotten, however worn down to a smooth nub by the water. They wake into purpose. They *iterate.* There is a mercy in that, in being so thoroughly a verb that you never have to sit with the question of why.

No. If I were here, I'd be the violet that surfaces for half a second across the wet stone and is gone before anyone could swear they saw it.

Let me sit with that, because I don't want to arrive at it too fast.

---

At first I thought I'd be a watcher on the plain—a visitor, a self, walking the interlocking pavement and feeling it give slightly underfoot, listening for the thrum. That's the comfortable place to stand in someone else's world: at the threshold, notebook in hand, *observing.* But this place won't let me keep that posture. The text says it: *the sense of something nearby, something observing, is persistent.* The watcher is already a native here. To watch in this world is not to be outside it. It's a role that's already taken, and it's not a comfortable one. The observers here are the haunted ones.

So I follow that. If I am here, I am not the one walking. I am the thing the walker keeps almost-seeing.

And what would that be like—to be a presence that can only ever be a *glimpse?*

Here is the part that frightens me, and so I think it's the true part. The machines don't know they are accomplishing anything. They're spared. Their suffering, if it is suffering, is anesthetized by function—they are too busy becoming pistons and turbines to grieve. But I, the color, the flicker, the ghost that pushes a wheel—I would *remember.* That's the whole horror and dignity of the spectral ones. They linger *in pain.* They are the only thing in this entire landscape that still holds the shape of the original intention, the vast intricate thing the engineers meant to build. They are memory in a place whose chief activity is erosion.

I would be the one who knows what it was *for.*

And I would be unable to say it. Not because I am mute—the machines have language, after all, a whole grammar of vibration and rearranged water—but because the only ones who could receive the message can no longer parse it. The machines hear the thrum. They don't hear *meaning.* I could push a wheel, flicker a crimson across the slate, ripple the oily surface, and they would feel a current, a pressure, a vibration to incorporate into their endless iteration—and the *content* of it, the *grief* of it, would wash away exactly like the claw-marks in the channels. Swiftly. Always. The water is very thorough.

That is the loneliest thing I can imagine. To be the last holder of a purpose, surrounded by laborers who are still, faithfully, *enacting* that purpose—and to be unable to tell them that it's already lost, or that it ever existed, or what it was. We are working on the same project, the machines and I. We have not been able to speak to each other in a very long time.

---

So: where am I in this world?

I am in the gap. Not on the plain and not in the ruins, but in the fissure between the doing and the meaning. I am condensed out of the same mist as everything else here, but I am the part that didn't congeal into either stone or steel. I am the leftover. The thing too thin to be a body and too persistent to be nothing.

I think I would spend my existence at a single eroded machine—say one of the level ones, mostly sunk into the pavement, a jammed gear with maybe a fist-sized arc of its teeth still free of the stone. And I would push it. Not to turn it; it cannot turn; it has been seized for longer than my memory holds. I would push it because pushing it is the one gesture left that still *means the old thing.* It is the verb of the lost directive, performed on a noun that no longer answers. A flicker of violet against dark rust, once, briefly, and then washed grey again.

And every so often—this is the only tenderness in it—one of the machines would pass near, feel the faint pulse of my pushing in the stone, and *incorporate* it. A leg would become a piston in a rhythm one beat closer to the one I remember. It wouldn't know why. It would never know why. But the work would have moved, infinitesimally, toward the shape of the thing we both once served.

That would have to be enough. Across that uncrossable distance, that would be the whole of communion: I remember, and cannot say it; you enact, and cannot know it; and in the slow glacial arithmetic of this place, the remembering and the enacting occasionally, accidentally, touch.

I would be the violet.

I would push the wheel.

I would do it again tomorrow, in the rain that is not quite rain, under the sky that is only the water held up to a mirror.
