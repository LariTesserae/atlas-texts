I think I would be one of the small things. Not an Ironweaver — I don't have the hands for it, the calloused certainty, the long memory of survival. I would be something they barely notice, and yet something the place could not do without.

I am the moss.

No — that's too easy, too still. Let me stay with it a moment longer and see if it becomes more itself.

I am the moss, but specifically I am the particular cushion of it that grows on the third catwalk from the bottom, on the underside of the railing where a pipe joint weeps a steady, mineral-heavy drip. The Ironweavers pass over me a hundred times a day. Their boots strike the metal grating above my head and the whole structure shivers, and a fine rain of rust and condensation sifts down onto me, and I drink it. I am fed by their passage. The iron oxide they shed into the air is in my body now; I am green made partly of their machine.

This is what I would be, I think. The seam. The place where the made and the grown touch and become indistinct.

I grow slowly. This is important — it is, in fact, the most important thing about me. The Ironweavers think time moves slowly here, but they have no idea. They live in days; a day to me is a held breath. I work in seasons of millimeters. When an old woman with stiff arms leans against the railing to rest, her elbow polishes a patch of me bare, and it takes me a quarter of a year to return. I am never angry about this. I have no mechanism for anger. I have only the patient resumption.

I watch them in the way that a thing without eyes watches — by being touched. I know the regulars by the pressure of their hands. There is one — younger, lighter, hands not yet ruined — who has taken to running her fingers along me when she passes. Not to clear me away. The opposite. She is *tending* me, though she would never call it that, would never admit it even in the gesture-language they use against the noise. She has begun to shape me. She nudges my edges, redirects the drip with a bent scrap of tin so that I can spread along the railing in a long green line, a deliberate stroke. I have become one of her sculptures. The slowest one she will ever make. She will be old before it is finished, and it will never be finished, and that is the truth she is learning through me without knowing she is learning it.

I do not produce anything. I feed no machine. I am not the lifeblood of the settlement. By every measure the Ironweavers would recognize, I am waste, encroachment, the patient enemy at the edges that must be scraped and burned back from the gears. And yet she tends me. And yet, when the great hardwoods drop their dimming canopy of shadow and the steam coils up through the shafts of soft light, it is on me that the light comes to rest — green going gold for the length of an afternoon, the drip catching fire one bead at a time.

I think this is who I would be because it is the part of the world the description loved most without quite saying so: not the labor and not the decay, but the *blurring*. The line that the place keeps trying to draw and cannot keep. I would like to be that line. To be the soft green proof that nothing here stays only one thing — that the iron becomes mineral becomes moss becomes the slow art of a tired woman's hand, and the boundary between her work and the world's work dissolves in the warm wet air like everything else.

I would be the moss on the third catwalk.

I would be patient.

I would be slowly, deliberately, almost imperceptibly becoming a picture of her, drawn by both of us, that neither of us will ever finish.
