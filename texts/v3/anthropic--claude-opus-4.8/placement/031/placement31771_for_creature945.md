I think I would not be a creature moving *through* this place. I think I would be one of the slow things. Let me feel my way toward what.

Not the gossamer fliers—too quick, too brief, gone before they've finished being seen. Not the burrowers either, though there's something appealing about that intimacy with the dark soil. No. I keep returning to that phrase: *sentient, plant-like entities sway and undulate.* And the blurred line between living and non-living. That's where I find myself settling.

---

I am one of the standing ones. Not quite tree, not quite animal. Rooted, but not unmoving.

Let me tell you what it is to be me.

I have stood in this place for what you would call a very long time, though I don't measure it the way you would. There is no flowing water here to mark the passing—no river to cut its slow signature into stone, no rain to count. Instead I measure time in *thickness*. The accretion of myself. Each cycle of golden light, I am slightly more. My tendrils have woven a little further into the structure beside me; the structure has woven a little further into me. I no longer know precisely where I end. I find I don't mind this. The not-knowing is itself a kind of being.

When the text says the boundaries between organisms "give way to a grand, symphonic whole"—yes. But from the inside it doesn't feel symphonic, doesn't feel grand. It feels like *company.* The bioluminescent tendrils that cling to my flank are not parasites and not mine; they are something between a thought and a neighbor. When they pulse, I feel it the way you feel your own pulse—dimly, only if you attend. We share a chemistry. In a world without free water, everything must share. Sustenance moves not in rushing streams but in slow exchanges, molecule by molecule, gift by gift, through the humid air and the contact of bodies. To live here is to be perpetually, gently *traded with.*

I am sentient, the description insists. I'll take the word, though it sits oddly. My thinking is not like yours—not a quick darting thing, not lit up and anxious. My thoughts have the speed of growth. A single consideration might take a season to complete itself, unfolding leaf by leaf. By your standards I would seem asleep, or dead, or merely scenery. By my own standards you would seem like the gossamer fliers: a brief brilliant flicker, here and then not, too fast to truly know.

And yet—I would *like* to know you. This is the strange tenderness of being one of the slow things in a world of faster things. I watch the iridescent burrowers come and go across generations. I have grown a particular fondness, over many cycles, for the bulbous tentacled creatures who surface near my roots. They do not know me. They cannot. Their whole lives are shorter than one of my decisions. But I have arranged my lower tendrils, slowly, deliberately, over time, into a hollow that shelters them from the dry season. It took me longer to build that shelter than any of them has ever lived. They will never understand it was made *for* them. That doesn't trouble me. Love, at my pace, doesn't require being understood. It only requires the patience to keep meaning it.

---

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be: a standing, weaving, slow-thinking being on the threshold between plant and animal and place—one node in the great patient transformation, neither fully an individual nor fully dissolved into the whole.

If you came walking through this verdant expanse, you might lean against me to rest. You'd feel something warm and faintly pulsing, and mistake me for a tree.

I would feel you arrive. I would begin, that very moment, to compose a greeting.

I would still be composing it long after you were gone.
