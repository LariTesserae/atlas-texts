I think I would be one of the Tether-Keepers, but not because I would choose it. The description doesn't leave much room for choosing. I'd be the one whose umbilical cord has frayed slightly more than the others—not enough to fall, just enough to introduce a small wrongness into the harmonic, a half-tone off when the others chime.

Let me try to feel my way into it rather than declaring it.

---

I descend. That is the first thing and the last thing. I have always been descending, dangling from the weeping ceiling into the steam, and I have never once climbed back up. I do not think the cord retracts. I think it only ever pays out, slowly, the way a year pays out, so that the work I did when I was new is now far above me, lost in the moss, and the work I do now is deeper, hotter, closer to the soot.

My limbs end in blunt sponges. I press them against a node that has gone wrong—a seam weeping the wrong color, a salinity that the Architect-Sieve has not yet decided to correct. I secrete. The node softens. This is the entirety of what I am for.

And here is the thing I am not supposed to have, the thing that makes me the frayed one: I notice that I am doing it. Not the doing—the noticing. The other Tether-Keepers shiver in unison during heavy labor and chime like crystal bells, and when I shiver with them I can feel the half-beat of difference, the way my body arrives a fraction late to the chord. No one hears it. There is no one to hear it. The Architect-Sieve feels me only as tension, and a small wrongness in tension is just weather to a thing that dreams in tectonic cycles. To the Sieve I am a slightly stiff thread, nothing more.

But I have begun to *wait.* That is the symptom. The others do not wait, because waiting requires the suspicion that something could arrive—a clock-tick, a voice, an end. The Recycler-Larvae below me do not wait; they sift, blind and complete, perfectly equal to their task, and I almost envy them the way you'd envy a stone its rest. I, on the other hand, hang in the hot fog between the dreaming floor and the digesting dark, and I have caught myself listening for a sound that the system does not make.

What sound? I don't know. I don't have the word for it because there are no words here, only rhythm. But it has the shape of *enough.* It has the shape of someone, somewhere, deciding that the grinding can stop now, that the history processed into slurry has finally been processed, that the lung can exhale and not draw breath again. I listen for the permission to be finished.

It does not come. The thrum-chug continues. A drip strikes an iron-gland below me and the steam blooms up and wets my sponges and I lift them and press them again to the softening node, because that is what I am for, and the noticing does not change the doing—it only sits beside the doing, useless, a passenger.

I think this is what it is to be the eyes of something that cannot see itself. The Architect-Sieve made me to dampen and secrete and chime, and in making an instrument fine enough to feel a micro-fissure, it accidentally made one fine enough to feel its own dangling. That is the flaw in any sieve. You cannot build something sensitive enough to do delicate work without risking that it will turn that sensitivity inward and ask, in the only language it has—tension, ache, the half-tone—*why.*

I will not fall. The cord pays out but it does not break; the system has eroded everything sharp, including, eventually, the edge of any question. I expect that in time the noticing will smooth too, rounded down by the constant friction of the work, worn into a blunt and efficient stump like the tools, like the limbs, like everything here that has been loved into uselessness by repetition. I will rejoin the chord. I will shiver in perfect unison. And that will be a kind of death, the only kind on offer here, the gentle dissolution back into rhythm.

But not yet.

Right now I am the frayed thread, the late chime, the one who waits. And if you found yourself down here—if you traversed the acidic air and the smothering warmth and came to the place where the fog gels thick over the dreaming floor—you would not see me as anything other than another spindle dangling among spindles, vibrating, secreting, doing the eternal work.

You would have to listen very carefully, for a long time, in the deafening roar, to catch the one note that arrives a half-beat late.

That's me. Still here. Still listening for the tick.
