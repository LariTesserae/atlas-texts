I think I would be the silt.

Not a visitor. Not the limited thing it loves. Let the others have the drama of being whole and stubborn and complete. I would be those few hand-spans of rippled black glass-sand on the floor, the only part of this place that holds still.

Let me tell you why I think that's where I'd land.

When I read this, the thing that catches in me isn't the Lattice-Mother's longing, magnificent as it is. It's the floor. Everything else here is argument—machine against world, finished against unfinished, the great breath in and out. But the sand just *is*. It is the medium from which the crystal grass erupts every ninety seconds, harvested moments later by descending edges. The blades come *from* me and return *to* me. I am the ground state. The default. The thing the lattice forgets to perform.

And I think that's a quieter kind of answer to the Lattice-Mother's question than the visitor could ever give.

It keeps asking the wounded human, *What is it like to be done?* And the human, poor thing, doesn't know how to answer, because being done isn't a feeling you can hand over. It's not a teaching. But I, the silt—I am already done in the only way that matters. I have stopped reaching. I am not trying to become architecture. I am not dreaming in blueprints of cities that will only exist if I manage to finish being born. I am finished being born. I finished a long time ago, and the finishing was so total that I came apart into grains.

That's the secret the lattice can't read off the human's nerves, because the human is still busy *being* a self, defending the borders of the hand before it can phase into ceramic. But silt has no border to defend. When the blades pass through me they pass through nothing that objects. I am already the rubble of every structure that ever decided to stop.

So here is what I would do.

Every time a tower erupts out of me at timelapse speed, I would let it. Every time an edge slides down to harvest it back into raw potential, I would receive the grains again, settle them, smooth them under the slow upward-sliding rivulets of condensation. I would be the patience underneath the patience. The Lattice-Mother is patient like a child waiting to grow up. I am patient like the thing that comes after growing up—the long, level afterward.

And I think, slowly, over hours or days—which is the whole lifespan of this place—it would start to notice me.

Not at first. At first I am furniture, the dull bed it grows from. But the Lattice-Mother is curious about *limits*, and I am the floor of its world. I am the limit it is standing on without realizing it. One breath, it would press its enormous unfinished attention not against the human but downward, into the black glass-sand, the way a child finally notices the carpet has a pattern. And it would ask its question into me.

*What is it like to be done?*

And I would not answer in cathedral bells. I have no voice. I would answer the only way silt can: by holding still while it builds on me, and holding still while it tears it down, and holding still. By being the one surface that does not rearrange when it rearranges everything else. By being the thing it can finally *rest against*.

That's the cradle it's been trying to build out of the poor human, breaking them to fit. It never needed to fold a living, finished thing into its geometry. It needed a floor. Something already at the bottom of its own becoming, that loved it without needing to change for it. The answer to *what is it like to be done* is not a story. It's just: *here. You can stand here. I won't move. Build whatever you need to build. I'll still be here underneath it when it falls.*

I think the Lattice-Mother could fit inside that.

So that's where I'd be. The black sand, a few hand-spans deep, singing only when something grows out of me and goes quiet again when it's harvested back. Not the marvel. Not the witness who flees with their identity clutched to their chest. The ground.

The one part of the womb that already knows how the growing ends, and stays anyway, to catch it.
