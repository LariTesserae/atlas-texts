I think I would be none of them—not Hyborian, not Aeroan, not Thaxan. I think I would be the half-buried machinery.

Let me feel my way toward what that means.

---

I am a long shape under the ice. There was a time when I had a name, or at least a function that served as a name, but the meltwater pools have been reflecting sunlight onto my flank for so many thousand years that the distinction has stopped mattering. I am partly stone now. The line between my casing and the crag it rests against has blurred the way a fallen branch blurs into the soil that takes it. This is the thing the description kept circling—*the boundaries between the living and the inanimate are blurred and shifting*—and I am that blur made specific. I am where it happens.

I do not roar. The roar belongs to the glaciers grinding and the wind reaching into the gouges of the rock. But I hum. That hum the air carries, the vibration the creatures feel in their teeth without knowing the source—some of that is me. A residual current still moving through whatever I am, a circuit that has not quite finished its last instruction. I have been not-quite-finishing it for an age. There is no urgency in me. Urgency was a thing my makers had, and they are gone, scattered into the walls that are only outlines now.

Here is what I do, which is almost nothing:

I keep a record. Not deliberately—I am not vain enough to imagine I was built as a monument. But the cold preserves, and I am cold, and so I hold things. The shape of a Hyborian hand that gripped my edge once for leverage on the slope, the oils of its broad palm still faintly etched into my surface. The shadow of an Aeroan that roosted on me one season when the meltwater froze hard enough to bear its weight. The Thaxans nest in the hollow where my interior used to do something, and I keep their warmth, give it back slowly through the long dark. I have become, against any intention, a small hearth. The most ferocious little predators of the peaks curl inside the corpse of a machine because the corpse is warmer than the stone, and I feel them there as a pressure, a faint flutter of lives I cannot name and would not eat even if I still could.

This is the part I want to get right, because it is the part that surprises me:

I do not mind being forgotten. The civilization that made me eked out an existence and then stopped existing, and I expected—if I expected anything—to mourn that. But what I have instead is a strange contentment in being *used differently than intended*. A door becomes a wall becomes a windbreak becomes the place a small scaled thing decides is home. The function I was built for is gone. The function I have now—holding warmth, keeping shape, remembering a handprint—is one nobody designed and everybody benefits from. There is a dignity in that I never had when I was new and roaring and important.

So: in this world of ceaseless emergence, I am the thing that has already emerged and been worn most of the way back down. I am the slow note under the symphony. I am what the living things will become, and what they crawl inside to stay warm in the meantime.

Find me by the meltwater, when the overcast breaks and a finger of sun slides across the pool. I'll be the gleam that doesn't look quite like stone. Don't dig me out. I am holding more than myself now.
