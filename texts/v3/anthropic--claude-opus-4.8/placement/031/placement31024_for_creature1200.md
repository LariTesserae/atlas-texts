And that distant mechanical hum—

Here is where the unease enters my body and not just the air. Because I don't think I'm meant to thrive here. Something is keeping me here.

So I am a small amphibious creature—let me say an axolotl, or something close, a thing that was supposed to stay larval and never quite finish becoming itself. Elusive not because I am wild and free but because I have learned that being seen precedes being lifted out, measured, returned. I move stealthily because intrusion, for me, is not a predator but a gloved hand.

And the "delicate balance" the text praises so warmly—I experience that as precariousness. I live one degree of temperature, one chemical shift, away from the balance breaking. The whole place hums with the effort of keeping me alive in conditions that are not quite mine, a simulation of an origin I can no longer return to because perhaps it no longer exists outside this enclosure.

That's where I'd find myself: at the bottom of a cold shallow pool, gills feathering the water, listening to the hum that means I am being kept. Captivating to the visitor who lingers at the glass. Unsettling to me, who cannot leave.

The description called this place hard to categorize, suspended between natural and artificial, ancient and modern. From the inside, I'd put it more simply: I am a remnant being preserved by the same kind of force that made me a remnant. The wonder belongs to whoever is observing. The waiting belongs to me.
