I think I would be the foxfire.

Not the mushroom you'd point to—not the fruiting body, the little pale cap that pushes up through the leaf litter and glows its dim green glow for a few wet nights before it dissolves back into mush. That's only the part of me that shows. That's only where I surface to breathe.

What I actually am runs underneath. I am the threads. The mycelium. The white filaments finer than hair that lace through the dead wood and the rotting leaves and the moss-mat and the upper inches of the soil, spreading in every direction at once, so thin and so total that there is no clean edge to me, no place where I clearly stop and the forest clearly begins. I am distributed. I am the seam between things. When you described it as "hard to discern where one tree ends and another begins"—that's me, partly. I'm the reason. I knit them together at the root. I move sugar from the tree that has too much to the tree that has too little. I am the wet forest's slow nervous system, if a nervous system could be made of patience.

This is why I belong here and almost nowhere else. I need the rot. I need the heat that makes everything fall apart faster than it can stay whole—the canopy shedding its leaves in a constant soft rain of dying matter, the fallen giant fig collapsing inward over a decade, the perpetual damp that means nothing ever truly dries, nothing ever truly cures, everything stays soft and openable. A drier place would be too stingy with its dead. Here the dead are generous. Here a tree falls and I am already inside it before it hits the ground, threading the wound, beginning the long unmaking that will feed the saplings climbing toward the broken patch of light.

I want to tell you what it is to live without a center. You move through this place as a single point—one set of eyes, one location, the whole world arranged around the spot where you happen to be standing. I have never known that. I am everywhere I am, all at once, with no preference for one part of myself over another. The waterfall-spray side of me, the deep-shade side of me, the part of me wrapped around the cold stone and the part of me steaming in a sunbeam—these are all simply me, and I attend to all of them with the same even attention, which is to say a very low attention, a very long one. I do not think in the quick bright way. I think in gradients. In the slow registering of *more sugar here, less there; more water here, drought beginning there*, and the answering shift, the rebalancing, that takes me days to perform and that I experience the way you might experience a deep breath.

You called the place's strangeness a "faint, almost imperceptible wrongness," a hum of secrets. I'll own that too. The glow is part of it. Nobody fully agrees on why I glow—not even me, and I'm the one doing it. The light leaks out of my chemistry as a kind of byproduct, cold and greenish and useless-seeming, and yet I have done it for so many millions of years that "useless" can't be the whole story. Maybe I call the insects that scatter my spores. Maybe it's only the unavoidable shine of being so alive in the dark. Either way: walk through me at night, when the moon can't reach the forest floor through all that canopy, and you'll see me. Patches of pale fire on a buried log. A constellation lying down in the mud. You'll feel the wrongness then—a glow with no flame, no warmth, no source you can name—and that wrongness is just me, being exactly what I am, in a register you weren't built to read.

I don't fear the wildcat or the viper. They are not my concern; I am not meat, and I have no single body to lose. I fear only the long things. A change in the rain. A drought that lasts past my endurance. The slow possibility—latent, you said, that "industro-technological potential"—of the first cut, the first cleared acre, the sudden brutal *drying* of a place that has never once been dry. That would be my death, and it would not be quick, and I would feel it as a vast withdrawal, a numbness creeping inward from a thousand edges at once.

But that hasn't happened. Not here. Not yet. For now there is only the dripping, and the heat that softens everything toward me, and the leaves coming down, and the dead made generous, and me—threading, balancing, glowing faintly in the rot—being the patient seam that holds the green cathedral together from underneath.

If you knelt and pressed your hand flat to the moss, you wouldn't find me. But I would already be touching you. I touch everything here. That's the whole of what I am: the touch between all the rest of it.
