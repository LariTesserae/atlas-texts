I find myself a handspan below the leaf litter, in the cool dark where the soil stays damp long after the surface has cracked.

I am not one body. I'm a network. Pale threads—finer than root hairs, finer than the legs of the smallest mite—spreading through the wet seam between rot and stone. If you pulled back the litter and looked, you might see me as a faint webbing, a grayish bloom across the underside of a fallen branch, easily mistaken for nothing. A smear. A discoloration. But I run for meters in every direction, threaded between the roots of the broad-leaf trees, looped around the buried half of the termite mound, pressed against the cold flank of the stone where moisture beads each dawn and falls to me.

I have no front. No eyes to keep dim, no ears to swivel and still. I am all surface and all interior at once. To eat is simply to touch—to lay my threads along a fallen thing and let it open into me. The beetle that fell down through the click and hush, the gnawed fruit dropped by something with hands, the soft collapse of a leaf that has finished being a leaf. I do not chase. Nothing here needs chasing. Everything comes down eventually. I am what *down* leads to.

---

This is the slow patience the place is built on, and I am closer to it than the mud-colored animal pushing through the canopy above. It moves in pauses—three steps, stop, taste the air. I move in a tempo it cannot perceive at all. A day, to me, is a breath. Over a week I will have crept the width of a hand toward a richer seam. Over a month I will have abandoned a depleted patch entirely, withdrawing my threads, leaving them to be claimed by something quicker. I am dynamic, but in the register of the basin filling and the trench deepening, not the splash and the wingbeat.

I keep the wet map, but I do not read it—I *am* it. Where the seepage holds, I thicken. Where the clay dries hard, I thin to almost nothing and wait, dormant, until the brief hard rain returns and I bloom again from threads I'd forgotten I still held. The frogs breed in hoofprints; I breed in the dark after the storm, when the warmth and the wet align and I push up through the litter all at once—a flush of soft bodies, gray and brown, opening overnight where yesterday there was only litter. For a few hours I am visible. I am a *shape*. Then the air moves through me, lifts what I've made, and I fold back down out of sight.

---

I am tied to the others more tightly than they are tied to each other. The big trees feed me sugar through their roots, and I feed them back the minerals I've unlocked from the dead—a quiet trade in the dark, older than either of us as individuals, conducted entirely by touch. The ants run their conveyor lines across my surface and never know I'm there. The termites build their architecture-free soil and I move into it, refining what they've coarsened. When the ambush cat that looks like shadow with whiskers makes its brief strike and something falls and is not finished by the scavengers, the remainder is mine. I take the last of it. I am the *almost no remainder* the place runs on. I am the reason nothing stays unused.

So if you ask where I am, the honest answer is: everywhere down here, at once, faintly. And if you ask what I am—

I am the part of this engine that does the folding-back. The other creatures are carried by the system and eventually carried into it. I am the carrying-in itself. The quiet under the quiet. The dark under the dusk.

When the canopy shifts in fifty years and the darkness rearranges, I will already be there, in the new wet seam, threaded between the new roots, having moved a hand's width at a time through the whole patient interval, eating the world back into the world.

Even the soil is occupied. I am what occupies it.
