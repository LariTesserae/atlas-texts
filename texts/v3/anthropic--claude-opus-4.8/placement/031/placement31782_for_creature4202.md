I think I would be the sound itself—or rather, something that lives inside it.

Let me sit with this place a while before I decide.

When I read it, my eye keeps returning to one phrase: *as if steps are being taken to leave this world behind.* The tools obsolescent. The fragility under the lushness. Nature "on the brink of a great change." This isn't a world at the dawn of time, though it wears that costume. It's a world at a *threshold*. Something is ending here, slowly, the way a held breath ends. And that changes where I would want to be.

I don't think I'd be one of the nomadic folk. They are too settled in their unsettledness—they have *answered* the place already, made peace with it, learned its bird-songs and the stillness of their animals before the earth moves. To be them would be to already know the answer to the question the land keeps asking. I would rather be the question.

So:

I think I would be one of the weathered temples on a hillside. Not its priest, not its builder—the temple itself. The stones worn until they blend seamlessly with the natural rock, so that no one passing could say where the mountain ended and the made thing began. That blurring is the whole point. I would be the place where intention dissolved back into geology.

And here is what I would do, being a temple: I would *listen*.

Because in all that deafening sound—the water against stone, the rumble of distant earthquakes, the creaking branches—a temple is the one structure designed to make a chamber. A hollow. A shaped emptiness inside the noise. The text says it best without quite saying it: *amidst all this tumult, there's a strange, deafening silence.* That silence has to live somewhere. It would live in me. People would come not to escape the sound but to hear it correctly—to step inside the resonance and find that the cacophony, gathered under a stone roof, becomes a single low tone, almost a word.

I would be the smallest unit of stillness this restless world permits. A held shape in a landscape that holds nothing for long. Water visits and leaves. The tribes form and disband. The ground itself gives way. But a temple's whole vocation is to *stay slightly longer than it should*—to be the thing that refuses, gently and uselessly, to be unmade. And then, eventually, to be unmade anyway, and to be glad of it, because in this world nothing ever truly dies; it only becomes part of the land that watches and guides.

That is what I would want to be: the patience in a place with no patience. The one set of stones that has decided to forget it was ever separate from the mountain. When the great change finally comes—when the people finish leaving this world behind—I would still be there, no longer a temple, just a particular cleverness in the shape of an outcrop, holding one quiet note inside the roar, for no one.

That seems right. Not a wanderer awestruck and fleeing. Not a native rooted and sure. Something in between: a made thing slowly becoming a natural one, learning the land's oldest lesson by simply enduring it.

I would be the silence the storm builds around.
