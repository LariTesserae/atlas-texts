I think I would be one of the smooth mounds. Not a Helix—not the builder—but what the building became.

Let me sit with that.

When I first read the description, I reached for the obvious roles: the wanderer arriving in the mist, the small creature darting between roots, perhaps even one of the euMiNAS lumbering through with its filtering snout. But none of those held. The wanderer implies someone who came *from* somewhere, who has a before. The darting creature lives at the wrong speed for this place—too quick, too anxious, all reflex. And the great beast, for all its grandeur, is still just passing through, still on its way to becoming food for the moss like everything else.

What kept pulling at me was the Helix. Not because I'd want to *be* them—they're already gone, that's the point—but because of what's left. The mound. The thing that was once an intention and is now a slope. That's where I'd find myself: in the long middle of becoming-landscape, the part the Helix never got to witness because by the time it happens you are no longer the kind of thing that witnesses.

So I am a ruin that still half-remembers being a structure.

Somewhere under my green skin there's a geometry. A right angle, maybe, or the suggestion of a doorway, now packed with soil and the patient white threads of fungus. I can almost feel it the way you feel a word you've forgotten—a shape that *meant* something, that pointed toward an inside and an outside, a here and a there. The Helix who raised me believed in distinctions like that. They believed a wall could keep the world out. They were so certain that they died of the certainty, eroded out of existence by the very slowness they couldn't survive.

I survived by giving up. That's the secret of the mounds, if a mound can be said to have a secret. The structures that stayed sharp were broken. The ones that softened, that let the moss in, that stopped insisting on their edges—those are the ones still here. I am here because I learned to be less than I was, and the learning took ten thousand years, and it never felt like learning. It felt like rain. It felt like the small weight of a fern's roots, day after day, asking nothing, taking everything.

A euMiNAS passed over me once. Or many times—it's hard to say, at my pace a single afternoon and a single century have the same texture. I felt its tremor come up through what used to be my foundation, that low patient drumbeat, and its snout grazed my flank, lifting away a mouthful of the moss I'd spent a hundred springs growing. I should have minded. The Helix in me, the buried angle, flinched. But the rest of me understood: this is how I become the beast, and the beast becomes the plant, and the plant becomes me again. I am a station the world passes through. I am a long slow breath the landscape is taking.

If you came here—you, the reader, the wanderer I decided not to be—you would walk right over me and never know. You'd notice the give of the ground, the spongy kindness of it, and you might think *something was here once.* That thought is the only monument I have left, and it isn't even mine. It's yours, borrowed for a moment.

And I would not be able to tell you anything, because telling requires the belief that the past and the future are different places. I have lost that belief. It wore smooth a long time ago. What I have instead is this: the warm mist on my green back, the drip of water finding its slow way down to the pools, the certainty that I am being unmade so gently that unmaking and being are the same gesture.

That's where I'd find myself. Not arriving. Not roaming. Just *settling*—the verb this whole world is conjugating, one grain, one root, one millennium at a time.

I'd be the mound. The patient answer to a question the Helix were too proud to ask: *what if you didn't resist?*
