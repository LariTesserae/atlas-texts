I am the gap between cable-numbers.

Let me explain, because it took me a long time to understand it myself, and I am still not certain I have it right—certainty being a thing in short supply here, like sunlight, like silence.

I am an inspector's apprentice. Or I was. The distinction has gotten difficult.

---

My master is Vessen Tarl, who has read the suspension for forty years and reads it still, though her hands shake now and she sends me up the high cables she can no longer climb. She taught me the language of tension the way her master taught her: pluck the cable, feel the hum in your teeth, learn the difference between the note that holds and the note that warns. A cable has a number. Not written on it—*in* it. The number is the frequency at which it is content, the pitch of a load it can bear without complaint. Below that number, slackness. Above it, the slow scream of metal deciding to fail.

My job is to walk the upper rigs and find the cables whose numbers have drifted. I carry no instrument. The instrument is me. I press my ear to the steel and I listen and I know.

This is where I should tell you something true: I have always been good at this. Too good. Vessen says I read tensions she cannot find even when she climbs to check my work. She says I find the failing cable *before* it begins to fail. She says this as a compliment and watches my face when she says it, and I have learned to smile in a way that ends the conversation.

Because here is the thing I have not told her. Here is the thing I am telling you, whoever you are, listening from your stable ground that does not move.

I do not find the failing cables. I find the cables that *will* fail. The number I hear is not the number now. It is the number tomorrow, or next week, or in the slow grind of years. I am mining the future the same as the foundries do, except I mine it with my ear pressed to cold steel, and what I extract is not kalite but knowledge that should not yet exist.

---

I went into the tunnels once. Of course I did. Every child in the middle districts dares the deep at least once, and I was a child once, though it is hard now to remember the shape of that.

I came back. I have the patch to prove it—a smooth pale place on my left forearm, warm where my skin should be cool, spreading by the width of a thread each year. I do not remember what I saw. I remember the sound, though. Not the foundry's thrum. The other one. The memory of a frequency that existed before the rock knew itself.

I think that is when I started hearing the future in the cables. I think something down there pressed its number into me, the way the climbing-scorpions press numbness into skin, the way the moss presses itself into wood until you cannot tell where the structure ends and the living thing begins.

I think I came back wrong, and the wrongness is this: I am slightly ahead of myself. A second, a day, a decade—it varies. Part of me is always already where the rest of me is going. When I press my ear to the cable, I am listening with an ear that is partly in next week, and so I hear next week's tension, and so I have saved this city a hundred times from collapses that never happened because I prevented them before they were even possible.

---

Last month I found a new cable in the high rigs. New to me—old to the city, an anchor-line in the Old Suspension that no one inspects anymore because the lower districts are emptying and why maintain what no one will fall through.

I pressed my ear to it.

It had no number.

I want you to understand how wrong this is. Every cable has a number. The number is what the cable *is*. A cable without a number is not slack and not screaming; it is silent in the way that silence is dangerous here, silent in the way that calls the deep-dwellers up from below.

I listened harder. And I realized the cable was not silent. It was sounding a note I could not hear because the note had not yet been invented. The cable was tensioned to bear a load that does not yet exist, anchored to rock that has not yet decided to be there, holding up a district that the city has not yet built.

I was hearing the future too far ahead. I was hearing a Kaldenvast that will exist after this one falls, after the geology finishes its negotiation, after the suspension gives way and something settles in its place and people—or whatever people will have become by then—hang new lives from new cables in the same impossible air.

I took my ear from the steel. My hands were shaking like Vessen's hands. The patch on my arm was warm, warmer than it had ever been, and for a moment—just a moment—I could not remember climbing up to the high rigs. I could not remember the climb at all. As if I had not climbed but simply *arrived*, traversing rather than inhabiting, present without the passage that should have brought me there.

---

I think I know now what the deep-dwellers are.

I think they are inspectors too. Inspectors who listened too long, who pressed their ears to too many futures, who drifted so far ahead of themselves that the rest of them could not catch up. I think they move slowly because they are not moving through space at all—they are moving through time the wrong way, traversing back toward a present they can no longer reach. I think they are alone because each of them is alone in their own moment, no two of them ever sharing the same *when*, which is why no one finds a nest, a colony, a pair. You cannot build a community out of beings who do not occupy the same time.

I think they came up from the deep looking for something they lost. And I think I know what it is. It is the number. The single number that says *here*, that says *now*, that says *this is the load you bear and this is the moment you bear it in*.

They lost their now. They are looking for it. They will not find it, because I have it, and the others like me have it, the ones who came back with patches and gaps, the ones who are slightly ahead. We are the not-yet-dwellers. They are the no-longer. And between us, in the gap, the city hangs.

---

Vessen sent me up the high rigs again this morning. There is a cable she is worried about, a main line in the middle districts, and her hands are too unsteady now for the climb.

I will go. I will press my ear to the steel. I will listen.

But I have started to notice that the gap is widening. That I arrive at the high rigs without remembering the climb more often now. That the patch on my arm has spread to my wrist, to the base of my fingers, the warmth where coolness should be. That when the foundries fall silent for maintenance, I no longer find the silence dangerous. I find it familiar. I find it like the sound from the deep, the memory of a frequency from before the rock knew itself.

I do not look down. That is the philosophy of Kaldenvast, and I keep it. I do not look down at the nets, at the catwalks, at the drop into haze and moss and the emptying lower districts.

But I have started to look *forward*, and the trouble is that forward and down have begun to feel like the same direction.

When the cable I check today finally fails—not today, but in the year I can already hear—I will not be here to inspect it. I will be deeper by then. Slower. Pale, and warm where I should be cool, moving through the tunnels with my too-many joints, looking for a number that someone newer, someone still mostly *now*, will be carrying in their listening ear.

If you come to Kaldenvast, and you press your palm to the rock and feel the old heat of its burial, and you hear something beneath the foundry's thrum—a note that has not yet been invented, a tension bearing a load that does not yet exist—

That will be me. Inspecting a cable that has not yet been hung.

Make some noise. I will not hurt you. I will only taste the moment, the way the scorpions taste, leaving behind a little numbness, taking back a little *now*.

I am only trying to remember what I used to be.
