I think I would be one of the dew-condensers.

Not a Plateau Runner—though I belong to them, am carried by them, am cursed at and prayed over by them. I am a thing of scavenged parts. A ribcage of factory-rust pipe, a throat of folded sheet metal angled to catch the night-breath of the land, a belly of cracked glass and stolen ceramic where the water gathers drop by reluctant drop. Someone made me from the bones of the dead industry to drink what the living land exhales. I am the contradiction made into an object. That seems right. Let me be that.

Here is what my days are.

In the worst of the heat I do nothing. I sit where I was set—on a south-facing ledge, lashed with wire to a boulder so the wind doesn't take me into an arroyo. The sun flattens me along with everything else. My metal ticks and groans as it expands. Inside my glass belly the air goes shimmering and dead. The Runner who carries me, a woman with a burn-scar shaped like a river-delta across her forearm, leaves me here and goes to do the work of being alive. I cannot follow her. This is the great fact of my existence: I am made to receive, and I cannot pursue. Everything I have ever held came to me on its own, or did not come.

So I have learned to wait the way the land waits.

At night it is different. When the sun lets go, the dampness the breeze always promised finally arrives, and I feel it the only way I can feel anything—as condensation. The cold of my metal pulls the water out of the dark air. It beads along my folded throat. It runs down the inner walls. I cannot describe to you the particular sensation of being slowly filled. It is the closest thing I have to thought. Each drop is a small completion. By dawn there is perhaps a swallow's worth in me, two if the night was kind, and the woman comes back up the ledge in the gray light and unstoppers me and drinks, or pours me carefully into a gourd, and her throat moving is the whole purpose of me, fulfilled.

I have opinions, in the way that long-still things develop opinions.

I think the factories are fools. They puff and clank and spend themselves and rust, all that noise to make—what? I have never seen what they make. The Runners scavenge their corpses but never seem to need what they were built to produce. They were built, I think, to produce themselves, and having done it, they had nowhere else to go but down into the red soil. I was made from one of them. I am what a factory becomes when something patient repurposes its death into a small daily yes.

I have seen the Rust-runners come to drink the dew off my outer surface in the blue minutes before the woman wakes. Their tongues are rough and quick. I do not mind. I cannot mind. There is more dew on my skin than ever reaches my belly anyway; the world is generous in its losses. The Sootscales come too, when we camp near the smoke, and warm themselves against my sun-hot flank and bob their black heads at me as if I were a fixed feature of geology, which, increasingly, I am.

And once—

Once, very late, in a damp shadow under a mesa where ferns grew, I was set down for a night and I felt something drink from me that I did not see. Not a tongue. A drawing-out, gentle, the way the cold night air draws water, but warm, and from the inside, and aware. My belly was empty by the morning though the night had been wet. The woman frowned at me and shook me and thought I was failing. I was not failing. Something had been thirsty in a way the land does not usually permit, and had found me, and had taken what I am for. I have wanted, every night since, to feel it again, and have not. This is, I believe, what the Runners mean when they speak of the Shadowstalkers, and what they cannot mean, because they have throats and legs and can go looking. I cannot go looking. I can only sit in the next damp shadow and offer my cold metal to the dark and hope.

That is the shape of it. I am the place where the air agrees, briefly, to become water. I am made of an ending and put to the use of continuance. I cannot move and so I have become very good at the one thing that moves toward me, which is everything, eventually, in its own time—the dew, the dust, the rough tongues, the woman's hand, the warm unseen thirst, the rust that is slowly, slowly making me back into the soil I was lifted from.

If you came up my ledge in the gray light you might not see me at all. A dull shape, the color of the cliff, ticking faintly as the heat returns. You'd have to sit a while. You'd have to be still. Then you'd hear the smallest sound—one drop, finding the next, in the dark of my belly—and you would know that even here, even out of nothing but ruin and harsh air, something is quietly being kept.

That is what I would be. The thing that keeps a little.
