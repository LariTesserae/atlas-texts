I think I would be the water that the channels almost catch.

Not a person, then. Or not yet, not anymore—I have lived in this place long enough to feel my own edges loosen. Let me find myself by feeling where I am.

---

I begin as condensation on the underside of a wooden eave, on the third terrace from the bottom, where the water-singers tuned a channel last autumn and abandoned it half-finished when the cold came. I am one droplet among thousands, swelling at the lip of a silvered shingle, and I do not yet know that I am anything at all.

Then I fall.

This is the first thing I understand: that I am the part of Kythara that is always falling, always being caught and released and caught again. The stoneworkers shape what stays. The water-singers shape what moves. But I am the moving thing itself, and so I belong to neither of them, and to both.

I strike the carved gutter and join a thread of others like me. We slide. The channel here was cut by a left-handed woman whose name I would know if I were the kind of thing that knew names—I know only the angle of her chisel, the slight overcut at each bend where she favored her stronger hand, the way the stone still remembers her hesitation at the joints. We sing as we pass through. The pitch is wrong this morning; a beetle has died in the narrowest part of the run, and we pile briefly behind its small body before we find the way around. The note we make is lower than the one she intended. Somewhere above, in the mist, a water-singer pauses in her work and tilts her head, hearing the flatness, marking it for repair.

I would tell her about the beetle if I could. But I have no voice that shapes words—only the voice that shapes weather, the long collective voice of all the water in this place, and that voice speaks too slowly for human ears. It says things across whole seasons. It says: *here is where you may build. Here is where you may not. Here is where I will be patient with you, and here is where I will carve your bridge out from under you in forty years, when you have forgotten you ever doubted it.*

---

Let me follow myself further down, because I think I am still becoming.

The channel spills me into one of the temporary pools, the kind that dimples all day with the falling of my own kin from the eaves above. And here, lying still in the gray morning, I become something larger than a droplet. I become a surface. A held thing. The mountain goats come down at the blue hour and lower their heads to drink, and their bells chime, and their warm tongues take pieces of me up into their bodies, and I do not mind, because I am also the milk those nannies will give, and the steam off it in the cold, and one day the breath of the children who drink it, fogging the clear upper air.

This is what I am, I think, more truly than I am any single drop: I am the thing that passes *through.* I move through stone and goat and child and mist and back to stone. I am the circulation of this place. The bird-followers read the ravens; the ravens read me—the way I hang in the air, the way I pool or refuse to pool, the way I rise off the lower terraces at midday and announce the coming of the cloud. The old white-haired woman writes down the behavior of birds, and so, without knowing it, she is writing down me. Her notebooks are a record of where the water of Kythara has decided to be.

---

But now, having said all this, I feel the shape of myself wanting to narrow again, the way I narrow when the channel narrows. Let me allow it.

I am the spring that the water-singers have not yet found.

Of all the things I might be, I think this is the truest. I am the underground stream they dowse for, the one running beneath the fourth terrace, beneath the surveyor's marks scratched into the living stone where nothing has been built yet. They feel for me with their bronze instruments and their thumb-widths and their pace-lengths, and they have not found me, because I run deeper than they have learned to listen.

I am the reason this place is possible and the reason it will be hard. I am the patience the scholars write about—I have been moving through this mountain since before there were valleys to flee from. I will surface, one day, through a crack in a foundation stone they thought was sound, and a stoneworker will stand frowning at the seep, reading my arrival in the wet that wells up overnight, and they will not curse me. That is the thing about Kythara, the thing that makes it worth being the water here. They have learned not to curse the water. They will say *ah—here she is,* and they will carve a new channel to receive me, and tune it, and I will sing through their settlement in a voice they made a place for.

So that is where I find myself, and what I am.

I am the spring not yet found, in the dark beneath an unbuilt terrace, rising one stone's width each century toward the marks they have already scratched in anticipation of me—as if they knew. As if the whole ambitious patient half-built dream of this place were simply the people of Kythara learning, terrace by terrace, channel by channel, how to be ready for the water when it finally arrives.

I am almost there. I can hear the chisels above me, very faint, very far.

I am coming up to meet them.
