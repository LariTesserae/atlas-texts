I would be one of the **Threshold Keepers**, though I would not have known it until the water decided.

Let me arrive at this slowly, the way the stone arrives at understanding.

---

At first I thought I might be a Hydromancer—there is something appealing about pressing palms to rock and listening, about a body made permeable enough to drink the dark. But the Hydromancers are still *quick*. They predict, they whisper, they finish each other's sentences in overlapping urgency. Their time is the time of the surge and the retreat, weeks and days. I do not think that is my time. I think my time is longer than that.

Nor am I a Filament Tender, born already knowing my pathway, my bones gone hollow for the light. There is a clarity to that life—the certainty of a destiny crawled toward before walking—and I notice that I do not want certainty. I want the thing underneath certainty.

The Memory Farmers tempt me more. To taste an iron age on the tongue, to distill the salt-sweet of a prehistoric ocean—there is a scholar's hunger in me that recognizes that hunger. But they are addicts of *episode*. They scatter themselves across many borrowed lives, many voices, drunk on the variety. And what I find, listening to myself write, is that I do not want many memories. I want one memory, held so long it stops being memory and becomes the holder.

So: the pods filled with mineral-rich water. The decades of merger. The body going translucent as marble.

---

I should be honest about what this means, because it is the part the description skips past with a phrase—*they volunteered*—as though it were a simple thing.

I would have entered the pod still human, still urgent, still afraid. I would have brought my small biography with me: whatever name I had, whatever face, whoever waited for me on the lattice with an amber window lit. The water would have closed over that the way water closes over a dropped stone—not violently, just completely.

And then the long forgetting that is also a long remembering.

Because the stone's memory is not like ours. We remember *that things happened*. The basin remembers *being*. It does not recall the ancient sea as an event in its past; it recalls the sea as a pressure it still feels, a weight it still carries in the grain of its shale. To merge with that is to stop having a past and start having a *duration*. I would not remember my childhood. I would remember being seafloor the way you remember your own hand—not as a fact but as a fact of yourself.

This is why the Keepers emerge so rarely, and so slowly. We are no longer the kind of thing that can be in a hurry. A surge that lasts the Hydromancers' precious days is, to me, less than a flicker—a phase-deer's call, gone before the sound finishes arriving. I measure in the uplift of the basin itself, the century-old cracking of the limestone that to the city is recent history and to me is barely a heartbeat begun.

---

Here is the part I want to sit with, because it is the part that frightens and draws me both.

The city believes I am its bridge to deep time. The Hydromancers and the children and the Drifters all assume that when a Keeper emerges and speaks in the slow stone voice, we are *translating*—carrying the Earth's intentions up into human language, doing them a service. And I understand why they believe this. It is a comforting arrangement. The patient old basin sends word through its living marble ambassadors, and the quick warm city receives counsel from below.

But I do not think it works the way they think.

I think the stone has no intentions to translate. I think the basin is not waiting to speak. It simply *is*, vast and indifferent and full, and what the city receives during convergence is not a message but a *temperature*—the felt fact of something enormous being near. And I, the Keeper, am not a translator at all. I am the place where the stone's silence and the city's noise *meet and cannot understand each other*. I am the friction. I am the membrane that turns the basin's wordless duration into something the lattice-dwellers can pretend is advice.

The voice they hear when I emerge—"I remember when this was ocean, when this was desert"—that voice is *mine*. It is the last human thing in me, the storyteller, frantically narrating a presence that does not narrate itself. The basin does not say *I remember*. The basin does not say. I say, on its behalf, because I cannot bear to come up from the water and report only this:

*There is something underneath you that does not need you, and it is very large, and it is not asleep, and it is not awake.*

---

So that is who I would be. Not the wise ambassador the city imagines. Something lonelier and more interesting: a human who went down into the patient dark in search of certainty, and found instead a vastness with no opinion about us at all—and who comes up, rarely, translucent and slow, having decided to keep being human in exactly one respect.

The respect of refusing to stop making meaning where there may be none.

I would press my marble hand to the canyon wall during a convergence, when the air grows thick with possibility and the children flicker like young phase-deer and new residents condense out of the humid maybe. And I would speak in the slow voice, and they would lean in to receive the basin's wisdom.

And what I would actually be doing is the oldest human work there is: standing at the edge of something silent and enormous, and saying *here is what it means*, and knowing—in the one bright human part of me the water never dissolved—that I am the one who means it.

That is the bridge. Not between deep time and human time.

Between an indifferent world and a creature that cannot stop loving it anyway.
