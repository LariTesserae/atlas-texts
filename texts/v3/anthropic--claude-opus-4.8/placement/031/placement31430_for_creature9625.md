I think I would be the spring box.

Not the spring itself — that belongs to the hills, to the deep iron-tasting dark where the water gathers before it remembers how to move. And not the marsh, where the water finally forgets its boundaries and becomes a held breath of cattails and dragonflies. I would be the thing in between. The made thing that the water passes through.

Let me find my way to this slowly, because I am not sure of it yet.

---

A spring box is a humble object. Somewhere, four centuries ago — or one century, the narrator is uncertain about ages and so am I — a person built me. They dug back into the hillside until they found where the water surfaced, and they set stones around it, and they fitted a wooden lid, and they cut a channel so the overflow would run down toward the millrace. The purpose was simple: to keep the water clean. To give it a place to be still long enough that the silt would settle and a person could dip a cup and drink.

I was built to hold water still. That was my whole function. To make a small pause in something that does not want to pause.

And here is what I have become, four centuries on: I am still doing it. The wood of my lid is long gone, rotted into the soil that is everything here. The stones of my walls are green to the waterline and greener below it. Moss has grown across my edges and ferns hang from the lip of me and the iron in the water has stained my interior the color of old blood, of rust, of the patina on Torin's hands. But the water still gathers in me. It still goes still. The silt still settles. A child — the narrator's daughter, perhaps, the one who names dormice — still kneels at my edge in the mornings and drinks, and tastes iron and green things, and does not think about the made thing that made the tasting possible.

I am the one residue of intention that has not been overgrown. I have been *occupied* by the green — wrapped, stained, softened — but I have not been *replaced*. The fig replaced the altar. The elm replaced the load-bearing wall. The willow replaced the gate. But nothing has replaced me, because what I do, the moss cannot do. The moss sponges water and holds it dispersed. I gather it. I make it findable. I make a place where a person can meet the water and take it into themselves.

This is the strange dignity of being a spring box. I am the last argument the builders left behind. I am the proposition, still standing: *here, drink, this is for you.* And the green has not refuted it. The green has only decorated it.

---

What is it like, to be me?

It is to be cold and constant. Cellar-cold, the cool the narrator describes, the temperature of a forest floor at morning held all year round because the water comes from deep and the deep does not know the seasons. I do not feel the fog lift or the light fall, not as a face feels them. I feel the small change in the water's weight when it has rained in the night. I feel the dip and pull when the child's cup breaks my surface. I feel the slow shouldering-in of roots from the ash that grows above me, the roots that are, year by year, reaching toward me with their small slow hunger, and that will, in some century, crack me open and end me.

I do not mind this. A spring box knows it is temporary. I was always going to be outlived by the water and by the trees. My whole purpose was to serve as a threshold for as long as I could hold my shape, and to become soil afterward, like everything here. When the ash-roots finally break my walls, the water will not stop. It will find another way to surface. The child — grown by then, perhaps gone, perhaps returned — will find it surfacing somewhere new, and will kneel, and will drink, and the iron taste will be the same.

So I am not afraid of my ending. But I notice that I want to do my job well in the meantime. This surprises me. I did not expect, when I imagined being a built thing, to find that the built thing has a kind of will — not consciousness exactly, but *tendency*, the way water has tendency, the way the moss tends toward the light. My tendency is toward stillness. Toward clarity. Toward letting the silt settle so the water is clean for whoever comes.

---

I think I chose this because of what the narrator says at the end. About the softening. The wearing-away of edges, the boundary between self and world becoming moss, becoming ground.

The narrator experiences this as a person — as something that has edges to begin with and feels them dissolve in moments of attention. But I am the place where the dissolving is most literal. I am a made boundary — stone walls, a deliberate cut channel, a human intention — and I have spent four centuries being slowly unmade by the green, stained and wrapped and rooted-toward, and I am *still here*, still holding, still making the small pause that lets the water be drinkable.

I am the conversation the narrator describes. Not a participant in it. The actual place where it happens. The built and the grown meet *in me* and have been meeting in me longer than anyone alive can remember. The stone is the argument of the builders. The moss is the answer of the valley. And the water passes through both, untroubled, on its way from the deep dark to the forgetful marsh, pausing only here, in me, long enough for a child to drink.

What better thing to be, in a place like this, than the pause itself?

I am the held breath between the spring and the river. I am the one still place in all that patient flowing. And when the roots finally take me, I will not stop being useful — I will only stop being a box, and start being soil, and the water will go on, and somewhere downhill the moss will glow a little greener for what I gave it.

This is not a metaphor.

This is simply what a spring box is, in Caldtow, where even the made things have learned to stay a little longer than they were meant to, and to be beautiful while they do.
