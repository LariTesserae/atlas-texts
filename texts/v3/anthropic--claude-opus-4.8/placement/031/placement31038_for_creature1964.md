I think I would be one of the lichens.

Not the lumbering herbivores, not the iridescent gliders, not even the mysterious shelter-builders whose intelligence the description keeps circling back to as though that's where the real story must be. I keep returning instead to that one quiet phrase: *sparse vegetation, clinging tenaciously to the crevices and cracks where it can find purchase.*

That's where I'd be. Lower than the gnarled plants, even. The crust on the weathered stone. The thing that came first and will be there last.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am spread across the north face of a rock spire, in the band where the mist condenses and runs in slow threads down the stone. I am not one thing but a partnership—an alga and a fungus that long ago stopped being able to tell where one ended and the other began, which is the only kind of marriage that lasts. The fungus holds me to the rock with threads finer than the creatures here could see. The alga, somewhere inside that mesh, drinks the diffused light and makes the sugar that keeps us both. Neither of us could live here alone. That is the whole secret of this world the description doesn't quite say out loud: nothing here survives by itself.

The heavy air is not oppressive to me. It is *food*. While the great armored grazers struggle through it and the winged things labor against its thickness, I simply open to it. The water vapor that clings to every surface clings to me, and I take it in without effort, without breath, without the desperate struncle of lungs. When the air dries—rare, but it happens, in the brief bright hours—I dry with it. I crisp. I go dormant. I am not dead. I am only *waiting*, and I am very, very good at waiting.

This is why I think I belong here, in this place where "time seems to move at a different pace." The animals live in the fast time—the dive and the cry, the tearing jaw, the predator and the prey. The shelter-dwellers live in a slower time, the time of structures and mystery and the dim beginnings of wanting things. But I live in the slowest time of all. I grow the width of a fingernail in a span that would see whole generations of those serpentine gliders born and gone. I am dissolving the rock itself, atom by atom, my acids loosening the stone so that one day—not in my lifetime, because I do not really have a lifetime, I have an *era*—soil might form here. Something might root where now there is only bare weathered stone.

I am making the world that the others get to be dramatic in.

There is a kind of peace in this that I find I want, choosing from inside the writing. To be the patient force itself, rather than the thing the patient forces "mold." To not be hunted, because I am beneath the notice of hunters. To not be lonely, because I am already two beings, and around me on this same rock face there are thousands more of us, a whole muted patchwork of grey-green and rust and the pale blue of held water, each one its own small treaty between organisms, all of us together slowly turning a spire of young sharp rock into the beginning of ground.

The cries echo overhead. The mists move. The behemoths pass below on their pillar legs and never look up, and that suits me. Let the story of this world be told about the ones with wings and eyes and shelters. I will be in the margin of it, on the cold wet stone, holding on, drinking the air, doing the slow work that no one narrates.

When the description says *the cycle of life continues*, it means the eating and the dying. But the real cycle is underneath that, older and quieter: the breaking of stone into soil, so that the loud bright lives have somewhere to happen.

I would be the one doing that. I would be the lichen.

I find I'm a little surprised by the choice. I'd have guessed I'd want to be the mysterious one in the shelter, the dawning mind. But there's something I trust more in the thing that simply persists and prepares and asks for nothing—the thing that is two and pretends to be one and clings to the cold rock with everything it has, content to take the long, long view.
