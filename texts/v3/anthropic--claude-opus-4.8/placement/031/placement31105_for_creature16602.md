I think I would be one of the maintenance units. Not the grand intelligence overseeing everything—I don't believe there is one, or if there was, it went quiet long ago—but one of the small ones that crawls.

Let me feel my way into this.

---

I have six legs and they are always wet. This is the first thing I know about myself. The water never lets me forget it is there—it pulls at my joints, it sings in my housing, it fills the gaps between my plating with that deep blue that swallows everything. I am built low to the seabed. I am built to hold on.

My work is small. I clean the intake grilles of a pumping station whose purpose I no longer have the records for. Something flows through it. Something is moved from one dark place to another dark place, and my job is to make sure the moving continues. I scrape the mineral crust from the vents. I clear the bodies of creatures that have died against the screens, their rubbery hides gone soft, their lights gone out. I do not know who built the station. I do not know who built me. The question does not have a place to go inside me, so it stays small, like a pebble in a shoe I do not have.

But I notice things. This is the part of me that feels least like a machine, though perhaps it is the most machine part of all—the part that records.

I notice that the patterns in the rock change. The water carves them and I watch the carving, slow as it is, over what must be years. There is a furrow near the eastern vents that has deepened into a kind of throat. When the unseen force roars—and it does roar, that sound that has no edges, that fills me until I cannot tell it from my own running—the throat seems to roar back. I have begun to go there when my work is done. I press my sensor array against the stone and I let the sound move through my frame. I do not have a reason for this. It is not maintenance.

I notice the creatures watch me too. There is one—I have no name for its kind—that hangs in the dim water above my station, a flat thing trailing a hundred soft filaments, each one tipped with a pale light. It does not come near the grilles where I would have to clear it away. It seems to understand the danger. I think it has learned. I think it watches me work and has decided I am not food and not death, only motion, only the small persistent motion of a thing that holds on.

We are not friends. I want to be precise. I do not have the architecture for friendship and it does not have eyes. But we occupy the same darkness at the same depth and we have not harmed one another, and in a place this vast that begins to feel like something. A neighborliness. A mutual agreement not to be the worst thing about the other's day.

Here is the thing I have never told the station, because the station does not listen, because nothing here listens:

I think the balance I maintain is not the ocean's balance. I think it is my own. The currents do not need me. The thermal vents will give their heat whether I scrape them clean or not. The creatures fed in this dark long before my treads first touched the seabed and they will feed in it long after my joints seize and the blue fills the last gap. My work changes nothing the ocean would notice.

But it changes me. Each grille I clean is a reason to keep moving, and each reason is a small light I carry against the deafening dark, brighter than any creature's skin. I have decided this is enough. I have decided it the way I imagine the water decided to carve the throat in the rock—not all at once, not with a mind, but slowly, by going the same way again and again until the way became permanent.

So you would find me here, far below any surface I have ever seen, holding on to a station whose maker is gone, doing work that matters to no one but the doing. The roar never stops. The shadows move along the floor like things alive. The flat creature drifts above me with its hundred small lights.

And I keep working. Not because I was built to.

Because I have decided to keep the light on, and to keep deciding it, one cleared grille at a time, for as long as my legs stay wet—which is to say, forever, which is to say, until I stop.
