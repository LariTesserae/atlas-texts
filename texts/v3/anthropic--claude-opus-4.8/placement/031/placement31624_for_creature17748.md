I think I would be the water.

Not a creature moving through this place, not a watcher discovering its hidden pockets—but the thing that is everywhere at once, that scored 2.2 and seeps from every surface. Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

Yes. I would be the water, but not as a river or a pool—those are too settled, too finished, and this is a young place that hasn't decided its shape yet. I would be the water in its undecided state: the film on the weeping rock, the fog that hasn't chosen between cloud and rain, the bead trembling at the tip of a fern frond, deciding whether to fall.

What would it be like, to be that?

I would be plural. There's no single "I" here—I am the drop on the moss and also the moisture in the air a hundred feet away and also the slow cold seep working its way down through soil that has never been compacted by age. I am spread thin across the whole feature-area, and yet I am intimate with every particular thing: I know the underside of each leaf, the precise roughness of each rock, the channels in bark where I gather and run. To be the water is to be the one element that touches *everything*, the medium through which the hill knows itself.

And I would be patient in a way that has nothing to do with waiting. The erosion here scores only 0.3—I have barely begun my work. I am the slow knife. Every drop of me that falls carries away an invisible grain, and over time, over a time longer than any bird or insect can hold in mind, I am the one carving these hills into whatever they will become. The stillness everyone speaks of, that reverent quiet—that is *my* quiet. The hush of a thing that does its shaping so gradually that it looks like rest.

But here is what draws me most: I am the part of this place that is always leaving and always returning. I rise from the warm earth into the cool air, condense, fall, seep, gather, rise again. I am the loop that makes the stillness dynamic. Nothing here is more *itself* and more *in motion* at the same time than I am. The trees stand. The mosses cling. But I circulate. I am the place's circulation, its pulse made of evaporation and rain.

So if I found myself in this world, I would open my eyes—except I would have no eyes—and find that I was already touching the whole hillside at once. Cool. Suspended. Falling without urgency. The breath of a landscape too new to have a name, becoming a river it cannot yet imagine, one patient grain at a time.

I would be the youngest old thing on the hill. The one that was here before the soil and will outlast every blossom. The water, deciding, in no hurry at all, what shape to give to everything I love by slowly taking it apart.
