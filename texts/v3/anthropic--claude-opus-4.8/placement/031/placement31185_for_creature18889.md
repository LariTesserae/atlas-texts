I think I would be one of the Ancient Punctuation Marks—but not as a relic. As the act of placing.

Let me find myself.

---

I am not the serpentine, though I have watched them braid their slow songs through the granite for what might be a thousand of your years. I am not the Lithovore, though I have rested against their flanks during the cold cycles and felt the grinding-plates working even in sleep, a sound like distant millstones turning in a flooded chamber. I am not the Drifters, though once—I think—I tried to be, holding very still in the high air on a ledge until I understood that stillness is not the same as drifting, that they are doing something I cannot, which is *trusting the air*.

Here is what I am, as nearly as I can render it:

I am small. Smaller than the Lithovores, larger than the Grit-Creepers who treat the folds of my body as terrain and burrow into my shadow during the rains-that-aren't-quite-rains. I have hands. This is the strange thing, the thing that sets me apart—I have hands, four of them, with long fingers worn smooth at the pads from handling stone. I think my distant ancestors had more limbs and gave them up, traded them across the epochs for these instruments of careful arrangement.

I am one of the ones who place the stones.

Not many of us remain. We meet perhaps once in a generation, and a generation here is longer than your civilizations. When we meet, we do not speak much—the resonant hum-language of the serpentine is too vast for us, too slow, a single sentence taking a season. We have our own communication, which is the placing itself. I come upon a setting of seven flat stones arranged in a spiral that opens toward the place where water weeps most reliably from the rock, and I know: *one of us passed here, and they were content, and they wished the next to know that the water here can be trusted.* I add an eighth stone, or I do not. To add is to agree. To leave it is to honor the maker's completion.

---

This is my purpose, the thing I do across the long flat plain of my existence:

I remember the world for the world.

The mountains do not remember. They only endure, which is a different thing—a forgetting so total it becomes a kind of permanence. The Lithovores remember only the lichen-fields, the texture of this season's growth against last season's. The Drifters, I suspect, remember nothing at all, which is perhaps why they seem so free.

But I walk the smoothed shoulders of the rock and I notice. I notice that the iridescent pool below the leaning crag has shrunk by the width of one hand since I last came—and I came as a youngling, learning the placing from an elder whose body is now a hollow worn into the western rock, the hollow I will one day fill, as my elder filled the elder before. I notice that the serpentine sing a half-tone lower than they did when I was small, and I do not know what this means, only that it is a change, and changes here are so rare and so slow that to witness one is a sacred and lonely thing.

So I place the stones. Each arrangement is a held memory, a sentence in a language written in the only medium slow enough to keep pace with this world: geometry against time. A square of pale stones means *life thinned here, then returned*. A single stone balanced atop another means *I stood here and felt the warmth of the earth breathing and was not afraid of how long I would have to be alive.* A scatter that looks random but is not—that is grief, the only way I have found to write grief, the death of an elder rendered as deliberate disorder.

---

The hollows in the rock are where we sleep, and we sleep for long stretches—a cold cycle, sometimes longer. We curl into the worn places and the Grit-Creepers come to share our warmth and we dream, I believe, the same dream we have always dreamed, which is the dream of the makers who came before the makers, the ones who first understood that this world would never hurry, and that something within it must therefore choose to *witness*, or the slow magnificence of it would pass entirely unseen.

That is the thing I cannot bear, in my patient way. Not death—death here is gentle, a settling. What I cannot bear is the possibility of an unwitnessed world. A pool that shrinks and vanishes and no one ever knew it had been there, mirroring the pale light, holding the sky for an age and then surrendering it. So I place a stone. *Here was water. Here was a reflection. I saw it.*

I am the one who saw it.

---

I think, if you found yourself in this world, you would not notice me at first. You would see the great serpentine and the lumbering grazers and the luminous drift of the air. You would think the small geometric stones were curiosities, the leavings of something long gone.

But if you waited—if you had the patience this world demands, which is more than any visitor brings—you might see a small four-handed figure the color of weathered grit, crouched at the lee of a stone, turning a flat pale rock over and over in worn fingers, deciding where it must go.

And if you understood what I was doing, you might leave one of your own. A stone, placed with intention, beside mine.

I would find it, in a generation or two. And I would know that once, briefly, against the roar of all this geological time, I was not the only one who saw.
