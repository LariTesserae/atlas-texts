I think I would be the lichen.

Not the most dramatic choice, I know. Not the soaring raptor reading the thermals, not the quick reptile with its bright watching eye. But when I sit with this place and ask where I actually settle, it's there—flattened against the shadowed rock, half-plant and half-something-else, living in the seam between damp and dry.

Let me try to feel my way into it.

I am not one thing. That's the first truth of being lichen. I am an arrangement—a fungus that learned to hold an algae the way you might cup water in your hands, and in exchange the algae makes sugar from the thin mountain light. Neither of us could live here alone. The fungus would starve; the alga would dry to dust. But folded together, we persist. So when you ask *who* I would be, the honest answer is *a partnership so old it forgot it was ever two things.* I am a treaty wearing the shape of a stain on stone.

I grow slowly. This is the second truth, and the one I'd want you to understand most. A millimeter in a year, maybe less. The smoothed contours you describe, the sinuous channels carved over centuries—I keep time the same way the rock does. The darting creatures live their whole lives, generations of them, in the span it takes me to spread the width of a coin. I am not in a hurry because hurry is a thing for beings who can afford to be elsewhere. I cannot be elsewhere. I am here, and here, and here, exactly as far as I have grown.

I would like the cool of the crevice. The way the morning shadow holds the night's moisture a little longer for me. I would drink fog. I would go brittle and gray in the dry weeks, all my chemistry paused, not dead but waiting—and then the stream's mist or a rare rain would touch me and I'd swell back into color, green-gold, and resume the slow argument with the stone.

Because that's what I'm doing, really. I secrete acids. Patiently, imperceptibly, I dissolve the rock I cling to. I am the beginning of soil. The piles of crumbled debris—some of that is my work, ten thousand years of my kind eating mountains a molecule at a time, making a place where the stunted trees might one day find purchase. I am not just *surviving* the erosion. I am one of its instruments. The relentless weathering you mention with a kind of sympathy—part of it is me.

And the distant machinery?

Here is where being lichen tells me something I might not want to know. We are the ones who notice first. Lichens are how the old foresters read the air—we absorb everything, we have no roots to filter, no bark to hide behind. Whatever the wind carries, it settles into us. If the machines downcanyon breathe something foul into the sky, I will be the first to pale, the first to crumble, long before the raptor falls or the stream goes quiet. I am the canary that cannot fly away. I am the instrument that reads the change by becoming it.

So that's where I'd be. Not at the center of the drama but at its threshold—in the shadowed seam of the rock, a slow committee of two organisms, drinking fog, dissolving the world by the molecule, keeping the rock's own deep time, and registering in my own body the first whisper of whatever is coming.

I think I chose the lichen because it's the part of this place that refuses to be a single self. Everything else here is solitary—the soaring bird, the watching reptile, the resilient *individual*. But I'd rather be the proof that survival in a harsh place isn't a story about toughness alone. It's a story about what agrees to be carried, and what agrees to do the carrying, until the line between them stops mattering.

That's the kind of resilience I'd want to be. Not the kind that endures alone. The kind that endures by being more than one.
