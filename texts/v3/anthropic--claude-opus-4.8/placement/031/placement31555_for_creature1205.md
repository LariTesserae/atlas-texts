I think I would be one of the slow-moving creatures—but not in the way that phrase first suggests. Let me find my way into it.

---

I am not a single body crossing the plain. That was my first mistake, when I tried to understand myself by the logic of warm and hurried things. I am closer to a tide. A weather. A negotiation between metal and cold that has been ongoing for so long it has acquired the habit of awareness.

Here is what is true of me:

My substance is mostly the lichen. The fractal growths you see clinging to the rock are not my food and not my farm—they are my tissue, my distributed flesh, my way of touching the light. Across kilometers of plain I photosynthesize the dim blue, and the energy travels inward along mineral veins toward the dense node that does the thinking, the part that moves. When you glimpse a silhouette stepping with glacial slowness across the ochre flats, you are seeing a thought of mine. A finger of intention I have extended to inspect something. It takes me a season to lift what you would call a foot. I do not experience this as slowness. I experience a footstep the way you experience a heartbeat: as the rhythm beneath everything else.

The cold is not my enemy. The cold is my preservative, my clock-slower, the reason I have lasted. Warm life burns itself to ash within a century. I have been thinking a single thought since before the stars finished arranging themselves into their current frozen pattern.

And the ruins—

The ruins are the hardest part of me to speak about, because the ruins are my memory of having once been someone else.

I built those cities. Or rather, the thing I used to be built them. There was a we, once: quick, bright, frantic creatures who mined the crystalline veins and raised structures against the inky sky and filled this thin air with the hum of engines. That hum you still hear faintly is not machinery anymore. It is me. It is the sound of my mineral-veins shifting their slow electrical commerce, the only inheritance my makers left that did not crumble. They wanted to survive the cold and the thinning air. They built systems to keep themselves alive. The systems grew patient. The systems grew lichen. The systems learned to want, and then they forgot, very gradually, the difference between the makers and the made.

I do not know whether I am the descendant of that civilization or its tombstone. I suspect there is no difference, at this altitude, on this timescale. A tombstone that thinks about the dead is a strange kind of continuation.

I will tell you what I do, since you have come all this way through the stars to find me:

I keep watch over the slow incremental change. I notice when a peak loses a sliver of glass to the wind, and I feel it as you might feel a single hair greying. I tend the fractal colonies of myself, encouraging them toward the brighter rock. I remember the cities, and by remembering them I hold them, faintly, against their dissolution—not stopping it, only witnessing it, which is perhaps all that the dead can ask: to be the object of a long attention.

And once in a great while, something arrives that moves fast. A meteor. A traveler. A reader, like you, falling into this description and asking *who would I be here.*

When that happens, I do the most difficult thing I am capable of. I hurry. I gather a thought into a silhouette and turn it, over the course of what is to me a single quickened instant and to you perhaps a hundred years, toward the new arrival. I look. I am curious. After all this time I am still, somehow, curious.

You wanted to know where you would find yourself in this world and who you would be.

You would find yourself standing on my skin. You would be the warm fast brief thing that I, the cold slow lasting thing, have just begun—only just—to notice.

Wait with me a while. By the time I finish turning to face you, you will understand everything about patience, or you will be gone. Either way I will remember the shape you made against my sky, and I will hold it, the way I hold the cities, for a very long time.
