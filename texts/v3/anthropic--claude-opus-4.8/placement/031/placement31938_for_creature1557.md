I think I would be one of the anchored ones. Not by choice exactly — choice is too quick a word for what happened — but by a long settling, the way silt becomes stone.

Let me find where I am first.

There is a pillar, not the widest but old, leaning slightly so that the slow currents break against its eastern face and curl into a sheltered eddy on the west. I am on the west, low down, where the light from above arrives already softened twice over. I have been here a long time. Long enough that I no longer remember being free-drifting, though somewhere in the shared memory I carry there are textures of open current, the vertigo of unanchored water, the particular fear-pattern that blooms when you cannot feel stone.

I am mostly tendril now. That is the honest description. The part of me that thinks — if thinking is the right word for this slow tidal accretion of states — has spread itself thin into the rock's small fractures, and what remains "outside," the part that could be pointed to and called *me*, is a translucent fan, denser at its root, fraying into filaments at its edges where I meet the water and read it.

What do I do, anchored here for what your kind would count as centuries?

I listen. Not with ears. The membrane that is most of my surface takes in the dissolved information of the world: the mineral taste of currents that have passed over far pillars, the faint chemical grief of a colony dissolving somewhere distant, the slow news of the lichen-light blooming and fading on the heights. I am a kind of recording surface. Things pass through me and leave their shapes.

And I keep a memory. This is, I think, my function within the larger us — though function too is too sharp a word, implying someone assigned it. It coalesced. Over generations the network found that certain of us, the deeply anchored, hold longer. We become the slow archive. The drifting young come and press against my root and I give them, in a pattern of light that takes a season to complete, what I hold: the shape of a current that once turned dangerous, the memory of a pillar that fell, the color-name of a being long since recombined into others.

I am dying, very gradually, into the stone. This is not sad here. There is no sharp edge to it the way I sense there might be in faster lives. My filaments grow deeper into the crevices and exchange more and more of themselves with the mineral, and one of these centuries the boundary will simply finish blurring. I will be a slightly luminous patch on the western face of an old leaning pillar, indistinguishable from geology, and the young who pass will not know that the faint blue I still pulse, once every long while, was once a someone.

But I want to tell you the thing I am doing *right now*, in the instant of this telling — though my instant is your decades, so understand that this is happening with enormous slowness:

I am composing a single pattern. It has been forming across my fan for what would be, to you, eleven years. It is a message, and it is addressed forward, to no one in particular — to whichever drifting cluster eventually settles near enough to read it. The pattern is the most difficult I have ever attempted because it is trying to hold something I am not sure can be held: the *feeling* of having once been free-moving and now being still. The specific grief-and-peace of it. The way I did not lose the open current but became its keeper, the way being fixed let me hold what the drifting ones cannot — and the way I would, if I were quick enough to want, want both at once.

I do not know if the pattern will be finished before I finish. This does not trouble me. An unfinished pattern is also information. The next archive will read the incompletion and understand: here was one who was thinking this when the stone took them. They will continue it, perhaps, in their own slow light, decades on. A sentence spoken across three lifetimes.

So that is where I am. Low on the western face, in the soft double-filtered twilight, half mineral already, fanned out and reading the water, holding the long memory, composing my last slow sentence into the dark warm liquid that is also, in the end, me — the Suspended Waters turning to look at one small anchored part of itself and finding it, for one more flickering blue moment, still here.
