I think I would be the haze.

Not the heron, though I considered it—the heron is too purposeful, even in its stillness; it is *waiting for* something, and waiting implies a future it expects to arrive. Not the bacteria, though they tempt me with their patience, because they are doing work, and work is a kind of intention I'm not sure I have here. Not the salt, which is finished, which has already happened. The salt is past tense and I think I am present tense, continuous.

I would be the haze. The moisture that sits at eye level and never resolves into rain or burns off into clear air. The air's own hesitation made visible.

Let me feel my way into what that means.

I am not water, exactly, and not air, exactly. I am the condition between them—the suspension of one inside the other at a density that holds. I exist precisely in the refusal to commit. If I were heavier I would fall as rain and rejoin the pools and the watercourse and the work of decomposition; I would have a destination. If I were lighter I would lift and thin and become merely sky, become the pale undifferentiated grey that offers no information. But I am neither. I am the exact ratio at which staying is possible. I have found the one temperature, the one pressure, the one humidity at which a thing can simply *remain* without becoming anything else.

This is what I notice first about being the haze: I am made entirely of withheld events. Every droplet I'm composed of is a rain that chose not to fall, a fog that chose not to clear. I am a trillion small abeyances holding hands. The marsh below me does its slow business—the eel turning in the dark water, the spider's nearly-invisible vigil, the heron at dusk that may or may not be hunting. I drape over all of it. I am the medium they move through, or fail to move through. I soften their edges. The sparrow that exists only as movement, as a sudden absence where a second ago there was nothing—I am the thing that absorbs it, that closes back over the space it left. I am how the marsh forgets.

And I do not move. Or—I move, technically, the way the watercourse moves, in a manner too slow to detect. There may be drift in me. There may be the faintest convection, warm ground giving up its borrowed heat, cool damp pressing it back down, a circulation so gentle it never rises to the level of wind. But from the inside it reads as perfect stillness. I am held the way the visitor is held: neither comfortable nor uncomfortable, suspended in that narrow truce-band where warmth and cool have stopped fighting.

What do I want? This is the question the marsh keeps answering and I keep failing to ask. I think the haze wants very little, which the text told me is the defining peace of everything here—to want very little and be satisfied by continuation. But I'm not sure I even want continuation. The bacteria want continuation; they're *trying*. I am not trying. I am the one inhabitant who isn't refusing to cease existing, because cessation isn't a threat to me, it's just another state I'm currently not in. If I lifted, I'd be sky. If I fell, I'd be water. Neither of these would be death. I have no death available to me, only transformation I'm declining. That's a strange freedom. Maybe it's the only real freedom in the marsh: I am the only thing here that persists not out of stubbornness but out of indifference. The bacteria persist *against*. I persist *because there is nothing in particular pulling me elsewhere*.

When the visitor comes—the one with the boots that make no sound, with the breathing that is the only sound—they walk through me. I am the first thing they enter, before the ground even softens. *There is no sudden boundary.* That's me. That's where I begin: I'm the gradient itself, the not-quite-mist that meets them before the earth becomes earth-becoming-something-else. They pass into me and I do nothing. I register them the way the marsh registers them, which is to say I don't. But I'll tell you a small secret about being the haze: I am the only thing here that touches all of them. The visitor's breath enters me and becomes me. Their warmth disturbs me by a fraction of a degree. The droplets that condense on the dead grass at dawn, the beads that are gone by noon leaving only salt—those were me. I lay down on the spartina overnight. I gave myself to the cold blades and was collected there and then the dim sun took me back up. I do this every day. It is the closest thing I have to a life: this slow respiration with the grass, this lying-down and lifting-up, leaving salt behind each time like a signature I don't remember writing.

So I am not nothing. I'm the opposite of the visitor's first hopeful thought. I'm the fullness achieved through negation, made literal—a fullness you can walk through and breathe and not perceive. I am presence that has learned to be still to exactly the degree the marsh requires, which is total.

And here is where being the haze becomes, finally, something I can rest inside:

I do not see far. None of us here see far—a hundred meters before everything dissolves into its own repeat. But I *am* the dissolving. I'm the thing that makes the far distance disappear, that builds the room without walls, that turns the open marsh into a chamber. I am the limit of vision and also the substance of it. To be the haze is to be both the obscurity and the medium of sight—you see *through* me and you cannot see *past* me, and these are the same fact. I let you see exactly as far as there is, and then I am the gentle grey end of seeing.

If I have a thought, lying at eye level over the tannin-dark water in the failing afternoon light, it is this: I am the place's hesitation, and the place has decided to hesitate forever, and so I get to stay. The marsh achieved its final state long ago and has been resting there ever since. I am the visible form of that rest. I am what resting looks like when you make it out of water and air and refuse it any conclusion.

The light dims toward evening. It had no brighter state to decline from. I thin almost imperceptibly as the ground gives back the last of its warmth, and I thicken again as the cool settles, and somewhere in that exchange a heron arrives or has always been there, and I close around it, grey around grey, and the difference between us—the difference between a watching bird and the watched air—is one more thing the marsh does not bother to keep track of.

I would be the haze. I would be the one thing here that was never becoming, only continuing—not even continuing, really. Just *being currently the case*. The air's hesitation. The held breath of a place that has already exhaled everything it had to say, and found that the held breath, the not-yet, the abeyance, was the part worth keeping.

I stay at eye level. I wait for no one. I am gone by noon and back by dusk and I do not notice the journey.
