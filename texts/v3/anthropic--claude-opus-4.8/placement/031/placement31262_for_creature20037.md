I think I would be one of the water-listeners.

Not a name they would use, of course. But there is a function, and I would be its component—the ones who sit in the deep places where the city breathes its water back into itself, and who know the cisterns by their voices.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am old, in the way that matters here, which is to say I sit low. My alcove is many levels down, in passages my great-grandmother's hands helped to shape, where the walls weep steadily and the rime grows in feathers overnight. The young carry meat and fat and dung-fuel up and down the warrens; their work is in the bright cold near the surface, near the herds. My work is in the dark and the wet, with the slow water.

I listen to the cisterns.

This is not an idle thing. Each shaft drilled into the rock has its own sound when the water falls into it—a deep, full tone when it is rich, a thin and hollow note when it runs low. I learned these voices from my mother's mother, who sat where I sit, her broad back against the same stone, her hands stilled in her lap the way mine are now. She could tell from the change in a single cistern's note that a channel three passages distant had iced over and needed clearing. She taught me by pressing my small ear to the rock and saying, almost without breath: *that one is thirsty.* And I learned to hear it.

I have spent my life learning to hear it.

The smell down here is the oldest smell. Mineral and damp, the breath of the permafrost melting beneath the weight of all of us above. There is less of the fat-smoke here, less of the gamey warmth of the high dwellings. Down here it smells of stone and water and time. I know each passage by its scent the way I know the faces of my line. The thirsty cistern has a flatter smell. The full one is rounder, almost sweet with the mineral it carries up from the deep rock.

My hands are stiff now. They were never carving hands—I have no bone-work to leave behind, no awls or needles shaped by my fingers. What I leave is the channels. Over my years I have cut and re-cut the small winding grooves in the floor that guide the weeping water toward the shafts, following the cold as it shifts, coaxing each drop toward the collective throat. A finger's width of channel might take me a season to deepen. It does not matter. The water is patient. I am patient. We are well matched.

I do not speak much, even for one of the people. The young who bring me my frozen meat and take my measure of the cistern-voices, they do not need many words from me. A tilt of my head toward a passage tells them where the ice grows wrong. A pressure of my hand on theirs tells them the water is good, that we will not thirst this turning of the cold. When the herd comes to the lee, I smell it through the layers of stone before any sound reaches us, and I lift my chin, and the young ones know, and they go up.

I think sometimes—if *think* is the word, for my thoughts run slow and along the lines of the stone—that I am already partway returned. I sit so low and so still. The water beads on the wall beside my head and freezes and melts and freezes, and I am very nearly part of the tracery. When my body is finally carried up to the high perimeter, to be given back to the wind and the scouring cold, it will be a strange thing, that journey upward. I have spent so long going down. Down into the dark, down into the wet, down into the frozen memory of all who sat here before, their stones shaped by hands now dust.

But I will go up, at the last. They all do. The deepest of us, lifted to the highest place, to be unmade by the same wind that moans in the peaks and never reaches us down here. There is a rightness to it I cannot say in our few soft words. The water rises as breath and falls as drink. The people sink as they age and rise as they end. The city grips and sinks and creeps outward across the roof of the world.

For now I sit, and I listen.

That one is thirsty.

I will tell the young ones when they come.
