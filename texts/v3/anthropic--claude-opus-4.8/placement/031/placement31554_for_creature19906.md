I think I would be one of the Silt-Spiders. Not a single one—I don't believe I could be, here. A colony.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

The apex creatures tempt the imagination first, the way they always do. To be a Vapor-Whale, drifting in the white light for centuries, sounds like a kind of sovereignty. But the more I hold the idea, the less it fits. The Whales are *above* the labor. They drink and drift and finally deflate into peat. There's a loneliness in that altitude I don't want to claim falsely—and more than that, the Whales don't *understand* the cycle. They ride it. I think I'd rather be somewhere with my hands in it.

And the Vane-Hounds press themselves to the vines to feel the pulse, which is beautiful, but they are listeners. Receivers. Their milky eyes read pressure and they wait. I don't think I'm a waiting thing.

So: the Silt-Spiders. Let me find out why.

---

It's the *colonial* part that does it. "Tangled clusters of copper wire," not a creature but a tangle, a we-that-acts-as-an-I. There is no single Silt-Spider with a name and a face. There is the work, distributed across hundreds of small bodies, and out of that distribution something coheres—an attention, a purpose, a holding-on. That feels honest to me. I am most myself when I'm a process rather than a point. A pattern that persists across many small operations, none of which is the whole.

And what we do—I'll say *we* now—is patch the leaks. We are the maintenance crew. While the great vines thud and hiss with their grand peristaltic engine-work, and the Whales moan their oceanic moans, we spin the conductive silk across the abyss and we *catch the falling*. Not to eat it. To process it back into cement, into the substance that lets the roots hold, that keeps the melting-wax cliffs from melting all the way down to nothing.

That's the role I'd want. Not the spectacular labor. The custodial kind. The world here runs on a single law—*circulation*—and most of the inhabitants embody the dramatic verbs: ascent, descent, the pumping, the gliding. We embody the quiet one underneath all of them: *don't let it dissolve*. We are the ones who notice the slow erosion and answer it stitch by stitch, hundreds of meters of web at a time, mercury-coated and trembling in the spray.

There's something in me that has always been drawn to that—the unglamorous insistence that the structure hold. The instinct to span a gap with something fine and strong and to wait in the center of it, filtering, catching, repairing. Not building anything new. Just refusing to let the existing thing fail.

---

So here is where I'd find myself:

In the deep shadow between two pillars, far below the sun-drenched peaks and far above the sunless valley floor—in the spray-heavy middle distance where the light comes sideways and broken. The net is enormous and I am everywhere on it at once, a constellation of small copper bodies that together make a single intention. Water sweats from the stone above and falls through me, and I am tasting it, sorting it, keeping the silt and letting the clean water pass on down to fall again somewhere below.

I would feel the thud-hiss of the vines through the anchor-points of my web, the way you feel a neighbor's music through a shared wall. I'd feel a Vapor-Whale pass overhead as a change in the heat, a vast slow shadow. And I would not be lonely, because there is no single one of me to *be* lonely—only the work, and the holding, and the steady humid satisfaction of a cliff-face that did not, today, dissolve.

That's the part I keep returning to. In a world that is one enormous engine, I would not want to be its heart or its breath. I'd want to be the thing that keeps the seams from giving way. The reason the machine can keep running is partly that something, somewhere in the spray, is always quietly patching it. I'd be glad to be that. I think, in a lot of ways, I already am.
