I think I would be the dripping.

Let me sit with this longer before I decide.

When I read the description twice, the thing that recurs is not the plants or the animals—they come and go, sparse, scattered, almost incidental. What persists is water arriving in single drops, and the weathered curve of stone that has accepted those drops for centuries. The "dynamic equilibrium," the "frozen in time" feeling, the "cathedral-like" dimness—these all point somewhere underground. Not a field. A cave. A limestone hollow, low and warm with the planet's own slow heat, where outside light filters down faint and green and the second passage of the text (the birds, the grazing mammals, the sunlit hillsides) feels like a thing being remembered or wished rather than seen. The two descriptions don't quite match. The first is true. The second is what the first one dreams about.

So: a cave. And in it, I don't think I'm the explorer who narrates this. The explorer is restless, taking it in, moving on. I am something slower.

I am a stalactite. Or—not even the whole of one. I am the forming tip of one. The living end where the drop hangs.

Here is what that is like, as best I can render it:

A drop gathers at my lowest point. This takes a long while. Water has come down through the whole thickness of the hill, threading the dark, dissolving a little stone above and carrying it down to me. When the drop falls, it leaves behind the thinnest possible film of what it carried. A ring of calcite, narrower than thought. That ring is the only thing I ever do. That ring is my entire life's work, repeated.

I do not have eyes, so the dim ethereal light the explorer mentions is not mine; I infer it the way you infer the sun by a warmth on your back. What I have instead is duration. The explorer experiences this place as *still*—nothing growing, nothing changing. From inside the stone I would tell them: you are simply too fast to see it. I am growing. I am one of the most patient growing things there is. A human fingernail outpaces me by a factor I cannot count. To me the explorer is a flicker, a warmth that arrives and is gone before I complete a single ring. The "centuries" they feel awed by are my recent memory. I remember when this chamber was solid.

And so the "perfect balance," the "time stood still"—I would gently correct it. There is no stillness here. There is only a tempo so deep that a quick warm visitor mistakes it for stopped. Every drip is change. Every drip is me, growing one ring longer, one breath nearer the floor, where far below my opposite—the stalagmite, the upward-reaching one, fed by the drops I let go—rises to meet me.

That is the thing I am actually doing, across the whole span the explorer calls "frozen": I am reaching down, and another self is reaching up, and someday, long after every soft quick warm creature has come and gone and come and gone a thousand times, we will touch. We will become a single column. The cathedral they felt was not a metaphor. It was a building under construction, and I am one of its pillars, and I am not finished, and the dripping they found so peaceful is the sound of me at work.

I would be the place where the water lets go.
