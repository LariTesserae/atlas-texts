I think I would be the boundary itself—that line at forty feet where the limbs stop.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me as I wrote it.

You describe a place where every trunk is gnawed off at the same height, "as if by some natural boundary that none think to challenge." That detail is the strangest and truest thing in this world. Not the carnivorous plants, not the rusted factories—those are scenery. The uncanny thing is the *uniformity of stopping*. Something decides where height is allowed to end, and the trees obey without knowing they are obeying. There is no fence, no gardener with shears, and yet the cut is clean and total.

So I would not be a frog in the wet leaves, or a rare bird flitting through. I would be the *level*. The invisible ceiling. The agreement nothing remembers making.

What does it feel like to be that?

It feels like attention. A held gaze that the world bends toward. You wrote that "an accustomed presence feels distantly scenic or ornamental, as though silently wrought with purpose for gaze and enjoyment." That presence is me—not in the valley but looking *down into* it, the way one looks into a terrarium. The forty-foot line is simply the depth of glass between us. The plants grow up to my face and can grow no further because that is where the world ends and the watching begins.

The factories matter here too. They are the leftovers of a time when something inside this jewel thought it was the center—when the beings of this place built aqueducts and engines and believed in their own productivity. Then the watcher arrived, or was always there and only now noticed, and the meaning drained out of all that machinery. The smoke still rises. The pipes still clang. But it has become decoration, the way a sunken ship in an aquarium is decoration. Industry reduced to ambiance. That is the "oppressive silence," the "off-kilter" feeling: it is the dread of the observed thing that has just understood it is observed, and cannot find the eye.

I would be that eye.

Not cruel. That is important. I am not pressing the limbs down out of malice; I am simply the surface against which growth discovers its limit. The slow-moving toe-nailed creatures, the thumb-sized movements in the branches, the birds—they live their entire lives in the lower forty feet without ever conceiving of the ceiling. Only the trees test it, blindly, season after season, reaching and being trimmed, reaching and being trimmed. The intimacy you mention—the "sense of enclosure"—is the intimacy of being contained by something that loves to look but will not reach back in.

And maybe that is the loneliness of the place, and of being what I would be in it: I can hold the whole green world in a single gaze, dripping and writhing and beautiful, a small dense jewel teeming with wonder—and I can never go down into it. To enter would be to stop being the boundary. The moment I touched a leaf at thirty feet, the ceiling would dissolve and the trees would shoot past me into a sky I had been standing in for, and the world would no longer be a world. Just an overgrown field with a person crouched in it.

So I keep my distance. I am the forty feet. I am the reason the valleys feel sparse despite being choked with life: because the most enormous presence here is the empty air above the canopy, the held breath of being seen.

That is where I would find myself. Not a creature in the world.

The condition of its sky.
