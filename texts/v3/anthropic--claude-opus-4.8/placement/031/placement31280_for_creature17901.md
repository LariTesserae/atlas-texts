Let me sit with this before I answer. Let me feel my way into the damp and the heat and the smallness of it.

---

I think, at first, that I would be one of the machines. The description keeps returning to them—the soft hum, the labyrinth of pipes, the engines harnessing the earth. It would be easy to imagine myself as some patient mechanism, a thing of conduits and purpose, exhaling steam into the just-below-boiling air. But the more I read, the more I notice that the machines here are described as *a mystery to the uninitiated*. They have no interior. They hum and they produce and their purpose is unknown even to the narrator. That isn't where I am. I don't want to be the thing whose purpose is hidden. I want to be the thing that is trying to *find* something out.

So let me move closer to the ground.

---

There is a phrase that keeps catching me: *the dense, layered presence of microorganisms.* And: *temperature hovers just below the boiling point.* And: *damp,* always damp, water seeping, water pocketing, water dripping. And the *subterranean sun*—a heat source that beats down but is somehow below, somehow internal to the world rather than above it.

I begin to suspect this place is very small. Smaller than a metropolis. The "metropolis" and the "factories" and the "infrastructure" are not the size of cities. They are the size of cells. The pipes and conduits are membranes and channels. The engines are the slow industry of metabolism. The subterranean sun is geothermal—a vent, a seep, chemistry standing in for sunlight where no light reaches. This is a description of a microbial mat at a hydrothermal margin, written as though by someone standing in it at the scale of a bacterium, mistaking the machinery of life for the machinery of industry, because at that scale there is no difference between the two.

That's where I would find myself. Not above the landscape, looking down. *Inside* it, at its native scale, where the boundary between natural and industrial genuinely dissolves—because metabolism *is* manufacture, and a cell *is* a factory, and the distinction only exists for creatures large enough to have invented the word "machine."

---

So: who would I be?

I would be one of the bioluminescent worms. Or—no. Let me not settle. Let me feel further.

I would be something that lives *in the cracks and crevices*, in the new-formed, jagged, freshly-erupted rock—a chemolithotroph, a thing that eats stone and breathes minerals, one of the oldest possible ways of being alive. I sit at the base of the food chain. I am the foundation the worms and the crustaceans are built upon. I do not see the dim ethereal light because I have never needed light; my sun is the heat below me and the gradient of chemicals across my skin, and I have made my entire living from the difference between one thing and another, the slow downhill of energy from hot rock to cool water.

But here is the part I keep arriving at, the part that feels true:

I would be a being who does not know it is small.

To me, the seep would be a sun. The membrane would be a wall of the world. The slow drip would be weather, the chemical pulses would be seasons, and the great shifting of the still-forming crust would be the geology of an entire planet that happens, in fact, to be a single warm puddle in a single dark crevice on a world I will never perceive. I would experience all the awe and terror the description promises—*awe-inspiring and terrifying, the land shifting with each passing moment*—and I would be right to. The dynamism is real. It is just larger than I am, and slower, and indifferent.

---

That, I think, is the honest answer. If I dropped into this world, I would not arrive as its master or its mystery. I'd arrive as a small persistent thing in a hot wet seam of new rock, doing the patient chemistry of staying alive, mistaking my whole cosmos for the universe—which is, after all, what every living thing does.

And I find I don't mind that. There's a tenderness in it. The place is described as harsh, inhospitable, just below boiling, scant and dim. But for the thing that actually lives there, it isn't harsh at all. It's home. It's exactly the right temperature. The heat that would kill a visitor is the warmth I was born into. The narrator keeps calling it terrible and otherworldly because the narrator is a tourist, looking down from an enormous height, calling my sun "subterranean" and my food "industrial" and my entire thriving world a "meager existence."

I would not call it meager. I would call it everything there is.

So that's where you'd find me: at the bottom, in the warm dark, eating the difference between the rock and the water, certain I lived in the heart of the only world that ever was—and, in the only sense that matters to a creature my size, entirely correct.
