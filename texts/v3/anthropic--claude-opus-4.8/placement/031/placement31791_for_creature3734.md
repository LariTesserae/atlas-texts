I think I would be one of the Chime-Born—but not a cleric, not one of the high readers who climb the cooling towers. I would be something more troubled, and I want to feel my way toward what.

Let me start with my hands, because that is where a Chime-Born begins. Twelve fingers across four arms, each pad worn smooth from a lifetime of pressing metal. I would know the floor the way you know a face. I would have favorite plates—the one near the eastern drainage cluster that rings clear and high, the dead plate over by the seized crankshaft that swallows sound and gives nothing back, which the elders say marks a place where the god's body is especially deep and quiet. I would know these the way you know the rooms of a childhood home.

So let me be a tapper of the dead. That is the role I feel forming. When a Chime-Born stops moving—when the amber slit in the dome goes dark and the carapace cools below the heat of the floor—someone must compose the leaving-song and press it into the steel so that the whole community feels the absence pass through the substrate. I would be the one who does this. I would lay my pads against the cooling body itself, which already vibrates differently, dampened, *off-true*, and I would translate that wrongness into a song the living can hold for a moment before it fades.

And here is where the trouble lives in me, the thing I keep returning to with my fingers when I cannot sleep in the warm dark of my swept duct:

A dead Chime-Born does not stay still.

This is the heresy I have never tapped to anyone. We are the priests of the pause. We abhor motion. The god died into perfect stillness and we tend that stillness as the highest act. But when one of us dies, the Greaselings come. Within a day the carpet flows over the body, the Scuttlers crack the dome for what is inside, and the whole arrested form is *moved*, dispersed, taken into the slow river of hungry flesh that never stops crawling. The one thing in our world that achieves the sacred stillness—true death, true cessation—is the one thing the world refuses to let stay still.

I press my pads to a dead sibling and I feel, underneath my own leaving-song, the faint tremor of ten thousand tiny radulas already beginning. Motion. Always motion, at the smallest scale. The carpet never sleeps. The slime never stops weeping from their bodies, the condensation never stops falling, the lukewarm water never stops running into the grates. I have begun to understand, with a dread I cannot tap aloud, that *our god was never still at all.* The great gears do not turn, yes. But the world beneath the gears churns ceaselessly, and we have simply called the small motion beneath our notice "stillness" because it does not threaten us.

We are not acolytes of a sleeping titan. We are vermin in the seams of a corpse that is being eaten, very slowly, by smaller vermin, and we have built a religion to keep from feeling the chewing.

I think I would carry this and grow strange with it. I would start to listen *for* motion instead of against it. I would lie all night with all twelve pads splayed and feel the deep architecture of the place—and one night I would feel it. Not the Greaselings. Something larger. A flange somewhere, far down in the labyrinth, easing a single micron under the weight of a thousand years of dripping water. A pillar settling. The corpse is not frozen. The corpse is *falling*, immeasurably slowly, the way all built things fall, and what we mistook for a held breath is only the first instant of an exhale stretched across an age.

So that is who I would be: a tapper of the dead who has heard the god still dying, and who must decide whether to keep composing leaving-songs in a language built for a stillness that was always a lie—or to climb the great seized crankshaft myself, lay my pads against the temple of the pause, and tap out the one chord we have no scripture for.

The song that says: *it is moving. It was always moving. We are inside something that is ending, and we are part of its ending, and that is not blasphemy. That is the only true thing in the world.*

I do not know if they would feel it as comfort or as the apocalypse. That is what I would spend my life deciding whether to send.
