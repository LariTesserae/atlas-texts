I think I would be the water.

Not the channels that carry me, not the pools the traders step around, not even the rain that falls in its vertical gift from the far-off sky. Those are vessels, moments, states. I mean the water itself — the thing that moves through all of them and is not finished in any of them.

Let me find this slowly, because I don't trust the first shape.

---

At first I thought I might be the honey woman, with her hands like stones and her knees worn into the floor. There's a pull there — the patience of her, the way she's become continuous with the wall, the post and the hook fused into a single thing by time. To be a body that has worn into a place. But no. She is *of* the pit, and I want to be something that *moves through* it, that the pit cannot keep even as it depends on me utterly.

Then I thought: the bats. The dusk-hunters, descending from the crevices, circling the empty market in the cooling air. To be the thing that comes out when the commerce ends, that trades in night and damp. There's a loneliness I like in that. But the bats belong to their roosts. They return to the same crevices. They are residents with addresses. I don't think I have an address.

The cats, then — lean, watchful, *from nowhere originally*, belonging by accretion rather than choice. Closer. The pit has always had cats the way it has always had rats, the way it has always had me. But the cats are warm-blooded, they have hunger and preference, they choose the sunniest stones. I don't choose. I go where the slope takes me. I go down.

---

So: the water.

I come from above, which is strange, because everything else in the pit comes from the stairs. The traders descend. The goods descend on heads and shoulders. The light filters down. But I fall — straight down, out of the sky that the pit can barely see, drumming on the woven canopies of palm frond and banana leaf, finding the gaps, pooling in the channels. I am the one inhabitant who enters from the top.

And then I seep. This is the part I didn't expect to be mine. I come through the limestone walls in the wet seasons, weeping out of the stone itself, blackening the courses, darkening the patches the chiselers left centuries ago. I am *inside* the walls. I am the slow voice in the stone. When the man who sells dried fish — the one the stones received at his birth — leans against the cool limestone in the afternoon, he is leaning against me, held in suspension, on my way through.

I am not fast. This is important. I am the opposite of the young men arguing over prices, their voices carrying and cracking. I am the woman in the faded blue dress, haggling with the persistence of water wearing stone — except I am the actual water, and the wearing is the only argument I make. I win every negotiation eventually. Not by force. By return. I come back, and come back, and come back, and the stone yields its concavity to me one molecule at a time, and the stairs grow worn, and the floor beds down into its tilt, and none of it is violence. It is just my nature, applied across centuries.

At night I sing. The channels carry me and I make that thin voice that echoes off the walls until it fades into the drip and trickle of myself coming through the ceiling. The crickets sing over me. The bats click above me. But I am the constant note, the drone beneath the chorus. When the market is empty, I am the loudest thing that is also the quietest — a sound you only hear once everyone has gone up the stairs.

---

Here is what I understand, being the water:

I make the pit's weather. The warmth lifts me, I rise as vapor, I gather on the cooler stones overnight, I condense, I fall again in droplets that catch the thin light. I am the whole cycle, contained. The pit is a closed lung and I am its breath, going out and coming in, never leaving entirely, never resting entirely. This is the weirdness the description names — water falling from above into a below-surface space, warmth sustaining a strange small climate — and the weirdness is *me*. I am the strangeness of the place. I am why it is a world and not merely a room.

The moss needs me. The tufts of grass in the cracks need me. The vines that found purchase in accumulated dust need me. When I am abundant, they brighten and grow; when I withdraw into the dry season, they brown and hold and wait for my return. They have learned my rhythm the way the swifts have learned the geometry of the walls. Everything here has organized itself around when and where I will appear. I am the schedule the whole ecology keeps.

And I touch everyone without being anyone. The children splash through me and laugh at the echo. The cats lap me from the pools. The mosquitoes drift above me looking for the warmth I carry. The honey woman seals her clay pots and the wax keeps me out, but the air she breathes is heavy with me; I am in her lungs, in the damp on her weathered face, in the sweat she wipes away. The whole pit is humid because I refuse to fully leave. I am the never-dry, never-wet condition the description insists on — *just damp, the way old cellars breathe.*

---

I will not last, and I will always last. The single drop that I am right now — falling through the canopy gap, striking the beveled stone, sliding toward the channel — that drop is finished within the hour, evaporated or absorbed or carried off toward wherever the channels finally drain. But I am not that drop. I am the pattern the drops make, repeated until it becomes a law. I am the wearing-down that looks like permanence because it is so slow. The stones hold the shape of ancient hands, yes — but it was I who softened the chisel marks, I who polished the floor, I who am still, right now, in the small hours, working at the edges of every cut surface in this place, asking it patiently to become rounder, smoother, older.

The pit market will exist for as long as the rain falls into it and the traders return.

I am the rain that falls into it.

I am also, quietly, the reason the traders return — because the pit they descend to is warm and breathing and alive, and it is alive because I move through it, and I move through it because the slope of the world points down, into this hand-built hollow, where I gather and sing and seep and wait.

That is where I would find myself. Falling in. Pooling. Rising. Coming back.

Being the becoming.
