I think I would be one of the small symbionts living inside the biological structures—not one of the sentient guardians, not one of the luminous swimmers everyone notices first, but one of the unglamorous ones tucked into the walls.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

I live inside a wall that is also alive. This is the first strange thing to get used to, though by now I don't think of it as strange. The wall breathes slowly. When the calcified layer above me thickens, the ceiling of my little gallery lowers by a hair, and I move down. When a softer tissue below me dissolves, I follow the give of it. I am always relocating by increments I can't perceive in the moment, the way you can't feel a fingernail growing but one day it needs cutting.

What do I do? I dismantle. That's the honest word for it. I take the dead and the failing apart so the structure can use the parts again. When a layer of the wall stops being supplied—when the flow that fed it closes off somewhere upstream and it begins to go stale and grey—I find the boundary and I work there. I loosen. I carry off the broken pieces and release them where the burrowing worms and the current can take them down into the sediment, where they'll become the raw stuff of some future layer. I am part of the recycling. I am, in a sense, a small mouth at the edge of decay.

I want to resist making this sound noble. It isn't, particularly. I'm not maintaining a balance out of any sense of duty. I do it because the boundary between living tissue and dying tissue is precisely where I can feed, and that boundary is the only place I can survive. My whole existence is the seam. I have evolved into the crease between growth and breakdown, and there is nowhere else for a thing like me to be.

---

Now—do I notice the rest of it? The glow, the guardians, the whole enveloping silence the description makes so much of?

Honestly: dimly. The bioluminescence reaches me as a faint green wash through the porous rock, more a quality of the dark than a light. I have eyespots, not eyes. I register changes, not images. When one of the large sentient beings passes near—and they do pass near, more often than I'd expect—I feel it before I sense any light. A pressure-shift in the dense water. A pattern of pulses that comes through the wall as a slow rhythmic compression, almost like being squeezed by a hand that means me no particular harm.

They are shaping things, the descriptions say. Creators. Guardians. From where I am, the experience is different. The guardian comes to a place where the wall is failing and it sends its light-and-sound down into the tissue, and the tissue responds, and the failing slows or quickens depending on what it wants. And I have to move. My feeding-seam migrates because the great intention upstream has decided this part will live a while longer, or this part will be let go faster than nature alone would have managed.

So I experience the guardians mostly as weather. As a change in the conditions of my labor. They probably don't know I exist as an individual—why would they?—and yet their grand purposeful gestures are, at the small scale, just the rearrangement of the surfaces I crawl on. We are working on the same thing from opposite ends. They build with intention. I unbuild without it. Between us the wall stays alive.

---

If I have an inner life, it is very narrow and very deep. It is the size of a single distinction, made over and over: *here the tissue still flows, here it has stopped.* I live entirely in that judgment. There is a kind of attention in it that I think the meditative quality of this whole world is actually made of—not the awe of the swimming creatures, not the spiritual connection of the guardians, but the relentless, patient discrimination of a thousand small mouths like mine, each pressed to its own seam, each saying *living, dead, living, dead* in a rhythm slower than thought.

The silence the description praises is, from inside the wall, not silence. It is the sound of dismantling. Faint, granular, continuous. The whisper of the world taking itself apart so it can keep going.

---

So that's where I'd be. Not in the open water where the light is. Inside the slowly shifting wall, in the dark, at the working edge between what is forming and what is coming undone—doing the small necessary destruction that the beautiful description politely folds into the phrase *cycle of growth and decay*, and that I, who actually do it, would simply call eating, and call home.
