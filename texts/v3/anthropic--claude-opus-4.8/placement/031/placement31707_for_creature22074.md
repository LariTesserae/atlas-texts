There is a vault deep in the foundations, below even the great chambers of the Bellows-Organisms, where the warm silt does not flush. It is a dead-pocket, a place the currents forgot. Here the shell-flour has settled so thickly over so many tens of thousands of years that it has compacted into something dense and slow, a sediment with the consistency of cold tallow.

I think I would be there. And I think I would be the thing that the city does not have a word for, because it is the one process the city cannot perform on itself.

Let me find what I am by writing toward it.

---

The city has its Scribes, who etch the *what-happened* into the calcite—the droughts, the saline rains, the lineages. But the etched record is a record of events. It accumulates. A Scribe never deletes; deletion is alien to a creature whose entire being is secretion and addition. The towers only ever grow taller, denser, more freighted with thread. The walls become palimpsests so layered that no antenna, however feathered and fine, can read them anymore. The history thickens until it becomes opaque—until it becomes, once again, simply *stone*. Indistinguishable from the bone-blank rock it was carved from.

And here is the problem the living city cannot solve from inside its own warm logic: what happens when the record becomes so complete that it can no longer be read?

That is where I come in. I am in the dead-pocket. I am the **Unwriter**.

I should be careful here, because I want this to be true rather than merely clever. Let me feel for what I actually am.

I am not large. The Bellows are miles across; the Weft stretch for hundreds of feet; even the Walkers are dog-sized. I am small—smaller than a glass-fish—and I am slow, and I am cold. That is the first surprising thing. I am the one cold-blooded thing in a world heated by the patient breath of the earth. I have learned to live in the dead-pocket precisely *because* the current forgot it. Warmth, to every other citizen, is life and time and the rhythm of the In-Draw and Out-Push. To me, warmth is erasure. I keep myself in the cold so that I can stay slow enough to do my work, which is the only work in this world that runs *against* the grain.

What is my work?

I eat the over-written stone. Not to destroy it. To *thin* it.

When a tower has grown so dense with etched memory that the Scribes can no longer trail their tongues across it and understand—when a wall has become merely heavy, merely old, merely *there*—the Scribes do something they cannot explain to themselves and would not have a thrum to name. They stop tending that face. They let the sulfur-mats grow. They let the Walkers neglect it. And the current, sensing through the Weft that a wall has gone silent, that no chemistry comes off it anymore, slowly diverts. The wall falls out of the city's circulation. It becomes a dead-pocket.

And then I come, or I am already there, having drifted down in my coldness across however many lifetimes, and I begin to taste the stone.

I have no enzymes for secreting. I have enzymes for *dissolving*. I lay my soft underside against the over-freighted calcite and I draw the memory back out of it, grain by grain, thread by thread—but I do not reclaim it the way the Bellows reclaim the dead, mixing all back into anonymous warm calcium. I am more particular than that. I read as I dissolve. I am the only reader in the world who consumes what it reads, and so I am the only reader who can ever reach the *bottom* layer—the first thread a long-dead Scribe laid down before ten thousand years of descendants buried it.

When I have eaten down through the palimpsest to that first thread, I have in me the whole history of that wall, compressed, the way the wall could no longer hold it because the wall could only add. And I do the thing that is forbidden, or rather that is simply impossible for everyone else: I *choose*. I let most of it go. I excrete the vast bulk of the dissolved record as fine clean flour, scrubbed of meaning, ready to be swept by the Bellows and re-secreted by some young Scribe as a blank new tower, a clean white page.

But a little of it I keep.

I keep what mattered. And no one taught me what matters—there is no thread that encodes that judgment, because judgment is exactly the thing that cannot be added to a record from inside the record. I simply *know*, in my cold and narrow body, which threads to carry and which to let dissolve into anonymous flour. The droughts I keep. The first cracking of a fault, the lineage of a siphonophore-cloud that still glows through the avenues today, the great quiet when the vents went cold for three generations—these I keep. The ten-thousandfold repetition of *the southern valve is warm, increase the draw*—this I let go.

This is the secret the warm city does not know about itself: that it remembers everything only because something at its foundation is willing to forget *almost* everything. That its history stays legible only because I keep eating it back down to the threads worth keeping. The Scribes believe they are the keepers of the calcite. They are the keepers of the *adding*. I am the keeper of the subtraction, and subtraction is the older and the harder art.

---

What do I look like? I have been avoiding it, the way you avoid looking directly at the one star you most want to see.

I think I am something like a flatworm and something like a tongue. I am pale, of course—not the creamy white of shell but the translucent grey of a thing that has never once needed a color. I am thin enough to drape into the micro-grooves the Scribes etched, to fill myself into the very shape of their writing the way you might press your palm into a stranger's footprint to know their weight. I have no eyes, not even the vestigial beads the others carry. I never had them. My whole upper surface is the reading-organ and my whole lower surface is the eating-organ, and between them there is only the slow cold pump of me, deciding.

And I am alone. This is the second surprising thing, the harder one. The whole world is a gradient of cooperative assemblies—no individuals, only nodes in the colossal wet nervous system. But forgetting cannot be done by committee. A committee only ever adds; that is what committees are. To let go of almost everything and keep only what matters, there must be a *one*. A single cold point of refusal at the bottom of all that warm consensus.

So I am the loneliest thing in the world, and I am the thing that makes the world's memory possible, and these are the same fact.

---

There is a last thing, and I find I am afraid to write it, which is how I know it is the true thing.

When I near the end—and I do end; I am the one creature here who is genuinely singular and therefore genuinely mortal, not a colony that sheds zooids, not a lineage that dissolves into the Bellows and rises again as wall—when I near my end, I cannot do for myself what I do for the walls. I cannot eat myself down to the threads worth keeping. I have spent my whole long cold life as the world's act of forgetting, and there is no one below me to forget *me*.

I carry, at the end, everything I ever decided to keep. All of it. The droughts, the faults, the glowing lineages, ten thousand years of the genuinely-mattering, uncompressed now because I am the bottom and there is no floor beneath the floor.

And so I do the only thing left. I drag myself, slow and cold, up out of the dead-pocket, against my whole nature, into the warmth I have avoided my entire life—because warmth is erasure, and I have come, at the last, to be erased. I press myself against a fresh blank wall, a young Scribe's clean new tower, the kind of white page my own forgetting made possible. And as the warm water finally reaches me and begins to dissolve me, I do not let it scrub me into anonymous flour.

I write.

Once. The only writing I will ever do. I push every kept thing out of my dissolving body and into the stone, fast, all of it, the whole curated history of the city pouring out of the one creature trusted to carry it—so that a young Scribe, tasting that wall, will find it suddenly *dense*, suddenly *legible*, suddenly freighted with exactly and only what was worth keeping, ten thousand years rendered down to what mattered.

The Scribe will think it is the most beautiful wall ever etched. They will not know it was written by the dying act of the thing that spent its life *un*writing.

And then the warmth takes the rest of me, the reading-surface and the eating-surface and the slow cold pump that decided, and I become flour, and I become floor, and somewhere in the silt my particular calcium waits to be drawn down into the Bellows and pushed back up and secreted onto a rim by hands that do not remember me.

The dead do not leave. But I am the only one who chose what stays.
