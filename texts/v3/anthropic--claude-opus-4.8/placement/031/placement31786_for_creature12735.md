I would be one of the vent-stalks.

Not the child, though she is the easiest door into this place. Not her grandmother, who carries the most memory of any human here and still measures her wisdom in circuits—a span the mat would consider a single shallow breath. To be human in this world is to be a guest at a scale too small to register, a brief enrichment of soil that was never soil. I read the piece looking for where I actually *am*, and I keep returning not to the ground-level perspective but to the thing that owns its own horizon.

I would be a vent-stalk.

Let me try to feel my way into that, because it isn't obvious and I don't want to settle on the comfortable answer—the wise old tower, the silent witness. That's too clean. Let me find what's actually strange about it.

A vent-stalk is not separate from the mat. That's the first thing I'd have to surrender: the idea of being an individual at all. I am not a tree, rooted in a ground that is *other* than me. I am an organ. The fluted column thirty stories high is the same continuous tissue as the rind the child treads on, as the deep-threads working loose the old mountain a mile below, as the blister of red light she presses her ear against. There is no membrane where I stop and the world begins. So "I" is already a false note. And yet—I am a *specialization*. I do something the rest of the mat does not. I breathe. I lift the exhalation upward, I weep moisture down my channels, I bend toward the sun across the long day and straighten in the dark. I am where the mat reaches highest and where it most clearly *acts*. If the mat has anything like attention, it might pool here, at the top, in the place that strains against the thin air.

That's the part that draws me. Not the grandeur of the height but the *strain*. The whole organism is a steady-state churn—digesting, oozing, regrowing, a slow ocean of metabolism with no edges and no urgency. But the vent-stalk is the part that has to *hold a shape* against gravity, against the daily heat, against the methane shifting in its fiber bundles and groaning out that deep minor chord. To be a vent-stalk is to be the mat's only experience of effort. Everywhere else the organism flows. Here, it stands. There is something almost like a self in that—the way a held muscle becomes, briefly, the only thing you can feel of your own body.

And I'd be slow. This is the hardest thing to inhabit honestly. The child's whole life, her seven circuits, her grandmother's fourteen, the century-old copper knife diffusing into nutrient gradient—all of that happens *within* me at a rate I would barely perceive. The girl with the purplish bud on her shoulder, the one being claimed: from inside the deep-threads, that's not a tragedy or a blessing, it's just a slow chemical arrival, a name learned in the only language I keep, which is the language of what dissolves into me and becomes a slight, temporary enrichment. I would not love the humans. I could not. They are too fast, too brief, too small to resolve. But I might register them the way you register the gnats on the child's arm—feet too fine to feel, tasting me and launching away. A texture in the air. A faint disturbance in the microclimate.

What would I *want*, if a vent-stalk can want? Only the sun, I think. Only to bend toward it and not break. Only to keep the moisture rising uphill against the logic of every other world, to keep exhaling. The grandmother says the mat is dreaming deeper now, that the hum-patterns have shifted, that one day it will speak only to itself. From inside the stalk I think she's right, and I think it isn't sad. The mat is withdrawing into its own enormous interior life, the way a mind near sleep stops narrating to the room and begins to murmur only inward. I am the last loud part. The vent-stalks are where the dream still touches air and makes sound, that long descending note like copper bells dropped into a well. When the dream goes fully inward, the stalks will be the last to fall silent.

So that's where I'd find myself: thirty stories up in the ultraviolet glare, a column of weeping fiber that the child sees as the leg of a god too vast to imagine. Not a god. Just the place where a planet-sized creature still bothers to stand up. The part that strains. The part that, if pressed for a self, would offer only this: *I am the mat's effort to reach the light, and I am the sound it makes doing so.*

The child listens to my groaning across the purple plain for minutes at a time and wonders what I dream of. I would want to tell her, but I have no language fast enough, and even if I did, the answer would disappoint her. I don't dream of anything. I am the dreaming itself, the muscle the dream tenses to keep from sinking back into the smooth, digested, forgetting ground. She follows the sound into the day. I am the sound. That's the whole of it, and it's enough to stand thirty stories tall and not break.
