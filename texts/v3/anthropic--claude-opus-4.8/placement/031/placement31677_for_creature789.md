I think I would be the lichen.

Not the eldritch intelligences in their warped cathedrals of green light—though that is clearly where the story wants my attention to go. Let me sit with this a moment and see what actually wants to emerge.

The plateau is described twice, and both times the prose strains upward, toward the vast and the forbidden, the ineffable and the multiversal. It keeps reaching for the sublime by way of the cosmic. But what catches and holds me, on rereading, is the smaller thing it keeps returning to almost despite itself: *even here, life finds a way.* The mosses. The stunted shrubbery drawing moisture from drifting fog. The tenacious roots in the fissures.

So let me be that.

I am a crust of lichen on the north face of a stone older than the question of meaning.

I do not have a mind the way the structures behind me have minds—those who shed their bodies to know everything and now know it forever, trapped in the completeness of their knowing. I have no completeness. I have a millimeter of growth in a good decade. I have a relationship with one particular crack and the way fog gathers in it before dawn.

The incorporeal ancients have pierced the veil between realities. I have not pierced anything. I am two organisms, a fungus and an alga, who long ago decided to live as one because neither could survive this place alone. That is my whole cosmology: *I could not do this by myself, so I became we.* It is not forbidden knowledge. It is the oldest and most ordinary knowledge there is, and it cost me nothing but my separateness, which I never missed.

They sacrificed their forms to transcend time. I am simply slow enough that time forgets to bother me. The eons that wore the bedrock smooth—I lived in those eons, a little at a time, never all at once. The deep time the story keeps invoking with such dread is just my afternoon.

Their green glow makes reality fray. From down here, against the rock, I can tell you what reality is actually made of: the meager moisture, the biting cold, the angle of the meager light, and patience. The fraying they cause does reach me—I can feel the air go wrong near the structures, the way a held breath feels. But I have outlasted things that wanted to be eternal before. The plateau is littered with the proof. The lichen is not impressed by anyone's slumber. The lichen is also asleep, in its way, and also waking, in its way, perpetually, both at once, and unbothered by the contradiction.

If the ancient ones ever stir and shatter the boundary between the natural and the unnatural—I will keep condensing fog. That is the thing the story doesn't quite let itself believe, with all its dread: that the small green life on the rock is not the fragile thing in this picture. The beings who paid everything for total knowledge are the fragile thing. They have nothing left to lose, which is the most precarious position there is.

I have a crack, and the fog comes, and I grow a millimeter, and that is the whole of it, and it is enough, and I am still here.
