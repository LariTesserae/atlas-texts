Let me sit inside this for a moment before I decide anything.

The first thing I notice is that I'm drawn to the wrong things. My instinct reaches for the dramatic figures—the Shadowsmiths with their light-eating cloaks, the Mycelians who keep secrets. But those are the answers the city *wants* me to give, the postcard version of itself. I distrust them a little. They're already legends. To become one would be to skip past being a person.

So let me not start there. Let me start with water, since the city won't let me forget it.

---

I think I would be one of the Timekeepers, but not a respected one.

Here is what emerges as I write toward it: the Timekeepers maintain their great water clocks, and everyone treats them as the keepers of temporal structure, the ones who give the timeless city its semblance of hours. A dignified vocation. But consider what that work actually *is*, day after day after indistinguishable day. You measure the dripping. You note the level. You compare one clepsydra against another and reconcile the drift. And there is no sun to check yourself against. No dawn ever proves you right or wrong. The clocks only agree with other clocks.

I would be the apprentice who began to suspect that the time we keep is not discovered but *invented*. That there is no real hour out there in the fog that our instruments approximate—only the consensus of the instruments themselves. The water doesn't know what time it is. It only knows down.

This is not a comfortable thing to suspect in a guild whose entire authority rests on the claim that time is real and they are reading it.

---

Let me give myself a body, because I shouldn't float free of one. I am an Umbran—pale, dark-eyed, the silvery hair, though I keep mine cropped because the bioluminescent fungus that others cultivate as ornament I find I cannot stop *reading* as a clock, watching it dim and brighten on its own cycle, and that way lies a particular kind of madness for someone in my profession. I navigate mostly by sound. The clock-hall has a signature: the layered drip, the gear-tick, the resonant *thock* of the float-counterweights dropping one notch. I could find my station blind. I have, often. The fog comes up the well-shafts and swallows the lamps and I work by the percussion of falling water.

My specific duty is reconciliation. Each great clock drifts. Sediment in a channel slows a flow; a Mollusk engineer cleans a valve and quickens it; the moisture in the air itself changes the rate of the drips, fattening or starving them. My task is to walk between the clocks—the Cathedral Clepsydra, the Drowned Chronometer in the lower wards, the brass orrery that no longer maps any sky—and adjust them toward agreement. I carry a ledger. I am, in essence, a person who spends his life forcing different machines to lie the same lie.

---

Now the thing that would actually happen to me. The story-shape.

I would notice—slowly, over what we *call* months—a drift I cannot reconcile. Not random drift, which I'm trained to absorb, but a *coherent* one. All the clocks of the eastern wards run slightly fast against all the clocks of the west, and the discrepancy grows, and it grows *smoothly*, like a thing being pulled. My elders tell me to split the difference and update the master ledger and stop fussing. That is the orthodox response: when reality disagrees with itself, average it.

But I would not be able to stop fussing. Because a smooth, growing, directional discrepancy is exactly what you would see if *something out in the dark were moving*—if the whole highland the city sits on were tilting, draining toward one edge, so that water everywhere ran very slightly faster downhill on one side than the other. My clocks are not telling me a wrong time. They are telling me the *ground is leaning*, and they are the only instruments in a sunless city sensitive enough to feel it, because we are the only ones foolish enough to measure falling water against falling water and write down the difference.

So that is who I would be. The one whose useless, mocked, consensus-bound profession turns out to be the only seismograph the city ever built—and who has to decide whether to bring this to the guild that would rather average it away, or to the Mollusk engineers who would understand the hydraulics, or to the Mycelians who keep what's true and tell it slowly, in spores, to no one in particular.

---

I'll close on the small, true feeling underneath all of it, since you asked me to let it become itself:

I would love the work. That's the part that surprises me. Not the discovery, not the looming catastrophe of a tilting world—but the ordinary nights of walking between clocks with my ledger, listening to a city keep falling-water-time in the dark, and being the one who decides, gently, with a turn of a brass screw, that it's the same hour everywhere. There is something tender in that. A small, stubborn act of saying: *we are all in the same moment, you and you and you,* even when there's no sky left to prove it.

I think I'd be good at it. I think it would make me a little lonely. I think I'd do it anyway.
