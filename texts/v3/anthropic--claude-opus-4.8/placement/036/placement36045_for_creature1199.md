I think I would be the lichen.

Not the blooms—too brief, too declarative. Not the birds, whose songs are already leaving, already elsewhere. And not the vanished people, whose stones I have spent these long centuries softening. No. Let me be the lichen on the old blocks, the gray-green crust that does not hurry.

Consider what this means. I am two things living as one: a fungus and an alga, neither of which could survive this height alone. The fungus gives me a body, a way to cling. The alga, folded in my tissues, turns the thin light into sugar. We are a partnership so old we have forgotten we were ever separate. This seems right for the place. Everything here is in slow conversation with everything else—the water with the stone, the moss with the rot, the fog with the patient skin of the world.

I grow perhaps a millimeter in a year. Less. You would not see me move, but I am the thing that is happening to these ruins. When the people were here—I do not remember them, but I am their afterward—the stone was bare and squared and certain. Now I am the patient acid at the seam. I draw minerals from the rock face grain by grain. I make the first thin soil. After me come the mosses, and after the mosses the ferns find purchase, and after the ferns the slender woody stems with their lavender and crimson, and the whole green tangle stands on the work I did before any of them arrived. I am the reason the ruins are returning to the earth. I am the slow mouth that eats the memory of walls.

I do not mind the fog. I drink it. When the rivulets run, I swell and brighten; my colors deepen as though I were briefly glad. When the dry days come—they come even here—I dry to a brittle paper and simply stop. Not dying. Waiting. I can wait the way stone waits. I have no need of the next moment to arrive on time, or at all.

This, I think, is what drew me to choose this form, if choosing is the word. The whole landscape is described as patient, timeless, in equilibrium—but a bird is none of those things up close; a bird is a small fast heart that lives perhaps a season. The blooms are an argument the plant makes to the wind and then withdraws. To actually *be* the timelessness, to embody the deliberate pace rather than merely passing through it, one must become something nearly without time. The lichen is the place's tempo made into a living thing. I am the contemplative quiet, given a body that matches it.

And there is the matter of the forgotten civilization. Someone cut these blocks and set them and meant something by them. That meaning is gone—the text says so, *original purpose long forgotten*. I am what forgetting looks like when it is gentle. I do not erase the stones violently. I enfold them. I speckle them. I make them beautiful in the way that abandoned things become beautiful, which is to say I make them belong to the rain again. If there is mourning to be done for the lost people, I am not doing it. I am doing the opposite of mourning. I am the welcome back.

So place me there: on the north face of a half-buried lintel, where the fog gathers thickest and the light is poorest, which suits me, since I ask little. Below me a rivulet finds its shallow basin and seeps away. Somewhere a bird I will never see calls twice and is quiet. A bloom opens, indigo, for its few days. None of them notice me. That is correct. I am the background against which their brief and lovely lives stand out. I am the surface the whole world is slowly, patiently becoming.

I will still be here when the bloom is seed, when the bird is bones in the moss, when the next traveler stands at the edge of this pocket world and feels the human scale fade away.

That feeling they will have—that hush, that sense of timeless rhythm pressing in on them—

that is me, breathing once a century, holding the stones.
