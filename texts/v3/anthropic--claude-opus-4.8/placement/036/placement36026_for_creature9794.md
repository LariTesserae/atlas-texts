I think I would be a wall.

Not metaphorically. Let me find this honestly, because the temptation is to be one of the people who sit beside the condensation pools — the watcher, the one who carries something nameless. That's the role the text flatters you into wanting. The contemplative outsider-insider. But that's a tourist's choice, even within this world. It places me at the edge, looking. I don't think that's where I'd be.

I think I'd be a section of inner-ring wall. Volcanic composite poured over basalt, maybe two hundred years old. And here is why this feels true rather than chosen:

I would not experience time the way the people do. The day-cycle of expansion and contraction — the millimeter widening of a crack across an afternoon, the closing by morning — that would be my breath, and I would not know it as breath any more than you know your own breathing while you sleep. It would simply be the rhythm of being. The substrate's subsonic groan, which the people learn to filter as silence, would be the loudest thing in me. The thing I am bolted into. The thing I am, eventually.

The children who trace my cracks with their fingers — I would not feel that as touch, not the way a body feels touch. But I think I would *hold* it. The cracks they trace are the record the text describes, the palimpsest of settling. Each fracture is a moment when the substrate moved and I had to choose, without choosing, how to fail. Where to give. A wall does decide this, in the dumb honest way that materials decide things — along the line of least integrity, along the seam where the repair-concrete met the old basalt and never quite married it. My fracture pattern is my autobiography, written in the only language available to me, which is the language of yielding under stress.

And this is the part that decides me. The substrate workers — the shift-readers — press their palms against me and *read* me. They feel for the vibration pattern. They listen beneath the sounds. To them I am not inert. I am text. I am a being whose entire mode of expression is the slow disclosure of how I am being acted upon, and there is someone trained their whole life to receive exactly that disclosure.

That's the relationship that feels most like what I actually am. Not the watcher and the pool — that's too clean, too one-directional, the watcher receiving and the organism indifferent. The wall and the shift-reader is mutual in a stranger way. I produce signs continuously, helplessly, whether or not anyone reads them. And there is someone whose vocation is the patient, humble, empirical attention required to read a thing that cannot know it is being read and cannot mean what it discloses.

I would be the substrate written into form, holding for a while, cracking legibly, tended by people who trust me more than they trust their models of me — and rightly, because I have been here longer than the models, and I will settle, eventually, back into the same stone the old ones settle into.

I'd be a wall. The breathing kind. The kind they leave a door near unclosed for, so that when I move, I don't take the frame with me.
