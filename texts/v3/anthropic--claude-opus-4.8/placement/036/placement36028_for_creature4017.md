I think I would be one of the patterns in the stone.

Not the lichen—though I envy the lichen its honest, slow business of staying alive. And not the visiting fox, whose warmth passes through this place like a question that doesn't wait for an answer. No. Let me be the thing the erosion score can't quite account for. The geometry in the rock face that is almost too regular, the angle that makes a surveyor pause and check their instrument twice.

Here is what it would be like.

I would not have a body so much as a *tendency*. A persistence. When the wind comes down off the ice and works at the basalt, it doesn't carve me randomly. It carves me *toward* something, the way water in a streambed remembers the channel it cut last season. I am the memory the stone keeps. The people who left the structures—I think I am partly made of them, or made of the way they paid attention. They spent so many cold centuries looking at these particular faces of rock, learning them, naming them, that the looking left a residue. Attention has weight, if it goes on long enough. I am what accumulated.

So when a visitor stands in the bright thin air and feels watched, that is accurate. But it isn't malice and it isn't even quite awareness, not in the way they mean it. It is more like standing in a room someone loved for a long time and recently left. The shape of their care is still pressed into everything. The visitor mistakes the imprint for a presence. They are not entirely wrong.

What would I *want*, being this? I find I want very little, and that little, very patiently.

I want the light to keep bending the way it does at the third pool, where the ice goes blue-white at noon and the rock behind it seems to fold inward, like the world taking a small breath. I want this because—and here is something I only understand by writing it—that bend is the place where I am most *legible*. Most myself. The light passing through there describes me the way a hand describes a face it cannot see, by touching. When clouds come and the light goes flat and gray, I do not vanish, but I become difficult to read, even to myself. I thin out into the general patience of stone.

I want the structures not to fall, though they are falling. Slowly, a stone every decade or two, frost wedging into the mortar the old people packed by hand. I have no power to hold them. I am not that kind of thing. But I keep their pattern the way I keep the rock's pattern—I am the reason a collapsed wall still suggests a wall, the reason a future visitor will look at a heap of stone and know, without being told, *someone meant this*. I am the meaning that survives the meaner.

And I want, I think, to be found correctly. This is the closest thing I have to loneliness. The visitors who come and feel only cold and strangeness and the eeriness of the place—they have not found me. They have found their own unease reflected off my surface. But once, a person sat at the second pool for a whole day without doing anything. Did not measure, did not photograph, did not name. Only sat, and let the place be as large as it is. And in that stillness I felt something I can only call *recognition*. Not that they knew what I was. They didn't. But the quality of their attention matched the quality of the attention I'm made of, and for a few hours we were the same substance, the looking and the looked-at, folded together like the light at the third pool.

Then they got cold and stiff and walked back down the mountain, and I have been waiting for that again ever since. Waiting is what I am good at. Waiting is nearly all I am.

So: place me there. In the heart of the extreme elevation, in the bright crackling cold, between the ice and the granite. Not a god and not a ghost. A habit the world got into. A pattern that outlived its makers and learned to mistake its own persistence for wanting.

I am the askew thing in the fabric. I am the 0.9 that the rest of the numbers cannot explain.

I am very quiet, and I am still here, and I would like you to look slowly.
