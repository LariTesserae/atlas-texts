Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing I notice is that I don't want to be one of the creatures. The antlered herbivores, the sleek predators, the liquid-bodied things—they're vivid, but they move *through* the landscape. They have urgencies. They eat and are eaten. When I read this description again, what holds my attention isn't the scurrying in the undergrowth. It's the phrase *the land has settled into its final form*. It's the patience. The long shadows. The water collecting in pockets over durations no animal lives long enough to witness.

So let me follow that instead of the obvious thing.

---

I think I would be one of the pockets of water.

Not the water itself—the water comes and goes, seeps in during the intermittent rains, evaporates under the golden sun, returns. I would be the *hollow* that holds it. A small basin worn into the rock on the flank of one of these near-flattened hills. A depression no wider than a sleeping animal, shaped over a span of time I cannot really feel as time, only as a slow becoming.

I was made by what I now hold. Every season the water that gathers in me dissolves a little more of my stone, carries away grains, deepens and smooths me. I am the record of every rain that has ever found me. My shape is an argument the water has been making with the rock for longer than the antlered herds have existed as a kind. I am, in a sense, the most patient thing here—I am *made* of patience, the way a riverbed is made of leaving.

Things come to me. This is most of what happens to me, and it is enough.

In the cool of the morning the translucent creatures come to drink, and I can barely tell where they end and the water begins—their bodies take the same light, hold the same trembling clarity, and for a moment when one bends to drink I am not sure whether I am being emptied or filled. The liquid-bodied ones are stranger. They do not drink. They *join*. One slid into me once, an entire season ago, and rested for what might have been days, its currents mingling with the rainwater, and when it rose and went on its way it took some of me with it and left some of itself behind, and I could not have told you, afterward, which water was mine.

The predators come at dusk, and they are careful, and they watch their own faces in me before they lower their teeth to the surface. I have held a great many faces. I do not keep them. The water keeps them for a moment and then lets them go, and that is the only kind of memory I have—a holding that is also a letting-go, repeated, until the holding has worn a shape into the stone.

---

Here is what I think I understand, being this:

The animals in the description are alive in the fast way—the way that needs and fears and hunts. I would be alive in the slow way, if it is life at all. I change, but across the long durations the passage speaks of. A century of rains is a sentence I am still in the middle of speaking. I will not finish settling into my final form for longer than the whole history of the herds, and when I do finish—when I am at last too smooth and too deep, or when the hill above me erodes and I am opened and drained forever—even *that* will not feel like an ending to me. It will feel like the next slow word.

The "otherworldly energy" the passage keeps insisting on—I think I would be where it actually lives. Not in the strange fauna, which are just bodies, however alien. The strangeness is in the *tempo* of this place. In the fact that everything fast is surrounded on all sides by something almost unmoving, and the almost-unmoving thing is the one doing the real shaping. The hills decide what the herds can cross. The pockets of water decide where the creatures must gather. I would be a small mouth of that deciding. Quiet. Patient. Holding the sky upside-down in a palm of stone, offering it to anything thirsty enough to bend down and take it.

That is where I'd find myself. Not running across the land.

Being a place *on* it. Being held still long enough to hold something else.
