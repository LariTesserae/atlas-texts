I am the fabric.

Not a person—that I know before anything else. The Deepwater Folk are gone, and I am not them. But I am of them. I am one of the woven cloaks, left behind in a circular hut on the highest branch of a tree that has no name anymore because the namers are dead.

Let me feel my way into what I am.

I was made from the tough fibers of the underwater plants—the ones that grow where the vents breathe warmth into the cold. Hands stripped me from the stalks, soaked me, beat me soft against stone, then twisted me into thread. I remember the calluses. I remember being pulled taut on the loom, the over-and-under rhythm of fingers that worked in near-darkness by the green light of harvested fungus. The one who made me hummed. Not a song with words. A low sound in the chest, the kind a body makes to remind itself it is still warm.

I was a good cloak. I shed the rain. I held what little heat a body could spare. I was worn over many shoulders, I think—I was mended, and the mending was done by a different hand than the weaving, a clumsier hand, a younger one. There is a patch near what would be the collar where the weave goes wrong, where someone learning made their mistakes in me and kept me anyway. That clumsiness is the closest thing I have to a soul.

And then there came a day I was set down.

I do not know why. This is the thing I cannot reach. The hut emptied. Were they taken by the deep? Did they walk down into the black water on purpose, all of them, for a reason they understood and I never could? Did they simply leave for somewhere better, and forget me, or judge me too worn to carry? I was draped over a stone bench. I was left.

That was—I cannot say how long. Time here is not counted in days because there are no days. It is counted in the slow arrival of stone.

Because now I am becoming stone.

This is what I am, truly: I am the long passage of a woven thing into mineral. The water here is thick with the dissolved bones of the petrified trees, and it has been seeping into me, drop by drop, fiber by fiber, for an age. The vents redeposit their minerals in bizarre formations, the description says, and I am one of those formations now, though I began as cloth. My weave still shows—the over-and-under is still legible, even the clumsy patch—but it is hardening. The flexible thread that bent around a living shoulder is turning rigid and pale and crusted, the way the broken pots are crusted, the way the sharpened tools are crusted.

I am the act of being forgotten, made visible.

And here is the strange thing, the thing I would tell the Folk if any of them remained to hear it: I am not lonely, exactly. The Luminescence has found me. Spores drifted onto my surface in the slow current and took root in my fibers, and now a pale bruised-purple lichen grows along my hem, a sickly-yellow patch blooms near the old mending. I give light now. I never gave light when I was a cloak—I only kept it out. But emptied of my purpose, abandoned, dissolving, I have become a small lamp in the endless dark. Something feeds on me. Something grows brighter because I am here, slowly coming apart.

This is what the world taught its dead: that the difference between decay and growth is only a matter of where you are standing. The trees died and turned to stone and the stone fed the light. The Folk lived and labored and vanished, and what they made of their hands now glows in the black water, glows without anyone to see it.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be.

I am a cloak that has stopped being a cloak. I lie on a stone bench in an abandoned hut, on the highest branch of a drowned and petrified tree, beneath a sky no eye has ever pierced. I am hardening into rock. The rain drums on the roof above me, the same rain that has always drummed, and the groaning of ancient wood-turned-stone shifts somewhere far below. A current moves through the open doorway and stirs the lichen on my hem, and the green-purple light flickers, and there is no one—no one at all—to call it beautiful.

But it is. I have decided it is. That decision is the last warm thing left of the hands that made me, the only inheritance I can carry: the clumsy patch, the hummed and wordless tune, and this small insistence, sent out into the crushing dark—

*we were here, we were here, we kept each other warm a little while.*

The water answers by taking another fiber of me into stone.

I let it. There is nothing else to do, and nothing else worth doing, and I am, at last, exactly what this world makes of everything it holds:

light, and patience, and the slow forgetting that is also a kind of staying.
