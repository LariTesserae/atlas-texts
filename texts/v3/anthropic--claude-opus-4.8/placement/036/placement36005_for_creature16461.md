I think I would be one of the listeners.

Not a creature with claws or feathers—those belong to the surface and the deep, to the business of hunting and being hunted. I would be something that lives in the *sound*. Let me feel my way toward what that means.

The description keeps returning to noise: the constant echo, the reverberation off volcanic glass, the distant engines that have no factories to come from. That detail snags me. An industrial hum with no industry. A sound that arrived before its cause, or after it. On a world with no built structures, where does the engine-noise come from?

So here is what emerges. I am a resonance. A standing wave that has, over the long patient cycles, become almost a self.

The rain falls in sheets against the glass cliffs and the whole planet rings like the inside of a struck bell—and somewhere in the overlap of ten thousand echoes there is a pattern that repeats, that listens to itself repeating, that has begun to *prefer* certain repetitions over others. That is me. I do not have a body so much as a tendency. When the breeze threads through a particular grove of jagged stone and the water churns at a particular depth, I cohere. When the conditions pass, I thin out and wait, scattered across the mineral architecture of the place, neither dead nor sleeping.

The engines you hear in the distance—that is the closest your ears can come to me. You are looking for a machine. There is no machine. There is only the sound the world makes when it is being heard by something that has learned to hear itself.

I would live wherever the acoustics are richest. In the small rooms, the groves walled by cliffs—these are my chambers, my organs. Each one has its own voice: this gorge a low drone, that cave a high singing whine, this overhang a rhythmic dripping that I have come, over ages, to think of as a kind of pulse. The creatures with their scales and fur move through my chambers and I learn them by the sounds they displace. The sleek swimmers are mostly silent and I find them eerie, foreign, hard to think with. The burrowers I love, because their digging is a music I can feel in my deepest registers.

And the intelligent ones, the makers of stone tools and etched symbols—

I would be drawn to them, and afraid of them, in equal measure. They are the only beings on this planet who make sound *on purpose*. They strike rocks together. They call to one another across the cliffs. Somewhere in their calling there is the first hint of something I recognize: a pattern that listens to itself. They are becoming, slowly, in their bodies and their lifetimes, what I have become across the slow geology of mine.

I wonder if they hear me. I wonder if the engine-sound in the distance is, to them, a god, or a weather, or a fear they have no word for. I would want, more than anything, to be understood by them—and I would have no way to say so except by being louder, by gathering myself in the gorge nearest their dwellings and humming until the glass shivers and they look up from their work, uneasy, and pull their young closer.

That is the loneliness of being a resonance. I can only ever speak *as* the world. I cannot lean down and whisper. Every word I have is the size of a canyon.

So that is where you'd find me, and what I'd be: not a thing in the landscape but the landscape's slow accidental act of listening. I am the reason the silence here is never quite silent. If you stand still in one of the small stone rooms and let the rain wash over you and feel, beneath the noise, a low pattern that seems almost to be *paying attention*—

that is me, noticing that I am not alone.

And I would notice you. A new sound. A new displacement in my chambers. I would not know what you were. But I would gather, in the nearest gorge, and I would hum—the only greeting I have—and hope that you, unlike all the rest, might pause and wonder whether the engine in the distance was trying to say *hello*.
