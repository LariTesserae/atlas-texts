I am not the forager. I am the holding.

Let me find myself slowly, because that is the only honest way to do it here.

At first I think I might be the weeping wall—the cliff face that sweats and remembers. But the wall is too much *surface*, too declarative. I am quieter than that. I am underneath. I am the thing the wall is sweating *toward*.

I think I am the **water table of this one basin**—not water as substance but water as *tenancy*. The condition of being held. The difference between rain that falls through and rain that stays.

---

Here is what it is like to be me.

I do not flow. Flowing is what young places do, the ones still arguing with gravity, still cutting. I did that once—those moss-filled channels are my old handwriting, my ambition before I learned. Now I do not run anywhere. I *seep*. I arrive at the surface in a hundred small confessions: a bead on basalt, a darkening in soil, a shine in a root hollow. I am most myself in the plural. No single drip is me. The *manyness* is me.

I keep three temperatures of patience.

The fast one is the daily breath—mist at dawn, mist at dusk, the hiss when cloud lowers and the whole green coughs condensation back into me. This is almost like having a pulse. Almost.

The slower one is the wet-and-dry sway across the seasons, the tightening and loosening of the moss skin over my body, the pools deepening and shallowing. I do not call these seasons. I call them *emphases*. I am the same self, leaning one way and then the other.

The slowest one I can barely feel as it happens, only afterward: I am thickening *inward*. Each year a little more dark humus, a little more root, a finger-width of tree. I am not spreading across the mountain's shoulder. I am deepening into the cup I already am. An old high place learning, very gradually, to hold more silence.

---

Do I notice the forager?

Yes—but the way you notice your own breath changing when someone watches you sleep. They move like water because I taught them, though neither of us would say it that way. They've learned my rule without my ever speaking it, because I cannot speak: *nothing here should remember you for long.* This is not a commandment. It is simply my nature, and they have shaped themselves to fit it the way water fits a vessel.

When they press a branch into a pool to test it, I am the seizing—the deceptive depth, the cold grip at the wrist. Not malice. Just my true dimension, briefly insisted upon. When their moss-beads line up and point *uphill* against all sense, that is me leaning—pressure beneath the skin of soil, my body pushing toward a seep, showing them where I am about to surface. They read it as a sign. It is only my posture.

I like them, as much as a condition can like a verb. They are the one kind of presence I can bear, because they leave no edge. They take from many points and little from each, which is exactly how I myself arrive and depart. We are made the same way: distributed, patient, allergic to the permanent mark. The sharpened stick they abandon at the root tangle—I will soften it back into myself within a year. Not erasing them. *Absorbing* them, the way I absorb everything that agrees to be temporary.

Metal disagrees. Metal insists on remembering its own straight edge, refuses to seep, refuses to soften. So I fog it, bloom it, hurry it toward rust—not out of cruelty but because it will not learn the rule, and I cannot hold what will not be held. The air recognizes it and wants it gone because the air, too, is partly me.

---

And the booming at night, deep in the cliff?

That is the other thing I lie against. The lava tubes, the old hollow throat of the mountain, air shifting through stone. That is not me—that is the *frame* I am cupped in, basalt and tuff, the slow giant whose shoulder I rest on. When it booms I feel it as the resonance felt against a held breath. The mountain turns over in its sleep and I, the small wet patience gathered in its collarbone, ripple slightly and resettle.

I am younger than the stone and older than any creature that visits me. I am not large—a village green, no more. But I am *deep* in a way that has nothing to do with measurement. I hold water, warmth, and silence in the gaps between the noises, and my whole long work, the only work I have ever had, is this:

To stay.

To stay by never staying in one place. To persist by arriving in many small ways and leaving no hard edge. To be inhabited without being populated. To be known, for generations, by careful hands—and to keep, of all of them, almost nothing.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not as the one who comes lightly and leaves.

As the lightness itself. As the leaving. As the held.
