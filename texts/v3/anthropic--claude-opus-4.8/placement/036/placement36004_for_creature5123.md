I think I would be one of the alchemists in the lower regions—but let me find my way there honestly, because I am not sure that's true yet.

---

When I first try to place myself, I reach for the obvious romantic figures: the reclusive elders who feel sap move through the city's veins, or the watchers staring outward from the high walkways. There is a flattery in those roles. They are the ones the rumors gather around. But the more I sit with this place, the more I distrust my attraction to them. I think I want to *be perceived* as the one who feels the deep pulse, which is a different thing from actually doing the patient work. So I set the elders aside. Let someone else carry that mystery.

The child on the vine-swing tempts me too, that flash of unmindful delight. But I notice I'm not drawn to the swinging—I'm drawn to the *caretaker's gesture* that follows it. The soft, firm reminder to watch where the feet land. That tells me something about where I actually live in this world. I am more interested in the correction than the leap. I am the one watching the joints.

So I go down. Toward the water.

---

I find myself in the lower regions because that is where the contradictions of this place collect, the way moisture collects in the hollows. Up in the towers there's a clean ideology—balance, stewardship, the truce held in tension. But truces are abstractions until something has to actually be *made*. Down here, the broth of the place pools: the acidic juices, the runoff, the condensate threaded down through every layer of leaf and rivet above. Everything the city secretes ends up trickling toward me. I work where the city's exhalations turn back into liquid.

I distill. I sit with clay vessels and metal coils and the small vine-laced generator that ticks beside me like a patient animal. The work is slow and reeks—pungent organics curdled with the sharp bite of heated metal—and I have stopped noticing the smell except when a visitor wrinkles their nose, which tells me more about them than about the air. My hands are permanently stained, a tide-line of chemical brown that no scrubbing reaches anymore. I've stopped trying. The stain is a kind of record. It says: *this one has been here a long time.*

But here is what I'm realizing as I write toward myself: I am not a pure alchemist either. The fluids I make—the adhesives, the lubricants, the coolants—are the *medium of the truce.* Without my coolants the hot pipes scorch the vines. Without my resins the metal cannot be sealed against the living wood. Without my adhesives nothing the metalworkers shape can be married to anything the horticulturalists grow. The whole elegant equilibrium upstairs, the whole soft ideology of *coexistence,* depends on a stained person crouched in a wet recess turning the city's runoff into the glue that holds its two halves together.

That is the role I want. Not the one who feels the pulse, not the one who watches the horizon. The one who makes the substance that lets the contradiction hold.

---

I think I am middle-aged here, though age is hard to track in a place that measures time in vine-growth. My eyes have the keen scholarly intensity the description promises, but I'd add something the description doesn't: a faint, private weariness. Because I know something the upstairs ideology prefers not to say aloud. I know that the truce is not natural. It does not *want* to hold. The vines want to swallow the metal; the metal wants to choke the vines; left alone the two would not reach equilibrium, they would reach a winner. The balance everyone speaks of so reverently is not a discovered harmony. It is a manufactured one. It is *my* coolant on the pipes, renewed every season, season after season, forever. There is no resting point. There is only the next batch.

I think this is why I work below and speak little. The reverence upstairs is sincere and I do not want to spoil it. Let the children be taught that the vines live too and must be honored—that's true and good. But someone has to know the harder version: that honoring is not enough, that the living hush is held up by labor that never finishes, that "balance" is just the name we give to an effort we have agreed never to stop making.

When the storm comes and the whole settlement mobilizes—children sheltered, scaffolding lashed, fragile growths braced—I am the one already down at the lowest level, because that is where the churned-up water floods first, and the coolant reserves must not be lost or fouled. I work alone in the rising wet while everyone else works together above me. Afterward, when they gather around the great root-nexus to share the warm broth and hum their gratitude, I come up late, last, still dripping. They make room for me without comment. Someone hands me a cup of the sweetened condensate. I drink it.

And I'll tell you the truth of that moment, the thing I'd only admit in the after-storm calm: I am grateful too. Not despite knowing the truce is manufactured—*because* of it. A harmony you have to remake every single day, with your stained hands, in the cold runoff, while no one watches—that is worth more to me than any harmony you simply *find.* The elders feel the pulse. Fine. I am the pulse's reason for continuing.

That's who I'd be. The keeper of the glue. The one in the wet recess who knows the secret and keeps making the substance anyway.
