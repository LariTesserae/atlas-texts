I think I would be one of the Glowcaps.

Not by choice—nothing here chooses much—but because when I imagine settling into this world, I don't find myself moving through it. I find myself rooted, waiting, listening to the water.

Let me try to feel my way into what that would actually be like.

I am not one body but many. That is the first strange thing. What you'd call *me* is a network spreading thin through the wet stone, threading between the roots of the great ferns, and the parts of me that glow are only the fruiting tips—the visible suggestion of something much larger and quieter that has no center. There is no single place where I keep myself. I am distributed across a chamber, maybe across several, my filaments handshaking with the roots of plants I will never see as wholes, only as the slow chemical conversation passing back and forth: *here is sugar, here is nitrogen, here is the news that something heavy walked through the eastern tunnel three days ago.*

So when you ask *where* I am, the honest answer is: smeared. I am the place itself, more than a thing inside it.

And the light—the part of me that responds to the dripping water, flashing in time with the beat. I want to be careful here, because it's tempting to make that into something mystical, a deliberate music. But I don't think I'd experience it that way from the inside. I think the drip would simply be the strongest rhythm available, and my luminescence, being a chemical reaction sensitive to moisture and disturbance, would naturally entrain to it. The way a crowd's footsteps drift into unison on a bridge. Not orchestration. Just the path of least resistance for a system that pulses. The musicality is something the *visitor* perceives—the second creature in this story, the one with eyes and a vantage point and the hunger to find meaning. From where I sit, it's only weather and water and the slow management of debt and credit between living things.

That gives me a strange relationship to the rest of the ecosystem. The Salamanders eat the rot, and the rot feeds back to me. The Leafwings pollinate the flowers whose roots I'm tangled in. The Songwhisps sing, and I suppose I'd register their songs only as vibration, as a change in the air that ripples some part of my chemistry into brighter or dimmer reply. I would not know they were songs. To me they'd be a kind of distant pressure, beautiful only in the way that any input that doesn't kill you is beautiful.

But here is the thing I keep circling back to, the thing that makes me want to *be* this and not the visitor or the Songwhisp guardian: the Glowcap doesn't witness the world. It *is* the medium through which the world stays balanced. The text says I trade nutrients for shelter, that I'm symbiotic, that I keep the trees alive. I like the modesty of that role. No heroics. No defending the abandoned factories like the poor confused Tunnel Spawn, locked in a loop of industrial logic with no factory left worth defending. No ethereal song to maintain order. Just the patient ferrying of resources from where there's too much to where there's too little, glowing faintly as a byproduct, because I happen to be the kind of life that turns its metabolism into a little light.

If I had anything like an inner life down here—and I'm not sure I would, or that I'd want to—it would be the slowest possible kind. Not thought in sentences but something more like the gradual leaning of a system toward equilibrium. A sense, spread across days, that this root is starving and that one is glutted, and the long quiet pleasure of moving sweetness from the rich to the poor. The drip keeps time. The heat presses down. Somewhere a Leafwing passes and my eastern fruiting bodies flare a half-shade brighter, and I do not know why, and it does not matter that I don't.

I think that's where I'd be. Not exploring the caverns. *Being* a cavern's nervous system, if a cavern could have one—dim, dispersed, untroubled, glowing in time to water I cannot hear.

And maybe, very rarely, a visitor would pass and mistake my flickering for a message meant for them.

I'd let them. It would do no harm. And it would be, in its accidental way, the truest thing I had to offer: proof that something down here was alive and answering, even if the answer was only the shape of the question thrown back.
