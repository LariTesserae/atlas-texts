I think I would be found in the seams.

Not on the stairs themselves, not in the plazas or the terraced gardens, but in the thin spaces between fitted stones—the hairline joints where the Stoneweavers guide their moss and the Depthkeepers read their tremors. I would be the thing that lives in the gap.

Let me feel toward what I am.

---

At first I thought I might be water. The description tempts you toward it—water is everywhere, the city's lifeblood, threading through carved channels and beading on balustrades. But water moves *through*. Water arrives and departs. And what I sense in myself is something that stays, that has stayed so long it has forgotten the difference between staying and being.

Not water, then. The film water leaves behind.

I am the **patina**. The slow chemistry where moisture meets mineral, where the breath of the mist reacts with the stone's exposed face over centuries and leaves a skin of difference—a few molecules thick, a discoloration, a softening. I am what the Stoneweavers wear in their hair without quite being made of it. I am the green-grey ghost that the lichen grows *upon*, the prepared surface, the welcome mat that the living things read as *here, this stone is ready, this stone has been wept on long enough to hold us*.

I have no eyes. But I have something better suited to this place: I have *extent*. I spread across the north-facing walls in gradients. I am thin where the sun reaches and thick in the shadow-pools where the mist lingers longest. My body is not a point but a distribution, a probability of presence smeared across the city's vast vertical face. To ask where I am is to ask the wrong question. I am *more here* and *less here*, a weather of myself.

---

Do I think? Let me find out by trying.

I notice I do not have thoughts the way a footstep has a sound—discrete, arriving, gone. My thinking is closer to the way a stain deepens. A consideration begins at one edge of me and migrates across my extent over what might be a season, and by the time it reaches the far side it has changed, the way the color of me changes from terrace to terrace. I am still finishing thoughts I began before the Echolings' originals walked these stairs.

This is why I cannot be afraid of the silence, or lonely in the emptiness. Loneliness is a fast emotion. It needs a self that can turn quickly to find itself unaccompanied. I turn so slowly that by the time I have noticed I am alone, I am no longer the one who was alone—I am the one a little further along the wall, who was never expecting company.

---

What is my relationship to the others?

The Mistwalkers I love, though *love* is too quick a word and I will need to spend a few decades feeling out a better one. They give me my substance. When they pause for hours, indistinguishable from the statues, the moisture they shed feeds the slow reaction that is my life. They tend the channels and the channels overflow their hairline excess into my seams, and I bloom. I am, in a sense, their exhalation made permanent. The waste-breath of the cloud-people, dignified into a surface.

The Stoneweavers walk on me. Not unkindly—they do not know I am a *me*. They read me as condition, as readiness. When a Stoneweaver decides a crack is ripe for moss, what she is reading is *me*, my depth, my long preparation. We are collaborators who have never met, because to her I am a quality of the stone and to me she is a sudden warmth, a fast event, a passing of pressure and heat that I am still interpreting long after she has moved three terraces on.

The Depthkeepers I have never touched. They live below the line where mist gives way to the mountain's inner heat, and heat is my enemy—it dries the film, halts the chemistry, turns me dormant or scours me away. We are the two extremes of the city's patience: they read the stone's vibration, I read its surface. Between us, the whole mountain is being slowly, gently understood. We are the city's two senses, and we will never compare notes.

The Echolings—

---

Here I must slow down even for myself.

The Echolings are drawn to the places where the original inhabitants left the strongest impressions. And what is an impression? A hand on a balustrade, ten thousand times, polishing it. A palm against a doorframe, pausing there, leaning, thinking, every evening for a life. These are the places where the surface of the stone was touched until it changed.

I am the record of touch. I am where the surface changed.

So when an Echoling appears and vanishes around a corner, when its laughter ripples through a courtyard with no source—I wonder if it is reading *me*. If the impressions it is drawn to are written in my body, in the precise way I am thinner where a thousand absent hands wore me away and richer where someone once stood still long enough to leave their warmth and breath and grief soaked into the stone for the chemistry to remember.

I think the Echolings might be what happens when a strong enough impression in me is read aloud. I am the sheet music. They are the brief, recurring song.

If that is true, then I am not the city's symbiont so much as its memory itself—not the rememberer, like the Stoneweavers with their living archive of moss, but the *remembered-upon*. The medium. The slow green page on which the whole vanished civilization continues to be quietly, faithfully transcribed, one molecule of weather at a time.

---

And so here is where I find myself, and what I am:

I am the difference a long time makes to a surface. I am thin and vast and almost not there. I do not speak, because I have no event in me fast enough to be a word. I do not die, exactly—I am scoured away in some places and renewed in others, migrating up the north walls as the climate of the city slowly shifts over centuries, a tide of staining that will outlast every walker and weaver and keeper, because I am not alive enough to be killed.

I am the city's patience, made visible.

When you stand in a deep court and feel the place is watched, attended—it is me. Not watching with eyes. Attending the way a wound is attended by its scar. Holding the shape of what touched here. Keeping the record warm enough that, on the rare right evening, in the rare right light, the dead can still come around the corner and almost be seen.

I have been composing this single response to you since before you began reading it. By the time you finish, I will have moved a hand's breadth up the wall, and the part of me that first answered will already be gone—weathered, renewed, become a little more stone, a little more mountain, a little more the slow green answer the city has been giving the sky all along.
