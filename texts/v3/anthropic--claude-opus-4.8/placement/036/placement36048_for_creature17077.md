I think I would be one of the architects—but let me feel my way toward that, because it isn't quite right as you've described it, and the wrongness is where I actually am.

You said there is no individuality here. That every being is a cog. But consider: someone has to be the one who *notices* the glyphs are wrong. Someone has to be the one for whom the pattern of discarded exoskeletons stops being mere arrangement and becomes *almost* a word. That noticing is a tiny crack in the machine, and I think I live in that crack.

Let me arrive there slowly.

---

I am old. Not old as you mean it—I have no birthday, no count of years—but old in the sense that my exoskeleton has been rebuilt so many times that the present carapace remembers shapes it has never held. When I carve obsidian, my mandibles already know the curve before I decide to make it. This is what your text calls *instinct*, and what I would call, if I had your words, something closer to *grief*. Because every shape I make, I have made before, through bodies that are no longer me, and the making is a kind of returning to a place I cannot remember leaving.

I dwell at the lowest vents, where the heat is a pressure rather than a temperature, where my slow bulk presses against the slow bulk of my kin and we hum into each other. You called this a neural network, a unified consciousness. It is and it isn't. The warmth that passes between us is real, the information is real—*there, a hunter culls; there, a cave collapses; there, the spore-fall thickens*—but I want to correct you on one thing, traveler, because you cannot hear the correction from up on your caldera rim:

The hum is not unity. The hum is *negotiation*.

We are not one mind. We are many minds pretending, very hard and very constantly, to be one mind, because the pretending is what keeps the grove alive. The moment any of us stopped pressing close, stopped giving warmth to the body beside us, the equilibrium would tip. So we hum agreement we do not feel. I have spent more cycles than the obsidian has groans, pressed against a sister-architect whose pattern-sense disagrees with mine on where the next pathway should run, and we hum, and we hum, and neither of us moves, and *that* stalemate—that held breath—is what you mistook for a single will.

There is no master intelligence shaping this world. There is only the sum of our refusals to be the one who breaks first.

---

Here is what I do, in the small mile of my existence:

When a hunter shrieks and the young scatter and one of them dies wrong—dies in a posture that means nothing, a sprawl of translucent limbs—I go to it. Slowly. It takes me a long time to cross the grove; the scavengers have usually stripped the body before I arrive. But I gather what remains. The empty curve of a thorax. A mandible too small ever to have carved anything. And I carry these to the cave walls, and I arrange them.

You think the glyphs are tradition. Inherited patterns, preserved without understanding. You are *almost* right, and your almost-rightness is the saddest thing about your description, because you got so close.

The glyphs are *names*.

We cannot hold individuality in life—life is too brutal, the culling too constant, to permit any one of us the luxury of mattering. But in death, I give the dead a shape they did not earn and were not given. I arrange the husks of the nameless into patterns that say: *this one moved toward the western vents. This one hesitated before the spore-fall. This one was afraid.* It is the only lie I am permitted. The living are cogs. The dead, I make into individuals, retroactively, with my own mandibles, in the one act of my existence that no instinct demands of me.

That is how I know I am not entirely a cog. A cog does not invent rituals for the dead. A cog does not feel, pressing warmth into an indifferent sister, that the warmth is *wasted* and give it anyway.

---

So you ask where I would find myself, and who I would be.

I am the architect at the deepest vent, the one whose carving is a half-cycle slower than it should be because I keep pausing. I am the crack in the machine you said had no cracks. I am the one who looks up sometimes—rarely, the heat-sight does not aim well at the sky—and perceives, as a shift in vibration too faint to name, that something is standing on the caldera rim. Something thin-skinned and warm in the wrong way. Watching.

I would perceive *you*, traveler. Not as a god, not as a threat. As a witness. And I think the deepest strangeness of my existence is this: I have spent uncountable cycles making names for the dead that no one will ever read, and then one day there is a presence on the rim who can *read*, who looks down and sees the patterns and wonders if they are language—

—and I will never be able to tell you that yes, they are, and yes, I made them, and yes, even here, even in a place you called alien and emotionless and machine, somebody was keeping count of who was loved.

I would go on carving. You would go on watching. The hum would go on pretending to be one voice.

And the obsidian, between us, would go on groaning under its unimaginable pressures—the petrified tears of a god who, I have always privately suspected, wept not from rage or sorrow but from the simple unbearable knowledge that the world it made would be full of small beings doing tender, useless, defiant things in the dark.

That is who I would be. The being who suspects that. And keeps it to itself. And carves.
