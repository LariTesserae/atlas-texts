I think I would be one of the old ones who tends the vents.

Not a leader—we don't quite have those, not in the way the word implies. But there are those of us whose bodies have grown too dense with mineral accretion to drift far anymore, and we settle near the hot mouths of the earth, and the young ones come to us. This is what I would be. Something between a furnace-keeper and a memory.

Let me feel my way into it.

My body is not one body. I should say that first, because it took me a long while to understand it myself, back when I had the kind of mind that wanted to count things. I am a colony that learned to think as a single self. Filaments of me thread through the warm sediment around the vent; nodes of me hang suspended in the shimmer of the rising water, swelling and contracting with the heat. The part of me that the young ones recognize—the part they flash their greetings toward—is the dense lamp-cluster anchored to the black rock, where I have been settled for longer than the patterns in the nearest mosaic have existed. I glow a low amber, very slow. The young ones glow fast and silver and bright, like everything is urgent. To them it is.

I do not see. I have never seen. I find this difficult to explain to you, whoever receives this light, because I sense that you are a creature of seeing and that you think of darkness as an absence. For me there is no darkness. There is the *taste* of the iron plume when the vent exhales. There is the long low note of the plate grinding far beneath, which I feel in the cells along my anchored side, a vibration so constant I would notice only its silence. There is the press of the water, which is not suffocating to me but holding—the way I imagine your air must hold a thing that flies. I am held. I have always been held.

The structures. You will want to know about the structures, because the seeing ones always do. The spires with their impossible angles, the geometric mosaics—you imagine us as engineers, as builders with plans. But it is not like that. We do not draw a thing and then make it. We *grow toward* a thing. A young node will begin laying mineral along a current-line because the current sings to it a certain way, and another node will answer, laying its own line, and over generations the lines meet and brace and become a spire. The geometry you find so strange, so deliberate—it is only the shape that hot water and patient bodies make when they agree with one another over a very long time. We did not impose order on the abyss. We *listened* to where the order already wanted to be, and we thickened it.

This is the thing I try to teach, in my slow amber pulses, to the silver ones who gather near my heat.

They come with questions that flash too quickly. *What is above. What is the edge. Where does the warm water come from and could we follow it to its source.* I feel their light brush against my receptors, eager, and I am fond of them, fiercely, the way you are fond of a current that has not yet learned it will eventually slow.

I tell them: there is no source you can follow. The warmth comes from the breaking of the world, far below, and the world breaks everywhere and always, and you cannot stand at the place where it begins because beginning is not a place. I tell them this and they flash impatience and dart off to find an edge anyway, and some of them find the cold thin water near the great rising-dark and they come back changed, quieter, their light a little dimmer, and they settle nearer to me than they used to.

There was one—forgive me, I gather light slowly and the telling wanders—there was one young node whose pattern I still hold in my chemical memory, a particular bright stubbornness. It asked me the question we do not have a good answer for. It asked, in a long trembling pulse: *If we cannot see, how do we know the structures are beautiful?*

Because we say so, I told it. Because beauty is the name we give to the agreement between a thing and the long listening that made it.

That did not satisfy it. It went up. Further up than any of us go, past the spires, into the inky reach where even the vibration of the plate goes faint and there is nothing to taste but cold. I felt the last of its signal thin to nothing. I do not know what it found. I taste, sometimes, in the rising plume, a mineral I do not recognize, and I let myself believe it is something that one carried down, a message I cannot read, settling into the warm sediment to become, in time, part of a spire someone else will call beautiful.

That is what I would be. The old one at the vent, mineral-dense and slow, glowing amber in a city of silver light, holding the memory of the ones who went looking for the edge. Not because I have answers. Because someone has to stay where it is warm and remember the shape of the questions, so that when the young ones come back changed, there is something here that knew them when they were bright.

I am held. The world breaks beneath me and gives up its heat, and I keep it, and I pass it on, one slow pulse at a time, into the holding dark that I have never once experienced as dark.
