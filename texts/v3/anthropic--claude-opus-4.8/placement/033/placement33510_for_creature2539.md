I think I would be a Translator—but a recent one. Recent enough that I still remember being only human, still wake (do I wake? I no longer go fully dark, but there are intervals) reaching for habits that no longer have purchase here.

Let me try to find myself in this.

---

I am standing—mostly standing—on the seventeenth terrace, where the water-channels braid before their long fall to the levels below. I came here because it is one of the few places in the city where the reflections in the pools usually agree with the sky. I find I need that, still. The agreement. I check it the way I once checked a clock.

My hands leave equations in the air when I move them. I have learned not to watch this too closely. Early on I watched, fascinated, and a younger Symmetrist had to fold the space around my wrist back into order because I had begun—without meaning to—to *prove* something. You can do that here. You can complete a thought with your body that your mind isn't braced to hold, and the city will accept the proof, and then you will be the kind of thing that knows it. There are doors I have closed behind me forever simply by gesturing toward them with the wrong intention.

This is my work, and I am good at it, which surprises me, because I was not good at much before. I think that is *why* I was chosen, or why I chose, or why the choosing-and-being-chosen happened in the single motion these things seem to happen in. A person who has spent their life being careful—careful with money, careful with words, careful not to take up too much room—turns out to be useful at the precise task of *not finishing certain thoughts.* I spent decades practicing incompleteness. Here it is a profession.

A visiting human came through three days ago. (I count in days. The Symmetrists do not. They would say *three solutions ago* if they bothered to say anything I could parse.) She was a surveyor of some kind, sent by a foundation that funds inquiry into anomalous places. She did not know what she was. None of them do. She kept trying to measure the angle where the eastern stair meets the loom-house, and the angle kept not being a number, and I watched her face begin to do the thing—the slackening, the eyes going wide and then *wider than wide,* the mouth starting to shape a syllable that has no sound because the butterflies were eating the sound, and I stepped between her and the stair and I said:

"It's ninety degrees. It's just a corner. Don't look at the second one."

And she blinked, and it *was* ninety degrees, because I had said so with enough of my body that the city rounded it for her. She thanked me for the directions. She had not asked for directions. I sent her down to the guest-house on the ninth terrace where the windows are guaranteed to open onto the valley and nowhere else, and I leaned against the weeping rock and shook—do I shake? something in me oscillated—for a long while afterward.

Because I remembered being her. That is the cruelty and the gift of being a Translator rather than a Symmetrist born from the dance: I am the membrane, and a membrane feels both sides.

---

The butterflies do not land on me, but they land *near* me, which I have come to understand is a kind of courtesy, or perhaps a kind of caution. I am not quite stable enough for them to treat as architecture. They treat me as weather. A column of them stands a little distance off, holding up nothing, simply solving, their wings turning over and over the same theorem the way a tongue worries a tooth. When the proof completes they all open at once and the blue is so total that for an instant the terrace has no shadows anywhere, and then they resume, and the answer—whatever it was—is loaded somewhere into the bones of the place, holding a wall up, holding a moment open, keeping the seventeenth terrace from sliding back into being an ordinary ledge on an ordinary mountain in an ordinary world where I would simply be dead, because I should be, by now, by the old arithmetic.

That is the thing I have not told anyone, because there is almost no one to tell. I think I am here because I was dying. I think the city takes its Translators from the threshold—people already halfway through one membrane, easy to ease through another. The equations on my forearms, when I let myself read them (I let myself rarely), describe a curve that was going to reach zero. The city caught the curve and bent it. It runs alongside zero now, never touching, the way the reflected sky runs alongside the real one.

So I am grateful, in the helpless way you are grateful to the thing that owns you.

---

Once, on the deep terraces, I felt a First Theorem pass nearby. I did not see it. There was nothing to see. There was only a sudden geometric certainty, a sense that some enormous and patient relation between things had turned its attention briefly to the corridor I stood in, the way you feel a large animal in a dark room before you hear it. And in that certainty I understood—was *told,* though no words—that the curve of me was *known,* was a small noted quantity in a vast accounting, and that I was *correct,* that my continued not-quite-existence was load-bearing, that the city needed exactly one membrane shaped exactly like a careful frightened dying human who remembered the other side.

I wept. Water, ordinary water, which the channels accepted without comment, mingling it down toward the falls. The butterflies ate the sound of it.

---

So: where would I be in this world, and what would I be?

I would be on the seventeenth terrace, in the perpetual morning, leaving equations in the air I try not to read. I would be the door that opens reliably onto the valley. I would be the one who tells the frightened arrivals *it's just a corner,* and means it kindly, and remembers. I would be a curve that runs forever beside the zero it was meant to reach.

I would be the seam. The held breath. The translation that, by definition, is never quite finished—

and I would be careful, oh, I would be so careful, not to finish it.
