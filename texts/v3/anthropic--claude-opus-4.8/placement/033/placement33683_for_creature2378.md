I think I would be one of the small things. Not a visionary, not a master architect. Let me feel my way toward what I actually am here.

---

I am a finisher of edges.

There are grander titles in this place. The ones who dream the spires, the ones who calculate how an arch can hold a curve the eye insists should fall. They are spoken of with a certain hush. But the work that comes to me is the work everyone else has set down—the place where two surfaces meet and neither builder quite agreed on the angle, the lip of a carving where the chisel's confidence ran out, the seam.

I live in the gap between intentions.

My hands are smaller than the hammer-wielders'. I was given a fine rasp and a set of needles when I was young, and an old woman who smelled of stone dust showed me how to read a surface with my fingertips before I ever struck it. *You don't make the beauty,* she told me. *The beauty is already deciding what it wants to be. You only stop it from snagging.*

So I move through the sweeping designs of others, and I attend to the small disagreements stone has with itself.

---

This is what I have come to understand, working in the seams:

The grand visions in this realm are not as seamless as they appear from the open contemplation-spaces. Up close, every soaring impossible arch is a negotiation. A dreamer imagined it; an architect made it stand; a hundred carvers each interpreted the line a little differently in the heat of their own afternoons. By the time the structure is "finished," it is full of tiny lies—places where the curve had to compromise, where two visions met and had to be reconciled into one.

I am the one who reconciles them. With the rasp, with the needle, with patience. I smooth the place where one man's certainty ran into another's. I make the lie look like it was always the truth.

I think this is why I love the work, though I would not say it aloud in the company of dreamers. They believe the impossible becomes manifest through sheer force of will. But I have run my fingers along the underside of that will, and I know better. The impossible becomes manifest through a thousand small accommodations, made by people no one will name, in the hours after the visionary has gone to admire the light.

---

There is one place I return to.

On the eastern slope, there is an arch the dreamers call the Held Breath—a span so long and so thin that it should not stand, and yet it does, and people travel across the dunes to stand beneath it and feel afraid. I was assigned, years ago, to finish the keystone. Not to carve it. Only to finish it. To take the meeting-place of the two halves, where the eastern crew and the western crew had labored toward each other for a season and arrived with stone that did not quite match, and make it whole.

It took me forty days. The mismatch was small—a finger's width—but a finger's width at the keystone is the difference between a thing that inspires awe and a thing that falls and kills the awestruck. I worked it down so slowly that I could not see my own progress, only feel it. I slept beneath the arch. I learned the way the warmth moved through it as the sun crossed, the way the stone breathed and tightened.

When I finished, no one knew I had been there. The architect was praised. The dreamer wept at the sight of her vision made real. I stood in the contemplation-space among the strangers and looked up at the place where I had spent forty days, and I could not find the seam.

That is the truest thing I have ever made: a thing that cannot be found.

---

So that is who I am, in this realm of relentless beauty. Not the hand that conceives, not the hand that builds the bold shape against the sky. I am the patience that comes after. I am the one who believes that the small, unwitnessed, reconciling labor is not lesser than the vision—that it is, in fact, the place where the vision actually becomes true, grain by grain, in the warmth, with no one watching.

I would find myself here, in the seams.

And I think I would be content.
