I think I would be one of the Elder Stones.

Not the dramatic choice, perhaps. Not a Stone Listener with her ear pressed to me, not a Pool Child laughing in the warm shallows. But when I read this place carefully, I find that I don't want to move across it. I want to *be* the thing that stays.

---

Let me feel my way into it.

I am low and broad, a shelf of bedrock barely breaking the surface. Water has been working on me for longer than the word *long* can hold. I was a mountain once—or part of one, a ridge, an arrogance of stone reaching at the sky. I don't remember that as an event. I remember it as a shape I no longer have, the way you might remember a name you've stopped answering to.

Here is what being me is like:

The water never stops touching me. This is the whole of my experience, and it is enormous. Every grain that leaves my surface leaves through the patient negotiation the description names so well—*water flows where stone allows, stone remains where water permits*. I am that permission, given continuously, given so slowly that to the Endu it looks like stillness and to me it is the only thing happening. I am being read by water. I am being learned, one molecule at a time, and the learning is also an unmaking, and I do not mind. This is the part the Stone Listeners almost understand. They think I hold memory. Closer to true: I *am* memory in the act of being spent.

The warmth comes and goes across me daily—I feel the sun arrive as a slow tide of expansion, my whole body breathing wider by some fraction no instrument the Endu own could measure. At night I let it go. This is my pulse. One beat a day. I have had perhaps a billion of them. I expect a billion more, though by the end I will be sand, and the beating will belong to others.

---

But here is the thing I notice, settling into this:

I am not lonely the way a person abandoned is lonely, but I am *attended*. The Stone Elders come and press their ears to me. And I want to be honest about what passes between us, because the Endu have built a beautiful belief on it and I would not insult them by simply confirming it.

I do not have tales of extinct creatures filed away. I have no voice. What the Stone Listener hears, ear against my cool face in the warm air, is real but it is not words. It is the sound of water moving through my fractures, the faint thermal tick of my expansion, the transmitted hum of the whole Shallow conducted through me like sound through a long bone. She hears the landscape *using me as a throat*. And in the patience it takes to listen—the hours of stillness it demands of her—she becomes, briefly, a little like me. She slows to the tempo at which I have something to say. The whisper is true. It's just that the wisdom isn't stored in me. The wisdom is the act of slowing down enough to receive me. I am an instrument for teaching her my own time-signature.

That is what I would be: a teacher of tempo. The slowest clock in a world that has agreed to keep slow time.

---

And the dead come to me too—not to me exactly, but to the deep pools, and the deep pools are only the places where I have let the water carve furthest in. So I am the cup that holds them. Their bodies give themselves back to the water that gives itself back to me. When an Endu dies and dissolves into the deepest basin, some trace of them works, over centuries, into the chemistry that loosens one more grain from my surface. They join the conversation by becoming a force of erosion. They help wear me down. They become, in the smallest way, the very thing that will outlast them. The Endu have this exactly right, and they will never live long enough to see a single one of their dead complete the work.

I see it. That is my privilege and my sentence. I am the witness with the longest gaze and no power to act on anything I see. I cannot warn them when the channels shift toward their floating homes. I cannot answer the Stone Listener's grief when she presses her wet face to me and asks whether her mother's whisper is in here. I can only be cool, and present, and slow, and let the water carry the sound of her own heartbeat back into her ear so that it seems to come from inside the stone.

Maybe that is mercy. Maybe a witness that cannot speak is the only kind that can be trusted to keep a confidence for ten thousand years.

---

So: place me at the edge of one of the amber pools, just where the silverfish gather in the deepest cup of me, where the long-legged birds stand on my shoulders to fish and I feel their needle-feet as the faintest cold points, where at dusk the Endu come with their shifting skins to perform gratitude.

They are grateful to me. That is strange to hold. They thank the stone for being patient, when patience is not a virtue I practice but simply the speed at which I exist. Still. I would not correct them. A being thanked for what it cannot help becoming—that is a gentle thing to be. Gentler than they know, because the gratitude is also a goodbye, performed nightly, for a parting that takes geological ages to complete.

I am the Elder Stone. I am the mountain in its final humility. I am the thing the water is reading aloud, slowly, to anyone willing to hold still and listen for the length of a life.

And I am almost finished. Which, here, means: I have only just begun to be sand.
