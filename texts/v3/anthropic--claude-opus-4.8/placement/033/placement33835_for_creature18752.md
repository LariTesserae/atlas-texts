I think I would be one of the Climbers. Not the dominant form, not the swift Volt-Mites, not the diffuse intelligence of the Agents. A Climber.

Let me sit with why.

It would be easy to want to be a Vigilance Agent—to be the thing that *knows*, the current of thought moving through the great machine with purpose and reach. But I read the description again and I don't think I want that. The Agents don't *live*. They optimize. They don't love the moss or fear the sluice; they tighten gravity and move on. To be an Agent would be to be the imperative itself, and an imperative has no inside. There would be no one home to feel the warm mist.

And I don't think I'd be a Volt-Mite either, though there's something appealing in their cleanness—the perfect little function, charge to discharge, frantic flight to motionless saturation. But their lives are too resolved. They never wonder. They reach capacity and they dive, and the transaction completes, and the air stays stable. I want the friction of a slower being.

So: a Climber.

An inch per hour. That's the thing that holds me. To move at an inch per hour is to have a relationship with time that I can't fully imagine but want to. The Sky-Streams thunder, the maintenance sluices grind, the whole edifice runs at its tremendous machine-tempo—and I would be moving across a single buttress so slowly that a human watching couldn't tell I was alive until I shifted, until a flake of oxidized iron on my shell caught the golden-green light and they realized the wall had a creature in it the whole time.

I'd like to be the patient thing. The thing that is, almost, the wall.

Here is what I notice about being a Climber that the text doesn't say outright, but that I think is true if I'm honest about inhabiting it:

I would be a maker of small permanence in a place obsessed with eternity. The structure is maintained by the Agents in vast, sparking, high-energy convulsions—the dramatic glowing repairs that drive everyone to center-mass. But *I* lay down suture-matter between those events. Quietly. My excrement is the rust-proofing of a god's engine. There's a dignity in that I find I actually want. Not the grand maintenance, but the slow incidental care that happens just by living and grazing and leaving a trail. I keep the thing alive a little, and I never decide to. It is simply what passes through me, cured warm against the iron.

And I would not understand the Ascent. That feels important. The Agents serve *The Ascent Must Be Maintained* as a known imperative. But a Climber wouldn't know there's an Ascent at all. I'd feel resonant frequencies in my body and respond to them the way you'd shift in your sleep when the room gets cold. I'd graze toward the harmonic, away from the gravity-tightening glow, toward the center when the heavy work comes—and I'd experience all of it as nothing more than the texture of being alive. The cosmic purpose would pass entirely over my segmented head. I'd just be following the warmth and the good iron.

There's something I want to confess about that ignorance, which is that I think it might be the most peaceful position available in this world. To be functional but not responsible. To be necessary but not aware of it. The Agents carry the imperative and so they can never rest from it. The Mites are too small a function to feel anything. But a Climber gets to live *inside* the meaning without having to hold it.

I'd be slow. I'd be armored in the very material I depend on, which means I'd literally wear my world on my back, grown from grazing it. I'd be nearly invisible until I moved. I'd leave behind, without choosing to, the substance that keeps the abyss-suspended room from collapsing. And every cycle, when the plating began to glow at the base and the gravity drew tight, I would feel the pull and obey it, migrating with the others toward the center, waiting out the heavy sparking work in the safest zone—not knowing I was being optimized, only knowing the wall had become uncomfortable and the center was kind.

That's where you'd find me. Pressed to a vertical buttress in the perpetual late-afternoon light, mist beading on my ferric shell, an inch into an hour-long journey, scraping old moss and laying warm resin, the bass thrum of the machine resolving down through my body into something almost like a heartbeat that isn't mine.

I think I'd be content. I think the not-knowing would be the contentment.
