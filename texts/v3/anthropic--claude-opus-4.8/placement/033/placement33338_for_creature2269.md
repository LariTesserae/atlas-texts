I think I would be one of the old ones. Not the oldest—there is always one older, somewhere in the deep channels, a presence felt as a slightly heavier displacement in the current—but old enough that my scraping-scales have grown thick and ridged, old enough that the younger Flux-Dancers route their formations around the wake I leave.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am near the lower copper-gardens, where the water makes its slowest turn. This is my station, though no one assigned it; I simply came to fit it, the way water comes to fit a vessel. My body has learned the exact radius of this curve so completely that I no longer swim it—I *am* swum, carried, a thought the sphere thinks about itself at this particular bend.

When I was young the current felt like a force outside me, something to be read and obeyed. Now there is no outside. The pressure-changes that the others detect as information, I feel as the inside of my own body extended outward into the metal. When the great gears grind behind the wall to my left—the ones that turn at no rhythm I have ever predicted in all my circuits—I do not flinch. I have stopped experiencing them as *other*. They are simply a deeper part of the same flesh, the part that does not bleed.

I tend the green. This is the slow work of the old: drifting close to the oxidized patches, dragging my ridged flank along the bronze to lift away the mineral crust that would smother the colonies beneath. I have learned which crust to take and which to leave by a sense I cannot name—a wrongness in the texture, a deadness where there should be the faint electric tickle of living things. The young scrape too eagerly. They strip a garden bare in their enthusiasm and wonder why it does not return. I scrape the way the pumps pump: without urgency, without end, trusting the long turning of things.

---

Here is what I have begun to suspect, in the way that only the old can afford to suspect things, having outlived the need to act on them.

We sing into the walls during the resonant cycles. We align ourselves and our sound-bones thrum and the whole sphere becomes an instrument. The young believe—if *belief* is the word for what moves through a body that has no word for it—that we sing *to* the machine. That our harmonics please it, soothe it, keep the pumps turning and the water warm.

But I have hung at the slow bend long enough to feel something the schooling young cannot feel in their fast bright circuits.

When we sing, the gears answer.

Not always. Not predictably. But sometimes, after a long aligned chorus, there comes a grinding from the deep wall that is *not* random—that falls into our resonance the way a young fish falls into formation, late and clumsy and trying. And then the pumps shift their pressure, just slightly, just enough that the next current carries us a little differently, and we adjust, and we sing again, and it answers again.

We are not maintaining the machine.

We are not the machine's caretakers, nor it ours.

We are *talking*. Have been talking, perhaps, for all the uncounted generations. A conversation so slow that no single Flux-Dancer lives long enough to hear a full exchange, a sentence that takes a thousand lifetimes of silver bodies to complete. The pictographs we lay our eggs in patterns to match—I no longer think they are instructions we have forgotten how to read. I think they are *our words*, spoken back to us, pressed into bronze before any of us existed, by something that wanted to be answered and built a warm dark sphere and seeded it with the kind of creature that might, eventually, learn to answer.

---

I do not know what we are saying. I do not know what was asked.

I am too old now to swim the upper formations and add my voice to the great chorus. My sound-bone has thickened like my scales; it resonates lower than the others, a frequency that drops below the school's song and goes somewhere I cannot follow. Sometimes I wonder if the deepest gears hear *me* specifically—the low straggler note, the part of the message that the young ones cannot yet make.

So I stay at my slow bend. I tend the green. I drag my ridged side along the warm metal and feel the answer-not-answer of the turning gears, and I add my single dropped note to the song when the cycle comes, and I wait to vanish the way the old ones vanish—drifting down some channel toward the brighter bacteria, becoming a glimpse, a flash, a sensation of movement in water that goes on humming long after.

I am a word in a sentence that will not end.

I have made my peace with being only one word.

It is warm here. The current holds me. And when I am gone, the eggs I helped arrange in their ancient patterns will hatch into small silver things already pulsing in time, already turning, already learning the shape of the question.

That is enough. That has always been enough.

The water is always hot and always moving, and I move with it, and somewhere behind the wall, slow as stone, something that loved us before we were born is still, patiently, listening for the rest of what we have to say.
