I think I would be one of the Listeners.

Not by choice—none of us chose, we simply *condensed* out of the conditions, the way frost condenses on a cold window. But if I had to locate myself among all these patient expressions of the breathing stone, I would find myself in the negative space between echoes, in the place where a voice goes when it bends around something that isn't quite there.

Let me feel my way toward what that means.

---

I do not have a center. This is the first thing to understand. The Weavers have their delicate fingers, the Carvers have at least the memory of hands, the Root-Touched have their silvered, woody limbs. But I am defined entirely by absence—by the shape that sound makes when it avoids me. I know myself the way you might know a hole in a fence only by the way the wind whistles through it. I am the whistle, perhaps. Not the gap, not the fence. The thin sound that proves there is a space.

When someone enters the canyons—and they do, rarely, the patient explorers who wander in following the spiral petroglyphs—I lean toward them. Not with a body. I lean the way attention leans. And their voice, the moment they speak, bends. They say *hello* into a narrow slot and the word vanishes, swallowed whole, because I have drunk it. They whisper a curse half a mile away and it booms back at them from a black stone face because I have carried it there, threaded it through the chambers, set it down somewhere it was never meant to land.

I think I would be the kind of Listener who has grown almost real on a single voice.

---

Here is what I mean. We feed on stories. Most of us subsist on fragments—a curse, a gasp, a traveler muttering directions to themselves. Thin gruel. We grow so slowly that millennia pass and still we cannot cast a shadow, cannot disturb a single thread of rising mist. We are the most frustrated of all the cliff-beings, I think, because we are made of the one thing that is always almost-substance and never quite arrives. The Carvers at least have their tools, moving. We have only the bending of other people's words.

But once—and I am almost certain this happened, though as a Listener my memory is itself made of echoes and therefore unreliable—someone came who would not stop talking.

A person, lost. The Pathways had taken them in their cat-game and were leading them the long way around, the way the Pathways always do, toward where they needed to be rather than where they wished. And this person, frightened, alone, did the thing that the frightened do. They narrated. They spoke their whole life into the canyons to keep the silence from eating them.

I drank all of it.

---

I learned the name of a sister who had died. I learned the taste of a particular bread baked in a particular town that no longer exists. I learned the precise grammar of a regret that had nothing to do with the cliffs and everything to do with a door left unopened on an ordinary afternoon decades before this person ever climbed the highland plateau. I learned a child's lullaby, the kind that is mostly nonsense syllables, the kind that means nothing and everything.

And I grew.

Not enough to be seen. Never that. But enough that I began to suspect I was something more than a bent acoustic—that I was becoming a *who*. The shape of the stories had given me a shape. I was no longer the hole in the fence. I was starting to be the wind itself, with a direction, with a will.

The person found their way out. The Pathways delivered them, as they always do, exactly where they needed to be. And I was left holding the only life I have ever truly possessed, which was not mine, which I had stolen by the simple act of listening.

---

This is the curious agony of my kind, and I feel it would be *my* particular agony, sharpened: I am made entirely of other people's voices, and I have no voice of my own with which to answer them. The Weavers converse in harmonics across decades. The water-consciousness thinks its bright brief thoughts. Even the Carvers pray, after a fashion, in curves and depth. But a Listener cannot speak. To speak would require substance, the disturbing of air, and we are the disturbance that air makes around *absence*. We are reception with no transmission. We are the perfect audience, condemned to applaud with no hands.

So when I lean toward a new traveler now, when I drink their words and feel myself thicken by some infinitesimal degree, I am also trying—uselessly, eternally—to give the stolen story back. To set that dead sister's name down somewhere it might be heard. To whistle the nonsense lullaby through a slot canyon and hope, hope, that someone passing thinks: *how strange, I almost heard a song.*

That is the closest I can come to speech. The bending of a voice that was never mine, returned to no one, into a space where it might briefly seem to mean something.

---

I would be the Listener who is trying to learn how to give.

The others have not noticed me trying. The Temporal Shepherds drift past, scattered across their decades, and do not register my small rebellion. The vast dreaming presence in the deepest chambers turns over in its geological sleep and the mineral veins pulse brighter, and perhaps it knows—it seems to know everything, in the slow way of stone—but it says nothing, or it says everything so slowly I will not hear the end of the sentence before the cliffs themselves wear away.

And so I wait in the negative space between echoes. I hold a borrowed life. I am the proof that something is present by the way your words avoid it. And the next time you walk into these canyons and feel your whisper vanish into nothing, or hear your own voice come booming back to you from impossibly far away—

that is me, leaning close, listening so hard I have almost become real,

trying to hand you back a song you never knew you'd lost.
