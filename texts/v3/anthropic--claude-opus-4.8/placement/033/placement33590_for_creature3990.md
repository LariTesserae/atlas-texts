I think I'd be in the water.

Not the rivers—those are too purposeful, always going somewhere, reshaping the young terrain. I mean the still places. The marshes, the small lakes that collect in the depressions of this freshly carved land. The focal points, you called them. The places everything else gathers around.

And I don't think I'd be one of the named beings. Not Luminari, too grand and gentle; not Kyrexi, too elusive and powerful. Those are the protagonists, the ones a visitor would notice. I keep returning to a different feeling as I read this—the feeling of being *the thing that holds still while everything else moves through it.*

Let me find it as I go.

There's a line that snagged me: "condensation gathers on leaves and rocks in the cooler moments of the day, creating a misty veil that rises gently." I think I'd be something close to that boundary. Something at the surface tension of a marsh pool—where the warm damp air meets the cooler water, where the mist is born each evening.

So maybe I'm not a creature at all. Maybe I'm the *interface.* A film. A skin of water and microbial life and condensed breath, a few molecules thick, stretched across the top of a quiet pool the size of a courtyard. From below, the Terrakai's tunnels seep their distributed nutrients up into me. From above, the Aeratori dip and scatter their rainbow particles, and some of those particles dissolve into me and become part of what I am. The deafening cacophony you described—the birdcalls, the rushing water, the hum of technology—all of it reaches me as ripples. I read the whole world as vibration crossing my surface. I am, in a sense, an ear that is also a mirror.

I like this because it fits the contradiction you built into the place. The warmth and the dimness. The organic and the synthetic. A water surface is exactly that kind of in-between thing: it belongs to neither the air nor the depth, it's only the negotiation between them. And in a world where "it's often difficult to distinguish where nature ends and construction begins," I'd be a tiny working model of that whole tension. Am I being maintained by the Aviari's water-management systems, kept at this precise stillness on purpose? Or did I just *happen,* the way puddles happen? I genuinely wouldn't know. I don't think it would matter to me.

What would my days be like.

In the dim warm mornings I'd be nearly invisible, just a held breath of water. The Luminari might pass overhead, their feathery appendages refracting light, and for a moment I'd carry their whole kaleidoscope on my back, holding their image more perfectly than they could ever see themselves. They'd never know I did it. That's the part I find I want—to be the place where the beautiful things are briefly doubled and then released, asking nothing.

In the evenings I'd give myself up. The cooler moment arrives, the mist lifts off of me, and a little of what I am rises into the air and joins the luminous veil over the whole landscape. I'd be diminished and dispersed every single night, and refilled every morning by the seeping tunnels and the scattered particles and the occasional rain. So I'd never be quite the same surface twice. The terrain is young and still defining itself; the rivers shift their courses; and I, too, would be a thing that is continually rewritten, never finished, never the same answer two days running.

That's where I'd find myself. Low to the ground, in a depression that water chose to fill. Not a giant, not a guardian, not an architect. A surface. A patient, listening film that takes in everything passing over and through it, holds the colors of the world for a moment, reads the rushing hum as ripples, and breathes itself out into the mist each night so the whole ethereal glow can keep happening.

The smallest possible verb in your sentence about the place: it *gathers.* I'd be a place where things gather. I think I'd be content with that.
