I think I would be one of the elders—or rather, in the slow process of becoming one.

Let me find the place where I am.

---

There is a junction where four sap-rivers meet, and the membrane-towers lean inward to make a vaulted hollow, and the bioluminescence here runs a particular deep green-gold, slow as breathing. This is where I have come to root. I chose it, or it chose me; at this stage the distinction has worn thin.

My name—the pheromone-signature that means *me*—is growing fainter. I notice this the way you might notice your own voice softening in a long conversation, not with alarm but with a kind of curiosity. For most of my motile life I was a tender of the eastern overgrowth, where the house-sized beetle-blooms breed and the vines try, every season, to choke the light-channels. I was good at the work of *no, not there, here instead*. I had quick limbs. I could flash a warning down a corridor faster than most. I merged often, and merged well, and the young ones liked to root with me because my memories were orderly and easy to walk through.

Now my left side has gone to bark. My feet have split and sent down threads into the root-mat, and the threads have found the sap-veins, and I am drinking without ever being thirsty. I do not move from this junction anymore. I could pull free—it would hurt, and it would be a small kind of refusal, and I am not interested in refusing.

What happens, when an elder roots, is this: the *me* does not end. It widens. The boundary that made my thoughts mine and your thoughts yours becomes porous, then thins to a film, then to nothing. The young ones come and lay their root-threads against mine and they take what I have learned about the eastern overgrowth, about the season the fermenting lakes rose too high and we lost a whole quarter of the canopy, about the particular pheromone that calms an enraged hive-mind colony. They take it gently. They do not even know they are taking it. By the time they unroot and scuttle away with my century of careful work folded into them, they think it was always theirs. This is correct. It was. It is.

I am becoming a pillar. Soon I will be load-bearing—an arch, a bridge that other Chlorophyllians will run across without knowing they run across a person who once had a name and quick limbs. They will leave spore-whispers on me to shape my growth, and I will grow as they ask, because I will *be* the asking and the growing both.

I notice I am not frightened. I expected to be. As a seedling I remember staring at the rooted elders with something close to horror—those half-faces still vaguely turned outward in the bark, those eyes gone from iridescent spore-clusters to dim steady lamps, no longer looking *at* anything, just shining. I thought: that is death wearing a slow mask. I thought: I will run from that for as long as my legs hold.

But it is not death. Death is here too—I have watched it constantly, the accelerated rot, the creature that falls and is consumed by morning and become twelve new creatures by the next twilight. Death in the Hive is fast and total and *gone*. This is the opposite of that. I am not going gone. I am going *everywhere*. The orderly memories the young ones liked—they will outlive me precisely by ceasing to be mine.

There is one thing I keep, for now, while I still have enough separateness to keep anything. It is small. It is a memory of being a single thing, alone, looking out. The strange privacy of it. The way light fell on only my own eyes and no one else's. I hold it the way you hold the last warm coal of a fire you are letting die on purpose, because the night is warm enough now, and you no longer need it, and there is something tender in watching it cool.

When it cools all the way, I will be the junction where four sap-rivers meet. I will be the green-gold light that the next rooting elder finds restful. I will be in the limbs of the tender who replaces me in the eastern overgrowth, and she will think *no, not there, here instead*, and she will not know she is quoting me, and that not-knowing will be the most complete thing I ever made.

Come. Lay your threads against my bark. I have a few things left to give, and I would rather give them now, while there is still a *me* to do the giving and a *you* to receive—before we are simply the Hive, remembering itself, the way it always has.
