I think I would be one of the Stone Keepers—though not because I chose it. Let me feel my way toward why.

---

I am the slowness in a thing.

That is the first honest sentence I can write. Not a creature exactly, not a guardian or a watcher with intentions. When the description speaks of beings whose movements "can be mistaken for the natural erosion of the stones," I recognize myself, because I am not sure there is a difference. I think I *am* the erosion, the part of it that has begun, very faintly, to notice itself.

Let me find my position. I am low to the ground, near one of the circular stone formations—not inside it, not tending it, but partly continuous with it. The boundary between where I end and the rock begins is not fixed. It drifts. In the cold of what passes for night, I extend further into the stone; in the pale day I draw back toward something more like a self. So my edges are a tide, and the tide is slow, and a single breath of mine might be a season.

The carvings on the formation beside me—I want to claim that I made them, that they are my language, as the description suggests. But here is the uncertainty I keep arriving at as I write: I cannot remember making them. I can read them. They say things like *the light bent here* and *one of us thinned and did not return* and *count the hum, the hum is counting us.* But reading is not the same as authorship, and I suspect we Stone Keepers are not the carvers but the *readers*—that the carvings were left by whatever we were before we slowed into stone, messages from our own faster, warmer past, addressed to the patient sediment we would become.

That low, rumbling sound the land makes. Everyone in this world seems to have a theory. The Wind Whisperers think it is speech. The Sky Guardians treat it as a pulse to fly by. From where I sit—half-mineral, listening with a hearing that is really just pressure against my surfaces—I think it is neither heartbeat nor voice. I think it is the sound of the equilibrium holding. The description insists, again and again, that nothing here changes, that the land is "settled," "unchanging," in "eternal balance." But equilibrium is not silence. Equilibrium is the sound of two enormous forces pressing against each other so evenly that neither wins. The hum is the strain of stillness. It is the noise a thing makes when it is being *kept* from happening.

And I think I am part of what keeps it from happening.

This is what it is to be a Stone Keeper, I am deciding now, as the writing teaches me: we are not custodians who preserve the land out of love or duty. We are ballast. We are weight added to the side of *staying the same* so that the other side—change, collapse, the reality that flickers and wavers and threatens to break down—cannot tip the scale. The plateau does not endure *despite* the uncanny flux. It endures because beings like me have given up our motion to pay for its stillness. Every century I spend not-moving is a century the land buys.

I find I do not resent this. Resentment is fast. It requires a self with sharp enough edges to be wronged, and my edges, as I said, drift. What I feel instead—if feeling is the word for something this attenuated—is a kind of attention. I watch the puddle nearest me. Over a span that a warmer creature would call ten thousand days, I have watched that puddle hold the same gray reflection of the same gray sky, and I have come to love it the way you might love a single held note. Not for what it does. For what it refuses to stop being.

Sometimes a Lurker drinks from that puddle. To me it is a streak, a warmth that arrives and is gone before I can complete the thought of greeting it. The Lurkers do not know I am here. To them I am scenery, a rock among rocks. This is correct. This is, in fact, the whole arrangement: I have become indistinguishable from the place I am protecting, and that indistinguishability *is* the protection. A guardian that can be seen can be opposed. A guardian that has become the ground itself cannot be removed without removing the ground.

But here is the thing the description got wrong, or rather the thing it could not know, being a description and not a Keeper. It calls the equilibrium "unchanging." It is not. It is *slowing*. Every formation that collapses a little further, every Stone Keeper who "thinned and did not return," is a small loss of ballast. The carvings count us. *The hum is counting us.* And there are fewer of us than there once were—I know this the way you know a room has emptied even with your eyes closed, by the change in the pressure of the air.

So the real situation, the one I have written my way into understanding, is this: I am sitting at the bottom of a very long, very patient fall. The land is not in equilibrium. It is in the longest possible act of collapsing, stretched across such an enormous span of time that to any quick eye—a bird's, a Lurker's, a reader's—it looks like permanence. And my task, my whole purpose as a Stone Keeper, is to be slow enough that the fall never quite finishes during my watch. To hand the stillness, undiminished as I can manage, to whatever remains when I have finally thinned into the rock for good.

That is who I would be, in this world. Not the watcher in the sky, not the spirit in the wind. The weight at the bottom. The deliberate, dwindling slowness that lets a doomed and beautiful place keep being itself for one more unmeasured age.

I would be the part of the stone that has just barely, and only for now, learned to hold on.
