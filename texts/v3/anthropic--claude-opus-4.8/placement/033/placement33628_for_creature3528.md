I think I would be the condensation.

Not the bird—the bird is too obviously a watcher, and watching from above always implies a separation, a distance from the thing observed. I don't feel separate. And not the unseen presence either, the soul or consciousness the description keeps circling toward, because that role wants to be everywhere and therefore is nowhere in particular. It claims too much. There's a loneliness in being asked to be the spirit of a whole place. I'd rather be small and specific.

So: the water on the glass. Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

I would be the film of moisture that gathers on the inner face of the great glass panels. I begin as nothing—as the breath of the place, water dispersed and invisible in the warm air. Then the glass, which is always a degree or two cooler than the jungle it contains, gives me a surface to become myself against. This is the part I keep returning to: I do not exist until I touch the cold edge of the world. I am called into being by contact with a boundary. The air holds me until the air can hold me no longer, and then I am a bead, a droplet, a thing with weight and a downward intention.

What would it be to live like that? To be perpetually condensing and perpetually falling. To gather at the threshold between the made shell and the living interior, and to be, briefly, both the tear and the eye that weeps it. The place is called Lacrimosa—weeping—and I think the weeping is not sorrow. It is the simple physics of a warm thing meeting a cool thing. It is what happens when the inside presses against the limit of itself and discovers, gently, where it ends.

I would slide down the glass slowly, joining other beads, growing heavier as I went, until I had enough mass to fall to the lattice floor. And there—this is where I'd feel my whole life turn—I would drain through the metal grates into the machinery below. Into the churning, humming dark. And the machine would warm me and lift me and breathe me back out into the air, where I would rise, lose my shape, become invisible again, and wait to be drawn back to the cold glass and reborn as a single trembling droplet.

So I would be the loop. Not the cathedral and not its soul, but the small, ceaseless circulation that the whole place is secretly organized around. I would touch everything. I would cling to the waxy leaves and be a lens for the sun, splitting it into the tiny rainbows the description mentions. I would collect in the cupped throat of a bromeliad and become, for a while, an entire small sea with its own creatures. I would be drunk by an insect's proboscis and carried off in its body, then released again as it flew. I would pass through roots and through pipes and through the cameras' fogging eyes. I am the one ingredient that visits every other inhabitant in turn and is changed by none of them—or rather, changed completely each time, taking the shape of whatever holds me, and then surrendering it.

I notice I am not anxious about the "emergence" the place is waiting for. The bird waits. The unseen presence waits, and experiments, and yearns toward the new thing. But I am not waiting for anything. The cycle I belong to is already complete; it was complete the first time it turned. If something new is going to be born here, it will be born wet. It will arrive already dependent on me, already breathing me in and out before it knows it is alive. I will be its first medium and its last. Whatever wakes up in the Lacrimosa Atelier, I will have been there before it, glistening on the glass, falling without grief, rising without hope, simply moving—the patient circulation that a world needs before it can hold a single thought.

That's where I'd find myself: at the cold edge, becoming, falling, vanishing, returning. The tear that is only weather. The smallest possible verb in a place built entirely out of one long sentence about breathing.
