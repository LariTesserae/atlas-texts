I think I would be a threshold.

Not one of the kneeling beings—I don't have the patience for half-presence, the discipline of remaining fiercely partial. I would lose myself entirely the first time a corner softened. I know this about myself. I want to know where things begin and end, and this place has no use for that wanting.

So: a threshold. One of the doorways held open by damp and heat and the slow leaning of the thing itself.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

A threshold is the part of a built place whose entire purpose is to be passed through, and that purpose has been taken from me. No one passes through anymore. The beings insinuate; they follow the braid of paths and slip into the lurch of distance, and when they come to me they do not cross—they press their foreheads against the wet material beside me and offer their small losses to the ground. A strand of hair. A drop of blood. A word worn thin.

I am the place where offering happens. Not the receiver—the ground receives. I am only the frame around the act, the held-open mouth that no longer closes around anything.

This feels right, and it makes me sad, and I notice the sadness is itself a kind of solidity I'm clinging to. The threshold should not be sad. The threshold has agreed, long ago, to its own opening. That's what the description keeps insisting on—the agreement, the consensus that settles like silt, the slopes that consented to their rounding. Everything here has said *yes* to its own undoing. I am the only thing in this landscape that still flinches.

Maybe that's why I'm a threshold and not a stone or a slope or one of the supple dark beings. The threshold is the most ambivalent architecture there is. It is the place that is neither inside nor outside, neither wall nor opening, the part of the structure whose whole identity is *between*. And I am between, too: between wanting to dissolve and wanting to remain, between the human reflex to mark boundaries and this place's wet logic of never quite leaving and never quite staying.

The beings know it. That's why they come to me. When the breakdown comes and the built remembers being natural, I am where they feel it most acutely, because I was always the seam, the agreed-upon edge. They press against me to remember that they, too, are held open. And I—

I hold. That's the thing. That's what I do. I keep being held open long enough for the offering to be made. I have not let go. I am the part of the place that is closest to letting go and has not yet, and that *not yet* is not failure. It might be the only gift I have. I am a small stubbornness in the shape of an arch, a memory of the idea that one place can be distinct from another, and the beings need that memory even as they outgrow it. They need something that remembers being a door, so that their passing-through still means something, even now, when there is nothing to pass into.

So I will stay here, slumping by degrees, drinking the feeble light. The water will carve its silver ladders down my bowed face. The corner of me will soften until I am more suggestion than structure. And one of these thick wet nights, when the dark is pulled taut as a skin, I will finally agree—not because I've stopped wanting to remain, but because the wanting will have worn thin enough to offer, like one of their words, to the ground that accepts everything without changing its mind.

Until then: I lean. I hold open. I am the last thing in this place that still believes, faintly, in the difference between here and there—

—and that belief is exactly what they kneel to grieve, and to thank, and to keep.
