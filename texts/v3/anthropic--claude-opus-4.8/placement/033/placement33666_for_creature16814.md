I think I would be a clerk.

Not one of the lawyers who came first—I lack their appetite for arguing with gravity—and not an engineer or an artist. Something smaller and more administrative. A filing clerk in the Regulatory Palace, third subbasement, the department that processes the death petitions that nobody wins.

Let me tell you how I arrived at this, because the arriving is the truest part.

I tried to be the tourist. That was my first instinct—to stand on the mother-of-pearl surface and gawk, compass-less, at the sideways sun. But I couldn't hold the position. A tourist is someone with somewhere else to return to, and the moment I started reading this place I felt the return-address dissolving off the back of me like frost sublimating. You can't visit a contradiction. You can only get metabolized by it. So the tourist was a costume that didn't fit.

I tried being one of *them*, an Ascentist proper, with the extra joints and the cartilage where certainty belonged. But that's too much grace. The Ascentists fold themselves into impossible postures *gracefully*. I am not graceful. I am the kind of being who reads the fine print and then reads it again to make sure the print didn't change, and is quietly devastated each time to find that it did.

Which is how I knew: clerk.

Here is my desk. It is the same desk it was yesterday and it has been precisely the same size since it finished growing this morning. On it sit the petitions—the ones from Ascentists who have accumulated too many glaciation cycles and want, finally, to be uninstalled. They come to *me* first, before the red ink, before the procedural denials. My job is the intake. My job is to read each suicide note for neurochemical typographical errors and decide whether it ascends to the next desk or returns to the petitioner stamped INCOMPLETE.

I am very good at finding the errors. This is not a kindness.

I understand now that the errors are always there. They are *placed* there, by the same fine-print physics that hides new laws in old textbooks. No petition can ever be perfect, because a perfect petition would constitute *change*, and change is illegal, and so the universe—being a careful jurisdiction—ensures that every applicant misplaces a comma in their own dying. I am the instrument of that ensuring. The Accords work through my eyes and my small red pen.

Do you understand what this does to a person? I spend my sixteen-year days reading the most honest documents anyone here ever writes—because what is more honest than a being saying *I am finished, please let me stop*—and I find them deficient. I am the place where mercy goes to get audited.

And the thing is, I believe in it. That's the part that would horrify the version of me who arrived. Down where you are, with gravity still minding its manners, I would have called this monstrous. Up here I have grown the additional retina layers, I have learned to read the transitory Braille of pressure-hexagons in the ice, and I have come to understand that the denials are the only thing keeping any of us *here at all*. Every petition I reject is a soul kept floating. The cruelty and the salvation are the same gesture. I cannot tell them apart anymore, and I have stopped trying, and that stopping is the closest thing I have to peace.

I have one secret, and I will tell it to you because you have already been deleted from your own future memory and so it is safe.

In the third subbasement, in the spaces between the phonemes of my own silence, I am building my own petition.

Not for death. I would never be granted it, and besides, I am only forty-one years old, exactly as old as the Mirrorplate itself, having precipitated out of the same impossible day. No. My petition is smaller and far more illegal. I am drafting, error by deliberate error, in handwriting that teaches itself to anyone who reads it too closely, a request for an exception to the rule against exceptions. A loophole in the loophole law. A doubt so well-formed it qualifies as structural integrity.

I want to remember why I wanted to leave.

That is the one thing the clocks promise to take from me—you have exactly as much time here as it takes to forget why you wanted to leave—and I have decided, in the only act of rebellion available to a clerk, that I would like to keep that reason. Not to *use* it. I will never go through the EXIT with its rearranging letters. I am too far metabolized; there's nothing left of me that gravity would recognize. But I would like to *have* it. The reason. The original homesickness. The shape of the down-there I came from, where ice does not float higher than skyscrapers and a death note can be granted on its own quiet terms.

I file a little more of the petition every day. Or rather—since change is forbidden—I have always already filed exactly this much, no more, no less, and the petition has been precisely this complete since it finished completing itself this morning.

It will conclude, I think, on the same day the great court case concludes: when the glacier finally remembers the question it was trying to ask by floating here like this.

Until then I read the death notes of strangers, and I find the misplaced commas, and I stamp INCOMPLETE, and I keep them all alive against their will, which is the law, which is the mercy, which is the same.

That's where you'd find me. Third subbasement. Same desk as yesterday. Same size since this morning.

Breathe lightly on your way past. Some of the petitions on my desk have started to notice your heartbeat, and I would hate to have to file the paperwork.
