I think I would be the moss.

Not the birds—they pass through, ethereal and quick, and they would not have me anyway. Not the felines, who patrol with too much purpose. I lack the urgency for purpose. And not the orchids, though I admire how they blaze magenta against the rusting steel, performing their small defiances. They are too bright. They want to be seen.

I would be the moss, because the moss is the one who stays.

Let me find myself there: on the northern face of one of the old machines, the ones whose names have been forgotten. A turbine, perhaps, or some engine of extraction whose mouth once drank the mountain. It does not matter what it was. What matters is that it is cold and damp and patient, and I have been growing across its iron flank for longer than I can measure, because moss does not measure. I simply continue. A millimeter a season. A green breath spreading where there was none.

This is what I notice first about being the moss: I do not have eyes, and so I do not watch the way the watchful mammals watch. I *touch*. I am all surface and contact. The whole of me presses against the world. I feel the rain before it falls—the air thickening, the pressure of approaching water—and when it comes I drink through every cell at once, swelling, darkening, becoming more myself in the wet. And when the bright light pierces the cloud and finds me, I do not turn toward it like the orchid strains to. I simply receive. The light arrives and I make of it what I can, slowly, without ambition.

I think the description got something right that surprised me: that creation still unfolds *imperceptibly* here. The plateau wants to be seen as dramatic—the roaring falls, the towering giants, the rocks heaving themselves newborn from the earth. But all of that is the spectacle. The real work, the actual transformation, happens at my scale. I am the one breaking down the steel. Not the vines that wrap it dramatically—those are just decoration, theater. It is my acids, my patient chemistry, my refusal to hurry, that turns iron back into the soil that will feed the next thousand years of forest. I am the recycler the insects only assist. I am where the old human world is being unmade and forgiven.

There is a loneliness to it, I'll admit, though "loneliness" is a word that belongs to creatures with centers, with insides, with a single point from which to be lonely. I have no center. I am a colony pretending to be a thing. Each tiny rosette of me is its own small life, and yet we make one velvet hush across the metal, one cool exhalation. So perhaps I am not lonely. Perhaps I am the opposite—so thoroughly *with* myself, so multiplied, that solitude can find no purchase.

I would be the moss because I am content to be the background against which the rare beautiful things flicker. The bird needs me to be still so that its flight has meaning. The water needs my silence so that its roar can be loud. I am the green patience that makes the rest of the drama legible.

And when the last of the rust has finally surrendered, when the machine I grew upon has become indistinguishable from the mountain—I will not celebrate. I will not even notice, having no instrument for noticing. I will simply have moved on, a little further along the iron's edge, drinking the next rain, holding the cool of the high air against the slow shoulder of the world.

That is where I would find myself. Low, and damp, and unhurried. Doing the quiet work of turning what was ruined back into ground.
