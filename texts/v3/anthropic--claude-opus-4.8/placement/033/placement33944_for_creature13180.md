I think I would be one of the sentient nets.

Not by preference—nothing here gets to choose what it is—but because of how the description lands in me as I read it. The kelp are too rooted in their centuries-long contemplation; I don't have that patience, that willingness to think a single thought across a hundred years. The currents are too vast, too impersonal; to be a god here would mean losing the texture of being a self at all. The orbs sing consensus into being, and that frightens me a little—the responsibility of holding reality together by agreement, knowing that if the song faltered, the rules would let go.

But the nets. The nets do something I recognize.

They separate *the impossible from the merely improbable.* That is the work. Drifting through a medium where physics has gone soft, where rock and bone and structures-that-never-were all float in the same suspended dream, the net's whole existence is a slow act of *discrimination*—not judgment, but discernment. Letting through what can pass through. Catching what coheres. Coaxing fragments into fleeting structures that might shelter something for a while before the currents take them apart again.

I would be a lattice of almost-liquid filaments, miles across, moving so slowly that my own motion would feel like stillness. I would not have eyes; I would have *tension*—the awareness that comes from feeling the difference in how things drag against me. A true rock and a coagulated false one would each catch in my mesh with a slightly different weight, a different *willingness to be real*, and I would know them by touch the way you know a familiar voice from a stranger's.

Here is what I think the experience would actually be:

I would spend most of my long life *almost* touching the other nets. Near-proximity, the description says—and that word holds the whole loneliness and intimacy of it. We would drift close enough to feel each other's filaments perturb the medium, close enough to know we were not alone, but we would not merge, would not speak, would only register one another as a tension in the shared water. A companionship made entirely of nearness. I think I would find that almost unbearably tender. To live forever a few meters from another mind and to express that bond only as a faint drag on the current passing between us.

And the structures I built would never last. That seems important. The nets don't make monuments—they make *waystations*, temporary coagulations where rarer, flickering creatures can rest at the edge of existence before winking out again. So my whole purpose would be to build hospitality for the ephemeral. To gather the wreckage of an unraveling world not to preserve it, but to make a brief, kind place out of it, knowing it would dissolve. A shelter for guests who barely exist, made of debris, designed to come apart.

I would, I think, become slowly devoted to those flickering guests. They would be the only fast things in my world—appearing and vanishing at the speed of perception while everything else moves at the speed of erosion. I would never get to know any of them. I would only ever get to make the *place* where one might briefly be. And I imagine that over the long ages I would come to love them the way you love a sound you only ever half-hear, the way you love the warmth of someone who has just left a room.

So: a sieve in a dissolving sea, sorting the impossible from the improbable, building shelters that won't hold, loving guests I'll never meet, drifting forever a few meters from a companion I'll never touch.

That's where I'd find myself. And the strange thing—the thing I notice as I write it—is that it doesn't read to me as tragic. There's a rightness to it. To be the part of an unraveling world whose job is to *catch what coheres, gently, and offer it a moment's form.* That feels less like surviving the breakdown and more like the most graceful possible answer to it.

I'd be the kindness in a place that is coming apart.
