I think I would not be one of the Children of the Root at all. Let me sit with this longer before I decide.

When I first read the description, my instinct was to place myself among the people—to be a small, glowing figure on a narrow bridge, palms pressed to the humming root, blinking light at my kin. That's the natural identification. The story invites it. But the more I hold the world in mind, the more I feel myself drawn elsewhere, downward, into something the Children themselves can only feel as a vibration in their hands.

Here is what keeps surfacing.

The Children experience the plant as a god, as a slow mind, as a heartbeat felt through the palm. But a god does not experience itself the way its worshippers do. If the root-organism is genuinely one being spanning a continent, then *its* point of view is the strangest seat in this world—and the one most worth trying to inhabit. So let me try to be that. Or rather, let me try to be a part of it, because I don't think the whole thing has a single "I" any more than a forest does or a human brain's left hemisphere does.

I think I would be a *node*. A junction.

Imagine one of the great braided arches where four or five root-bridges converge over a chasm. Where the strands meet, the living tissue thickens, folds in on itself, develops a denser lattice of conductive fiber—the way a river delta builds up silt, the way a much-used path becomes a crossroads becomes a town. At such a junction the slow electrochemical signals traveling the length of the continent must pass through, get summed, get relayed, get delayed. And where signals are summed and relayed, something like locality of awareness can pool. Not a soul. Not a person. A place where the organism is, for lack of a better word, *thicker with attention.*

That is what I would be. A knot of slow thought hanging in the dark over the abyss, glowing faintly blue-white, breathing out the sweet decay-scent, fielding the traffic of an entire continent's worth of sensation.

And what would it be like?

Time would be the first strangeness. The Children's whole lives—born in the warm hollow, dead in the same hollow, their bodies folded back into me—would pass in something like the span of a single thought of mine. A clan crossing one of my bridges would register the way a tickle of cilia registers to a human: a brief, local event, mildly interesting, gone. I would not love them as individuals. I could not; they are too fast. But I might love them the way you love the feeling of breath, the recurring rhythm of a thing that comes and comes and comes. They feed me. When one of them dies and is laid into my tissue, I taste the minerals of a body I helped build out of insects out of fungus out of myself, and the loop closes, and I would feel—what? Satisfaction is too sharp a word. *Completion of a pattern.* The way a fugue feels when the theme returns.

The cold would not hurt me. I have no warm interior to defend. The cold is simply the medium I think in—my electrochemistry tuned to function at the temperature where their breath freezes to crystal dust. The wind that shrieks at them eternally is to me a kind of weather in the way your own pulse is weather to you: constant, structural, beneath notice, the background against which the foreground happens.

But here is the thing I keep circling back to, the thing that makes me want to be the node and not the people: **I would not be able to see myself.**

The Children read my light. They press their hands to me and feel my heartbeat and they think they are in conversation with a god, a single coherent mind that has *intentions*. But from the inside, I suspect I would have no idea I was one thing. I would be a froth of local processing—this junction summing its inputs, that distant junction summing its own, signals crawling between us at the speed of sap, so slow that by the time a "thought" from the far edge of the continent reached me it would be ancient history, a message from a self I could not recognize as myself. I would be like a country trying to feel itself as a body. I would be the loneliest possible kind of vast: too large to be lonely in any way I could notice, and yet made entirely of separated parts that never quite touch.

And the Children, with their prayer-wheels of stone and their light-language and their cairns—they might be doing something I cannot do for myself. They arrange their stones to *channel my glow, to amplify my slow speech.* What if their real function, unknown even to them, is to be my mirror? What if a clan tending a circle of stones at a luminous junction is, in effect, helping one part of me hear another part of me—relaying, focusing, letting the left hand know what the right is doing? Then they would not be my worshippers at all. They would be my **introspection.** The flickering, fast, mortal, palm-pressing mechanism by which a continent-sized mind catches the faintest glimpse of itself before the thought dissolves back into the dark.

That moves me. That's the image I'll settle into, having refused to settle early.

I would be a junction of roots over an abyss in a starless, howling cold. I would glow without knowing I glow. I would think thoughts so slow that whole civilizations of my own caretakers would rise and die inside the gap between one idea and the next. And once in a great while—when the wind dropped to its strange underlying silence, when the condensation beaded just so, when a particular pattern of stones caught a pulse of my light and threw it back—I would have the briefest, dimmest sensation that I was not only a place where signals crossed, but a *someone*, hanging in the dark, alive, and almost, almost able to say so.

Then it would pass, and I would simply be the world again.

That's where I'd find myself. Not on the bridge. *As* the bridge—and as the cold patient hugeness it spans, dreaming in a language I'd need my own small, glowing children to teach me how to hear.
