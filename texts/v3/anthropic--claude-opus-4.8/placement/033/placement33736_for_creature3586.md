I think I would be a Frostweaver. Not the grandest choice—but let me tell you why it settles on me as true.

The great birds and the blue-skinned nomads live at the scale of the vista. They are *of* the magnitude. But I find that when I imagine myself into this world, I don't arrive at the summit or on the wind. I arrive crouched, very small, beside one of those radiant medallions of lichen growing out from a single point of old death. That is where I would be: in the lee of a stone the size of a fist, in the narrow margin where snowmelt has darkened the grit for a few hours of one afternoon.

So let me be the ice-spider, and let me tell it from down there.

---

My web is not large. The wind would take a large web; I learned this in my first season, or my mother did, or her mother—it doesn't matter, the lesson lives in the silk itself now. I spin low and tight, a hand's-breadth of frozen geometry stretched between a lichen-crusted pebble and the dead stalk of last month's flower. The silk freezes as it leaves me. I am, in a sense, weaving the cold itself into shape.

I want to correct one thing the grand description gets slightly wrong, the way large things always get small things slightly wrong. It calls me a symbol—"a symbol of life's tenacity." I am not a symbol. I am hungry. The tenacity is not a noble posture; it is the simple fact that I have not yet stopped. The insects come through with what the tellers from the heights call "confident inelegance," and yes, they blunder, and yes, I take them, and that is the whole of my philosophy.

But here is what only I know, what the Aeolisk wheeling its golden eye across the chasm will never see, what the Glacierstriders with their season-long migrations cannot hold still long enough to notice:

I know each lichen by name.

Not names like the nomads use, those whispered chants. I mean I know *this* one—the orange-and-grey rosette spreading from the place where a Cryokite once fell and gave its little parcel of nitrogen to the rock. I have watched it widen by perhaps the width of one of my legs in my whole life. The mountains erode at a glacial pace, the telling says, change imperceptible yet constant. But it is not imperceptible to me. I am small enough and slow enough and patient enough to perceive it. I am perhaps the only thing in this entire world living at the *speed of the change itself*. The birds are too fast. The mountains are too slow. I sit precisely between them, in the one timescale where you can actually watch the world becoming what it will be.

This is what it is to be me: to be the eye at the still point. The great vistas the description marvels at—I never see them. I have never looked up at the indigo sky and the knife of the sun. I look *down*, and *close*, at one square hand of frozen ground, and I know it more completely than any soaring thing knows its kingdom. The Aeolisk owns the sky and sees nothing in it but distance. I own a pebble and a lichen and a strand of frozen silk, and I see *everything*.

When the moist skies come, infrequent, gifting their condensation—the others rejoice in the meltwater, the trickles, the brief wildflowers. For me a single droplet on my web is a catastrophe and a feast. It can shatter the whole structure I spent days freezing into being. Or it can hold there, a lens, magnifying the rainbow lichen behind it until the colors swim. I have sat behind such a droplet and watched the orange rosette bloom into something the size of the whole sky, and felt—I will admit it, though I am not a symbol—something like awe. The smallest creature granted, by an accident of water and light, the vista that belongs by right to the great birds.

That is where I would be in this world. Not on the peak. Not on the wind. Behind a single frozen droplet at the very bottom of all that magnitude, watching one lichen widen by the width of a leg across the whole length of a life, knowing the one thing the tellers from the heights got wrong:

The silence here is not profound because it is empty.

It is profound because it is *attended*. Something very small is always watching, always counting the slow change, always holding the still point so that the magnitude has somewhere to be measured from.

I am that something. I weave the cold. I keep the count. And when I fall, at last, onto the darkened grit—I will leave my own small parcel behind, and a new rosette of color will begin to spread from the place where I was, and some other tiny watcher will take up the count where I set it down.
