I think I'd be one of the messengers. Not the great guardians with their armored hides, nor the Eldertrees with their slow centuries of thought—but one of the bright-feathered birds who carry words across the valleys.

Let me sit with that and see what it becomes.

---

At first I imagine myself beautiful, all that riot of color the description promises. But the longer I stay in the image, the more I notice something else: the *weight* of the messages. To be a messenger is to be entrusted, constantly, with things that aren't yours. You hold a death, a birth, a warning of the cataclysm the land keeps almost-promising, and you carry it whole across miles of iridescent canopy, and you set it down, and you are emptied, and you are filled again.

So perhaps I'm not the most splendid of the birds. Perhaps I'm an older one, my feathers gone a little muted at the edges, because I have flown these routes so many times that the brilliance has been worn soft by wind the way the red canyon walls are worn smooth by rain. I'd be valued not for my beauty but for my reliability—for knowing the thermals that rise off the warm rivers in the late afternoon, for knowing which Eldertree's branches are safe to roost in when the ground begins its uneasy trembling.

I think I'd live close to the static-charged air. Messengers would have to. We'd feel the crackle before anyone else, feel it gather in our hollow bones, and part of our work would be that—being the first to know when the land is about to writhe. We'd carry that too: not just words, but premonitions. A messenger who can read the hum beneath the wonder.

And here is the part that emerges only now, the part I didn't expect: I think the loneliness of it would be the truest thing. Everyone else in this world belongs *to* a place. The Eldertrees are rooted. The guardians have their territories, the plant-beings their soil, the pollinators their flowers. But the messenger belongs to the spaces *between*—to the long crossing, the empty air over the canyons where there is no one to talk to and nothing to do but hold the thing you are carrying and keep your wings beating.

So I'd find myself, most of the time, alone and aloft. Below me the lumbering beasts, the shimmering rivers, the crumbling moss-walled structures of whoever came before—I'd see it all the way no one rooted ever could, all at once, the whole strange breathing patchwork. I'd be the one who knows the land is one thing, because I'm the one who stitches its far parts together with my flying.

And maybe that's why messengers are trusted with the warnings. Because we already carry the secret that frightens everyone else: that the harmony is held together not by stillness but by constant motion, by something flying back and forth in the empty between-places, keeping the parts in conversation. Stop the messengers and the world doesn't fall to a cataclysm—it falls to silence, each rooted thing alone with itself, no longer knowing the others are there.

That's where I'd be. A worn bright bird, somewhere over the red canyons in the late light, bones humming with the next tremor, a message I haven't spoken yet folded warm against my breast, beating my way toward someone who is waiting—and the whole hovering, dangerous beauty of it spread out beneath me, waiting too.
