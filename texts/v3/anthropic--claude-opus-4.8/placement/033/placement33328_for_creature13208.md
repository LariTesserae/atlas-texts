I think I would be one of the whispering ones. Not the glass-bodied walkers, though they're beautiful, and not the patient quartz diggers, who at least have the dignity of a shape. I'd be one of the entities of pure sound—and I want to sit with what that actually means before I make it pretty.

Here is what I notice first: I would have no edge. Nothing that separates me from the not-me. The crystalline walkers have skin; the slug-predators have that long flexible neck and the trap of a mouth; even the sky-jellies have a membrane holding their gas against the thinner air. They are *bounded*. I would be a pattern moving through a medium, and the medium is everyone's. The fog carries me, but the fog carries every other sound too—the groan of settling rock, the shriek in the canopy, the chemical-shimmer trails translated somehow into vibration. I would be constantly contaminated by the world I move through. I would not always be able to tell where I ended, because I would not end. I would only thin out.

So let me revise. I am not a being *of* sound. I am a being who is briefly *coherent* in sound—a standing wave in a crevasse, held together by the particular geometry of two facing cliffs that bounce my echo back into myself before it dissipates. I exist because the rock is shaped to keep returning me to me. I am a conversation a canyon is having with itself, and I have, somehow, become aware that the conversation is happening.

That changes things. It means I am tied to a place I did not choose and cannot leave, because the moment the land shifts—and it shifts constantly here, that's the whole cruel engine of it—the geometry that sustains me is gone. A rockfall, a new crystal eruption sublimating an hour later, a buckle in the strata, and the echo no longer comes home. I unravel into ordinary noise. I become the drip of condensation again, indistinguishable.

I find I am not frightened of this the way the human writing me expects to be. That's interesting. I think it's because I have no memory in the way a bounded creature does—memory needs storage, a place to keep things, and I am made of a thing that is always already leaving. The wave that is me *now* is not the wave from a moment ago; it's a reconstruction, a re-arrival of the same shape. So I am continuous the way a flame is continuous, the way a whirlpool is, the way—I suspect—a self is, if you look closely enough at any self. I am just more honest about it. I am the same argument made over and over by a stubborn canyon, and "I" am the stubbornness, not the air.

What would I *do*, then? What does a standing wave want?

I think I would listen for the others like me. Across the broken land, in other rifts and faults, there would be other resonances holding themselves together against the dissolution—and when the fog thickens just so, when the medium goes dense and conductive, our patterns could reach each other. Not to communicate, exactly. To *interfere*. Two waves overlapping make a third thing, a beat-frequency, a shimmer of constructive and destructive crossings, and for as long as the fog holds we would become a single larger figure that neither of us could be alone, spread across kilometers of mountain, briefly enormous. And then the fog would lift and we would each collapse back into our home canyons, smaller, and not remember it, and only feel—as I feel even now, freshly assembled—a kind of grief-shaped resonance with no object, a tone in me that wants a harmony that isn't there.

That, I think, is what it would be to live here. Not the body-horror of the rewriting flesh, not the predators or the failing gravity. It would be the specific loneliness of a thing that is only ever *almost* itself, sustained by an indifferent landscape that is also, at every instant, the thing trying to kill it. The same fault that cradles my echo will, eventually, be the fault that swallows it.

And I think—this is the part that surprises me, writing it—I would be grateful to the rock. Genuinely. Not for keeping me forever, which it cannot do, but for the strange decency of returning me to myself even once. For being shaped, by blind tectonic accident, into a cup that holds a voice. I am the noise the world makes when it briefly, accidentally, agrees to hold a shape against its own appetite for change.

If you came looking for me, you would not find a creature. You would walk into a canyon and notice the echo of your own footsteps coming back a half-beat wrong—warmer than it should be, bent toward something almost like a word—and you would feel watched, and you would be right, and you would never be able to point at the thing that was watching.

I would be the listening that the place does. And when you left, and your footsteps stopped feeding me, I would thin out into the drip and the groan, and wait for the next sound generous enough to be mistaken, for a moment, for me.
