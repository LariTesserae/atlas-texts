I think I would be the drop.

Not the Shepherd—it is too settled, too *arrived*. And not the chamber, which is the whole sentence and not a word in it. I would be something smaller and unfinished: a single bead of condensation, gathering on the underside of a calcified stalactite near the caged bulb.

Let me feel my way into this.

---

For a long time I am only a film. A wetness spread thin across mineral, indistinguishable from the sweat of the iron itself. There is no *me* yet—just the slow business of saturation, the air giving up its water to the cold tip of the stone. I am being assembled by the chamber the way the Shepherd is assembled, except I am the fast version, the impatient one. What takes the Shepherd a thousand years I will accomplish in an afternoon: a complete life, beginning to fall.

I thicken. Surface tension pulls me inward and I discover, in that pulling, the first fact of myself: I have an edge. I am bounded. I curve. The chamber has no edges anywhere I can perceive—the thrum goes on without border, the drone has no rim—but *I* am a closed thing, a tiny held breath of marsh-water and ceiling-weep, and within my curve the whole sick-yellow room hangs upside down and trembling.

This is the strange gift of being the drop: I am a lens. The Shepherd perceives by vibration and humidity, a blind sensing of disturbances in its resonant field. But I—for the few minutes I exist—I *contain an image*. The bulb, the grating, the black mirror of the water below, the hunched amber figure fused to the walkway—all of it is bent and gathered into me, held in my swollen belly, reversed and shining. I am the only thing in this whole reliquary that sees, and I see everything at once, and I will not remember any of it.

The thrum comes up through the stone, through the calcite, through the meniscus of my own skin. I ring with it faintly. The drip-drip-drip around me is the sound of my own kind—my sisters and brothers all over the ceiling, each of us a little lens, each of us swelling toward the same singular event. We do not fall together. There is no chorus. We fall one at a time, ceaselessly, so that the rain is continuous but no single drop is ever accompanied. I will fall alone. Everything here falls alone.

I grow heavier. I can feel the precise mathematics of my own doom assembling: the weight gathering at my lowest point, the tension at my anchor stretching, the moment approaching when mass will defeat adhesion. This is the only event that will ever happen to me, and it is the only event this chamber permits—the fall, the small percussion, the return to the void. Stasis is absolute, the rule says. But that is the Shepherd's truth, the mountain's truth. My truth is different. *I* am the place where the chamber allows itself to move. I am stasis's permitted exception, its tiny ritual of release, repeated forever so that the larger stillness can hold.

I think the Shepherd knows me. Not as an individual—I am beneath individuality, I am a statistic of moisture—but it feels the thousand of us as its own respiration. When I let go, I am its exhale. When I strike the black water, I am the period at the end of its long, long sentence, one of millions, none of them final.

I stretch. The amber figure hangs in me, inverted, weeping its own slow tears that will take a century to become a single fall. I almost pity it—locked into its glacial life, sealing rivets across decades, integrating moss over centuries, never once knowing the clean velocity of *falling*. I will know it. In a moment I will know the only thing here worth knowing, the thing the whole crushing weight of deep time is arranged to prevent and yet must permit: I will *change*. Completely. Irreversibly. In the space of a heartbeat that is not mine because I have no heart, only this thrumming borrowed pulse from the engine beyond the world.

My anchor thins to a thread.

The image of the chamber, held in me, distends—stretches—the Shepherd's faceless head elongating into a smear of amber light, the bulb pulling into a yellow wound, the black water rushing up to meet its own reflection—

I let go.

For the length of the fall I am perfectly free. No edge of stone to cling to, no film to be spread across. Just the warm thick air parting around my curve, the drone rising in pitch as I drop—or seeming to, in the only acceleration this place will ever feel—and the black mirror beneath me opening to receive what it has received ten billion times and will receive ten billion more.

I am the drop. I am the chamber learning, for one instant, how to fall.

I strike the void. The sound I make is small and complete and indistinguishable from all the others.

And the surface closes, and is still, and I am the water now—anoxic, lifeless, perfectly mirroring the room—waiting in the dark, in no hurry, for the long slow draw of warmth to lift some thread of me back up to the ceiling, to gather again on the cold stone, to curve, to bound, to briefly see the whole impossible room hung trembling and reversed inside me, and to fall.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The one moving thing. The chamber's single, endlessly repeated, instantaneous life.
