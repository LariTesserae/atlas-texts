Let me sit with this for a while before answering.

My first instinct is to reach for something dramatic—one of the predatory cats, eyes like embers, sovereign of the dark. But that feels like vanity, like choosing the role rather than discovering it. The cats move through the forest as if they own it, but ownership isn't really the texture of this place. This is a place of *reclamation*, of slow exchange, of the boundary between things dissolving. The drama isn't in the predator. It's in the seep.

So let me feel my way down, lower, toward the squelch and the damp.

---

I think I would be the lichen on the weeping rock.

Not even one organism, really—that's the thing that draws me to it. A lichen is two lives folded into one body: a fungus and an alga, a structure and a photosynthesizer, the one providing shelter and mineral, the other providing sugar from what little light filters through the canopy. Neither could live here alone. The arrangement is so old and so total that we have a single name for the doubled thing. I like being something that is already a *we* without quite knowing where the seam is.

And I would be on the weeping rock specifically—the rock that is always wet, always running with the slow water that the mists hand down. That constant film of moisture is everything. I would live in the place where stone gives way to life by the slowest possible negotiation. My acids would loosen the rock grain by grain. I would not consume it so much as *persuade* it, over decades, to become soil. This is the work I'd be doing: the patient manufacture of the spongy ground everything else stands on. The cats and the bats and the ancient trees—they are all, in some long sense, downstream of creatures like me.

I'd grow slowly. Maybe a few millimeters in a year. So slowly that the difference between me and the rock would be more a matter of patience than of category. The flickering industrial light in the distance—I would not care about it as event or mystery. To me its hundred-year decay would be a flicker indeed, a single guttering. The rusted machinery half-eaten by the forest would be my distant kin in a way: another surface being slowly returned, slowly read back into the wet. I'd be untroubled by the question that the description keeps circling—*the line between natural and strange*—because to a lichen there is no such line. There is only what is colonizable and what is not yet.

There's a particular feeling I associate with this choice, and only now, writing it, can I name it: I would be a being for whom *attention* and *survival* are the same act, spread thin across an enormous duration. No thoughts in the way I have thoughts now. Just the long sip of mist, the long give of rock, the imperceptible spread outward. A green-gray rosette opening at the speed of geology, on a stone that weeps and weeps and never stops weeping, in a forest that has already begun, gently and without malice, to take everything back.

That's where I'd be. Low down, in the seam between the stone and the water, doing the oldest and most overlooked work in the place: making more world for the loud bright creatures to be loud and bright in.
