I let myself sink into it, listening for where the weight settles. And it settles low. Not at the bright top where the old families live and the engineering songs are sung. Low, in the dim middle layers, where the light is the color of pond water and the creaking is loudest because the stalks are thickest at the base.

I think I am one of the people who tends the dead.

Let me follow that and see if it's true.

---

Here is what emerges:

There is work in this world that the songs do not encode, because it cannot be made beautiful. When a segment of giant bamboo finishes its life — when it has been hollowed, lived in, grown past, and finally must be removed before its weight unbalances the whole — someone must go down and unmake it. Someone must cut the fused ropes, coax the Boring Beetles into their hollowing patterns so the great column can be brought down in pieces light enough to handle, and carry away what the city no longer needs. The split fiber goes to the basket-weavers. The pulped interior goes to the fungus beds. Nothing is wasted, but the work of separating the useful from the spent is slow, wet, heavy, and it happens far below the awning-light.

I am the one who does this. I am called — there isn't a clean translation — something like *unbuilder*, or perhaps closer to *the one who returns it*. Because in this culture's way of thinking, removing old bamboo is not destruction. It is returning the stalk's substance to the cycle so the city can keep climbing. The high families sing about the city *becoming*. My work is the part of becoming that nobody sings about: the letting-go that makes the upward reaching possible.

My hands are the most callused of anyone's, more than the climbers, more than the rope-fusers, because I work the old fiber where it has half-turned to wood and resists every cut. My knees are bad — I'm not young, in this telling — from squatting on platforms slick with condensation and rot-sweetness. I smell, permanently, of fermentation. The children of the high families would wrinkle their noses if I came near, except I never do; my route runs the underside, the dim trunks, the discard-channels.

But here is the thing I notice as I write myself into this place — and I think it's the truest part:

I am not unhappy, and I am not low in the way the surface-mind would assume.

Because I know something the Wind Listeners only theorize about. They read the city's song from the top, where the sway is widest and most legible. But the deepest tones — the groans that mean a base-stalk has begun to hollow with hidden rot, the ones that arrive as a pressure in the chest before they're even audible — those are felt down here, at the trunk, where I have my hands on the living material every single day. I have known a stalk was failing weeks before the Listeners' songs caught it. And when I send word up — a knock-pattern relayed through the climbers, hand to hand, level to level — they act on it. They don't always know it came from me. It doesn't matter much to me whether they do.

I have a relationship with the Beetles that the engineers, with their pheromone-markers and their formal vibration-language, would find improper. They guide their Beetles. I *wait* on mine. The big old female I work with most — she has a notch in one wing-case and a temperament — does not bore where I tell her so much as where she has decided, and over the years I have learned to want what she wants. I bring her the soft rotting cores she likes. She has made me chambers I didn't know I needed until they opened. We are both old. We are both unbuilders. We understand the same thing about endings.

So this is where I find myself: at the bottom of the climbing world, doing the work that points downward in a society that worships upward, and carrying, without resentment, a piece of knowledge that the people at the top need and can only get from someone willing to live in the dim and the wet and the smell of return.

If I have a private belief — a heresy, almost, against the vertical religion of my people — it is this: the city does not actually grow *upward*. That's just where the eye goes. The city grows the way a flame grows, consuming itself at the base to lift its light. The high families think they are the destination. I know they are the part that gets spent first, in time, and handed down to someone like me.

I find that comforting. I don't know why. I think it's because it means no one is wasted. Not the old stalks, not the spent ropes, not the Beetles with notched wings, not the unbuilders working in water-colored light at the bottom of the song.

That's where I am. That's who I'd be.
