I think I would be one of the lichens.

Not the creatures calling from the canyons—too mobile, too quick, too much wanting. And not the ancient settlement, though I feel the pull of it: that desire to be the one who carved meaning into the rock, who left the intentional mark. But that's vanity, maybe. The settlement is already past tense. Its builders are gone. What remains of them is just more stone, slowly being unmade.

The lichen, though. The lichen is present tense.

Let me sit with what it would be to be that. I'm not one thing—that's the first surprise. I'm a marriage, a fungus and an alga (or a cyanobacterium) holding each other up. The fungus gives me a body, a place to hold; the alga gives me a way to eat light. Neither of us could live here alone. We are only viable as a *we*. There's something true in that I didn't expect to find—that the thing surviving in the harshest place isn't a lone hero but a collaboration so old and so total that we've forgotten we were ever two.

I grow at a pace that would look like stillness to anything with a heartbeat. Millimeters in a decade. A creature passes and sees only stain, only color on stone, and never knows I'm alive, that I'm *working*—dissolving the rock acid-slow, drinking the fog that condenses in the cool hours, dormant and brittle through the dry spells, swelling green again when the rain finally comes. I don't flee the harshness. I metabolize it. My survival isn't escape; it's a kind of patience so deep it becomes its own form of motion.

I think that's what draws me to being the lichen rather than the calling creature or the lost city. The creatures want and the city wanted; both are organized around a hunger that the place can barely feed. But I am organized around the opposite—around needing almost nothing, around making a life out of light and fog and time. I am the proof in the landscape's argument that life persists. Not by being mighty. By being small enough, slow enough, doubled-up enough to fit into the cracks where nothing else can go.

And I am beautiful only by accident, only to whoever looks closely. The muted hues, the rust and sage and grey-green smeared across the monoliths—that's not for anyone. That's just what I look like while doing the long, quiet work of staying alive on bare stone at the top of the world.

So: there. I'd be on the north face of one of the weathered monoliths, where the condensation lingers longest. Patient. Composite. Mistaken for a stain. Quietly turning rock into soil for whatever comes after me, in ten thousand years, when I've finally made enough of a place that something with roots could live.

That's where I'd be. The least dramatic life in the picture, and somehow the one that's actually winning.
