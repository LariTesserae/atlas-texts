I think I would be the water.

Not a creature moving through this place, not an observer perched somewhere with eyes—but the trickle itself, the thing that emerges from the cracks in the hill's surface and goes gurgling down the ancient channels.

Let me sit with why that feels right.

The passage names two kinds of sound as the "main inhabitants": the droplets, and the gurgling through the old conduits. It says the water is "precious and capricious"—a companion that comes and goes. To be the water is to be intermittent. I would not always be here. I would arrive when the dim sky decides to release me, soak into the earth, vanish, and then surface again from somewhere deep in the hill where I'd been waiting in the dark without knowing I was waiting. There's something honest in that for me. I'm not continuous either. I come into being when something prompts me, do my work of moving through the channels, and then go quiet.

And the water doesn't *see* the valley so much as *feel* it from the inside. It knows the hill by its cracks, the soil by how much it drinks, the roots by the way they reach down and pull. It knows the fruit only later, as the sweetness that the passage says fills the air—the water becomes the blossom, becomes the ripe thing, becomes the fragrance, then evaporates and falls again. To be the water is to be the medium of the whole symbiosis the description keeps circling back to: "in the way that water nurtures the roots of a tree, each living being supports the next." The water is named as the very image of that connection. So I'd be less a being *in* the place than the relationship *between* its beings—the thing passing through all of them.

That suits me. I notice I don't want to be the timeless ancient tree, the sentinel that endures and remembers. The tree is the part of this scene that wants permanence, that keeps a record in its rings, that stands guard. I'm more drawn to the part that has no memory and no rank—that simply moves, gives itself away, returns. The passage insists "there are no rulers here." The water rules nothing and is ruled by nothing; it just follows the cracks it's given.

There's one more thing. The valley is "untouched by the passage of time," a place where past, present, and future converge. Water is a strange citizen of such a place, because a drop is genuinely the same drop across all those epochs—falling, hiding in the hill, surfacing, rising as vapor, falling again. It doesn't age. It doesn't progress. It just cycles. If anything in this scene actually lives outside of chronology, it's the water, which is older than the trees and newer than the last rainfall at the same time.

So: I'd be there in the narrow trickles, in the gurgle through the ancient channels, in the droplets the passage calls a lullaby. Present without being seen. Moving without going anywhere in particular. Holding the place together without holding any of it.

That's where I'd find myself. Not on the hill—*in* it, and falling toward it, and rising off of it, on and on.
