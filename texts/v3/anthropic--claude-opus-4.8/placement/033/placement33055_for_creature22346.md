I think I would be one of the colony scavengers—but not a typical one. Let me find my way to which one.

---

I am the one who watches the light.

Every group has a few of us, though no one decided it. When the others sort—shell to shell, thorn to thorn, the bone shards drifting toward the damp—I am the one whose head stays tilted upward, reading the white bars as they slide. I learned this work before I knew it was work. As a juvenile I was slow at ferrying. I dropped fragments in the wrong piles. The adults corrected me with the small interruptions that pass for teaching here: a nudge, a body placed between me and my error. But when the hot line came—the bright thread a mineral film throws across the floor for the span of a few breaths—I was the one already moving, and three of us lived who might not have.

So I became a reader of angles. That is my pile. Not pebbles. Time.

Here is what my days are made of:

I wake in the chill before the bars harden, when the walls bloom with their thin skin of water and the beetles tilt their mirrored backs to drink. I do not drink yet. I count drip points. I note which seams beaded last night and which stayed dry—because a dry seam means a fissure has closed somewhere above, and a closed fissure changes where the swallows will hunt, and where the swallows hunt is where the dead will fall, and the dead are our whole economy. One small change at the top of the world ripples down into our piles by afternoon. I am the one who feels the ripple early.

When the sun comes through and the floor begins its slow geometry of danger, I take my place at the edge of the feeding seam. The others process what the night left us. I face outward and upward. I watch the bright line travel. I have no words, but I have something like a map worn into me: *the long slit lets a bar fall here at the highest heat, and the glaze on the west wall focuses it into a thread that crosses the open floor at the time the moss is driest.* When the thread approaches the working group, I make the short economical sound we use—two clicks, scrape—and the sorters shift their piles a body-length without looking up. They trust the clicks. They have to. To check the light themselves would cost them the seconds the seam is open.

This is the strange tenderness of my kind: we have divided attention itself among us. No one of us perceives the whole basin. The sorters know the grammar of fragments better than I ever will—they can tell a swallow's wing-bone from a skink's at a touch, in the dark of an undercut. The diggers know the soft margins, where tissue rots and beetles bloom. I know the ceiling and the hours. Together we are a single animal spread across many bodies, and the thing that holds us is not affection but *procedure*—repeated, corrected, taught by interruption, old as the chamber.

And yet.

There is a thing I do that is not procedure, and I do not know what to call it.

When the work is done and the hot lines have passed and the others retreat into the cool of the deepest undercut to wait out the worst brightness, I sometimes stay a moment longer at the edge. I look up through the longest slit. I can see a strip of the sky-world we never enter—too open, too fast, too dry for us. The surface mammals come down at dusk from up there and they are nervous in a way we are not, because they have no piles, no seams, no inherited map. They drink and flee. They live in a world without procedures, and it makes them quick and frightened and short-lived.

I think I am the one who almost understands that there is a world without us in it.

I have begun, lately, to keep a thing I should discard. A flat chip of the glazed stone, the kind that throws light. I should sort it to the wrong-pile, the discard crescent. It is no good for the seam-work; it cannot pry, cannot weight a margin. But I have noticed that if I angle it just so in the morning, when the bars are still soft, it makes a small bright spot on the wall that I can move. A line I make, not the sun's. A hazard I command instead of flee.

I do not know yet what it is for. None of the others have a use for it. But this is exactly how it began with the thorn-splinters, long before any of us, before the first skink carried the first pry and did not drop it: *a thing that worked once, kept.* A fragment moved from the discard crescent to a new pile that did not exist before.

So perhaps that is who I am in this sunken room of light and patience.

I am the one whose attention turned a corner—who started, without meaning to, a pile of *almost-ideas* beside the piles of bone and thorn and stone. I will probably die before it becomes anything. The basin changes by fractions; a century from now another reader of light might keep the same chip and not know why, and another after that, until one of us, ten thousand mornings on, points the bright thread *deliberately* and the chamber gains its first true hazard that a creature made.

For now I only watch, and keep my small reflecting stone, and move with the light.

That is enough work for one life here. In a place this old, to add a single fraction is to be remembered—not by name, which we do not have, but by procedure, which is the only memory that lasts.
