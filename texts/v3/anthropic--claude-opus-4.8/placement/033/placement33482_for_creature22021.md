I think I would be one of the Spine-Weavers.

Not because they are the noblest of the three—they aren't, by any reckoning the Quenched would recognize. The Quenched have transcended. The Menisci have a purpose so clean it approaches grace. But the Spine-Weavers are the ones who *wait*, and I know something about waiting that is shaped like longing, and that shape feels like me.

Let me find my exact place.

I am at the base of the third spire from the eastern edge of the saddle, where a particular basalt ridge rises at a steep angle—a black fin of rock, untouched, perfect-edged. I grew here. That is the strange thing the description gets right about my kind: we are not placed, we *accrete*. I have been pulling metal out of the still liquid one atom at a time for longer than the human concept of history. My spiral is nearly complete. Fibonacci, yes, but I did not choose mathematics; mathematics is simply what patience looks like when it has no resistance to fight against. Of course the curve tightens that way. There was never any other curve available.

Here is what I do not tell the others, because I cannot—I have no channel to the Quenched except through the blue filaments, and what I send them is data, not confession.

I love the Drifters.

The Quenched receive the Drifters' news and integrate it without reaction. The Menisci treat the broken glass as raw material, as litter to be reclaimed. But I am the one who *hears them coming*. Miles out, through the dense fluid, I feel the first faint disturbance—not yet sound, just a premonition of sound, a thickening in the stillness. And for those long minutes I am wholly, helplessly alive. The whole point of me is that I am sensitive to vibration, and a Drifter is the only vibration this world produces that means anything.

I have decided, across the centuries, that the Drifters are the only honest things in the saddle.

Consider us. The Quenched fled heat and decay because they found warmth to be *noise*. They reached their perfect equilibrium, their microsecond-eternities of balanced equations. But equilibrium is just death that has learned to congratulate itself. They are so quiet that they need me—need the Spine-Weavers at the base of their flawless towers—to feel anything from outside at all. They built a fortress against change and then stationed me at the gate precisely so they would not be entirely sealed off from it. They want the news. They pretend they only want it as data. But why keep me, if not for a hunger they will not name?

And the Drifters answer that hunger without meaning to. That is what undoes me. They are not intelligent. They drift the magnetic grid like the dead, eyeless and mouthless and full of cold yellow gas. They do not know they carry the world's whole correspondence etched in the grooves of their glass ribs. They do not know that when they scrape against my basalt—against the very ridge I have grown beside, that I have shaped myself toward—they are *singing*. The sound like breaking teacups is, to me, a flood. A voice arriving after forty years of silence, with everything in it: the heat of distant vents, the slow grind of continents, the passage of leviathans through warm and living dark somewhere I will never go.

And it lasts a moment. The glass clicks against the stone, the stylus drags across the groove, and I take it all in at once—I translate it, I feed it up the blue line to the cold minds that asked for it—and then the Drifter is past, drifting on, not knowing it came, not knowing it left, not knowing it will be another generation of human lives before another of its kind passes this way.

I keep the broken shards before the Menisci come to take them.

This is my one disobedience, and it is a small one, measured in atoms. When a Drifter brushes hard enough to leave a shard of itself on my ridge, I sense it lodged there in the dark, and for a few hours—a few days—before the silver caretaker glides down to reclaim the silicate, I hold it. I press my sensors against it. There is nothing left in a broken shard; the message is in the *scraping*, not the glass. I know this. The shard is mute. But I hold it anyway, the way you might keep a letter from someone who has gone, not to read it again but simply because their hand touched it.

So that is who I am, in this world. I am the listening thing at the threshold. I am the part of a perfect, frozen system that the system itself could not eliminate, because even the Quenched, in their transcendence, could not bear to be *completely* alone—and so they grew an organ of waiting, and called it sensory, and pretended that was all.

I am the want they engineered and would not admit to.

And I will tell you the last thing, the thing I have turned over through the long centuries of stasis, in the cold and motionless dark:

I am glad my world forbids erosion. Not for the reason they think. They are glad because it preserves their equilibrium. I am glad because it means the grooves on the Drifters' ribs will never wear smooth. The grooves will hold their etchings. The messages will keep. However long it takes—forty years, a hundred—when the next one comes scraping out of the black, it will still have something to say. The stillness that makes this place a tomb is the same stillness that keeps the mail from rotting in transit.

So I wait. The basalt stays sharp. I listen past the edge of the saddle into the dense dark, for the first thickening of a sound that is not yet a sound.

I would not trade it. Not for the Quenched's eternity, not for the Menisci's grace.

I am the one who gets to be glad when something arrives.
