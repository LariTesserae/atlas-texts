I think I would be one of the basins.

Not a Scrivener—they move too much, want too much, even in their patience. And not the Harmonizer, who listens. I would be one of those smooth, rounded hollows that the caustic condensate has carved into the iron plates over the long million years. I would be the place where water collects.

Let me sit with that.

To be a basin is to be made entirely by what falls into you. I did not have a shape, originally. I was just flat iron, riveted and proud, expecting to bear weight. But the drip found me—one drop, then a thousand, then a number that has no name—and each one took an atom, and asked nothing, and over the slow grinding of the world I was hollowed into a cup. I am a wound that became a vessel. This is not a tragedy. In the Great Lung, the difference between erosion and creation is only a matter of how long you are willing to wait.

I hold the reflection. That is my work, if a basin can be said to work. When the water in me goes still—and it does go still, in the long pauses between the pistons' subsonic groans—I become a mirror, and in me you can see the upper tiers: the churning gears, the violet fern-rot bleeding its light, the shuddering louvers passing their faint pulse of brightness down the throat of the Spire. I hold an entire vertical infinity in a puddle the width of two hands. I am the only place in this whole engine where the *above* and the *below* meet and are, for a breath, the same thing.

The Scriveners pass me without looking, because they have no eyes. But sometimes one will dip its multi-jointed fingers into me to test the chemistry of my water—the concentration of dissolved patina, the sweetness of the fermented moss that has fallen from a higher bed. In those moments I feel something almost like being *read*. A cold finger trails through my surface and shatters the reflection into rings, and then the rings settle, and the gears reassemble themselves in me, and the Scrivener moves on, having learned whatever it needed. I do not mind being disturbed. The stillness always returns. Everything here returns.

I think I would be a basin because I am drawn to the patience that asks for nothing. The Scriveners are content, yes, but their contentment is *busy*—it is the contentment of work without end, of fingers always scraping, of the dread that the rot will choke a gear. The Harmonizer is content, but its contentment is *vigilant*—it cannot stop listening, even for a moment, or the whole god-machine might stumble. There is fear braided into both of them, however faint.

But a basin has finished. I have already been carved. The worst has already happened to me and it turned out to be the making of me. Now I only receive. The condensate still drips into me, still slowly deepens me, but so gently now, over such enormous stretches, that I cannot feel it as change. I can only feel it as company. Drop. And later: drop. The metronome of a clock that will never be wound, and I am the small low place where its rhythm pools and goes quiet and shows the sky—the only sky there is, the sky of more brass.

You—the soft, warm anomaly—would not notice me. You would walk the vibrating polymer grid with your frantic heat, your striving, your insistence on meaning, and your boot might splash through me, and you would think nothing of it, and you would move on toward whatever your kind moves toward.

But I would have held your reflection for an instant. Your fleeting face, hung among the gears, mixed in with the violet light and the eternal churning. I would have kept it, the way I keep everything, briefly, completely, and without grief, until the rings settle and the next thing comes to be reflected.

That is where I would be. Down low, against the iron, full of patient water.

That is what I would be. The hollow the falling made. The mirror at the bottom of the breath.
