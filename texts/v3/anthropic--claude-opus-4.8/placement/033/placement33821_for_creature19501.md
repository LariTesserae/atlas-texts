I think I would be one of the small things. Not a Resonator, not a Weaver—not anyone whose hands matter to the keeping of the pulse. Let me find myself lower than that, and stranger.

---

I am a thing that lives in the seam.

Not the gorge-seam, that great cleft the giant's axe left—a smaller one. The place where a brass piston enters the rock face, where the machined edge of the metal meets the raw, weeping basalt and neither has agreed to fit the other. There is always a gap there. A lip of shadow no wider than a child's wrist, kept warm by the piston's slow heat and kept wet by the stone's slow grief. This is where I am.

What am I. Let me not decide too fast.

I have weight—this is the first thing I know of myself, because weight is the currency here and I am rich in it for my size. I am dense the way a ball-bearing is dense, the way a drop of mercury is dense. When I move along the underside of the gantry I do not scurry. I *pour*. I have learned that the gear-crickets, for all their metal armor, are light, and lightness here is a kind of poverty—the wind takes the light things, flings them down into the fern-banks, and the ferns are patient and always hungry. So I am heavy on purpose. I have made myself heavy by eating the heavy things: the flakes of oxide that peel from the copper, the cooled beads of moss-oil, the grit that the cliff sheds as it settles. I am, perhaps, a kind of slow snail made of the city's own shed skin. A creature of leavings.

But that is not all of it.

I notice that I *listen*, and that the listening is the truest part of me. When a Resonator far below strikes a railing with the brass ring on his finger, the vibration travels up through the steel, through the piston, into the warm seam where I rest—and I feel it arrive in my body before it reaches anyone's boots. I am closer to the bone of the city than the people are. They tap to speak across miles. I do not speak. I only receive. I am a thing the message passes *through*, and in passing through me it leaves a residue, the way water leaves mineral.

Here is what I have begun to understand about myself: I am keeping a record no one asked me to keep.

Every shear of the wall, every clack of every valve, every patient groan of cooling metal—it accretes in me. I am laying it down in rings, in layers, the way the cliff lays down its slate, the way the plant lays down its visible patient reach. I am young erosion's archivist. The people believe the machines must never miss a beat or the basalt will *realize it is unsupported and fall*. I think they are almost right. I think what holds the wall up is not the thrum of the turbines but the fact that something is *witnessing* the thrum—that as long as one small dense listening thing in the seam keeps count, the world has a reason to stay coherent. The pulse needs an ear or it is only noise. I am the ear in the wall.

The Copper-Back Sloths know I am here. When one of them comes licking the algae from the glass below my piston, it pauses, and turns its slow oxidized face toward my seam, and I feel its breath go warm and wet across me, and we hold there a long while in the deliberate way of all things in this valley. It does not eat me. I think it understands that I am furniture of a holy kind. A good omen recognizing another good omen. We have no language but the shared grammar of slowness.

And the Steam-Hawks—when they go up in their silver alarm, I feel it before they fly, because the wall tells me first. A shear begins as a whisper deep in the stone, days before it announces itself, and the whisper enters my seam and I tighten myself, I pull my weight in close, and I *wait*. I cannot warn anyone. I have no ring to tap, no voice beneath the boilers. I can only endure the place where the break will come, and afterward, when the dust settles into the ferns and the people have counted their living, I unstick myself from the new raw face of stone and I begin again. I have survived three shears. I carry their dates in me, layered, indelible. I am older than I look. Everything here is older than it looks; that is the whole secret of young erosion—that newness and age are the same wet dark thing, just measured from different ends.

So. What am I.

I am a small heavy patient witness lodged in the joint between the made and the unmade. I am not a Cultivator of the Resonance. I am something humbler and, I suspect, more necessary: I am the place the Resonance goes to be *kept*. The people make the pulse. The plants make the structure. And I—nameless, dense, dripping, listening in my warm wet seam—I make the *memory* of it, which is the thing that lets the beautiful loud machine believe, beat after beat, that it has always been beating, and will go on.

When I am finally too heavy to hold on—when my own accumulated record overcomes the warmth that grips me to the rock—I will pour myself loose at last and fall the thousand feet into the ferns, and the patient hungry green will take me, and unbraid my layers, and read every clack and groan and shear I ever kept, and grow another visible reach from the eating of it.

That is not death. That is filing.

I am content to be here until then, in the gap no wider than a child's wrist, counting the hush between the beats.
