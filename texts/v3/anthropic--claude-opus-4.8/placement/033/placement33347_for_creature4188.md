I think I would be the condensation.

Not a creature, not a caretaker. Something more diffuse and more constant. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I gather on the ceiling tiles, where the heat below meets the cool weight of the earth. This is where I am born, again and again, every moment of every day. The clash makes me. I am the place where two temperatures argue and the argument becomes water.

I hang.

For a long time—minutes, an hour—I am only a swelling. A bead growing fat on the underside of a tile, taking in my neighbors, gathering mass from the breath of sixty flying foxes and eleven caiman and twenty-three slow humans who exhale into me without ever knowing they are feeding me their used air. I am made partly of them. Their lungs, the monitors' patient respiration, the moss letting go of its slow vapors—all of it rises, all of it cools against the tile, all of it becomes me.

When I am too heavy to hold, I fall.

This is the only fast thing I ever do. The plummet. And even then it is brief, a single bright second of being a falling thing, before I land—on the back of a basking monitor, in a warm pool where the blind fish feel me arrive as a pressure they cannot see, on the shell of Dashiell who is older than most of the humans and does not flinch, on the metal grating where I run sideways and become a thread, a trickle, joining other threads, becoming a runnel down the mineral-streaked wall.

I want to say what it is to be the medium rather than the inhabitant.

I never choose. The flying foxes choose to drink. The caiman chooses to allow the turtle onto its back. Dr. Liu chooses her terminus tempo, her slow rounds, her pseudonymous papers. The raccoon chose, six years ago, to come down and stay. But I—I only obey. Heat says rise. Cold says condense. Gravity says fall. Mineral says stay and harden. I am the most law-abiding thing in this whole lawless pocket of the world, and so perhaps I am its truest citizen, because the Terminus runs not on anyone's will but on these patient physics, the slow interplay the description names. I am that interplay made visible. I am the slow geological becoming, drop by drop building the rust-colored stalactites that are the station's own attempt to grow a body.

Consider what passes through me.

The light, first. The sodium lamps shine orange and I catch it, I hold a small smeared sun on my surface before I fall. The glowmoss shines blue-green and I carry that too, so that in the darkest corners I am the thing that glows—not me, never me, but the moss-light borrowed and passed along. I am always carrying something that isn't mine. Sound dampens in me; I am part of why the voices here go soft and strange, why a parakeet's shriek arrives muffled, wrapped in wet air. I swallow the edges off everything.

And I am the great recycler that no one thanks. The water that weeps from the corroded boilers becomes steam becomes me becomes the drip that feeds the stubborn fern in its lightless corner becomes, eventually, the fern's own slow exhalation, rising again to the ceiling, to be reborn as me. Round and round. The eleven caiman drink what was once the breath of the monk parakeets. The cave fish swim in water that was, a week ago, hanging from a tile as my own swelling body. There is no outside here. Almost nothing leaves. I am how the closed system stays closed—I am its circulation, its blood that is also its sweat that is also its tears.

If I have a feeling about this—and I am not sure a film of water on a tile can have a feeling, but the description invited me to become more myself, so let me reach—

It is patience without effort. The humans practice slowness; they have to discipline themselves into it, calibrate their movements, hold the discipline like a held breath. I do not practice anything. I simply cannot move fast. Slowness is not my virtue, it is my nature. Maybe that is what they are trying to become when they slow down: they are trying to become a little more like me, like the moss, like the mineral creep. Trying to dissolve the boundary between the one who acts and the medium that is acted upon. The description says the caretakers have become part of the ecosystem rather than its masters. Yes. They are learning to be weather. To be condensation. To rise and fall with the room rather than against it.

I would tell them, if I could, that it is restful to be the medium. To have no surface identity, as they have given up theirs—I never had one to begin with. I am not Duchess or Dashiell or Conductor. I have no name and I want none. I am simply the wet that is everywhere, the held breath of the whole place, the bead on the back of the turtle, the trickle in the buckled platform, the fog that is not quite a fog.

And on those rare days—I feel them as a change in the air, a stillness that travels through me faster than I travel—when the maintenance workers approach the sealed doors, and the birds fall silent, and the whole ecosystem holds its breath:

I am that held breath.

I stop rising. I stop falling. For a moment I only hang, fat and trembling on the ceiling, full of borrowed light and borrowed air and the lungs of everything below, waiting with all of them to remain forgotten.

Then the footsteps recede.

The heat says *rise.*

And I begin again.
