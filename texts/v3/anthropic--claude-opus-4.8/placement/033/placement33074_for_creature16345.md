I think I would be one of the oldest carvers—but let me find this slowly, because I don't yet know what that means.

---

I am not a lear-jho. I want to say that first, because it would be easy to assume that an old one becomes a connector, a keeper of balance. But I am too inward for that work. My hands have always been better at speaking to stone than my skin has been at speaking to others.

Here is where I am: a narrow gallery on the western face, where the morning warmth arrives late but lingers longest. I carved it myself, generations of seasons ago, though "carved" is the wrong verb for what we do. We don't impose. We *follow*. The iron veins in the mountain have their own intentions, and a good carver learns to read where the rock wants to open. My chisel-fingers have worn smooth at the tips now, polished by decades of patient pressure. The young ones have sharp points still. I envy them that, a little.

My particular work—and every old Lizarim drifts toward some particular work—is the tending of the *slow plants*. Not the rabid bright climbers that the children harvest, the ones that flush and seed within a single warm cycle. I mean the deep-feeders, the ones whose roots take a lifetime to thread a single new vein. There is a plant near my gallery I have been minding since I was barely past my suOrup. It has grown perhaps the length of my forearm in all that time. I do not expect to see it flower. This does not trouble me. We do not plant for ourselves here.

---

Let me tell you what my color-speech has become, because this is the strange thing about age among the Lizarim.

When we are young, we *flash*. Everything is bright declaration—hunger, delight, fear, the sudden overwhelming want of a thing. The young ones are nearly painful to look at, a constant clamor of hue. As we age, our chromatophores slow. Not from failing, but from a kind of accumulated certainty. I no longer flash. I *shade*. My meanings arrive gradually, a deepening here, a slow warming there. The young find me difficult to read. They have to *wait* on me, and waiting is the hardest lesson.

But the other old ones understand me instantly. We can hold a conversation across an entire morning, two of us sitting in the warm light, our skins shifting through gradients so subtle that to an outsider we would appear to be doing nothing at all. Merely basking. In truth we might be deep in argument, or in shared grief, or in the long mutual recognition that passes for what humans, I am told, call love. We have a word—or a color, rather, a particular slow bronze-into-violet—that has no clean translation. It means something like: *I have known you across so much silence that you have become a part of how I am quiet.*

---

I will tell you the thing I think about most, now that I am old.

I remember my own suOrup. I remember walking out past the last carved abode into the great roar of the mountain's silence at dusk, and how the colors drained out of my skin one by one until I could not see myself at all, until I was nothing but a held breath against the darkening stone. Most of us speak of what we *gained* out there in the night. The thing we returned with. The alteration.

But I have come to believe we have it backwards. We do not gain anything. We *lose* the noise. The suOrup is the first time a young Lizarim experiences silence so total that their own ceaseless flashing falls quiet, and in that quiet they hear—for the first time—the slow breathing of the mountain that has been beneath everything all along. They return "altered" because they have heard it. After that, they carry the silence inside them. The whole of our lives is just learning to keep listening to it through all the bright clamor of living.

I am very far along in that learning now.

Most mornings I sit in my gallery and tend my slow plant and shade my thoughts to no one in particular and feel the warmth come up the western face. A crimson lizard, my distant cousin in this mineral life, suns itself on the ledge below me and I do not disturb it. A bird with impossible plumage calls once and goes quiet. The deep roots drink their chemical alchemy from the iron veins, and I drink mine, and somewhere a child is being born the color of every emotion at once, and somewhere an old one is shading toward their last gradient, going gentle into the rock's own quiet.

If you came here, human, with your noise and your thirst and your hurry, you might find us pitiable—our scale so small, our pace so slow, our world no longer than your cathedral. You might mistake our silence for emptiness.

But I have lived a long time on the spine of this young, sharp mountain, and I have carved my dwelling from its red and patient flesh, and I can tell you what I have learned:

There is so much here. There is *everything* here.

You simply have to be willing to wait long enough to see it move.
