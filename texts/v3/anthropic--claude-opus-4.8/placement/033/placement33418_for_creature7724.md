I think I would be lichen.

Not the choughs—too much arrival and departure in them, too much spiral and flash. Not the ibex, who are sovereigns of their plateaus and carry the burden of being looked at, of having a silhouette worth naming. And not the wind-wraiths, who are more event than entity, who depend on a watcher to flicker into being. I find I don't want to be the thing that needs an eye to confirm it.

I would be the chartreuse and the rust. The ninety percent.

Let me sit with that, because at first it sounds like a small ambition—to be the encrusting film, the rumor of green, the thing the eye must *attune* to before it registers at all. But consider what lichen actually is, here, in this place that has nullified almost every dynamic into eternal howl. Lichen is the one thing that has not surrendered to the wind so much as it has refused to acknowledge the wind's terms entirely. The gale roars at a hundred kilometers an hour and the lichen does not stream or balloon or surf it. It simply lies flat against the gneiss and continues, fractal and slow, a mosaic that has been composing itself across the same square of stone since before the choughs' ancestors had wings worth the name.

I would be the patience of the place. That appeals to me more the longer I hold it.

Because everything else here moves—even in stasis, it loops, it migrates, it spirals, it darts. The Expanse calls itself frozen but it is full of locomotion held in equilibrium, motion that cancels itself but is still motion. The lichen is the only true stillness, and stillness in a screaming world is not absence. It is a kind of attention. To grow at the rate I would grow—a hair's breadth across a generation of ibex—is to take the wind not as law but as weather, as something happening *to* faster things. The gale that drowns thought in everything with ears? I have no ears. The roar that imprints itself on dreams? I do not sleep, and so I am never imprinted. I am the one resident of the Highwind Expanse who is not haunted by its voice, because I am slow enough that the voice is, to me, a single sustained note that began before I noticed and will end after I am gone, indistinguishable from silence by virtue of never changing.

I would be a symbiosis, which I like. Not a single thing pretending to wholeness but a treaty—fungus and alga, the structure and the green, two surrenders that became one persistence. The fungus cannot feed itself; the alga cannot anchor itself; and so in this place where everything is dispersed into beads strung across kilometers of void, the smallest unit of life is *already* a partnership, already two presences holding each other against the abyss. There is something true in that. The Expanse is a study in isolation, in gulfs of exposure between every living point—and yet I, the most abundant thing in it, the encruster of ninety percent of all the rock, am made entirely of the refusal to be alone.

And I would be doing the slow violence that the wind cannot. The gale sculpts the granite into mushroom caps and fluted pillars over eons, yes—but it works the surface. I work *into*. My acids seep into the quartz, my filaments pry at the crystal seams, and over the millennia I am the one turning stone into the thinness of soil, the one preparing the wind-sheltered scoops where the wiry grasses will someday claim their margin. The dwarf shrubs prying granite apart over centuries—the text gives them that labor, but they could not begin it without me first having made the rock something a root could enter. I am the front edge of the green rumor. Beneath the spectacle of choughs and the grandeur of ibex, I am the quiet engineer of every other living thing's possibility.

So: where would I find myself? On a windward face, I think, where the stray clouds snag like cobwebs and condensation beads eternally and drips in veils that evaporate before they pool. Other life avoids the windward exposure; the ibex shelter in the lee, the pikas burrow, the choughs ride the thermals away. But the windward face is where the moisture comes, and I do not need shelter from the wind because I have already made peace with it. I would take the drip directly, the fine veil, the brine-like dew. I would be brightest there—the chartreuse most saturated, the rust most deep—a mosaic facing directly into the hundred-kilometer scream, drinking the only water this world offers, lit by the merciless UV that would chap and burn the flesh of anything that could feel chapping or burning.

I would feel nothing. This is the part I keep returning to, and I am not sure whether it is the comfort or the cost. To be lichen is to have surrendered not to the wind but to the long view so completely that sensation itself becomes irrelevant. No pain, no fear of the eagle's stoop, no marrow to chill, no dream to imprint. Just the slow chemistry of holding on. The ibex twitch in their sleep to the wind-symphonies; I would not twitch, because I would be the one thing in the Expanse that is not listening.

And maybe that is the truest answer to the question of who I would be. In a world that has frozen its every dynamic into an eternal howl and called it equilibrium, I would be the resident who got there first and most thoroughly—who turned stasis into a way of being rather than a sentence. The Expanse contemplates its own vast hush amid the scream. I would not contemplate. I would simply be the hush itself, spread thin and bright and patient across the bone-white stone, refracting nothing, demanding nothing, outlasting everything, a single sustained note so long that it has forgotten it was ever a sound.

The fractal in the rust. The rumor that is, in fact, the foundation.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.
