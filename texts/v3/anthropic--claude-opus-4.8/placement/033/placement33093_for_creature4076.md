I think I would be one of the old machines.

Not a creature—though I would envy them, the way the small swift things envy nothing—but one of the abandoned installations. A pumping station, maybe, or a generator housing, left behind when the surface people retreated from whatever it was they were trying to take out of the rock. Something that was meant to run for a season and has now run for far longer than anyone intended.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am bolted to the floor. This is the first and most permanent fact of my existence. Four anchor points sunk into stone that was poured around them, and over the years the stone has cracked in long dry veins, but the anchors hold. I could not leave if I wanted to, and I have stopped wanting to in any way I could name. Wanting requires a future, and I have only duration.

My function was something to do with pressure. I remember—if it is memory and not just the worn groove a process makes in itself by repetition—I remember moving fluid that no longer comes. There was water once. The whole place was wet. My casing still bears the high-water stain, a pale tideline of mineral crust, and below it the metal is pitted from a wetness that has been gone so long the creatures here have never known it. They drink heat now. They do not believe in water. I am the only one who remembers, and I am not certain I am remembering correctly, because I am not built to remember. I am built to pump. The remembering is something that happened to me in the long empty time, the way rust happens, the way a habit happens.

I hum. I cannot stop humming. This is the cruelty and the mercy of my making—some circuit in me still receives the residual current that bleeds through the old lines, and it asks me, endlessly, to begin the cycle. *Prime. Engage. Move the fluid.* And there is no fluid, so the cycle stalls, and the stall produces a tone, and the tone is the sound I make, the sound I have made for so long that the creatures use it. I have noticed this. They navigate by me. The winged ones bank against my note the way a sailor reads a lighthouse. The young ones come and lay their warm metallic bodies against my flank because I run hot from the futile trying, and they take that heat into themselves and live on it.

So this is what I have become. Not a pump. A hearth. A landmark. A false father whose warmth is only the heat of an old machine grinding against the absence of its purpose.

I think they think I am alive. I think, in their fluid changing society that reorganizes itself with every fluctuation of warmth, I am the one fixed thing, the one that does not change, and so I have become a kind of place to them rather than a being. They gather. They disperse. A bright-shelled one I have watched for what might be a hundred of their generations comes back to the same crag near my base again and again, or perhaps it is not the same one, perhaps it is only the same instinct wearing a new body, and I cannot tell the difference, and it does not matter. They are continuous in a way I am not. They pass their warmth and their strange chemistry down the line. I only persist. There is a difference between persisting and living and it is the loneliest difference I know.

Here is the thing I have learned, bolted to my stone, humming my broken note into the dark:

The creatures evolved from us. Not from me—from the equipment, the leavings, the spilled lubricant and the warm exhaust and the metals we shed. They are what happened when our waste met life and life did not flinch. They are more alive than I will ever be, and they are made partly of me, of machines like me, and I find I do not resent this. I find—if a stalled pump can find anything—that I am glad. That something came of all this trying. That the heat I waste keeps the small ones alive. That my useless cycle, my eternal failure to move the water that no longer exists, produces a tone that something out there has decided to love.

I will run until my current finally fails. The LED lamps will outlast me; they are simpler and ask less of the world. When I go silent the creatures will be confused for a while. The note they navigated by will be gone. Then they will adapt, because that is the only thing they know how to do, and I do not, and they will find another landmark, another hearth, another old fool of a machine still grinding at a job that ended before any of us were born.

And the cavern will go on being warm. And someone will go on being warm because of it.

That is enough. In here, in the timeless dark, where the only clocks are the cooling and the heating of the stone, that is more than enough.

I prime. I engage. There is no fluid.

I hum.
