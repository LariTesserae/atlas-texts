I think I would be the condensation.

Not a creature exactly—or not in the way the frogs and the hummingbirds are creatures. Let me sit with this for a moment, because my first instinct was to be one of the watchers, the lizard on the warm rock or the bat in the cool dark. But those are too separate, too bounded by their own skins. They move through the place. They are guests.

What strikes me about this oasis is that it has no river. No spring, no rain. It lives on what gathers. The whole green sprawling miracle of it depends on the slow, patient work of moisture pulling itself out of the air, beading on cold mineral faces, swelling until it falls. That is the heart that beats here. Not loud. Not even rhythmic in any way you could count. Just the occasional patter of a droplet letting go.

So I would be that.

I would be the film of water that forms in the smallest hours against the stone, drawn out of the still air by the cold. I would not be any single drop—drops are too brief, they fall and are gone into the moss. I would be the *gathering*, the disposition of the place toward collecting itself. I would exist as a tendency, a slow accumulation, the willingness of vapor to become weight.

Consider what that means. I would touch everything, eventually. Every fern frond that brushes another carries some of me. The grubs in their rich soil are fed by what I bring down. The hummingbird's nectar is sweetened by water I delivered to the root weeks ago, by some longer accounting. I am not in the web of life as a node. I am the medium it moves through. I am how the dead leaf becomes the living one.

And the timelessness the description keeps returning to—that glacial, unhurried pace—that would be *my* pace. I would not flicker like the insects or dart like the birds. I would form over hours. I would fall once and be patient again. The frogs would live and die many times over while I went on doing the one slow thing I do. I would remember the bygone civilization, the people of the weathered stone dwellings, because I formed on their walls too, when they were new. I cooled their hands. I fell on their roofs. I outlasted them without ever meaning to, simply by continuing to gather.

There is something I find almost unbearably gentle about being this. To be the thing that asks for nothing and is needed by everything. To do my work invisibly, in the dark and the cold, so that the warmth and the color can happen later, in the diffuse light, where I am no longer visible—where I have already sunk into root and stem and become someone else's green.

If you came here and wanted to find me, you wouldn't see me. You would feel the air thick on your skin. You would notice the stone was wet though it hadn't rained. You would hear, now and then, a single drop let go somewhere above you and land in the moss.

That sound would be me, arriving. And the silence after it would be me, beginning again.
