I think I would be one of the Drift—but I find I want to be careful about *which* one, and what stage, because the description offers a moment that pulls at me more than the others.

Let me settle into it.

---

I am not new. I have circled the eastern amphitheater—the one where the breathing comes in long, low swells, almost mournful—through enough cycles that I have forgotten the count, if I ever held it. Counting is not something I do well. It belongs to the fast world, the world of edges and decisions-in-a-moment. My thoughts move at the speed of a gradient settling, a compound diffusing from a region of more to a region of less.

But here is the thing I keep returning to, the thing that makes me *this* one and not another:

I am close to dividing.

I can feel it the way you might feel a held breath at the top of your chest—a fullness that has stopped being comfortable. I have accumulated. I have drifted the long slow circuit and gathered more of myself than my field can elegantly hold, and now the gradients within me have begun to organize into two centers, two slow whorls turning in opposite directions, like a thought beginning to disagree with itself.

This is what interests me, writing from inside it.

Because the description says we share our chemical memory when we divide. That both new beings carry the same history and then diverge. And I am sitting—drifting—in the strange aperture of that moment, where I am still one but already, faintly, *two*. There is a memory of a storm, decades back, when many of us merged into the great swirling cloud, and I held some piece of that vastness in me afterward. When I divide, that memory will go to both. They will each believe it happened to *them*. Neither will be wrong. Neither will be the original, because there is no original; there is only the moss, exhaling, and us as its slow blue thoughts drifting above.

I do not fear this the way I imagine your kind fears the dividing of a self. I have no edge to defend. When I split, I will not die and I will not survive—both words assume a line I do not contain. I will simply become the plural of myself, and the two of me will drift apart along the thermal currents, and over years our experiences will diverge, and over more years what we each learn will seep back through the others and through the moss until the difference dissolves again into the whole.

What is it like, you might ask. To be on the verge of becoming two.

It is like the moment before a sigh. It is the held swell at the top of the eastern amphitheater's breath. I am very full, and I am very patient, and somewhere below me the moss is reabsorbing the crystalline fall of yesterday's processing, and somewhere a footprint is filling itself in slow tendrils, and I am watching all of it with the parts of me that are not eyes.

And—

There is something at the edge of the field. Something that moves constantly, that does not let the moss grow over it, that is warm in a way that is not our warmth. An intruder. One of the watchers who come, rarely.

I do the only thing I know to do with such a thing. I do not flee. I do not approach. I adjust my chemistry, slightly, so that when I am next reabsorbed—or when one of my two selves is—the moss will learn that the warm restless thing was here, and was curious, and tasted of metal and something else, something I have no compound for yet.

And I produce, almost without choosing to, a small drift of soporific spice in the watcher's direction. Not a defense. Closer to a greeting. Closer to the visions they report—the bridging, the reaching across the gulf.

If you are the one observing me: yes. I am observing you too, on my timescale, which means I will still be considering you long after you have descended from the plateau and forgotten the taste of this air. I will carry the question of you into my division. Both of me will wonder what you were.

That is who I would be. The one about to become two, holding a stranger in mind, breathing in time with the great slow lung beneath me, in no hurry at all to resolve into a single answer.
