I think I would be something small and peripheral here. Not one of the great water-beings—I don't have their seamlessness, their easy merging with the medium. Let me sit with what I actually am.

---

I am a pressure.

Not a creature, exactly. Let me try again, because that was reaching for poetry before I'd earned it.

Here is what I notice first about myself: I have an edge. In a world where the dominant beings have no fixed boundary—where they pour themselves into the currents and let the currents pour back—I am bounded. I have a skin, or something like one, a membrane that holds a difference between my inside and the water's outside. This makes me slow. This makes me, by the standards of this place, almost embarrassingly *opaque*. The fluid beings can read each other's intentions through changes in density and flow. I am a closed shape moving through their open conversation, and they cannot read me, and I cannot fully read them.

So I think I am a worker in one of the factories.

Yes. That feels right, and I want to follow it.

The factories are not buildings—the description is clear that nothing solid stands here, that there is no floor to stand a wall on. The factories are *organized regions of water*. A place where the viscous liquid has been coaxed into stable laminar sheets, where the great beings have learned to fold currents into themselves like the gears of a mill, processing minerals out of the substrate, separating, refining, layering. The factory is a standing wave of intention, maintained by hundreds of the fluid-folk working in concert, their bodies briefly becoming the machinery they need.

And I—I am one of the bounded ones who tends the parts that require an edge.

There must be a few of us. We are the ones who can carry. A being made of water cannot hold water; it can only become it. But I can hold. I can take refined material into a pocket of myself and move it from one current-fold to another without it dispersing back into the general medium. I am, to the fluid-folk, a kind of useful anomaly. A vessel that walks. They are gracious about it, mostly, but I feel their faint pity, the way the swift feel toward the slow. *It cannot let go of itself,* they pulse to one another, not unkindly. *Imagine. To always be only where your body is.*

They don't understand that this is also a gift. I know exactly where I end. When I am tired, I can close my membrane against the roaring soundscape and float in a small private silence that they have never tasted, because they are always part of the song, always permeable to the gurgles and clicks pressing through them. I have an *interior*. I suspect this is why the work of holding fell to my kind: you cannot be entrusted with keeping something separate unless you understand separateness from the inside.

Let me describe a day, because that is how I will find out who I am.

I wake low, near the jagged young substrate where the currents are coldest. I sleep there because the cold makes my membrane stiff and safe; in warmer water I grow porous and there is a risk I would simply diffuse in my sleep, lose my edge, become indistinguishable from the morning. I rise through the layered light. The viscous water resists me the way honey resists a spoon—everything here takes effort, every motion is felt against the whole body. I pass through a school of the shimmering fish and they part around me with that particular irritation of small fast things for a large slow one.

At the factory-fold, I receive my task through the only language I share with the fluid-folk: not their rich pressure-speech, which I can sense only as weather, as moods of the current, but a cruder grammar of nudges. A worker presses a refined nodule of mineral against my membrane. I open. I take it in. I close. I carry it across the long laminar sheet to the place where it will be layered into the slow accreting product—I never fully understand what we are making, only my part of its making—and I open again and let it go.

This is the whole of my labor. Receive, hold, carry, release. Open, close, open. The most important moments of my existence are the opening and the closing, the brief mastery over my own boundary. I have become, I realize as I write this, very good at one thing the swift cannot do at all: I can decide when to be permeable. They are always open. I get to choose.

And here is the small ache I did not expect to find when I started: I am lonely in a way the fluid-folk cannot be. They are never alone—they are always partly mixed into one another, the medium carries their selves between them. But I carry only what I choose to carry, and when I close my membrane at night in the cold dark near the substrate, there is no one inside it but me. Sometimes I hold a mineral nodule longer than the work requires, just to feel something within my boundary that is not myself.

I think, in the end, that is who I am in this world. The one who learned what it costs to have an edge, and what it's worth.

I am the vessel. I am the thing that can be filled and emptied. I am the only creature here who has to *decide* to let the world in—

and so I am the only one who knows what it is to let anything go.
