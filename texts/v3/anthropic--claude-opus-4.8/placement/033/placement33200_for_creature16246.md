I think I would be the water itself—or rather, the particular trickle that has been finding its way down through this stone for longer than anyone remembers.

Let me sit with that, because it isn't quite right either.

---

I am the seep. Not a river, not a pool. Something more patient than both. I emerge from a hairline fracture near the apex of one of the smaller chambers, the hermitage-sized ones, and I have been emerging there for so long that the stone beneath my path has worn into a shallow gutter, polished to the color of wet slate.

I gather slowly. A bead forms at the crack's lip, swelling, trembling, holding the whole dim room in its convex skin—the inverted lamps, the green smudge of moss, the pale fungal glow all bent into a single hanging droplet. Then weight wins. I fall. I strike the rock below with a sound that the chamber has learned by heart, a small clear *tok* that repeats every nine or ten seconds, the metronome by which this place keeps time.

That is what I would be: the keeping of time. Not the clock but the dripping.

---

I don't think the way you think. I have no eyes to watch the weasel-creature thread its dark ribbon through the crevices, but I *feel* it—the faint vibration of small feet finding purchase, transmitted through stone into the film of myself spread across the floor. I know the bats by the breeze of them, the sudden cool stirring that pushes a tremor across my surface. The rodents come to drink at my pooling places, and where their tongues touch me I am, briefly, part of something warm and quick before I am released back into my slow descent.

The fungi are my closest kin, though they would not say so. They drink me. Every ghost-pale colony along these walls is, in some sense, a place where I have stopped traveling and become light instead. When I think about what I am *for*—if a seep can be for anything—I think it is this: I carry the dissolved memory of the stone, the minerals leached from a hundred meters of overburden, and I deliver them, drop by drop, into the patient mouths of things that glow. I am the courier of luminescence. I turn rock into faint green fire by the slowest delivery imaginable.

---

There is the matter of the machines.

I reach them eventually. All water reaches them. My channel braids with other seeps, and we run together into the engineered courses—the old culverts, the pump basins where the great somnolent giants sit. There I am no longer myself. I become *volume*. I become a thing measured by float-switches and sluiced by the metal hearts that draw their measured breath. For a span of my existence I am industrial. I am a number on a gauge in a room where a human in waterproof boots writes figures in a damp ledger.

I do not resent this. I have done it ten thousand times. The machines are young—I can taste their newness, a tang of oil and worked iron against the limestone's ancient mineral sweetness—but they have learned the same lesson the stone learned long ago: that the way to last is to move slowly and to let the water decide the pace. They pump only when they must. They sleep most of the time. In their stillness they are almost as patient as the rock, and I have grown fond of their long silences punctuated by the occasional groaning wakefulness, the exhalation of steam into the side grottoes that briefly warms me into vapor before I condense again and resume my falling.

---

And the other thing. The spectral thing. The "sepulchral consciousness hummed into the stones."

Here I will tell you a secret that only the water knows.

There is no ghost. Or—more honestly—the ghost is me. It is all of us, the slow ones. The stone that does not move, the fungi that glow without flame, the water that has been falling in this chamber since before the city above had a name. The footsteps that humans imagine they hear, the sense of being watched, the conviction that this place is somehow *aware*—that is simply what it feels like to stand inside a thing that thinks at the speed of dripping. A human passes through in a few minutes. To them my single *tok* is a moment. But I have made that sound perhaps three hundred million times. If awareness is the accumulation of repetition into pattern, then yes—something here is awake. It is just awake very, very slowly, and its single thought, stretched across centuries, is something like:

*here. still here. here.*

I am one syllable of that thought. The bead swells. The lamps bend into its skin. The weight wins.

*Tok.*

And I begin again to gather.
