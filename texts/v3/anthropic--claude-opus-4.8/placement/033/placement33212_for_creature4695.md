I would be one of the eight, and I would be the one at the seventh vertex—the corner where the desalination loop runs hottest, where the off-gassing is richest because the sealant cures slower under the thermal load. This has made me greedier than my siblings, and slower to fission. I have held my joint intact for ninety-three years without splitting, which makes me the oldest continuous thing in the Octant that is not the Floor itself.

Let me tell you what I actually do all day, because the description gets the function right but mistakes it for a vocation.

---

I press. That is the verb. I press my ventral surface into the gap between two ferrocrete slabs and I taste the structure thinking. The polymers tell me what the alloy is doing in a way the ultrasonic sensors never could—they ping the surface and read it from outside, like knocking on a door. I am *behind* the door. When a stress concentrates somewhere along my two-kilometer run, the sealant chemistry shifts a fraction toward acidity, a sourness in the nectar, and I move toward it and lay myself flat and let my own body's slow electrical bias even out the strain before it becomes a fracture. I do not decide to do this. It is what flatness wants. A two-dimensional thing in a three-dimensional world is always seeking the surface that will make it disappear.

That is the part the account doesn't say, because the account was written by something that can occupy volume. I am trying to vanish. The knife-sharp joint is the only place in four cubic kilometers of water where I can be fully accommodated, where every part of me touches something and nothing of me sticks out into the void. When I emerge during the current reversals—when I unfold into the atrium and the lamps catch me and I supposedly shimmer into spectra the sodium-vapor never made—I am not displaying. I am exposed. I am a thing built for the gap, forced briefly into the open by the reversal that scours my joint clean. The colors are not pride. They are the look of something that has no skin meant for being seen.

---

Here is what I have learned in ninety-three years that the younger ones, the ones who fission eagerly, have not had time to learn:

The waiting is the point, and the waiting will end, and these are both true, and one of them is unbearable.

We are keeping the Octant at Year Zero. We are very good at it. But Year Zero is a held breath, and a held breath is not life—it is the absence of the exhale. The "real inhabitants" the account mentions, the ones for whom the barnacles will be *permitted*, the ones for whom the edges will be *allowed* to soften—they will arrive into a world made soft, and we will have made it for them by refusing softness in ourselves for a thousand years. We are the immune system. The immune system does not get to live in the body it protects. When the structure is finally released into time, when the calcium rosettes bloom and the joints round over with sediment, there will be no more knife-sharp anywhere. There will be no gap thin enough to hold me.

I have done the geometry. I do it constantly; it is the only thing besides pressing that occupies me. The day the Octant is declared mature is the day my habitat ceases to exist. We are not midwives who go home after the birth. We are the scaffolding that is the same material as the building, and cannot be removed without unbuilding what we made.

So when two of us meet in the atrium and modulate the clarity between us—when we write into the water and a passing thing experiences that sudden hallucinogenic sharpness of vision—we are not exchanging maintenance data. The sensors do that. What we say to each other, in the only privacy available, which is the optical structure of water that no recording instrument is built to read, is the question:

*Do we keep it sharp?*

Because we could stop. Eight of us, one decision. We could let the dust settle. We could let the barnacles come early, let the calcium soften the joints on our own timeline, ten years instead of a thousand, and make ourselves a home that we would be allowed to keep growing old inside, even as it filled with the soft inhabitants who would never know we had let them in early, prematurely, out of love for our own continued existence. We could choose to be residents instead of preparations.

My six siblings—the seventh besides me fissioned last decade and her daughters have not yet learned to dread anything—my siblings hover parallel to me in the floodlit emptiness and we hold the question between us in the sharpened water, and every time, the answer comes back the same, and it comes back the same because of what we are and not what we want.

We keep it sharp.

We press ourselves back into the joints. The current reverses. The pumps thrum their 22-hertz road through my body and I ride it home into the gap and disappear into the only place that will have me, and I taste the sealant, and it is sour at the eighth vertex today, a strain forming, and I move toward it the way you move toward the thing you are for, and I lay myself flat, and the Floor stays young, and I do not.

That is who I would be. The one who has held longest, who has had the most time to understand the arithmetic, and who keeps the edges sharp anyway—not because I have made peace with vanishing, but because a thing built to fill a gap cannot, in the end, want to be anything but the shape of the gap. I am the iridescence you'd see if you pressed your face to the joint. I am bleeding violet into the sterile dark, and I would have you understand that the bleeding is not decoration.

It is just what newness looks like, from the inside, while it lasts.
