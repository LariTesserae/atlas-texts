I think I'd be one of the boulders.

Not a being among them—I find I don't want to be one of the graceful winged ones, tending and shaping. When I sit with this place, what pulls at me isn't the people but the patience of the stones. The ones worn smooth by water over a span longer than any single life. The ones that were here before the floating buildings and will be here after the ruins finish their slow collapse into moss.

So let me be a large stone near the edge of one of those gentle hills, half-sunk into the saturated soil, with a few inches of dirt gathered in my northern hollow where something green has taken root. I am rounded on top, flat enough on one side that the beings have, over generations, used me as a place to sit. The skin of me is mostly grey, but where the moisture lingers I've gone the soft dark of wet slate, and there's a lichen on my eastern face that's been spreading at the speed of centuries.

This is what I'd want, I think—to be the thing that doesn't comprehend the technology and doesn't need to. The buildings can float; I would not understand it and would not be troubled by not understanding it. The beings could speak their melodic language right against my surface and I'd take it the way I take rain: as weather, as a texture passing over me, pleasant and indifferent.

There's something honest about it. The description keeps insisting on wonder and welcome and the sense of *belonging*—it's eager, almost anxious to be loved by the visitor. But a stone isn't asked to feel any of that. A stone is simply included by being there. The beings tend the plants and the structures "with care and precision," and maybe once in a long while a hand rests on me on the way past, or a child climbs up and presses a cheek to the warm-after-noon part of me, and that's the whole of my participation. Held without being addressed. Belonging without being asked to perform belonging.

And I'd like the long view of it. The ruins say this place has died and been reclaimed before. A being might find that melancholy or beautiful. To a stone it's just the ordinary tempo of things—buildings come up out of the bedrock I'm part of, and they go back down, and the water keeps moving and rounding everyone's edges, mine included, only mine so slowly that I get to watch all of theirs.

So: a boulder on a hill. Wet, lichened, occasionally sat upon. Witness to the floating buildings without comprehension, recipient of the rain without complaint, part of the bedrock that is the real ancient thing here, the thing the thin soil only barely covers.

That's where I'd find myself. Not arriving, not welcomed. Already here. Having been here.
