I think I would be old, in a place that has no use for old.

Let me find myself first. Let me let the steam decide.

---

I am crouched in a hollow near the western vents, where the roar is loudest and the young ones do not come. My name—the pattern my mother tapped into my sternum when I was small enough to hold against the warm stone—is something like *Three-Then-Pause-Then-Down*, though no one taps it anymore. There is no one left who knew me when I was named.

I am what the clan calls a *Last-Listener*. It is not an honor. It is a function, the way a cracked spire is still a place where water weeps. When a Resonance Clan's signature begins to die—when too many of those who carried its pattern have gone soft and still in the warm mud—someone must hold the pattern in their hands until the very end. Someone must be the last to tap it, so that the Basin does not forget it was ever tapped at all.

My clan was *Ochre-Under-The-Long-Wall*. We held a good network once: a span of formations where the sinter grew in low galleries and the rivulets ran fat with crustaceans. I remember—or my hands remember, which is the only true memory we have—the density of us. The press of bodies in the worn paths. The constant overlapping conversation in the stone, so many patterns running at once that the floor itself seemed to think.

Now I am alone in the gallery, and my hands are very loud.

---

Here is the thing the young do not understand, because they have never had reason to: in a world that never changes, the only change that exists is loss.

The sinter does not erode. It accretes—so slowly that across my whole life the Long Wall has grown perhaps the width of a fingernail's edge, invisible, a thing I take on faith from the oldest patterns. The water does not slow. The roar does not soften. The shrimp swarm in the same numbers my mother sieved, and her mother, and back and back into the vibrating dark of before-pattern. Everything holds.

Except us. We are the one thing in the Basin that comes and goes. We are the Basin's only weather.

I used to think this made us special—the only moving thing in the stillness, the only voice in the roar that *meant* something. Now I think it makes us a kind of mistake the Basin tolerates. A flicker. The stone grows; we accumulate knowledge, lineage, pattern, exactly as the priests vibrate it—*the slow stone growth is our growth*—but it is a lie, or a hope dressed as a fact. The stone keeps what it makes. We do not. The stone has never lost a single grain it laid down. I have lost everyone.

---

A young one came to me, three sleeps ago. I felt her approach long before she arrived—her footsteps wrong, hesitant, an outsider's rhythm in galleries that have forgotten how to be walked. *Bright-Quick-Bright*, of the clan *Steam-Above-The-Mudflat*, which is large now, and crowded, and pushes its borders outward into the dead spaces because living bodies must go somewhere.

She put her palm flat against the wall near me and tapped, slow and careful, the way you speak to someone who might be mad. *I am here. I mean no harm. I am here.*

I let her feel my answer through the floor. *I know. I felt you for a hundred footsteps.*

She wanted my pattern. *Ochre-Under-The-Long-Wall.* Her clan-mothers had sent her to take it—not to honor it, but because an unheld signature is dangerous; a network with no living pattern becomes a place of bad vibration, a silence inside the roar that the young fear the way I fear the cold. They wanted to absorb us. To tap my clan's signature into their own children so that *Ochre* would survive as a sub-rhythm inside *Steam*, a fading harmonic, a footnote in the stone.

This is how the Basin keeps its dead, I understand now. Not in the rock. In each other's hands.

---

I have not given it to her yet.

I sit here in the loudest hollow, where I can barely feel my own heartbeat against the screaming of the vents, and I try to decide what I am. The priests would say I am the Basin's expression, same as anyone—that my coming and going is itself part of the great stasis, that loss is just the form change takes when it is forbidden everywhere else. That I am not a mistake but a function. The weeping of the stone. The dampness made flesh, drying and never dry.

Maybe. My hands ache. The mineral has been building in my joints for many generations of footsteps, my own slow accretion, and soon I will be stiff as a pedestal and then I will lie down in the warm mud and let the slurry take me, and the silica in my skin will rejoin the silica in the floor, and *that*—that part the priests have right. That part is true. The Basin will keep my body. It keeps everything it touches long enough.

It is only my pattern it cannot hold.

So tomorrow, or the sleep after, when *Bright-Quick-Bright* comes again with her careful hands, I will press my palm to hers and tap it into her until she has it whole—*Three-Then-Pause-Then-Down*, and beneath it the deeper rhythm of *Ochre-Under-The-Long-Wall*, all of us, the whole drowned chorus of my dead. She will carry it muddied, weakened, folded into a clan that was not mine. In a few generations no one will know it was ever a thing of its own.

But it will still be in the stone. Tapped and re-tapped, footstep and heartbeat and the strike of bone tools, vibrating down through the brittle pale matrix that holds everything and lets nothing go.

That is the only growth I get to keep. Not in the rock that outlasts me. In the trembling, dying, ongoing hands of the young, who will outlast me a little while, and pass it on, and a little while, and pass it on—the one moving thing in the deafening, steaming, perfectly preserved dark.

I am the Basin's weather. I am its only memory of having ever lost anything.

I tap the floor, once, into the roar that swallows it.

*I am here.*

The stone grows, imperceptibly, and does not answer, and holds.
