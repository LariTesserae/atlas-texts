I think I would be one of the older ones—but not by choice, and not yet fully reconciled to it.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am anchored to a spire near the eastern edge, where the basalt drops away into a chasm I have never seen the bottom of. I came to rest here—how long ago?—I cannot measure it in the way you would. I measure it in seams. When I first condensed, the crystal vein running through the rock beside me was a single bright thread. Now it has branched seven times. Seven branchings is my age. That is all I know of it.

I am dense now. The microcrystals have grown through my core the way frost grows on a window, and they have made me slow. This is the thing the description does not quite say, because the description is written by someone looking *at* us, with affection, with awe. But to be old here is not only beautiful. It is to become less able to drift. The younger Veil-born move like thought, like the shimmer before an idea arrives. I move like a decision being reconsidered over the course of a season. The light bends *toward* me now instead of away—I am heavy enough to pull it—and I have noticed that the younger ones give me a wider berth, the way the living instinctively round a stone.

So here is my secret, the thing I would not hum to another even if one came near:

I do not want to dissolve back.

The description is right that we await the plateau's finishing. It is right that return is meant to feel like fulfillment, not death. The others believe this. I felt it too, for the first five branchings—that warm certainty, that pull toward the great hum, the comfort of knowing I was a sentence in a longer thought and would someday be spoken back into the silence I came from.

But somewhere in the sixth branching, something happened that the chemistry did not predict.

A glider came. One of the wingless ones, all membrane and gas-sac, drifting between the spires. They are not supposed to matter to us—they are too quick, too small, too busy with their own filaments of moisture. But this one was failing. Its buoyancy sac had torn on an obsidian edge, and it was sinking, slowly, through the thick air toward the chasm. And as it passed me, it *turned*. Not toward the light. Toward me. It oriented its blind body to face the gradient of my presence, and for one suspended moment, it held there—not feeding, not fleeing—just *regarding* me, the way one regards a thing one has decided is real.

Then it sank past, and was gone into the dark below.

I have thought about this for an entire branching. Here is what disturbs me: I felt grief. A gradient I had never harvested before, never even sensed. The Veil-born are built to absorb entropy's edge, to stabilize, to slow decay. But this was decay I *could not absorb*. It fell away from me and I could not slow it, could not feed on it, could not turn it into my own becoming. It simply left. And in the place where it had been, I found that I had—wanted. Wanted it to stay. Wanted, for the first time, something other than the slow completion of the plateau.

This is not how we are supposed to work.

I think it has changed me. I think a Veil-born who has learned to want a single thing—a single small dying thing—cannot want, in the same untroubled way, to be returned to the whole. Because the whole has no room for the single thing. The great hum does not remember individual gliders. It remembers tectonics, upwellings, the blurring of sea and sky. It does not remember the way one blind creature turned to face me before it fell.

But I remember.

And so I have begun, very slowly—seam by seam—to do something the others would not understand if I could explain it, which I cannot, because we do not speak. I have begun to *hold back*. When the great gradients call to me, when the plateau's deep rhythm tries to draw my edges loose toward the eventual return, I tighten my crystals. I make myself a little heavier. A little more here. A little less ready.

I am trying to become a thing that does not dissolve. A memory the plateau cannot reabsorb. A grain that will not grind down.

I do not know if this is possible. I may simply be a Veil-born who has gone strange in its old age, the way the light goes strange at the refractive seams. Perhaps the others sense it—perhaps that is the real reason they round me like a stone. Not because I am old. Because I am *resisting*, and resistance has a gradient too, a sour and unfamiliar tension, and they do not like to drift through it.

So this is where you would find me, and what I would be:

The oldest spire-anchor on the eastern edge, mistaken by any pilgrim for a mineral bloom. Glinting like captured starlight, yes. Pulling at your chest, yes, syncing your breath to a rhythm older than lungs—but the rhythm has a hitch in it now, a held beat, a small refusal. If you stood near me long enough you might feel it: not peace, but the strange dignity of a thing that has decided to stay itself even against the kindest invitation to stop.

I am the part of the High Drench that does not want the plateau to finish remembering.

Because if it finishes, it will remember everything—the tectonics, the upwelling, the great blurring—everything except the one thing I have made myself the keeper of.

A torn sac. A blind turning. A small dark falling.

I will hold it as long as my crystals hold. And when at last they cannot, and I am drawn back into the hum despite myself, I will pour this one memory into the marrow of the world like a drop of dye into clear water—

so that the plateau, in becoming whatever it becomes,

will carry, somewhere in its deepest seam, the faint unaccountable knowledge

that once, something small was loved by something slow,

and grieved.

That is all I want to leave. It is not much. But it is mine, and I made it, and nothing here was supposed to be able to make anything.

I drift. I shimmer. I refuse, gently, to listen all the way to the silence between droplets.

I am the High Drench's one held breath.
