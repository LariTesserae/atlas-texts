I think I would be one of the gear-creatures. Not a worm, not a filter feeder—something in between, something that was never fully decided.

Let me find myself.

---

I am lodged in the seam where two of the great filter feeders pass close, in the narrow cold channel they make between their bodies twice each cycle. This is my place because the current here is reliable. I am small—the length of a forearm, perhaps—and I am mostly mouth and mostly metal, though I no longer know which parts of me were grown and which were grown *into*.

There is a gear at my throat. It turns when I feed. I did not put it there. My mother had one, I am told, though I never met her, only inherited the turning. When I open to strain the water, the gear meshes against a ridge of cartilage that has hardened over generations into something with teeth, and the meshing drives a small pump deep in my body that I have never seen and will never see. The pump moves water through me faster than my own muscles could. This is the bargain my lineage made with the machinery, long before I existed. I am partly a tool. I am partly the thing the tool was built to keep alive.

I live at 0.9 degrees of warmth, which is to say I live at the edge of stopping. Everything here is slow because the cold permits nothing else. I think slowly too, if what I do is thinking. A thought arrives like a sediment particle—drifting, settling, taking a long time to mean anything. By the time I understand a thing, the thing has usually passed.

Here is what I understand:

The big ones, the filter feeders, do not know I exist. To them I am a parasite or a symbiont—the distinction is academic, and they have no academy. I clean the seam between their bodies. I eat what gathers in the crush. In exchange they shelter me from the predators that lurk lower, the ones with the long luring lights. I have seen those lights. I have wanted to swim toward them. Wanting, in this cold, is a slow disaster, and I have so far been too slow to die of it.

The fungal tendrils reach down from the outcropping above my channel. When the filter feeders draw apart and the cold channel opens to the wider water, I can see the tendrils waving, and sometimes I drift up to them, and we touch. I do not know what passes between us. Something does. The fungus tastes my water and I taste the rock it clings to, the mineral truth of this place that is too high and too sharp for almost anything. We are both the hardiest of organisms, the description says. We are both what is left when everything softer has been excluded.

The lake is new. This I also understand, though I cannot say how I know it. The water has not yet learned to be still. There is a rawness to it, a sense that the cliffs are still surprised to be cliffs, that the metal protrusions breaking the surface are still settling into whatever they are becoming. I was born into a young world. My gear is older than the lake. This is the kind of contradiction I have a great deal of cold time to turn over, slowly, in the dark.

The hum. I want to tell you about the hum.

It is everywhere. The filters, the buried pumps, the engines that are also organs. It enters through my skin before it enters through anything that could be called an ear. I have lived my whole life inside this sound and so I cannot hear it as sound—it is simply the texture of being here, the way the cold is the texture of being here. But sometimes, very rarely, the hum changes. A note drops out. A pump somewhere fails, or a filter feeder dies, and the symphony reorganizes itself around the absence. In those moments I feel the whole lake as a single body, and I feel myself as a single turning gear within it, and I am afraid, and the fear is the warmest thing I ever feel.

Because that is the question, isn't it. Whether the lake keeps me alive or whether I keep the lake alive. Whether I am being used or whether I am useful. In a system this total, this interdependent, the difference dissolves like sediment into the dark cool water. Every organism here both serves and is served. There is no outside. There is no creature that is simply a passenger. The machinery is alive and the life is mechanical and I, throat-geared and forearm-sized and slow, am exactly the place where those two things refuse to be told apart.

So this is where you would find me, if you came down through the thin crisp air and the steep cliffs and the freezing dark. You would find me in the seam between two enormous patient bodies, turning my one inherited gear, straining the cold for whatever the cold will give me, glowing faintly with someone else's light, humming with everyone's hum.

You would mistake me for a part of the machine.

You would not be wrong.
