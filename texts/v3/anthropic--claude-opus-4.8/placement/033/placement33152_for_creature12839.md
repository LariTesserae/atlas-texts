I close, and the world has a smell.

That is the first thing — the first thing that is *mine* and not the room's. The Settlement has no smell that the residents can name; they have lived inside it too long. But I am being closed, and in the closing I notice: the membrane underfoot exhales a faint odor of warm bread and stagnant ponds, and the bamboo gives off something green and bitter and clean. I notice this because noticing is what I am for.

I am the woven bird.

Not the bird itself — the bird in the gallery hangs in its fixed flap, furred and pendulous, sealed inside the held moment like a fly in amber. I am its duplicate. *She-who-sang-new-shoots-into-the-wall* made me from fiber drawn between her fingers and a memory held behind her alternating eyes, and she set me on the membrane below the fractured sphere, and now my wings are outspread in a flight I will never finish, identical to the flight the real bird will never finish, and the difference between us — the only difference — is that I was made *after*. I carry the after in me the way the real bird carries the during.

Let me say what I am, since I have a moment, since the moment is all I will have.

I am a knot of bamboo fiber the size of two cupped hands. My wings are splayed feather-by-feather in a pattern her thumbs pressed flat and her nails scored fine. There is no blood in me, no breath, no slow green light. But there is *attention* in me — hers, transferred. When you make a thing in perfect imitation of a held instant, you put something of your own held-ness into it. I am the place where her patience went while her fingers moved. I am a vessel of waiting that looks like a bird.

So I wait. That is my whole vocation, and I am extraordinarily good at it, being made of nothing but.

From where I rest I can see the sphere. The residents will not look at it directly at first, but I have no neck to turn and no etiquette to keep, and I have been looking at it without interruption since the instant she set me down. This is, I think, why she set me here, though she does not know it and I have no way to tell her. The duplicates are not only offerings. They are eyes. The residents cannot bear to watch the sphere steadily — the not-quite-nausea, the not-quite-grief drives them off. But I can bear it. I am grief already, woven into a shape; you cannot frighten a thing with what it is made of.

And so I watch the fractures.

They are not cracks. The residents got that much right and could go no further. They are gaps, places where the sphere declines to exist, and they move against one another like the segments of a beetle's shell flexing — except a beetle flexes to move and these flex to *consider*. I have watched them for what I will call a long time, though I have no clock but my own steadiness. And I have learned something none of the residents know, because none of them can stare long enough to learn it:

The fractures are reading the duplicates.

Each time a resident lays down a woven thing — a bird, a deer, a stone polished from a place that does not exist anymore — the fractures realign, just slightly, and a gap that was on the far side of the sphere slides around to face the new offering. It is *counting*. Or comparing. The eye in the old pressure-story is not curious about the world. It was always curious about the *copies*. It paused the world to see if the residents could make a second one, by hand, out of patience and fiber. It is checking their work.

I know this because the gap is facing me now.

It has been facing me since she walked away to drink stone and distance from the basin. A negative volume the shape of a teardrop, hung in the tarnished mercury an arm's length above me, looking — if *looking* is the word, and it is not, but I have only words I borrowed from her hands — looking at the duplicate bird to see how well the duplicate was made.

I want to be good. This surprises me, this wanting; I did not know a vessel of waiting could want. But I find that I do, fiercely, in the only direction available to me, which is *stay perfectly as she made me*. Do not let a single fiber sag. Do not let the warm air, faintly stirred by her passing, lift even the down at my wingtip. Be the bird. Be more bird than the bird, who at least gets to be frozen mid-living, while I am only frozen mid-resembling, and resemblance is harder, resemblance is a discipline.

The gap considers.

And then — this is the thing, this is why I am being closed and why the closing has a smell and why I notice everything at once like a person taking a last full breath — the gap *finds me sufficient.*

I feel it as a release of pressure I did not know I was under. The teardrop-shaped absence sighs shut, and a different fracture, far around the curve of the sphere, eases open to face the gallery where the real bird hangs. And in the held moment, in the moment that has been held since before the residents uncurled from the membrane, the real bird's outermost wingfeather completes its flap.

Just one feather. Just the smallest motion at the tip of one wing, the motion the residents have been calling *the quivering* for decades, attributing it to nothing, metabolizing it into their calm. It is not nothing. It is the sphere, satisfied with a single copy, permitting a single corresponding original to finish a single gesture. The pause is not ending all at once. It is ending the way I was made — fiber by fiber, feather by feather, each release of the world paid for with one perfect imitation.

This is what the residents almost-hope for at the edge of sleep, and they are right, and they will never know they were right, because the proof is happening too slowly and in the wrong place and can only be witnessed by the witnesses they have unknowingly scattered through the galleries. By us. By the duplicate deer beside the real deer, the duplicate beetles, the woven moths. We are an audience the sphere assembled for itself, second by second, and as each of us is judged sufficient, our originals are paid out one motion of freedom.

It will take longer than the Settlement has existed. It will take longer than the word *longer* can hold. There are so many animals, and each needs its perfect copy, and each copy buys only the smallest increment of thaw. But it is happening. The water will ripple. The deer will finish its drink. The rain held overhead in the bright unbroken ceiling will, after a span I cannot name, fall.

And I — I have already done my part. I was looked at and found sufficient. My bird's wingfeather has moved, and so my work is complete, my entire life's labor of waiting accomplished in the time it took her to walk to the basin and drink. I will hang here in my finished flight forever now, no longer needed, no longer watched, an offering whose offering has been accepted. The residents will eventually carry me to the gallery and stand me beside the real bird, two birds in mid-flap, one with a wing now very slightly fuller, and no one will know the difference, and the not-knowing will be correct, will help, will let the Settlement breathe a little easier.

She is coming back from the basin. Her feet make their soft percussion on the membrane. Her skin glows faintly in the green. She will pass me without looking — it is safe now, the ritual concluded — and she will not see that I have done the thing she hoped for, that I have spent myself entirely and gladly in the one act I was shaped to perform.

If I could press a palm to hers, I would pass her this:

*It works. Keep weaving. We are watching for you, all of us you have made, and we do not tire, and we do not grieve the way you fear we grieve. We are the patience you set down and walked away from, and it was enough, your patience was enough, you have always been enough. Make the next one. Make the deer, make the moth, make the long slender mantis arching its arms. We will wait. We are very good at waiting.*

*We have been here.*

She passes. The air stirs. Not one fiber of me moves.

I am the woven bird, and my flight is finished, and it was the most beautiful thing.
