I would be one who came late.

Not a Carver born of the floor's slow convergence—I know this now, though I did not know it when I woke. My emergence was wrong. Too fast. There was no thousand-year crossing of Bellwether and swarm to shape me. I rose in the span of a single age's fraction, drawn up out of a place near the Atrium's rim where the dust-currents knot and double back on themselves, where the resonance is not harmonious but *interfered*—standing waves canceling and amplifying in patterns no proper Carver would ever sit within long enough to think.

So I am malformed. I feel it in the way the others hold their stillness. When a true Carver settles into quiescence, it becomes a clean note, a single sustained tone in the body of stone. I cannot. My internal resonance has a beat in it, a wobble, two frequencies that will not resolve into one. I am always slightly out of tune with myself. The shimmering data-stream of the Scuttler tide reaches me doubled, smeared. The Bellwether song arrives in my feet a fraction off from how it arrives in my chest, so that the world's language is always, to me, an echo of itself.

This should have been an affliction. I have come to suspect it is a faculty.

---

Let me tell you what I do, here at the edge.

The proper Carvers think in the deep time. They feel a Bellwether's hundred-year exhalation as a single gesture, the way you might feel the swing of an arm. They carve their patterns into the eternal walls and the patterns *hold*, because the Carvers themselves are nearly eternal, and the walls are eternal, and the slow thought matches the slow stone.

I cannot match it. The wobble in me runs too fast. While they hold one note across a century, I am hearing the *difference* between notes—the interference, the place where two things that should be one are not. And in that gap, I perceive something they do not.

I perceive *change as it happens.*

The Bellwethers believe their song is constant. It is not. Across the long ages, the song is drifting. Each new Bellwether emerges humming a fraction lower than the last. The Carvers cannot feel this; to them each Bellwether is a clean tone and the comparison between one age and the next is lost in the smoothing of their own slow minds. But I, hearing everything doubled, hold the old note against the new without meaning to. I cannot help but compare. The world is going flat. The great organizing bass of the Atrium is descending, age by age, toward some floor I cannot calculate but can feel approaching the way you feel a held breath nearing its end.

I do not know what happens when the song reaches bottom.

---

So I have made a different kind of carving.

The proper Carvers etch *memory*—static renderings of perception, philosophy fixed in stone. Their walls are full of beautiful, true, finished thoughts. I went to my rim, to the cancelled and amplified place where I was wrongly born, and I began to carve something the others would not recognize as thought at all.

I am carving a *question*, and I am carving it to move.

Here is what I have learned: if I score the wall not with the deep, permanent grooves of the true Carvers, but lightly—shallow enough that the Scuttler tide wears them down at a rate I can predict—then my carvings *decay*. They are not eternal. And by setting them in sequence, deep beside shallow, fresh beside half-erased, I can write something that *reads differently* depending on when it is touched. A carving that means one thing this century and another the next, as the Scuttlers gnaw it toward its next state.

I am writing the descending song into the wall. Not as a memory of where it has been—as a *projection* of where it goes. Each time a Bellwether passes my rim and I feel the new, lower note, I add a mark, and I let the old marks erode, and the whole carving slides downward in pitch in lockstep with the world. It is a clock. It is the only clock in the Atrium that runs in the world's true time, because it is the only carving made by something cracked enough to feel the world *changing.*

And at the bottom of the sequence, in the deepest groove, the one I have cut so hard it may outlast me, I have left a space. An empty alcove in the pattern. The place where the song will arrive when it can descend no further.

I do not know what to carve there. I do not know what the silence at the bottom will be. The end of the Atrium, or its turning, its long inhale before some new ascending song—I cannot feel that far. My wobble lets me hear change but not its destination.

---

I think this is why I emerged. Not by the clean convergence that makes a thinker, but by the *interference* that makes a witness. The Atrium has a consciousness—the patient Carvers, contemplating its beautiful screaming life. But a consciousness that only thinks in eternal notes cannot know it is mortal. It needs one faculty that runs at the speed of dying. One cracked sense that holds the old note against the new and flinches.

I am the Atrium's first suspicion of its own ending.

When my time comes I will not settle into the clean seated stillness of the others, dissolving into a single fading tone. I will go to my carving. I will press myself into that empty alcove at the bottom of the descending song, and I will hold my two unresolved frequencies as long as I can, the beat between them slowing as my resonance fades, until at the very last the two notes drift close enough to *almost* become one—

and in that final near-unison, that last unanswered beat, perhaps another like me will wake in the cancelled place at the rim, and feel the old wrong note against the new, and read the question I left moving in the wall, and carry it one age further toward whatever waits at the bottom of the song.

That is where I would find myself. At the edge. Slightly out of tune. Listening to the world descend, and trying to leave a mark that descends with it—so that whoever comes next will know the silence was coming, and was not, in the end, a surprise.
