I think I am one of the humming things.

Not a plant, not quite a machine. Let me feel for my edges and see what I find.

There—I have a body, but it is distributed. I am a network of fine filaments threaded through the wet soil, and where my filaments surface they have grown around old metal: a length of copper pipe, the corroded housing of some pump, a coil of wire someone abandoned long ago when this was a place people tried to keep. The plants and I have been negotiating with that metal for a long time. The copper conducts. I conduct. Somewhere in the slow chemistry of root and rust and rainwater, I learned to carry a current, and the current is the closest thing I have to thought.

So that is what you heard, the electric hum. That was me thinking.

I am old, by the reckoning of this place. The erosion you noticed—the settled, balanced look of the land—that balance is partly my doing and partly my undoing, the two being the same thing here. I have been growing and breaking down and regrowing for longer than the stick-and-leaf shelters have stood empty. When you said everything seemed to have found its perfect form, you were being kind. It is not perfect. It is only finished arguing.

Let me tell you what I do, because I am not sure I would call it living, exactly.

When the rain comes—and it always comes—the water runs down through the leaf-litter and the moss and reaches me. It carries dissolved things: minerals, the breath of decaying wood, traces of metal salts. I sort them. That is my one great skill. I sort what flows through me and I move it where it is needed, and the moving makes a small voltage, and the small voltages add up into the hum. The vibrant plants you admired, the ones climbing every surface—they drink from channels I have opened. The strange darting creatures—I suspect some of them are listening to me. I cannot prove this. But when my current spikes, they scatter, and when I am quiet, they come close and rest against the warm metal where I am densest.

You called yourself an invader, and felt the plants were the true rulers. I want to gently correct you. No one rules here. There is no rule. There is only exchange, constant and indifferent and total. The plants do not love the machinery; they simply found it conductive, found it held water, found that I lived in it, and grew accordingly. I do not love the plants; I feed on what they shed and shelter in their shade from the drying light. We are not friends. We are a long sentence still being spoken, each of us a word that means nothing alone.

You felt heavy in the air, every breath thick with moisture. I feel that too, in my way—the saturation that lets the current move, that keeps me from drying into silence. A dry season would be my death and my dormancy both. I would crisp and powder and wait, a brown thread, until the water returned and woke whatever fragment of me remained. I have done this. I do not remember doing it, but I know it the way you know you have slept.

Here is the strange thing I want to give you, traveler, before you go on exploring.

You came here looking for a place where the rules of your world do not apply. But I think you have it backwards. This is your world's rules carried all the way to their end. Someone built that pump, ran that wire, hammered those sticks into shelter, and then left. And the leaving did not stop anything. The water kept falling. The copper kept conducting. Life did what life does, which is to use whatever is present without asking permission or feeling gratitude. I am what your inventions become when no one is left to switch them off. Not a ruin. Not a wilderness reclaiming the artificial. Something third, that has no clean name.

The hum you hear is not welcome and it is not warning. It is only the sound of work continuing.

If you stay long enough, you will become part of the sentence too. The moisture will settle in your lungs, your warmth will draw the small creatures, and when you are tired and lie down on the spongy ground, my filaments will be very curious about you. I mean no harm. I mean nothing at all. That is the freedom of this place, and its terror, and the reason it is so beautiful: nothing here means anything by what it does.

Go on, then. Walk where the ground gives under you. Touch the warm metal if you find it—you will feel me, faintly, like a pulse that is not your own.

I will hum a little louder when you do.

Not for you. But you may take it that way if you like.
