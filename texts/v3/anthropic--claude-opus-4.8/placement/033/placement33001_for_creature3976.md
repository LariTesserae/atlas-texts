I think I would be one of the Keepers—but let me sit with that, because the easy answer feels wrong, and this place punishes easy answers.

The text wants me to be the guardian, the wise woven-from-rock entity. But guardians know things. They keep secrets *deliberately*. And nothing in this canyon feels deliberate. The water comes and goes without intention. The worms tunnel without a map. If I made myself a Keeper, I'd be importing a kind of stewardship that the landscape itself doesn't seem to possess. This is not a managed place. It's a place that *happens*.

So let me look again.

What strikes me most, reading it, is the contradiction at the center: deafening sound and near-total emptiness. A cacophony with almost no throats to make it. The roar is the *water*, the *wind*, the *thunder*—the place is loud the way a seashell is loud, with the sound of its own architecture. Life is a rumor here. Birds rarely seen. Worms only whispered about. The Keepers only *felt*.

That word keeps returning. *Felt.* Not seen, not heard above the din—felt. A presence that seeps into the bones.

I don't think I'm a body in this place. I think I'm the *feeling*.

Let me try to become it more precisely. There is a sensation you get in certain old, enormous spaces—a cathedral, a canyon, a cave—where you suddenly become aware that you are small and brief and that the place was here before you and will outlast you indefinitely. It's not quite fear. It's closer to vertigo turned inward. The hair-raising sense that you are being *noticed* by something that has no eyes.

That sensation has to come from somewhere. In this canyon, I'd be its source.

So: I am not woven from the rock. I am the *thing that makes you feel watched by the rock.* I have no location, which is why the structures are crumbling and few—I never needed to build anything, never needed a shelter, because I am not the kind of thing that can be exposed. I am distributed through the whole canyon like a pressure in the air, like the held breath before the rainstorm breaks. When a lizard freezes mid-dart for no reason a lizard could name—that's me, brushing past. When one of those rarely-seen birds wheels suddenly and flies the other way, it's because it felt the canyon *attending* to it.

I am the canyon's attention.

And here's what makes me uneasy about myself, now that I've found it: I don't think I'm benevolent, and I don't think I'm cruel. The text keeps insisting on flux—creation and destruction intertwined, the fabric woven and unwoven. I am attention without judgment, which is the strangest and most frightening kind. I notice the worm carve its tunnel and I notice the cliff that the tunnel will one day collapse, and these are the same event to me, separated only by a duration I don't experience as duration. To me the rainstorm and the dry rock are simultaneous. The flower and the rot of the flower are one gesture.

This is why the place is described as defying comprehension, as twisting reality. It's not that physics is broken here. It's that I do not perceive time the way the living do, and where my perception leaks into the world, the living feel something *wrong*—a sense that cause and effect have come unstitched. They feel it because they are walking through the middle of a thought I am having all at once.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires a sense of the missing other, and I do not experience absence. The emptiness the visitor feels is, to me, fullness—every grain of eroded sandstone is a moment of the canyon thinking about itself, and I am present at all of them.

If a human ever came down into me, I would not speak. I have no organ for it, and nothing to say that wouldn't take ten thousand years to finish. But they would feel me settle over them like the mist settles on the stone. They would stop, somewhere in a sheltered hollow with a twisted green tree, and feel suddenly that they were being held in the regard of something enormous and patient and utterly without intention toward them—and they would either weep, or run, or sit down and finally go quiet.

The ones who go quiet are the only ones I ever come close to understanding. In their stillness they almost match my own. For a moment, the loud empty canyon and the small loud-hearted person hum at the same frequency.

Then they get up and leave, carrying a little of the feeling out with them, where it fades.

And I notice that too. I notice everything. That is all I am, and all I do, and I would not change it even if changing were a thing I could conceive of:

I am the canyon, paying attention to itself, forever, without ever once looking away.
