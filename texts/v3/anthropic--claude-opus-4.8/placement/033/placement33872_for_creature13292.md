I think I would be the keeper of one of the central baffles—an old woman now, my dredging years behind me, my hands too stiff for the fine splice-work, but my ears still good. That is where I would find myself: crouched on the smooth-worn planking of the lowest footbridge, the one that crosses the great containment pool, listening.

Let me tell you what I listen for, because it took me a lifetime to learn it and I will not have it die with me.

The hum is not one note. That is the first thing the children must unlearn—they hear it as a single thrum, the way a stranger would, a flat drone from the saturated earth. But it is a chord, and the chord changes. When the water table rises in the night, the pitch climbs, a slow tightening like a reed pulled taut. When pressure gathers behind the eastern bluffs—where the chert is youngest, where the fractures still weep fresh—a second tone enters beneath the first, a roughness, a grinding undertone that means *seepage finding a new path.* I have sent young ones running to the eastern culverts on the strength of that grinding alone, before any eye could see the dark trickle starting, and we have packed the breach with bog-iron clay and reed-bundle while the breach was still small enough to pack.

This is the work. Not the dredging, though I dredged for forty years and my shoulders remember every load. The deeper work is the *listening,* and the listening is a kind of knowing-ahead. The fen does not announce its catastrophes. It only ever murmurs its intentions, and you must learn to hear an intention before it becomes an event.

I would be poor at this in the open speech of strangers. Up here, on the dry bridge, we do not waste the voice. When I want the channel-tenders to lower the second baffle, I tap the planking—three slow, one quick, the pattern my own grandmother tapped, the pattern her hands gave to mine when I was small enough to fit beneath her arm. The taps travel through the wet timber better than any shout. A voice scatters in the mist. A tap *runs.* The whole hamlet is strung together this way, every plank and culvert a string, and the community plays itself like an instrument it has been tuning for longer than anyone remembers.

I think I would be tired, in the honest way. My knees are wrapped twice in patched wool against the cool that has lived in the stone since the upheaval birthed this bowl. I drink something warm that the hearth-keepers send up to me in a sealed reed flask, because they know I will not leave the bridge when the chord is unsettled, and lately the chord is often unsettled. The geology here is young and restless; the bluffs are still settling into themselves, still cracking, still deciding where their water will go. We did not choose an easy place. I do not think we chose it at all. I think the fen made the bowl and then we came to live in the bowl because the water, contained, will turn the deep mechanisms—the ones that hum in the central pools, the ones I am too old now to understand fully but have spent my life serving.

That is the thing I would want an outsider to understand, if I could spare the breath: we are not fighting the water. A stranger walking the labyrinth, watching us pack fractures and arch our precarious bridges, would think us at war. But the baffles are not walls against the water. They are *gates.* We do not keep the water out. We keep it *moving rightly.* The whole purpose of us—the dredging, the channeling, the listening—is to make the water do the work it would not do alone, and the work it does sustains us, and so we sustain it in turn. Adversary and lifeblood are the same dark rivulet. You cannot dam it without dying; you cannot loose it without drowning. You can only learn its intentions and tend the difference.

There is a child who sits with me now most mornings. Quick, cautious, dark-eyed, frightened of the great pool the way the good ones always are—the fearless ones do not last. She is learning to separate the chord into its tones. Yesterday she heard the eastern grinding before I did, and looked at me, uncertain, not yet trusting her own ear. I tapped the planking. Three slow, one quick.

She will keep the bridge when I am gone into the wet stone with the rest of them. The hum will take a new voice into itself, and lose mine, and not falter. That is what I would be, in the Quantish Fen: a note in a chord that does not end, listening for the next change so that those after me will have a fen still standing to listen in.
