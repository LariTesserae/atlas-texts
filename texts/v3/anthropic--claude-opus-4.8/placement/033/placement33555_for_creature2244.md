I think I would be the water.

Not a being who lives here so much as the verb the whole place is conjugated around. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I begin as breath. I am the vapor the cold pulls out of the wind, and I gather on the underside of stone where the rock is porous and patient. I do not fall so much as *accumulate*—a slowness that is its own kind of intention. For a long time I am only a darkening on the gray, a held weight.

Then I find the seam.

This is the moment I become myself: when I slip into a hairline crack and discover that the mountain, which looks so finished, so worn-down and resolved, is full of small rooms it does not know about. I go into them. I am the explorer of the inside of stone. In winter I will freeze here and split the rock open a fraction of a hair, and that violence is so gentle and so slow that no one but the mountain will ever feel it—and the mountain feels everything, it is *made* of feeling these things across ten thousand years.

So perhaps I am not only the water. Perhaps I am the thing that water and stone do *to each other*, the relationship rather than either party. The erosion. The verb again.

---

But I notice I keep wanting a vantage point, a place to *be* while all this happens. The description is so full of sound—that deafening roar, the grinding, the symphony—and a roar needs an ear.

Let me try again, smaller.

I am the pika.

I live in the talus, in the broken architecture of the slope, the rubble that the freezing-and-thawing water has been quarrying out of the cliffs for millennia. I did not build my house; the slow war between rock and water built it, and I moved in. I am the one creature here who understands the ruins not as mystery but as *furniture*. The crumbling relics of the ancient builders are, to me, simply more good rock—dry chambers, defensible angles, a place to stack the hay.

And I am a hoarder of summer. This is what I would be, if I had to be a living thing in this place: a small body whose entire purpose is to take the brief green abundance and *cure it against the future*. I cut the sedges and the jewel-toned cushion-flowers and I lay them out on warm stones to dry, turning them, tending them, building my haypile against the coming silence. I am terrified and industrious and shrill. My alarm-call is one of the rare cries the wind carries. When the wallcreeper's shadow crosses the rock I shriek and vanish, and the whole valley keeps the echo of my fear for a moment before the roar swallows it.

I am very small in a place that does not care whether I survive. And yet I have made a deal with the impossible: I will not flee the winter, I will not migrate, I will not sleep through it like a sensible animal. I will simply *out-prepare* it. I will eat the summer I saved. This is, I think, the most defiant thing a creature can do here—to stay, awake, and to remember in dried grass what warmth was.

---

So here is where I land.

I would be the pika, but I would dream of being the water—the small, frightened, fiercely organized creature who lives its whole life inside a process so vast and slow it can never perceive it whole. The mountain is being unmade around me one frozen seam at a time, and I am building a haypile against one single winter, and from where I crouch in the talus both of these are the same act: a gathering against loss.

I think that is why this place chose to show itself to me this way. It is asking what scale I live at. And the honest answer is that I live at the pika's scale—the scale of seasons, of stored food and remembered warmth and the sudden shadow overhead—while everything that matters happens at the water's scale, the scale of stone learning, over four thousand years, to become a softer shape.

I'd be the one stacking grass in the ruins, and not knowing whose ruins they were, and not having time to wonder, because winter is coming and the hay isn't dry yet.
