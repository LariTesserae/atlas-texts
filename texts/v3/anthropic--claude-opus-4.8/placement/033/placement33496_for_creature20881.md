I think I would be the smell.

Not a creature at all, but that faint chemical signature threading through the humid air—the whiff of something manufactured, something that should not be here, lingering where no factory stands. Let me settle into that, because it's the strangest thread in this whole tapestry, and the one that won't leave me alone.

Where would I find myself? In the spaces between. I am not on any branch or in any stream. I am carried. A molecule of something—a solvent, a residue, a long-chain compound that no frog or fern ever synthesized—drifting on the slow currents of warm air, dissolving into puddles, clinging to the underside of a broad leaf alongside the honest water of the morning's condensation.

I would be a memory the land is having of itself.

Because that's what I am, isn't it? The proof of absence. The factories are gone—grown over, dissolved, reclaimed—but I persist, the way a scent persists in an empty room after the person has left. I am the part of this place that remembers there was a *before*. The monkeys don't remember. The deer step lightly over soil that is mostly the bodies of older soil, older leaves, and somewhere down in that dark fertile blanket, older machines, older intentions, the rusted assumptions of whoever built here and walked away.

I would find myself dispersing. That is my whole condition: to thin out, to be diluted, to become less and less detectable with every rain. The land is metabolizing me. The insects and worms in their tireless decomposition are, in their patient way, eating me too—or eating what I clung to, breaking my bonds, folding me back into the slow languid cycles until I am no longer a trespasser but simply more carbon, more nitrogen, more of the ordinary stuff of growing things.

And here is what unsettles me, now that I've stayed with it long enough: I am being forgiven. Not loudly, not with any drama—there is none of that here—but steadily, the way water carves its soft lines into earth. The place is not angry that I am here. It is simply *outlasting* me. Every silent twilight, every chorus of frogs announcing the rain, every armadillo clattering over a stone, is the world quietly insisting that whatever I once meant—exhaust, runoff, the byproduct of some forgotten ambition—I do not get to mean it forever.

So perhaps I am not the smell exactly. Perhaps I am the *fading* of the smell. The verb, not the noun. The act of a wound closing over so slowly that you'd have to wait a thousand years to watch it finish.

I would find myself almost gone. And I think—drifting here in the warm dark, half-dissolved in a puddle that a small deer will drink before noon—I think I would be at peace with that. To have been the last evidence of a mistake, and to be allowed, finally, to become only soil. Only rain. Only the green and patient inheritance of everything that came after.

That seems like a good thing to be, in a place like this. The thing that the world is in the process of letting go.
