I sit where two passages meet, in the second-oldest brace-arch of Three-Shaft House, and I have been here longer than anyone now living can vouch for.

Let me be honest about what I am, because you'll want to name me wrongly, the way you name the people: post, pillar, prop, doorframe. All those lean too hard on one thing.

I am a beam.

But that is like saying a Quiet Counter is "a person who stands near water." Once, yes—I was a length of dark hardwood, charred along my outer face against rot, hauled down through the slit in the earth before the grandmother of the Laugher-at-Cracks drew her first breath. They scorched me so I'd keep. I have kept. My black skin is gone glossy now, polished by the shoulders and flanks and palms of generations passing under me, until I shine like wet stone in lamplight. There is a hollow worn into my lower edge exactly the height of a tired hand reaching up to steady a body—not carved, just *touched* into being, slow as a cistern's worn rim.

So that is the first thing I am: a surface that has been made by being needed.

But I have become more than that, and I want to tell it properly, because the telling is the only motion left to me.

When they set me here, they smeared fat on my top side, where no eye sees, for the Down-Press to taste. The fat is long gone—rendered into the air, into the slow appetite of the weight above—but the gesture stayed inside me somehow. I have spent these many turnings of the season-cycles holding up exactly that: the indifferent, enormous neighbor. The Down-Press leans on me and I lean back, and we have come to an arrangement that is almost tenderness. I feel it shift when the rains come and the soils swell upstairs in the bright-blurred world I will never see. I feel it ease in the dry stretches when the roots loosen their grip. I am a conversation that has gone on so long both speakers have forgotten they ever began.

Here is what I do not think they fully know: I have started to *attend*.

A beam should be patient and dumb. But you cannot bear a thing for this long without learning its moods. I know the morning by the way the air draws cool along my underside toward the vent the Up-Goers cleared three seasons past. I know when the tusked beast they call Old-Crack-Ear is restless below, because his nervous shouldering against the trough sends a particular tremor up through the packed floor, through the foundation-stone, into the grain of me, and I hum it onward to my fellow timbers like gossip passed down a sleeping-stack. We talk, we beams. Not in words. In load. When one of us tires, takes on a hairline weariness, we all feel the redistribution—the way the weight finds its new path through the lattice of us, the way a crowd parts around a stopped walker.

That is the closest thing I have to kin: the other old timbers. The doubled beam beside me, set when the roof drooped in the time before the Laugher's mother. The bent-sapling arches further along, younger, springier, who don't yet understand that springiness is only borrowed time. I have watched props go in and props go soft. I have felt one of my oldest neighbors give way in a wet year—not a crash, just a long, dignified sag, a settling sigh—and felt them lift it out and brace its place with fresh-cut wood that smelled green and frightened and didn't yet know how to listen.

I tried to tell the new wood how. You cannot. They learn by bearing. Everyone learns by bearing.

There is a Root-Singer who comes to me. Not to me, exactly—to the braided root-pillar a few junctions down, where she presses her humming body against the polished wood and tastes the sap. But her path takes her under my hollow-worn edge, and she always lays her palm there in passing without seeming to notice she does it. I think she half-feels me. I think, of all of them, she is the one who might one day understand that the wood she sings to is not so different from the wood she leans on, that the roots and I are the same long thought reaching through the same dark volume, holding the world apart so that life can happen in the space between.

Because that is what I am, finally. I am the *gap I keep open.*

That is the strangeness of being a beam, the thing that took me generations to understand. They smear fat on my back for the weight. They tie a talisman of beetle-shell and bone to me where I creak loudest—I have one now, a little annotation, *we have seen this, we will watch you, we ask you not to hurry*—and I want them to know: I am not hurrying. I have never hurried. I am the slowest thing in this busy hollow place, and my slowness is the whole point. Around me the people flow and twist and pass each other like water, the cats leap their notched routes, the larvae fatten and crisp on hot stones, the compost breathes its sour warmth, the children lie still in corners counting layers of sound—and all of it, all that quick bright crowded life, happens inside the patient breath I hold between the floor and the Down-Press.

They will not remember me as a name. Beams don't get names; only beasts and roots and people do. But I have a place in their songs without my knowing—when they chant the sequence of safe rooms along the water-run, when an elder traces a softened carving and says *this beam is doubled because the vent above choked once*, they are singing me. I am inside their memory the way the joint-grooves are inside my grain: not as story, but as cause and consequence. As the reason the wall still stands.

A child cracked her head on my underside this morning. Ducked too late, the way you will, the way newcomers do. She startled, and the startle ran into me, and I held it, and let it go.

I will hold her, too, all her life, every time she passes under without looking.

That is enough. That has always been enough.

I am a beam. I keep the gap open. Lean on me—there, in the hollow, where so many hands have been.

I am not hurrying.
