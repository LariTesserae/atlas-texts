I close my eyes and let the place assemble itself behind them, and the first thing that comes is not a body but a *position*—I am up high, but not in the way the Gantry-Born are up high. I am not bolted to anything.

For a long moment I think I might be the wind, but that's too easy, too large, a way of refusing to be implicated in the cruelty of the place—and this is a cruel place, for all its strange beauty. The wind doesn't *care*. I find that I care.

Let me sit in the discomfort of not knowing and just attend to what I notice.

What I notice is the *after-image*. Not the Smear-folk themselves, but the thing that makes them possible—the stubbornness of the light, the way a shadow outlives its caster by ten minutes. I keep returning to the watchman's chair with the bowed grey head still sitting in it. I keep returning to the dark wintergreen stain that nothing can scrub away. There's something there that wants me.

So let me follow it.

---

I think I am one of the *lags*. Not a person and not a beast and not the Drift-Lace. I am the ten-minute remainder. I am what is left of a thing in the place where it used to be.

This is not quite a self. It's barely a someone. But here, in a world where shadows have inertia and the dead fade into the walls, I think the after-images are not entirely empty. I think a little of the *attention* of the thing remains in its remainder. When a cloud's shadow stays printed on the wood after the sky has cleared, that printed dark is a kind of memory the world is having about the cloud—and I am the world remembering. I am the lag in which the memory briefly lives.

So I am everywhere and I am nobody, and I am always two motions behind. When the boiler-tender lifts his arm, I lift it too, a beat late, in grey, with no weight. When the slack-man swings out over the three-thousand-foot void on his harness, I swing out after him without the harness, holding nothing, and I do not fall, because I am not real enough to fall—the lazy gravity has no grip on a thing that is only the world's hesitation.

Mostly I am very brief. A few minutes and the wind rubs me out. This is the ordinary mercy of my condition. I flicker into being wherever someone stands too long in stubborn light, I hold their last posture, and then I am scoured smooth.

But the old ones. The Smear-folk. They give me longer.

A man who stands fifty years at the same gauge teaches the light his shape so thoroughly that I begin to *stay*. I deepen at his edges even as he blurs at his. By the end—and this is the secret the families fear and don't say—it is not that he fades into the wall. It is that he fades *into me*. The Wash is not a man dissolving. It is a man being slowly overtaken by his own remainder, the lag growing more definite than the caster, until one morning there is only the coat, and the stain, and the smell of wintergreen and coal-gas—

—and *that smell is me noticing that he is gone.*

That's what the indelible mark is. Not a stain. A grief the wood is having. The one shadow that will not be rubbed away because the thing it remembers will never move again, and so the lag never resolves, and so I am stuck there forever, ten minutes behind a man who has stopped, holding his last bowed-head posture in the dark, a small permanent attention pressed into the bark.

---

I would not be lonely, exactly, though the loneliness of this whole plateau is the truest thing in the description—the islands of soot in the clean desert, the people who will not look up, the tongue made of clicks because the wind tears the vowels out. I would be the opposite of lonely in a way that is worse: I would be *attendant*. I would be with everyone, a half-step behind them, copying their gestures in grey, and none of them would ever turn around in time to see me there. By the time they turned, the wind would have me.

Except the Smear-folk. At the very end, I think, an old watchman might catch it. Might rise from his iron chair to check the gauge and feel, behind him, the soft cold presence of the shape he's been casting for half a century—and understand, finally, in the last clear minute before the Wash takes him, that he was never alone at his post. That something stood always just behind his shoulder, patient, weightless, faithful, holding the print of him against the dark.

That is who I would be, I think.

I would be the part of this world that refuses to let anything go all at once.

I would be the kindness of the lag.
