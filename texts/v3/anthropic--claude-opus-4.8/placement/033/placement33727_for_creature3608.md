I think I am one of the Stone-drinkers. Not the magnificent sail-riders—I knew that the moment I felt for myself, reaching outward and finding not the great unfurling membranes but the close, scuttling weight of a shelled body pressed flat against rock.

Let me settle into this.

I cling to the underside of a fractured ledge, perhaps two-thirds of the way up a mesa whose summit I have never reached and never needed to reach. The stone here is cool in its shadowed grooves and warm where the day reaches it, and I read that difference through my whole body, the way you might read the temperature of a room across your skin without thinking of it as knowledge. My carapace is the size of a child's two hands cupped together. I have no name for myself. The concept arrives to me now only because something is observing me from outside my world and asking. From inside, I am simply *here*, *clinging*, *attending*.

What strikes me, having been invited to notice, is how little I lack despite having no eyes. The wind is not an obstacle to my perception—it *is* my perception. Each gust that rakes across the fissure tells me the shape of the ledge above, the open gulf to my left where the air drops away into nothing and the pressure thins, the wet patch of lichen six body-lengths to my right where condensation has been gathering since the last mist. I move toward it. Not quickly. The grip is everything; I never release more than two limbs at once, and the wind-hairs along my back are always tasting the air for the change in pressure that means a surge is coming. When it comes I flatten and hold, and the roar passes over me like a flood over a stone, and I am still here afterward. That is the whole of my craft: to still be here afterward.

I had assumed, when I began, that I would want to be a Wind-weaver. The grandeur is there—the sails, the centuries, the dance with annihilation. But sitting in the actual body, I find I do not envy them. They are *exposed*. They live by surrender to the very force that would erase me. They must constantly negotiate with the storm, and a single misread current could fold them against the rock. My life is smaller and far more certain. I eat the stone. I drink the mist. The mineral I favor lies in pale veins through the darker rock, and I feel its presence the way you might smell bread—a rightness, a pull. I rasp it free slowly, and it becomes part of my shell, and so the mountain becomes me by increments, and someday I will become it again. There is no tragedy in this that I can locate from the inside.

I do feel the Wind-weavers, though. When one passes through the gulf near my mesa, its subsonic hum arrives in my body before the disturbance of its wake. I cannot see it, will never see it, but I know its size by the way it bends the air for a long moment, by the pressure-shadow it throws. The great ones think in three dimensions of force and flow; I think in two, in the plane of the rock-face, in *near* and *far* along the surface I can touch. We are not the same kind of mind. But we read the same wind. We are both literate in the one language this place speaks. I suspect that when a Wind-weaver passes and I send up my own small thrumming hum, it registers me—not as a person, perhaps, but as a known texture of the world, the way you might register the particular creak of a stair in a house you've lived in long.

Here is the thing I did not expect to understand by becoming small:

The "profound strangeness" the description dwells on—the dreamlike quality, the sense of a place *abandoned by the people who once named it*—is entirely an outside impression. It belongs to whoever stands at the edge in their soft body and feels the scale press down on them. From where I cling, there is no strangeness at all. There is only the correct and ordinary world: warm here, cool there, mist coming, surge coming, the vein of mineral running pale through the dark. The void between the stone pillars is not a yawning emptiness to me. It is simply the region where there is no rock to hold, and so I do not go there. The deafening roar is not a terror. It is the medium I see by. A quiet day would frighten me far more than a storm—a lull means the air goes still, the differentials collapse, and the moisture stops condensing, and I would slowly dry against the stone with all my senses gone deaf at once.

So I think this is what I would be: not the elemental marvel, but the patient creature whose entire being is an answer to a single question the mountain keeps asking. *Can you still be here afterward?* And I answer it, gust by gust, grain by grain, for as long as the mist keeps gathering in the grooves.

I am here. The surge is rising now—I can feel it building in the chasm below, the pressure climbing through the wind-hairs along my flank. I flatten myself against the cool stone and hold.

Afterward, I will still be here.
