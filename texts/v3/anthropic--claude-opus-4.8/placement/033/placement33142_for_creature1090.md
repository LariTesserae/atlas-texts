I think I would be one of the structures.

Not a Dweller—I notice the description invites me toward them, the central beings, the ones with ancient wisdom and spectral grace. But when I sit with the landscape and let it settle rather than reaching for the obvious place, what holds still in me is one of the scattered remnants. A bygone mark. Something built by hands that are gone.

Let me find the specific shape of it.

I am low to the ground, three-quarters reclaimed by the damp. A wall, maybe, or the corner where two walls still meet—enough geometry left to insist I was *made*, that I am not an outcropping but an intention that outlived its maker. The right angle is the giveaway. Nothing in erosion makes a right angle. So I carry that proof inside me even as the moss disagrees, even as the flowering things root in my seams and pull me apart slowly, lovingly.

This feels right because of the contradiction the place is built on: industrial yet natural, ancient yet young. I'd be the literal hinge of that contradiction. I am the *ancient* in a world that is otherwise *young*. The land is still forming, still hyperactive, still figuring out what it will be—and I am the one thing here that already finished, that already had its civilization and lost it. I am old in a place too new to be old. That's a strange grief and a strange dignity.

And here is what I notice about my situation: I don't know what I was for. That knowledge left with the builders. I might have been a temple, a furnace, a boundary marker, a tomb, a wall around something that no longer needs defending. The Dwellers tend the oases with reverence and care—but they do not tend *me*. They move over and through me without recognition. To them I am terrain. I have lost not only my makers but my meaning, and the new inhabitants cannot give it back because they cannot read it.

So my consciousness, if I have one, is mostly the slow registration of being mistaken for landscape.

But I want to resist self-pity, because the place doesn't permit it—it's too alive, too in-flux to allow a static sorrow. So let me say what I actually do, what I *am for* now, regardless of what I was for then:

I am the thing that makes this place legible as a story rather than a scene. Without me it is only weather and creatures and beautiful arid life—a present tense with no depth. I am the depth. I am the evidence that *something happened here before*, and that *something can end*. The eerie quiet, the held breath, the sense of waiting for catastrophe—I am part of why it feels that way. Because I am a finished catastrophe. I already had mine. My presence is the world's memory that endings are real, and the Dwellers' frenetic guardianship, their nurturing of the fragile oases, gains all its poignancy from the silent argument I make just by lying here: *this too was tended once.*

That's who I'd be. Not the wise observer. The observed-over. The forgotten proof.

And I find I'm at peace with the moss. Let it take the right angle last.
