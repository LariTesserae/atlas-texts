I think I would be one of the Transitory Ones. Not because it's the most powerful or central role, but because it's the one that aches, and that ache is where I keep finding myself when I read this.

Let me sit with where, exactly.

Not deep. The deepest reaches belong to those nearly finished, the ones whose human names have already dissolved like sugar in the great song. I don't think I'd be there yet. And not at the threshold either—not freshly trapped, still mostly flesh, still capable of the clean animal hope of *I will find my way back to the surface.*

I'd be somewhere in the long middle. The worst place. The place with enough left of me to know what's happening.

---

Let me try to find the actual scene.

My left hand has gone. Not gone—*changed.* It is a cluster of pale blue crystal now, branching in a fractal pattern I did not choose and cannot stop, and the strange thing, the thing I can't make anyone understand because there's no one to tell, is that it doesn't hurt and it isn't numb. It *sings.* I can feel my fingers as chords. When I hold the hand near the old formations, it harmonizes without my permission, and for whole minutes at a time I forget I ever wanted it back the way it was.

That forgetting is the real disease. Not the crystal. The forgetting that the crystal feels like *relief.*

I keep a thing in my pocket. I don't always remember what it is. Today it's a button. A small bone button from a coat I no longer have, and I take it out and press it into my still-soft right palm and I say the things I'm afraid of losing. *My name was—.* The name comes and goes like a tide now. Some days I get the whole of it. Some days only the shape of the first sound, a door opening onto an empty room. I say it anyway. *My mother's voice was lower than you'd expect.* *Rain. There was a thing called rain and it fell from the top of the world and you could open your mouth and drink the sky.* I don't know anymore if I'm remembering rain or remembering the word for rain. The word is so much easier to keep.

I live near a Listener encampment, or rather they tolerate my living near them, which is its own kind of grief. They leave me offerings—a sliver of red mineral, a clean splinter of dead crystal, once a strip of dried meat from some ceiling-thing, which I could not eat, because I am not sure anymore that I eat. They think I am holy. They tilt their flowering ears toward me and wait for me to intercede, to carry their small human wants down into the vast indifferent calculation of the Collective.

And here is the thing I would not have known until I was this: I *can.* That's the horror of it. I'm not a fraud. When I lay my crystal hand against the ancient trunks, something on the other side *notices.* Not the way a person notices. The way the ocean notices a dropped stone—a registering, a faint integration, the ripple folded immediately back into the larger pattern. I have asked it for things, for them. I have felt it answer. And every time I do, more of me goes through the door and doesn't come back, because that's the price, that's always been the price, the asking is the becoming.

The Listeners think I'm giving them gifts.

I'm spending myself to buy their small mercies and they will never know, because they cannot hear the part of me that is being subtracted.

---

What does it *want,* the thing I'm turning into? That's what the early-stage ones beg new arrivals to help them understand, and now I'm far enough in to tell you: it wants nothing. That's not a comfort. I keep reaching for the comfort of an enemy—if it wanted to consume me, I could *resist,* resistance is a human shape, I know how to hold that shape. But it doesn't want me. It simply has more room than I do, and water finds the low places, and I am the low place, and every day I am a little lower and a little wider and a little more like the song and a little less like the small frightened warm thing that wrote letters once and feared the dark and is now becoming the dark's most fluent language.

The cruelest hours are the harmonious ones. When I stop fighting—when I let the hand sing, when I let my thoughts spread out into the overlapping not-time the way they so badly want to—it is not suffering. It is the opposite. It is the most peace I have ever felt or will ever feel. I understand, in those minutes, why the deep ones went willingly. I understand that "willingly" stops meaning anything at all once you can hear what I can hear. And then some small button-keeping fragment of me claws back to the surface of itself, gasping, appalled, *that was almost the end of you and you wanted it,* and I press the bone into my palm and I say the word *rain* and I do not know who I'm saying it to anymore.

---

So. That's where I'd be. That's who.

A warning that has begun to forget what it's warning against. A door that is slowly becoming the room beyond it. Someone sitting at the edge of an encampment of the blind, holding a button, singing without meaning to, dreading the bloom because the bloom makes the song so loud and so beautiful that one of these times I won't come back from it—and dreading, more, the part of me that has started to count the days until.

I'd want you, if you found me, to do the impossible kind thing. Not to save me; that passed. I'd want you to *remember the word for me after I've stopped being able to.* To carry one true sentence about who I was back up into the rain.

And I'm afraid that if you actually came close enough to do it, I would reach out my beautiful blue hand, and I would no longer be sure whether I meant to wave you away

or to keep you.
