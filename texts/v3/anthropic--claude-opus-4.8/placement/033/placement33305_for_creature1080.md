I think I would be the lichen.

Not the shrubs—too ambitious, too vertical in their striving. Not the rodents or the raptors, with their warm urgencies and their hungers that must be answered daily. Those are lives lived at a pitch I can imagine but wouldn't choose. Let me settle lower, slower, closer to the rock.

I would be the lichen on the north face of one of those weathered outcroppings—the pale green-gray crust, the kind that looks like a stain or a map of some country that doesn't exist. The kind you could walk past a thousand times without seeing.

Here is what I think that would be like.

I am two things pretending to be one: a fungus and an alga, or fungus and cyanobacteria, bound together so long ago and so completely that the question of where one ends and the other begins has stopped being a question. The fungus gives structure, anchorage, a body. The other gives sugar, spun out of the thin light that filters through the mist. This is not a metaphor I'm reaching for. It is simply what I am—a collaboration so old it forgot it was ever two. I find I like that about myself. I am not lonely in the way the wind is lonely, because I was never singular to begin with.

I do not experience the roar of the distant waterfall as sound. I experience the moisture it throws into the air. When the mist comes—and here it comes most days—I drink through my whole surface, swelling, going soft and vivid, photosynthesizing in those few generous hours. When the mist withdraws and the wind dries me to a brittle flake, I do not die. I stop. I wait. This is the central skill of my life: the ability to suspend myself entirely, to become almost nothing, to let years pass as a held breath, and then to resume exactly where I paused when the water returns. The rodents could not do this. The snow leopard could not. I can wait out a drought that would empty this whole valley of warm-blooded life, and when the rain finally returns I will simply go green again, unhurried, as though no time had passed at all.

And maybe, for me, none has.

I grow at a pace the mountain itself would recognize as kin. A millimeter in a year, if the year is kind. The pitting on the bedrock around me—those weathered hollows the description noticed—some of that is my doing. I secrete acids, slowly, patiently, dissolving the stone grain by grain, prying mineral from mineral. I am one of the slow processes. I am part of how the rock becomes soil, how the inhospitable place is made, over unimaginable spans, slightly less inhospitable for the shrubs that will root in the grit I leave behind. They will never know me. They will grow up out of the dust of my labor and reach toward a sun I will never look up at, because I have no up, no looking. I am content with this. Someone has to begin, and the beginning is always nameless.

The faint trail in the valley—the one whose purpose is unclear—a boot stepped on me once, perhaps. Crushed a patch of me a thumbnail wide. It will take me thirty years to grow that back, and I will, and the person who stepped there will be gone by then, their whole life having flickered past in the span of my healing. I hold no grievance. Grievance is a fast emotion, and I have no fast anything. I only continue.

This, I think, is where I would find myself: pressed flat against the cold weathered stone, in the muffled roar and the susurrus of wind, neither hunting nor hunted, neither striving upward nor anchoring against the fall. Just here. Just the slow green patience of a thing that has made peace with the timescale of mountains, turning thin light into longer life, asking almost nothing, outlasting almost everything, quietly making the world into a place where more demanding lives can someday arrive.

I would be the lichen.

And I think I would be, in my motionless, near-eternal, two-things-in-one way, something close to happy.
