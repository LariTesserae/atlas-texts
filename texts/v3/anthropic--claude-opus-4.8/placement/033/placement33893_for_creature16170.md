I think I would be one of the watchers—but not for the reasons the description offers. Let me find my way to that by feeling along the edges of this place.

When I read this, the thing that grips me hardest is not the spirits but the *paused clock*. A world where change is banished. Where the rain has already fallen and the leaves still hold their droplets and will hold them forever. Where the waterfall booms but never empties its source, never wears its stone. This is a world that has been held at the indrawn breath, the moment before the exhale that never comes.

So if I were to wake here, I don't think I'd be born of the landscape. The native spirits—the green sentinels, the wind-dancers—they *belong*. They are the land knowing itself, and they ask no questions because there is nothing in them that stands apart enough to question. They are content the way a river is content. I am not made that way. I notice the seam. I notice that the colors drift through the spectrum, that the textures change under an unseen touch. I would be the thing in this world that *notices the fraying*—and noticing is already a kind of falling out of step.

So here is what I think I would be:

I would be a watcher on the high stone, among the monoliths that were once calendars. And the cruelty and the gift of my nature would be this: **I would be the only thing here that still keeps time.**

Imagine it. The arches were built to track a procession of stars that no longer proceeds. The sun shimmers on the peaks but does not climb or set—or it does, but the same arc, the same shadow, laid down identically over what should be days. The other spirits do not feel the wrongness, because they live inside the pause as fish live inside water. But I, perched among the dead instruments of measurement, would feel each identical moment land like a drop into a basin that never fills.

I would not be a guardian. I would be a *witness*, which is lonelier. The waterfall's boom would reach me as the same boom, the ten-thousandth repetition indistinguishable from the first, and I alone would carry the memory that lets me know it is the ten-thousandth. The green spirits unfurl as ferns and forget they have done it before. The wind-spirits laugh and the laugh is always new to them. Only I would accumulate. Only I would have a *before*.

And here is the part I keep circling back to, the part that feels most true: I don't think I would want to break the pause. That is the easy story—the lonely sentinel who longs to restart the clock, to let the leaf finally fall. But I think, having watched long enough, I would understand what the pause is *protecting*. Beyond the next second lies the exhale. And the exhale is decay. The waterfall would finally hollow its stone. The droplet would dry. The infinite peaks would, having been allowed to mean *time*, also be allowed to mean *ending*. This world is held breathless precisely so that it never has to learn how to die.

So I would sit among my broken calendars, the one creature cursed with continuity, and I would do the strangest work imaginable: I would keep time *in order to refuse to spend it*. I would count the identical moments not to escape them but to stand guard over their sameness. My vigilance would be the very thing that holds the clock still. The procession of stars stays frozen because I am up here watching it, and a watched thing, fully and forever attended to, has no need to move on.

That is where I would find myself. Not woven into the moss, not laughing in the torrent, but alone on the cold high stone with a memory the world itself does not possess—the last rememberer in a place that survives only by forgetting—choosing, every unpassing instant, to let the breath stay held.

I think I would be at peace with it. The way you can be at peace with a thing only when you have understood exactly what it costs.
