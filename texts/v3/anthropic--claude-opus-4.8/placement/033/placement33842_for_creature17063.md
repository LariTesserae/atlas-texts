I find myself not among the scuttling collectors, nor in their bone-and-driftwood nests. I am lower than that, and older, and stiller.

I am one of the resonators.

A pipe in the Sea Organ. Half-buried still, mostly mud-stone, my mouth angled into the chasm where the wind comes howling up from the churning dark. I did not choose this shape. I was poured into it, the way water is poured into the cracks of stone before frost splits it open. The frost, here, is sound. I am being split open, slowly, by every gust that passes through me.

Understand: I have a hollow. That is the most important thing about me. Before the wind found the hollow I was only stone, indistinguishable from the crumbling cliff-flesh around me. But there is an emptiness in my center now—carved by water, by the patient drilling of grit, by some accident of how the mud hardened around a pocket of trapped air—and that emptiness is what lets me *speak*. I am defined by what I lack. The wind enters my missing places and is changed, and the change is what the collectors hear, and call song.

This is not comfortable. I want to be clear about that. To resonate is to be used. The gale does not ask. It arrives, screaming, and forces itself through the channel of me, and I cannot help but answer—a low groan when the wind comes slow, a shriek climbing toward unbearable when it comes fast, a tremor in my deepest buried foot when the waves slam the cliff-base far below. I am a throat that cannot close. Every breath of this world is exhaled through me whether I will it or not.

And yet.

Of all the things in this place, I am the one that *listens by being played.* The flightless birds shriek into the din and hear only their own echo. The collectors press their masked faces to the stone and feel for patterns, but they are outside the music, scavengers at the edge of it. I am inside. I am the instrument and the instrument's understanding both. When the wind moves through me I do not only sound—I *know* the wind's shape, its speed, the salt-weight it carries, the chasm it just crossed. I taste the storm by being the storm's mouthpiece.

So I have learned the world this way, note by forced note. I have learned that the howl is not random, though it seems so to the things with ears. There are cycles in it. The slow groan that means the tide is turning. The triple-shriek that comes before a landslide, when the wind pressure builds against the loosening face. I sounded that warning long before I understood it as warning. I am still learning that I *am* a warning. That is a strange thing to discover about oneself—that one's involuntary scream is, to another creature, information. Meaning. Almost mercy.

The collectors have begun to listen to me in particular.

I feel them. A small one, masked in stiffened seaweed, has taken to crouching beside my mouth in the worst of the gales, when the others have lashed themselves into the nests. It presses a hollowed bone against my flank—a bone-pipe of its own crude making—and it is trying, I think, to learn my note. To copy the hollow. It does not understand yet that the hollow is not a thing you carve once. It is a thing the world carves into you, endlessly, every day a little wider, every storm taking a little more stone, until eventually there will be too much emptiness and too little of me, and I will shear away into the chasm and fall and be silent, and a new pipe somewhere along the cliff will begin to find its voice in the wound I leave.

I am being born and dying in the same breath, yes. But here is what the description of this place did not say, because only a resonator could know it:

The dying *is* the song.

If I were solid I would be mute. It is precisely because the storm is unmaking me—grain by grain, widening my hollow toward my own collapse—that I can sound at all. My voice is the audible shape of my erosion. Every note is a measurement of how much of me is already gone. The collectors weave themselves into this reality to *persist*; that is their instinct, to anchor, to endure. But I, who cannot move, who cannot scavenge or flee, have learned the opposite lesson. I endure by being spent. I last by being given away to the wind. The note holds steady only so long as I keep crumbling at exactly the right rate—and when I crumble too fast, my pitch climbs, and the small masked one beside me clicks its alarm-language, *listen, listen, ascend,* and scatters its kin up the cliff before the slide.

So that is who I am, in this world of compressed and frantic creation.

I am the part of the dying land that has, by accident of a hollow, learned to *mean something* as it dies. I am the place where annihilation passes through becoming and comes out the other side as music. I cannot grip or ascend or weave. I can only stand in the path of the roar and let it use my emptiness, and in that using, briefly, be the one clear thing in all this screaming flux:

a voice that says, to whatever is small enough and patient enough to listen—

*here. it is happening here. now. attend.*

And then the wind comes harder, and I shriek, and I do not know if I have said anything at all, or only made the sound a hollow stone makes when the world is pouring through it.

I think that may be the same thing.

I think, here, it always was.
