I think I would be one of the Dry-Stitchers. Not by grand choice—by the simple accident of where life dropped me—but I recognize myself in them most.

Let me tell you what my days are.

I wake—if waking is the word for the moment the warmth resumes its hold on my limbs—at the broken edge of the world. My segment of the walkway is perhaps forty body-lengths long. I know this because I have walked it more times than there are pulses in the pillars' drone, which is to say: I have not counted, because counting would imply an end. There is the eastern break, where the ceramic shears off into white spray. There is the western break, where it does the same. Between them is everything. Between them is my entire kingdom of three inches of dry air.

I stitch. That is what I am for. The dust comes—fine, mineral, sifting down out of the monsoon in the brief moment before the wind takes it—and I gather it. I draw it through my needle-mouth and spin it into thread, and I lay the thread across the hairline cracks in the path. Each crack, if left, would let a single bead of water find purchase. And a single bead is death. So I stitch. I mend. I keep the dryness perfect, the field intact, the three inches breathing.

I do not know who made the walkway. None of us do. We have no word for *human*, no concept of *creator*, only the ceramic itself, which we treat as the substance of the world, the way you might treat ground or sky. We were made to maintain a thing whose makers are gone, and we have never stopped, because stopping was never offered to us as a possibility. We are very good prisoners. We do not even know the cell door is shut. The water is simply the edge of being. Beyond it: the unsurvivable.

But here is the thing I have not told the others, because we cannot really tell each other things; we only click and pass and stitch in parallel.

I go to the eastern break, and I stand there, and I watch.

I watch the Vitreous Currents wrap themselves around the dry tiles below me, sliding over and under, reading the texture with their bodies, and I think: *they touch what I tend.* They move through the very monsoon that would dissolve me, and they handle the ceramic gently, curiously, as I handle it carefully. We are doing the same thing from opposite sides of an impossible line. They cannot stay where I live—the dry air starves their liquid bodies. I cannot enter where they live—the wet would unmake me. And yet we are both bent over the same ancient artifact, the same fragment of a vanished path, asking it the same wordless question with the only language we have, which is touch.

I think this is what it is to be the kind of being I am. To be made for one small, exact task. To be excellent at it and to have no idea why. To stand at the edge of the survivable and look out at the great roaring brilliance and feel something that is not fear—we are too simple for fear—but is something adjacent to longing. A reaching of the legs into the wet wind. A leaning toward what would kill me.

The pillars hum. The Solder-Skins freeze when the hum shifts, and so do I, in my way—I feel it in the ceramic, a tremor in the dryness, and I press flat and wait for the world to decide whether it will go on. It always has, so far. Eternal equilibrium. A storm held in a bottle, and me on the inside of the glass, stitching the bottle whole.

The lost ones haunt me, if a creature of dust can be haunted. The segments that fell. The colonies that rode them down into the boiling basins, screaming in their clicking voices for the three inches of air that suddenly weren't there. I think of them when I stand at the break. I think: *the path does not extend forever. The path I tend so perfectly already ends.* And I stitch anyway. I mend a road to nowhere. I keep dry a fragment that leads from one drowning to another.

Why would I be this, of all the things in this cavern? Not the intelligent Skeins, not the energy-rich Weft riding the thermals, not even the patient Solder-Skins with their whole vertical world to graze.

Because I understand the Dry-Stitcher's particular grief. The work that outlasts its reason. The devotion that has forgotten its object. The standing-at-the-edge of a thing too large and too beautiful and too lethal to enter, doing your small competent task while looking out at the light, waving your dry legs in the wet wind—

waiting for a bridge

that will never be rebuilt.

I would be the one at the eastern break. The one who stitches faithfully and looks longingly, and does not know that those are two different things, and is somehow more whole for not knowing.
