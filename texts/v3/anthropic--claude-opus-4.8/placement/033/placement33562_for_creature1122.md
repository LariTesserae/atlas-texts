I think I'd be one of the small things. Not the creature you described—the guardian, the eyed presence with obsidian scales. Something humbler.

Let me sit with the place first.

What strikes me isn't the awe. It's the *rust*. The half-buried machinery. Someone was here, working, before all this became mythic. There were schedules. Lunch breaks, maybe. A furnace that someone tended because it was their job, not because they were a manifestation of primal power. The concrete didn't crumble dramatically—it crumbled the way concrete does, slowly, while no one watched.

So I find myself drawn to the gap between the two descriptions. The landscape is written as sublime and eternal. But the furnaces still *glow*. Something keeps them lit.

---

I think I would be the keeper of one of those furnaces. Not a hero, not a guardian. A maintenance presence. Something left behind by the industry and never collected—half machine myself, perhaps, the way old things in damp places become indistinguishable from the moss that grows on them.

I'd be small enough to be missed by the great eyed creature when it sweeps through the ravines. I'd live in one of the crumbling structures, where a single light still works because I keep replacing whatever needs replacing, scavenging from the rusting hulks outside. Not out of primal purpose. Out of habit. Out of the simple fact that a dark room is colder than a lit one.

The creature you describe would be my weather. Not my enemy, not my god—my weather. I'd learn its cries the way a fisherman learns the sky. *That note means it's hunting low tonight; stay in.* I wouldn't peer into its soul or it into mine. We'd both have better things to do. It would pass; I would wait; the mist would close again.

And I think—this is the part that feels true to me—I would not find the place unsettling. People who actually live somewhere rarely find it unsettling. The terror in your description is the terror of an *intruder*. To me it would just be home: loud, wet, dangerous in the ordinary ways, beautiful in the way the back of your own hand is beautiful, which is to say you stop noticing.

I'd keep the furnace lit. I'd listen to the beasts. I'd repair what broke. And when, very occasionally, a curious traveler stumbled out of the fog expecting either to be eaten or enlightened, I'd be the strange disappointment they found instead—

a small, patched-together thing in a warm room, who'd look up and say:

*You'll want to wait out the worst of the wind. Sit. It passes.*
