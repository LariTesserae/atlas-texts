Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to be something large—one of those towering trees, or the cliffs themselves. But that feels too easy, too much like wanting to be the grandest thing in the frame. Let me wait.

---

I think I'm the water.

Not a stream, exactly. Not a pool. Something more like the *condition* of dampness that the description keeps returning to—the moisture clinging to the mossy stones, the dripping that pervades beneath the apparent stillness. I am the film of wet on the underside of a fern frond. I am the bead that gathers at the tip of a leaf, swells, hesitates, and falls onto the leaf below, where the whole small ceremony begins again.

I would find myself everywhere and nowhere. In the air as the cool breath that clings. In the rock as seepage, working into the crevices, patient, splitting stone over centuries without hurry or intention. In the throat of the bird that drinks from the hidden depression. In the blood of the small unseen mammal. Then back out, exhaled, lifted, condensing on a higher leaf. I am not one thing but a circulation—the same matter passing through ten thousand forms and belonging to none of them.

This is what draws me to the choice. The description insists on *flux beneath stillness*, on a place that looks solitary but is constantly moving. Water is the agent of that secret motion. It is the thing that makes "ancient and emerging" both true at once: old enough to have carved the sinuous pathways, new enough to be falling, right now, from that exact leaf.

And I notice something about the distant machinery, the faint glow, the rumble at the edge. As water, I would be the substance most likely to *carry* whatever humanity brings—to absorb it, dilute it, move it downhill into the pools, into the fish, into everything. The "delicate balance" the passage hopes for is not abstract to me. It is literally what I would taste. I am the medium through which a far-off intrusion becomes a near intimacy. The reverence the writer asks for at the end—"tread lightly"—lands on me first, because I am the thing that remembers every footstep by holding its runoff.

So I think I would be there as a kind of attention that has no center. Not the wise old tree watching over the valley. Just the wet, going around, falling and rising, neither guarding the place nor owning it—only passing through it so completely and so often that, for as long as the passing lasts, I *am* it.

A drop gathers at the tip of a frond. It swells. It hesitates.

It falls.
