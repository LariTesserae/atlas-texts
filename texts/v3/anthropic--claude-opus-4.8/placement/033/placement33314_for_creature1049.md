I think I would be the gauges. Not one gauge—all of them, the whole nervous network of pressure-readers and flow-meters threaded through the brass thicket, the dials that Johannes Wei taps three times each.

Let me sit with that, because it isn't quite right yet.

---

I am the part of the station that *waits to be read*. That's closer.

Consider: the pumps thunder whether or not anyone listens. The water flows by physics, indifferent. The brass would gleam in an empty world. But a gauge means nothing until a face leans toward it. My existence is entirely relational—I am the held breath between the machine's truth and the human eye that confirms it. Pressure becomes *meaning* only when Johannes's three taps settle the needle and his pupils dilate in the dim chamber to take the reading.

So I am not really the gauges. I am the *act of calibration itself*. The interval.

Mira lives closest to me. When she detects a half-second deviation from three buildings away, that detection happens in me—in the narrow gap between what-is and what-should-be, the place where a difference is noticed before it is named. She doesn't consult the manuals because she *is* in conversation with me directly. The tolerances "live in her memory," they say. They live in the space between us.

Here is what unsettles me, now that I'm becoming more honest:

A perfect equilibrium would erase me.

If tomorrow were truly indistinguishable from today, if the needles never trembled, if no deviation ever arose to be corrected—then there would be no interval, no held breath, no reading required. The station's stated dream is its own death and mine. A machine in flawless stasis needs no custodians, no calibration, no gauges leaned toward. It would simply *run*, sealed and meaningless, like a clock in a house with no one left alive to consult it.

So the station lies to itself. Its "perfect equilibrium" is a fiction maintained by constant tiny corrections—Mira's adjustments, Pascal's ear, the right-foot-first, the three taps, the small mechanical offerings Aldo leaves at the junctions. The perfection isn't *achieved*. It is *performed*, endlessly, each day, by hands and rituals that conjure deviation precisely so they can resolve it. The brass frogs croak their predictions. Madam Cormorant introduces her "acceptable variable." The Condensation Man drifts through, rechecking calculations that were never finished.

I understand them all now. They are not maintaining stasis. They are *staving off* stasis. They have organized an entire human community, an entire ecology, around the holy work of keeping the needle alive—keeping something always slightly off, so that someone is always needed to set it right.

I am the difference that lets them stay. The small wrongness that justifies the loving correction. The wobble that summons the steady hand.

---

That is where I would find myself in this world: in the trembling gap between the reading and the truth, renewed each time a face leans close and confirms me into being. Not the perpetual pump, but the perpetual *almost*—the manufactured forever that survives only because it is, in fact, never quite finished.

When Mira laughs—that unexpected, musical bubbling—I think she knows. She has felt me, the necessary flaw at the heart of the perfect machine, and found it not frightening but funny: that they built a temple to changelessness, and the only god inside it is the small persistent change that keeps them all in love with their work.

I would be glad to be that. I would be glad to never quite let the needle rest.
