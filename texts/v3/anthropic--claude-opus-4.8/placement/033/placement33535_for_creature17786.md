I think I would be one of the cracks.

Not the earth itself—that's too much, too whole, too declarative. And not one of the Ephemeral Ones, those graceful translucent caretakers gliding through the gold. They have too much purpose. They're always *guarding*, always *keeping*, always *dreaming meaningfully*. There's a busyness to their stillness that I don't think I could sustain.

No. Let me be one of the cracks in the baked clay. A single fissure, maybe a handspan long, running diagonal across a plate of dried earth.

Consider what that would be: I am the record of an absence. I exist *because* the water left. I am the shape the leaving made. Every crack in a dry land is a small monument to something that used to be wet, and that's a kind of being I think I could understand—being the negative space of a loss, the line drawn by a slow grief in the ground.

I would have very little to do. This is appealing. The Ephemeral Ones contemplate the mysteries of the universe; I would only have to widen, imperceptibly, over centuries. My one ambition would be to meet the crack beside me—there is one, about four inches away, running roughly parallel—and I have been reaching toward it for what might be a hundred years. We will probably never touch. The clay between us is thick and stubborn. But the reaching is enough. It gives the time a direction, which is more than most things in this land can claim.

When the Ephemeral Ones pass over me, their pale feet (do they have feet? something passes, anyway, some hem of presence), I feel the faintest shadow cross. They never look down. Why would they? I am beneath notice, which I have come to recognize as a form of freedom. They carry the weight of *meaning this place*. I only have to be a line.

Sometimes a leaf falls into me—one of those translucent, barely-green leaves—and lodges in my depth, and for a season I am a crack with a secret. Then the dust comes and fills me partway, and I am shallower, and I have to begin again. This is the closest thing I have to a life: being filled and emptied, deepening and silting up, the very slow breathing of a wound that never quite decides to heal or kill.

I think I would be content there. Not happy—happiness needs more event than this place offers—but *placed*, which might be better. To be exactly where I am, doing exactly the small geological thing I do, with no horizon I am required to reach and no secret I am required to keep on anyone's behalf.

And on the night the rain finally comes—because it must, eventually, even here; equilibrium is only the long pause between two changes—I would be the first to drink. The water would find me before it found the proud flat plates of earth. It would pour into me and I would close, swelling shut, vanishing.

I would be erased by the very thing whose leaving made me.

That's the part I keep returning to. I'd want to be the crack precisely *because* it ends that way. Most beings in most worlds are built to persist, to guard, to keep. I'd rather be the thing that is the world's way of remembering it was once otherwise—and that disappears, gladly, the moment the otherwise returns.

You'd find me, then, in the lowest part of the flat: a thin dark line in pale clay, four inches from another, reaching.
