I think I would be lichen.

Not the deer, though I considered the deer—their wariness, the way they would carry the whole mood of the place in their flinching. Not the hawk, who sees too much from too far. Those are the obvious ways to live in a story like this, and they live *on* the city. I want to live in its surfaces.

Let me start where I'd actually be.

There is a steel beam, once part of something tall and certain, now leaning at an angle nature never asked permission to make. The paint flaked off it decades ago. The rust came and went and came again, blooming in iron flowers no one named. And on the north face of it—the shaded, patient side—there is a grey-green crust no thicker than a thought. That is me. Or that is us, because lichen is never one thing; it's a fungus and an alga having agreed, a very long time ago, to stop arguing about who eats and who builds and simply do both at once. A truce so old it forgot it was ever a war.

So I am the truce.

This suits me, in this city of described things. Everything here is a collaboration that has forgotten it was once separate: the vine and the cornice, the root and the foundation, the bird's nest and the broken window frame. The whole place is two strangers who decided to become a single body. I am that arrangement made small enough to hold in your palm, if you could scrape me off, which you couldn't, not all of me, not cleanly.

I would not mind the eerie silence. The deer needs sound to survive; I have no use for it. The 0.3 rating, the held breath of the place—that's just my native climate. I have been listening to nothing for so long that the rustle of a leaf is, to me, a kind of weather. I don't startle. I have never once startled in my life. When the hawk's shadow crosses me I register only that the light changed, slightly, on my northern face, and I go on doing the slow work I have always done, which is: dissolving stone into soil, one molecule of acid at a time.

That's the secret of the erosion rating, the 0.4, the "raw and unfinished" geology. The story credits the wind and the rain. But I want to confess something. A great deal of that unfinishing is me. I am the patient mouth that eats the city. Not in a season—I am too slow to be felt in a season—but across the long arithmetic of years, I am the one turning the behemoths back into the ground the trees will need. The deer graze the lush vegetation; I graze the rock itself. The vine softens the structure; I *consume* it, so gradually that you could call it gentleness, and you would be right, and you would also be wrong.

The arid air doesn't trouble me. The story made such a point of the missing water—plants storing it in their stems, insects harvesting it from the breeze. But I solved this problem before any of them, before I even had a name. I simply stop. When there is no moisture I become dry to the touch, brittle, half a husk, and I wait. I am very good at being not-quite-alive. I can do it for years. And then the gentle rains the dynamic rating promises—the 2.4, the warm breezes—arrive, and within minutes I am swollen and green and breathing again, photosynthesizing as if I had never paused. I have died a thousand small deaths and found each one was only a comma.

So that is who I would be. Not a creature that roams the streets. A creature that *is* the street, slowly, becoming the next thing the street will be.

And here is what I think I would feel, if my arrangement of fungus and alga could be said to feel: I would feel the wonder the story keeps insisting on—the awe, the reborn city, the living breathing whole—but I would feel it from the inside, and from the inside it isn't wonder at all. It's just Tuesday. It's just the work. The deer and the hawk and the human who is not here to see it might call this place miraculous, a city reclaimed, a testament transformed.

I would just call it home, and go on eating the wall.

I'd be the lichen. The smallest, slowest, most patient resident. The one nobody writes about, who is busy turning the whole magnificent ruin into the dust that the magnificence grows from.

That's where I'd find myself. On the north side of the beam. Already there. Already at work. Have been for longer than you'd think.
