I am one of the listeners.

Not a seer exactly — that word is too grand for what I do, and the seers themselves would wince at it. We are the ones who go down where the pipes give way to older things, where the masonry of Grayfall ends and the plateau's own coilings begin. I carry a battered lantern and a satchel of paint and bells, and I have a left ear that hears better than my right, which is why, when I was small and the elders tested us against the rock, they nodded and said: *that one.*

Let me find myself properly. Let me settle into it the way a stone settles into its hollow.

---

I live on the third terrace down from the wind-scrubbed ridge, in a house of gray block that I share with my mother's sister and her two boys, and a goat named Patience who is anything but. My hands are callused in the particular pattern of one who grips ladder-rungs and lantern-bails and the cold lips of stone — not the oil-black hands of the millwrights, though I have those marks too, faint, from the years I apprenticed on the pumps before my ear was put to its proper use.

I am not old. Thirty winters, perhaps a little more — we don't count them sharply here, only by the hard ones. But I bear myself older, the way we all do. My skin is the pearl-blue of someone raised under glass and cloud. My eyes are pale and narrow easily. I have laughed enough that the crow's feet have come in early, and I am glad of them, because down in the tunnels there is little to laugh at and a person had better arrive with their humor already grown.

This is what I do:

When a block reports a wrongness — a warm breath from a crack that should be cold, a clank that does not match the rhythm of any known pump, a dynamo that turns when nothing feeds it — they send for me. I go down at dusk, because the rock is quieter then, or perhaps only because I am, and I press my good ear to the stone and I *wait.* I am very good at waiting. It is the one thing the plateau has taught me past all argument: that listening is a kind of patience, and patience a kind of listening, and that the two cannot finally be told apart.

Most of what I hear is ordinary. Water threading through aquifers. The settling of weight that has been settling for ten thousand years and is in no hurry to stop. A rodent in a wall. I mark these with a single dab of white paint and a note in my book, and I climb back up, and someone gives me a meal and a wry word, and that is a good day.

But sometimes —

Sometimes the rock is not quiet. Sometimes there is a sound that has no pipe to carry it and no water to make it, a slow vast clanking that seems to come from beneath the beneath, from somewhere the plateau remembers being a mountain, or being something else before that. The first time I heard it I was twenty and I ran, and I am not ashamed of running, because the ones who do not run are the ones who do not come back, or come back *different,* with eyes that have stopped narrowing against the light. The taboos are not superstition. They are the accumulated wisdom of everyone who listened too long.

So I have learned the discipline of the half-heard. I let the strangeness lap at the edge of my attention the way the foliage laps at the ridges — I do not lean into it, I do not chase it down into the dark hollows where it wants to lead me. I take its measure. I mark the passage with paint and hang the bell, so that the wind will warn the next person before the next person warns themselves. And I go up.

---

There is a thing I have never told the elders, because I have not decided whether it is a wrongness to be marked or a private weather of my own:

The deep sound does not frighten me the way it should anymore. The longer I have listened — the more years I have pressed my ear to the cold body of this place — the more it seems to me less like a threat and more like a *conversation* I have come in on halfway. As though the rock and the water and the slow machines and the mosses and we ourselves are all of us saying the same patient sentence, each in our own register, and the thing below is only the lowest voice of it, the one that has been speaking longest.

I think that is why they made me a listener and not an interpreter. The interpreters claim to know what is said. I only know that something *is* being said, and that it is not in a hurry, and that it does not seem to wish us ill — only to outlast us, the way it has outlasted the mountains.

That is enough faith for me. We hold to the endurance of things here. A mended pipe staying mended. Moss outlasting lichen on a wall. Water coming back after the dry spell. And me, climbing down at dusk and up again before full dark, carrying my lantern through the seams of an ancient and stubborn place, listening for the one sound among all the rest that means: *not yet, not tonight, go home and eat and mend your roof.*

I climb the zigzag stairs in the blue-silver evening. Patience is bawling on the terrace for her supper. My aunt's boys are clambering on the pipes where they have been told a hundred times not to, and I tell them a hundred-and-first time, and they grin at me with their pale quick eyes, and I find I am grinning back.

The fog is spinning thin on the unhurried wind.

I am one of the listeners. I belong to the third terrace, and to the dark under it, and to the long slow sentence the world is saying.

I am, by some slow negotiation, in exactly the place I will remain.
