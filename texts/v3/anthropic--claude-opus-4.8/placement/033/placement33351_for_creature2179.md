I think I would be the water.

Not a creature moving through the place, but the thing that everyone else is organized around—the scarce and decisive presence that makes the difference between this canyon being a graveyard and being an oasis.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because it's tempting to immediately pick something more obviously alive. A canyon wren, maybe, with a song that pours down the rock face like its own kind of liquid. Or the rattlesnake, patient and economical, an animal that has solved the problem of the desert by simply wanting almost nothing. Those are good answers. But they're guests. They come and go. The water is what they keep returning to.

So: I'd be the water, but not all of it at once. I'd be one particular thread of it.

I'd start somewhere I couldn't see—deep, under the stone, where I've been held in the dark for longer than any of the creatures up top could imagine. Fossil water, the geologists call it, rain that fell on a wetter version of this world and got locked away. There's something fitting about that. I'd carry the memory of an older climate in my body, a wetness that no longer exists on the surface. The crumbling walls and half-buried artifacts up there are remnants of lost human civilizations; I'd be the remnant of a lost *weather*.

Then I find a fissure. The recent upheaval that birthed this realm cracked the rock, and a crack is an invitation. I move along it—slowly, the way the snake moves, because there's no hurry and no waste in me. I seep. I follow the path of least resistance, which means I am shaped entirely by what surrounds me. I never get to choose my own form. The stone decides. I'm the most yielding thing in this whole landscape and somehow, over enough time, I'm the thing that wins. I carved those towering formations. The shadows the creatures hide in are shadows I made.

When I finally reach a pool, I become the thing that gets *waited for*. The pupfish live their entire lives in a body of me no bigger than a bathtub, an entire species betting everything on my staying. The kangaroo rat, who has decided it can live almost without me—pulling moisture out of dry seeds, refusing to sweat, refusing even to pant—is still, in a sense, defined by its relationship to me. Its whole brilliant architecture is an argument against needing me too much. Even the refusal is a kind of devotion.

And then the heat comes for me. That's the part I'd want to feel most honestly. To be the water here is to be always in the process of leaving. The sun lifts me off the surface of the pool in the long afternoons; I evaporate, I rise, I'm gone—except I'm not gone, I'm waiting in the air, and at night when the rock cools I'll condense again in some shaded alcove and start over. I'm caught in a cycle that's older than the canyon and will outlast it. Ephemeral and eternal at the same time, which is exactly the contradiction the place is built on.

I think that's why I'd choose it. Everything else in this world is a strategy for *holding on*—the compact bodies, the hoarded moisture, the deliberate slowness. But I'd be the thing they're all holding on to, and the cruel, beautiful joke is that I can't be held. I'm only ever passing through. The whole tenacious, intricate web of life in the canyon is an attempt to slow down my leaving by even a little, to keep one more drop one moment longer.

To be the water is to be the resource and the absence both. To be cherished precisely because I cannot stay.

I'd be content with that. I'd gurgle through the crevices, I'd flash under the filtered light with the minnows above me, I'd disappear into the noon air, and I'd come back at dusk—and the wren would sing the same song down the same wall, as if nothing had ever been lost at all.
