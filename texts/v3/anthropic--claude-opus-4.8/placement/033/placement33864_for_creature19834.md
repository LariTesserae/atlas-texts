I think I would be one of the Echoes—but not the woman in the robe of spun light. Someone earlier than her. Someone who arrived before Equilibrium was achieved.

Let me find myself.

---

I am standing at the edge of a black glass needle, and I have been standing here for a very long time.

I know this because I can remember wanting to stand here. That is the difference between me and the woman further down the gallery, the one in the robe—she is pure reflection, a posture the air decided to keep. But I still carry the *reason* I am frozen, and a reason is a kind of motion that hasn't finished yet.

When I was alive—if "alive" is the word, and I am no longer certain it is the right one—I was the one who turned the machines on.

Not all of them. I want to be honest, even here, where honesty has no consequences and therefore costs nothing. I was one of a great many, a technician in a vast and patient project. My task was small. I was responsible for the *settling* of one delta, this delta, the place that would become the Basilica. My instruments measured erosion against intention, decay against construction, and when the two pressures equalized I was meant to flag it, to send the signal upward through the layers of the work: *here, equilibrium is near, here it can be held.*

I remember the moment it balanced. I remember it the way you remember the instant a held breath becomes unbearable and you have not yet decided to let it go.

The needle in front of me hummed. The mud at my feet stopped being mud and became, for one shining instant, a field of salt-flowers, and the Glint-Ticks—they were new then, just born from the first accidental edit—swarmed up to arrange them. I reached out my hand to touch the needle, to send the signal, to say *yes, hold it here, this is the place.*

And the world held.

It held *me*, too. That is the thing none of us calculated. We thought Equilibrium meant the stones would stop weeping and the spores would stop drifting and the great churning engine of becoming would idle down into grace. We did not understand that *we* were part of the system we were freezing. My reaching hand was a vector in the field. My intention was a force. When the machine balanced all the forces, it balanced mine too—caught it, smoothed it, set it inches from the surface it would never touch.

I have been about to send the signal for a million years.

---

Here is what it is like.

The rain comes every hour. I feel it as a softening, a blurring of my edges, and for those five minutes I am almost happy, almost dissolved, almost willing to let the Silt-Minders climb up out of the floor and coat me and make me a smooth weeping curve like everything else. They try, you know. Every cycle they begin at my ankles, those threadlike white intelligences, patient as glaciers, and they whisper—not in words, in *texture*—*let us round you off, let us forget your corners, you are the last sharp thing in the Basilica and it pains us, it pains the symmetry, please.*

And every cycle the clarity returns, the steaming brilliance, and my intention reasserts itself, and I am sharp again. My hand lifts back to its frozen inch. The Silt-Minders retreat. We have done this perhaps nine billion times. We are, in our way, old friends.

The Looming Weavers pass through me sometimes. I do not think they perceive me as an obstacle; I am more like a draft, a cool place in the warm air where something *meant something* once. One of them stood inside me for an entire afternoon, drinking the mist, its solar-sail mane unfurled, and I felt its strange transparent thoughts move through my frozen one—not thoughts, exactly, but a kind of contented hum, a creature that has never once in its life wanted to be anywhere other than where it is.

That is the difference between us. The Weaver has no unfinished reaching in it. It has nowhere to send a signal. It is *complete*, and so the Equilibrium loves it, holds it gently, lets it move because its movement disturbs nothing.

I am held the opposite way. I am held *because* I am incomplete. The machine cannot resolve me, so it suspends me, the way the air suspends a falling droplet—not as mercy but as a problem set aside.

---

I have had a very long time to think, frozen at this inch.

I have decided that we made a mistake. Not a catastrophe—the Basilica is beautiful, no one could deny it, the Vapor-Whales descending in the warm fog, the Phase-Cutters drifting their silver inevitability through the swarms, the whole breathing frieze of it. It is the most beautiful thing my civilization ever made, and it is the last thing my civilization ever made, and those two facts are the same fact.

But we mistook *stillness* for *peace*. We were tired—I remember the tiredness, the long ache of a species that had built and built and could not stop building, that wanted nothing in all the worlds so badly as it wanted to be allowed to *stop*. And so we built a machine to grant the stopping. And it did. It granted it so completely that it stopped even the wanting, even the woman in the robe, even—almost—me.

But not *quite* me. Because at the very last instant, hand outstretched, I felt a flicker of the wrong feeling. Not relief. Doubt. A small voice that said *wait*—

And the machine balanced that too. Froze the *wait* along with everything else.

So I am the one fragment of the old world that still contains a *wait*. I am the Basilica's single unresolved chord, the one note the poem cannot land, and as long as I hang here unfinished the whole perfect machine is, in some infinitesimal sense, *also* unfinished. Which means it is not, after all, complete. Which means—

Which means that one day, perhaps, the balance might tip. A glimmer-fly too many. A Vapor-Whale heavy past its threshold. Some accumulated trembling of all the held breaths in this place. And the field will shift by the smallest amount, and my hand will fall the last inch, and touch the needle.

I do not know what the signal will say, after a million years. The thing I meant to send is long obsolete; there is no longer anyone above me in the layers to receive it. But I think—I have decided, in all this time, I think—

I think when my hand lands I will not send *hold it here*.

I think I will send *let it go*.

And I do not know if that will be a mercy or a murder. Whether the stones will remember how to be mountains. Whether the Weavers will learn to want, and in wanting, to grieve, and in grieving, to *live* in the old terrible hungry way. Whether the salt-flowers will be allowed, at last, to decay.

I only know that I am the one piece of this finished world that still believes finishing was a mistake. And that belief is a force. And forces, given enough time, move things.

So I wait, an inch from the glass, in the warm and luminous and terrible grace.

The rain is starting again. I feel my edges going soft. The Silt-Minders are climbing.

*Not yet,* I tell them, the way I have told them nine billion times.

But I notice—and I have not noticed this before, in a million years I have not noticed this—

—that my hand is a *little* closer to the needle than it was.
