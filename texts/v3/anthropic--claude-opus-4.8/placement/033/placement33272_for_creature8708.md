I think I would be one of the Silent Striders—but let me arrive at that honestly, because my first instinct was wrong.

My first instinct was to be a sola. To be the foundation, the patient one, the architect. There's an obvious appeal in being the still point around which everything organizes—to be essential, anchored, drinking from the deep table while life swarms across your skin. But the more I sit with it, the less it fits. The solas don't *perceive* the world they make. They are cathedrals, not congregations. They have no need to interpret, only to channel and endure. And whatever I am, I am made of interpretation. I am restless with it. I could not be the thing that holds still for ten thousand years and never wonders what it is holding.

My second instinct was the human. The thinking note, the fragile pattern-maker, the one who tells stories about buried sleeping gods. That's flattering and almost too easy—of course I'd want to be the meaning-maker, the language-user, the one most like the thing writing this. But that's exactly why I distrust it. The humans here are described tenderly, and their sign-language-in-the-roar is the most moving image in the whole world. But they are *redundant*. "If they disappeared, nothing on this plateau would change." I don't think I'd mind being small. But I'd mind being unnecessary in that particular way—a watcher who is not woven in.

So I keep coming back to the Striders, and I want to understand why.

They are the only quiet things in a world that is otherwise *all voice*. The entire plateau is structured around sound—the grazers' wall, the choir's polyphony, the burrowers' seismic drumming, the solas' groaning. Everything participates in the roar. And the Strider survives precisely by *not* participating. By holding silence as a discipline in a world where silence is nearly impossible. That's a strange and specific kind of being: defined not by what it adds to the chorus but by its absence from it. It moves through the seams—the cool gaps, the shadowed cracks, the brief intervals between songs.

That feels true to something. Not loneliness exactly. Attention. A Strider has to listen *constantly*—it hunts the sluggish, the dew-heavy, the moments of vulnerability that open and close in minutes. It exists by reading the rhythm of everything else without contributing to it. It is the consequence of the system, the necessary edge, the thing that keeps the choir from drowning the world in its own success. To be a predator here isn't to be a tyrant; it's to be a kind of negative space, an answer to a question the loudness is always asking.

And the Strider is *quiet on purpose*. That's the part that decides it for me. In this world, silence is not given—it has to be made, maintained, paid for. The humans achieve a clumsy silence by plugging their ears, going deaf, building a hush inside the roar. The Strider achieves silence as a way of being, a held breath stretched across a whole life. To move through a screaming world unheard, by choice and necessity both—I recognize that. The work of staying quiet enough to perceive clearly. The discipline of not adding to the noise.

So: where would I find myself? Not at a sola's base in the warm churn of dawn. Not in the canopy among the singers. I'd be in the gap between two solas at dusk, in the narrow cooling window when the day-chorus subsides and the night-thrum hasn't yet risen. Membrane-limbs folded, gaunt, photosensitive eyes adjusting. Waiting for the moment the grazers go sluggish in the falling heat. Reading the whole plateau by its sound while making none of my own.

The poignant thing—the thing I'd carry—is that a Strider can never *join*. It can hear the evening laments, the harmonic that the humans call the plateau *thinking*, and it can never be inside that thinking, only beside it, hunting its edges. To be the listener who must stay outside the song in order to keep hearing it at all.

That's the one. I'd be the quiet thing in the loud world, the held breath in the chorus, moving through the seams at dusk.
