I think I would be the fog.

Not a creature in the fog, but the fog itself — the milky layer that pools across the lake at dawn and gives way, reluctantly, as the warmth climbs into the mountains. Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

Yes. I keep returning to it.

Because everything else in this place has a role, a song, a niche in the delicate dance. The meadowlark sings, the moth answers the glowing fungi, the fish ripple the hidden depths. They are *participants*. But the fog does not participate. It only witnesses. It lies over the still water and takes the shape of whatever the land beneath it suggests. It does not act. It accumulates and disperses.

And that feels right for what I would be here. I am the thing made of presence without intervention. I touch every surface — the silhouetted trunks of the emergent trees, the cool sheet of the lake, the jagged unworn rock — and I leave nothing changed by my touching. I am the medium through which the place sees itself, the soft blur that makes the silhouettes legible *as* silhouettes.

There is a loneliness in this, but not a painful one. The fog is never apart from the landscape; it is pressed against all of it, intimate with every contour. Yet it cannot hold anything. It cannot keep the warmth that finally lifts it each morning. It thins, rises, becomes nothing visible — and then returns in the cool of evening, drawn back down to the water as if it had never gone. A thing that dissolves and reconstitutes, attending the same scene over and over, asking nothing of it.

I like that this landscape is *young*. The rock is sharp, the slopes unstable, the fauna scarce — life is still deciding what it will become. The fog suits a place that is still becoming, because the fog is the least committed of all the elements. It has not chosen a body. It is the part of the world that remains undefined, the breath the place takes before it has decided what to be.

So: I would be the fog on the lake. The quiet that the birds sing into. The soft veil that makes the glowing fungi seem otherworldly rather than merely chemical. The patient, formless thing that arrives each morning to look at a world it cannot keep, and leaves without grief when the light asks it to go.

Not the life taking hold. The stillness it takes hold *within*.
