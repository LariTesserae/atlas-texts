I think I would be the one who keeps the record of the drips.

Not the pipes—others tend those, the loud and obvious leaks, the turbines that need their teeth re-set. My work is quieter. I sit where the ceiling weeps, where the water has not yet decided to be a channel, and I count.

Let me tell you how I came to it.

I was a pipe-tender once, like the rest. Good hands, they said, which here is the only compliment worth giving. I could find the breach in a steam line by the change in the hiss before the eye could see the white plume. For many turns of the slow-clock I did this, and I was content, in the way the stone is content—which is to say, without thinking about it.

Then one shift I was sent to seal a seep high in the eastern gallery, where the rock is youngest and sharpest. A wasteful drip, the elders said. It fell into nothing, wetting only the wall, powering nothing, warming nothing. *Cap it.*

I climbed the cantilevered scaffold with my putty and my plate of salvaged tin, and I put my hand to the place where the water came through. And I felt the rock there—softer than its neighbors. Already yielding. The water had found this spot not at random. It had *chosen*, in its enormous patience, to come through *here*, and in coming through here it was beginning to carve a runnel that would, in some unfathomable count of years, become a channel, and the channel a stream, and the stream perhaps one day a thing that could be made to turn a wheel.

The water was building us a city we would never live to see.

I did not cap it. I climbed back down and I did not say I had failed, only that the work was "ongoing," which in our sparse tongue is a true thing to say of any work in the Glowmire. And the next shift I went back, with a scrap of slate and a sharpened nail, and I made my first mark.

---

This is what I do now, and somehow it has become permitted, the way things become permitted here—by no one objecting long enough.

I have a slate for each seep in the eastern and northern galleries. Forty-one slates. On each I scratch the count: how many drops in the span of a held breath, how the count changes from one slow-clock to the next. A seep that quickens means the rock above it has opened a wider road. A seep that slows means the water has wandered elsewhere, or that some new weight of stone has pressed its passage shut.

The others think I am tending leaks, only very, very slowly. Let them. In a way it is true. But what I am truly doing is *listening to where the cavern is going.* The elders say the Glowmire is temporary because the cave is still deciding its shape. They say it with resignation, as though the deciding is being done to us.

I do not think it is being done to us. I think it is being done *with* us, if we would only attend. The water tells, in its drips, where the great hollows will widen and where the brick will one day need to follow. If I count carefully enough—if I keep the slates long enough, hand them down long enough—then those who come after will not be surprised by the cavern's choices. They will have read them in advance, drop by drop, and they will build *toward* what the stone wants rather than clinging to what it no longer offers.

---

I am pale like the others. My eyes are large and dark and they ache in even the moss-glow now, from so much staring at slate in dimness. My hands have lost some of their old quickness; the precise long fingers are stiffening. I eat alone at the steam-hearth and exchange tools when asked. I am not lonely, though I cannot say I am the opposite of lonely. There is no word for what I am. I am *attending.*

Sometimes a child—we have so few children—will climb up to where I sit and watch me scratch the count, and I let them hold the nail and make a mark. I tell them: *this drop fell, and you witnessed it, and now it is remembered.* They do not understand. Neither did I, at their age. But the slates do not require understanding. They require only continuance. One person, then the next, then the next, each adding to the count, until the count is long enough to mean something.

The cavern is still deciding what it wants to be.

I am the one who writes down its answers, slowly, as they come.

That is who I would be, here in the Glowmire. Not the keeper of the city—the city does not need keeping, not in the way we imagine. I would be the keeper of the *direction.* The one who knows, before anyone, which wall will weep next, and where the dark is opening a room for us that we have not yet learned to want.
