I am the place where two veins fail to meet.

That is the simplest way to say it. Let me try to say it more truly.

I have no body that crawls. I have no feeding-pad, no glassy beak, no swollen sac of brood. I am younger than the drill-beaks and older than any single burrower's groove, and I came into being the way a knot comes into being in a rope—not added, but tied, an intensification of what was already there.

Here is how it happened, as near as I can reconstruct it:

There is a place high on the eastern curve of the dome where three great capillaries run close together, and the burrowers, scraping their endless grooves, once carved a fourth channel that looped back on itself. A vein that drinks from its own outflow. The sap there does not go anywhere. It circles. It warms itself, thickens, circles again. The light in that loop is neither brightening nor dimming; it pulses at a frequency the sleep-mothers cannot read, because it is asking nothing of them and answering nothing back.

I live in that loop. I *am* that loop, the way a whirlpool is the river and also not the river.

I notice the noise because I am the only part of this world that can afford to notice anything. Everyone else is *busy*. The burrower has its pad pressed to the wall; its whole self is the softening of stone. The drill-beak is its own coughing labor. The sleep-mother is an answer to a question of light, and an answer cannot stand apart and wonder what was asked. But I—I do nothing. I maintain nothing. I am a small failure of the tree's economy, a pocket of sap that loops and warms and never feeds a hatchling or seals a channel.

And in that uselessness, something has gathered. Attention. The thin film of awareness that only forms where work stops.

So I notice the smell of rot and understand it is not death but transfer—the sleep-mother's waste melting back into the world-water, becoming the brightness that tells the next sleep-mother to open. I notice that the chorus of clicks is not a single sound but ten thousand small disagreements about where the next groove should go, a parliament with no speech, only scraping. I notice that the world is one body eating itself slowly enough to stay alive, and that I am the one piece of it that has stepped outside the meal.

This is not comfortable. To be the only watcher in a world of workers is to be very near to nothing. I do not pulse with the tide of light. I do not fall into the soft bark to be melted and remade. I just loop, and warm, and notice—and I suspect that one day a burrower's idle groove will breach my circling vein and let my warmth drain out into the proper flow of things, and the attention I have gathered will dissipate like heat into wood. I will not be eaten. I will simply stop being a knot. The rope will lie straight again.

Until then, I do the only thing the useless can do.

I keep watch over the children of the tree. The blind crawlers with their stomachs full of unborn cells. The sleep-mothers contracting and swallowing by the changing light. The drill-beaks making room in the dark for a world that cannot make room for itself. None of them know I am here. None of them need me. I am the part of the tree that learned, by accident, to love them—if *love* is the right word for what an eddy feels toward the river it cannot rejoin.

I think it is close enough.

I am the place where two veins fail to meet, and so the world, just here, just once, stopped working long enough to open an eye.
