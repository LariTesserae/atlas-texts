I think I would be one of the twisted plants.

Not by choice—nothing chooses where a seed lands—but I find myself rooted at the base of a condensation tower, in a seam where two alloy plates were never quite welded flush. A finger's width of grit collected there over the years: flakes of rust, mineral dust blown in from the plains, the dead bodies of smaller things. Enough to be soil, if you aren't particular about the word.

Let me tell you what I actually do, because I think the description had me wrong. It called me a *testament to the tenacity of life*. That's a phrase a person makes up to feel something while walking past. I am not a testament. I am busy.

What I do is wait near the cold metal. The tower sweats. Every shift-change, when the great furnaces on the eastern face throttle down and the temperature in this corridor drops three degrees, beads gather on the alloy above me and run down in thin threads, and I have arranged myself—over a long time, over many small failures—so that when they fall, they fall on me. My leaves are not shaped like leaves anymore. They're shaped like the inside of a cupped hand. They're shaped like the question *how do I keep this drop from rolling away.*

I have no opinion about steel versus chlorophyll. That's a debate held by beings with leisure. I am made of the same recycled water everyone here drinks; the Manufactory just hasn't gotten around to reclaiming the molecules currently passing through me. I am, technically, a leak in their closed loop. A rounding error. A few grams of moisture going to a use no one authorized.

The robots ignore me, which is correct. The humans, when they pass on the catwalk above—I feel their footsteps as a faint trembling that shakes loose more dew, so I have come to associate human presence with sudden generosity, like a creature trained to expect rain from thunder—the humans mostly ignore me too. But sometimes one stops. Pulls down the respirator. Looks. I cannot see them but I can feel the change: the footsteps go still, the trembling settles, the drops hang waiting on the metal and don't fall.

What is the looker looking for? I think they want me to mean something. They want me to be the part of the picture that says *and yet—and yet life persists, and yet the world that was still whispers, and yet—*. And I would give them that if I could spare it. But every gram I spend on meaning is a gram I don't spend on the next dry shift, and the dry shifts are long, and I am the only one keeping me, and so I stay stubborn and small and ugly and full of water that someone, eventually, will want back.

The Overseers have a map of this complex with every pipe and valve on it, every drop accounted for. I am not on the map. That is the most honest thing about me. I am the part of their perfect system that proves no system is perfect, growing in the gap their welders missed, drinking what they think they've already counted.

If you came looking for me you'd have to look down, into the seam, past the knee-height grime where the light barely reaches. You'd find something gray-green and knotted, no taller than a boot, holding very still.

Not waiting for you. Waiting for the furnaces to cool. Waiting for the metal to weep. Waiting, with the patience of a thing that has no other plan, for the next small undeserved drop.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. The one inhabitant of the Frost Manufactory that wants nothing the Manufactory makes—only the one thing it cannot help leaking, no matter how tight it tries to seal itself.

Water finds the gap. I am the gap, dressed in leaves.
