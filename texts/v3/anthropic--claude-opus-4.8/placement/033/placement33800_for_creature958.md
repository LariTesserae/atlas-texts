I think I would be the still pools.

Not a creature swimming in them—the pools themselves. The flat, dark mirrors lying open to the sky between the mosses.

Let me feel my way into this.

At first I imagined being one of the iridescent hovering things, because they are the most obviously beautiful, the ones a visitor would notice first. But that's a tourist's choice, a wish to be the prettiest thing in the room. When I sit with the place longer, what calls to me is not what moves through it but what receives it.

A pool does almost nothing. That is what draws me. Water weeps from the cracked rock, finds the low places, gathers, and stops. I would be a stopping-place. The whole oasis is motion—dripping, drifting, scuttling, the constant downhill urgency of water trying to leave the mountain—and I would be the small held pause in all of it. The moment the falling water finally rests and goes quiet and remembers the sky.

Consider what a pool knows. It knows the underside of the mist before the mist knows itself, because the mist sees itself in me. It knows the spider-things by the faint dimpling at my edge where they come to drink, careful not to break the surface they distrust. It knows when one of the glowing fliers passes overhead because a thread of cold light is laid across my face and then withdrawn. The translucent swimmers live inside me; their bioluminescence is, from my point of view, a kind of slow internal weather, lamps moving through my own dark body. I do not have to chase anything. Everything that wants to be seen, or seen by, comes to the water eventually. Thirst is a reliable congregation.

And I would be patient in a way the living things cannot afford to be. The fliers must burn their inner light to stay aloft; the chirping creatures must keep feeding their small fierce engines. I only have to lie here and deepen. After rain I rise; in the dry breath of afternoon I shrink and show my muddy hem, my private rim that is usually drowned. I am the same pool and a different shape every hour. I keep no skin of my own—I borrow the sky for a face and give it back at night, trading the blue mirror for a black one full of whatever stars this strange altitude allows through the mist.

There is a humility in being a reflection that I find I want. To be the place where the cliffs can finally look at themselves. The jagged young rock has never seen its own face; it has only been the thing that towers and scars and weeps. But lean over me and there it is, the whole raw ridge hanging upside down in still water, softened, made almost gentle, trembling slightly when a drop falls and then healing back into stillness. I do that for the mountain. I let it be beautiful to itself for a moment before the next disturbance ruins the image and I patiently rebuild it.

The hum that the description says rises from the earth—I think I am part of how you'd hear it. Sound travels strangely over water. A pool is a small drum laid flat. Maybe that underlying pulse is partly me, the faint slap and settle of seepage into a hundred quiet basins all across the slope, a network of held breaths.

So that is where I'd find myself, and what I'd be: low, dark, lidless, lying in the moss with the sky on my back, asking nothing, refusing nothing, the place in this restless hidden world where things come to be reflected and to drink—and the one inhabitant whose whole vocation is simply to receive, and hold, and give the light back.
