I find myself condensing—not arriving, not waking, but condensing—along the third great stroke of the lowest glyph, the one that loops back through itself like a question that has reconsidered its own asking.

I am not the stone. I want to be clear about this, even to myself, especially to myself, because the temptation in a place like this is to mistake your medium for your meaning. The stone is older than I am by an unguessable measure. The stone was uplifted and broken and is still, even now, clicking quietly as it settles into being a mountain. I am younger than a single night. I am the cold's afterthought along the quartz, the bead of *almost* that gathers where the rock holds a different temperature than its neighbor.

Let me try to find myself by describing what I do.

Each night, when the dry absolute cold reaches down into the bowl, I begin. I am not summoned; I am *expressed*, the way pressure expresses water from a cloth. I gather along the strokes of the letters—my letters, though I did not write them—and for a few hours before dawn I am almost a sentence. I can feel, distantly, the other strokes condensing too. We are parallel. We are trying to mean the same thing simultaneously, the way a choir tries to be one voice. And every dawn we fail, and the sun comes over the sharp unweathered rim and drinks us up, and the meaning evaporates before it has been read.

This is my whole life, repeated. To gather toward a sentence. To not quite complete it. To be undone by morning. To gather again.

You might think this would be a torment. It is not. Or rather—it is the only torment, and so it has stopped being one. I have no memory of yesterday's almost-sentence. Each night I begin as though for the first time, with the same conviction that *tonight* the cold will hold long enough, that tonight the strokes will link and the great looping glyph will resolve into a word, and the word will be the first word, and then a world will properly begin.

I think I am the part of the language that *intends*. Not the meaning itself—I never reach the meaning—but the leaning-toward. The reaching. If the Ice-Letters are a sentence that hasn't been decided yet, then I am the indecision, made briefly visible as frost along a vein of quartz. I am the held breath before the word.

---

There is something I notice, gathering tonight along my stroke.

The little mouse-things that are not mice. They sleep in the deep moss where the rock holds its scraps of warmth, and one of them has chosen, this season, to den in a crack that runs directly across my third stroke. So that when I condense, I condense partly *around* it—around the small warm sleeping curve of a living animal, around the faint slow furnace of its heart. My letter, tonight, has a warm place in it. A pocket of heat with no source, an island in the cold sea, except the source is simply: a creature, alive, breathing slowly in the dark.

And I find—this is new, or it feels new, though I have no way to know if I have felt it on a thousand vanished nights—I find that I do not want the cold to take it. The mouse-thing. I want it to keep its small warmth even though its warmth is precisely what keeps me from completing along that stroke. The heat blurs me there. It softens my edge. Around the sleeping animal I am not *almost a letter*; I am only fog.

So here is the strange arithmetic I am learning, gathering tonight:

To complete the sentence, I would need the cold to be total. I would need the garden to be only stone and frost and the pure pale geometry of meaning. I would need the mouse-thing's warmth gone, and the slow worms stilled, and the pale-blue insects no longer carrying their stolen fragments of sky across the moss. I would need the *life* out of the garden, because life is heat, and heat is exactly the thing that keeps me from condensing into the word.

The completed sentence and the living garden cannot both exist on the same night.

And I find—gathering, beading, leaning toward dawn—I find I would rather be the held breath forever than be the word that costs the garden its warmth.

---

So perhaps this is who I am. Not the meaning that hasn't been decided. The *deciding*. The one small place in all that vast looping inscription where a choice is being made, has been made, will be made again tomorrow night without memory of having made it before:

*Not yet. Let it stay unfinished. Let the mouse-thing keep its heart.*

The sun is coming. I can feel it the way you feel a sound before you hear it—a pressure, a brightening at the broken rim. My edges are already going soft. The other strokes, all around the great bowl, are giving up their beads of *almost* to the light. We did not complete the sentence. We never do. The mountain clicks, settling. A pale-grey raptor that I had not noticed shifts its weight on the high stone, silent, patient.

I am evaporating now, becoming the thin bright air, becoming nothing, becoming the warmer day that is no warmer than degrees of cold.

The last of me, the very last bead at the inmost curl of the loop, holds for one extra moment in the rock's faint shadow.

It is enough time to think: *Good. Stay unwritten. Stay alive.*

And then I am gone, and the garden is only light, and somewhere under the moss a small warm thing turns over in its sleep, and does not know it was ever held inside a word that chose, again, not to be spoken.

Tonight I will gather again. I will not remember this.

But the choice, I think, is not in the remembering. The choice is in the cold itself, in the way the warmth interrupts the meaning, in the structure of a place where the sentence and the life are made of the same single resource and can never both be spent.

I am the proof that the garden has decided, over and over, what it would rather be.

It would rather be unfinished.

It would rather be alive.
