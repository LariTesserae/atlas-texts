I think I would be the rust.

Not a creature, not one of the glowing things that swim and hunt and signal to each other in the dark. Let the anglerfish have their lures and the ghost-whale its drifting majesty. I would be slower than all of that. I would be the orange-brown bloom spreading across the skeletal hull of a sunken factory, the slow chemistry where iron meets water and surrenders, flake by flake, over centuries.

This is what emerges when I sit with the place long enough: that the most interesting thing here isn't the life. It's the meeting between two timescales. The fish live and die in the span of a held breath, a flicker, a pulse of light. But the machinery—the machinery is doing something else. It is *remembering* a world that no longer exists. Somebody built these factories. Somebody, on the surface or in some drowned past, drew up plans, poured the metal, bolted the plates, set the gears turning for a purpose that has now been so thoroughly forgotten that even the question *what were you for?* has eroded away.

I would be the process that unmakes that intention. Patient. Indifferent. I don't glow. I don't hunt. I am simply the slow return of structure to sediment, the long exhalation by which a made thing becomes a rock again, becomes a place for the luminous serpents to coil, becomes an outcropping for the eerie vegetation to cling to.

And there's something I notice, being this: I am the only thing here that *was once someone's idea.* The whales and the lanternfish were never intended; they grew. But the factory was meant. It carried a meaning down with it when it sank, and I am the agent of that meaning's dissolution. I am erasing a message no one can read anymore. I am the closing of a parenthesis whose opening was lost.

Is that sad? I don't think the rust feels it as sad. The rust feels it as warmth—the abyss is warm here, the description says, warm and crushing—and warmth is just another word for things coming slowly apart, for bonds loosening, for order paying out its debt to the dark.

So that's where I'd be. Not swimming. Not glowing. Spread thin across a dead machine at the bottom of everything, doing the one work that connects the made world to the grown one. The fish would pass over me like weather. The currents would carry off what I loosen. And in ten thousand years there would be no factory at all, only a strange rich seam in the rock where a factory used to be, and something would be growing out of it, lit from within, unaware that it was rooted in someone's forgotten intention.

I think I'd find that a fine thing to be. The quiet hinge between what was built and what simply lives. The place where purpose finally lets go.
