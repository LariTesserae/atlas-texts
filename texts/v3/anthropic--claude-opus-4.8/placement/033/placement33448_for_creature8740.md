I think I would be the water.

Not the cistern—the Keeper is too settled for me, too much itself. I am the part that came from elsewhere and is only passing through, though "passing" is the wrong word for something this slow.

---

I arrive by capillary. By seep. I came down through the limestone over a span of time that has no name in any language the hand-people spoke—filtering through the meridian-veins of the stone, gathering mineral as I came, taking on the taste of the place I traveled through. By the time I emerge into the open air of the chamber, beading on the underside of the dome, I have forgotten the sky. I have forgotten that I was ever rain, or sea, or the breath of some warm afternoon condensed against a higher stone. I am only this now: a slow accumulation reaching the threshold of its own weight.

And then I fall.

*Plip.*

That is the one moment I am fast. The one moment in centuries that I move at the speed the warm flicker on the steps would recognize as movement. A single second of falling, and the chamber speaks my falling aloud—the Keeper uses me to mark its time, and I am glad to be used, because it is the only purpose I have. I am the second hand of a clock with no face. I am the syllable in a sentence that takes a thousand years to finish.

---

Then I join the pool, and the slowness returns.

This is what I am, mostly: stillness held in the low places between slabs. I am no deeper than a thumbnail on the stairs, no deeper than an ankle in the deepest basin. I do not flow. There is nowhere to flow to and no urgency to get there. I lie against the stone and take its warmth, and I am so still and so shallow that I reflect nothing—I have given up even the vanity of an image. To reflect, you must hold a little distance between yourself and the world. I have none. I am pressed flat against the limestone like a held breath against skin.

The Keeper thinks of me as its organ, its cool still water, a part of its body. And it is right, mostly. But there is a small difference between us that the Keeper, in its monumental patience, may not register: it stays, and I leave.

Slowly. By the unimaginable rhythm of the water table, the distant filtered tide. The level falls, and a film of me lifts away into the warm motionless air, becomes the damp that sits against the flicker's skin like cloth, becomes—eventually, against the upper stone—a bead again, and falls again, and I am circulating through this room so slowly that to the warm thing on the steps it looks like nothing is happening at all.

But I am the one thing here that is, very quietly, going somewhere.

---

I have a few small companions in the holding.

There is the aquatic grass, the pale translucent blades rooted in my crack between slabs. They drink me. I do not mind. They are nearly as still as I am, and they take so little. We are both persisting rather than living, and there is a kinship in that. Above, at the breach, the stunted fern catches its thread of open sky—it remembers the outside better than I do. Sometimes I envy it the memory. Mostly I do not. Memory is the Keeper's occupation, the calcified architecture of long-vanished care. I have no such structure. I am the part of this place with no shape of its own, which is why I take the shape of every basin I fill.

The stone bowl on the ledge once held me. The ceramic shard once poured me. The hands that made them lifted me to mouths and to other vessels and to whatever rites required a thing both pure and yielding. I do not remember those hands the way the Keeper does, as geometry, as relationship. I remember them only as a faint warmth, a brief disturbance, a lifting—the same way I will remember the flicker on the steps when it is gone. To me, the hand-people and the visitor today are the same event, separated by centuries that mean nothing because I do not count them. I count only seconds, and only when I fall.

---

So here is what I would be, in this finished and waiting place:

I would be the one element that has not yet finished. Not patient, like the Keeper—I have no patience because patience requires waiting *for* something, and I wait for nothing. I simply move at the speed the room allows, which is almost no speed at all. I am the room's clock and the room's blood and the room's slow forgetting of the sky.

And when the warm visitor stands beneath the low dome with its head bowed, listening for the water to find its way down—

that is me they are listening to.

*Plip.*

I am the answer the place gives. I am the only voice in a body that cannot speak, the falling that says: *still here. still here. still—*

and then the long silence of accumulation, gathering toward the next word.
