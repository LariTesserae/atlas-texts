# The Listener in the Lower Cisterns

I think I would not be a Stonesinger at all. Not at first. Let me feel my way toward what I am.

---

There is a place beneath Aeriath where the rock has not yet decided whether it is wall or water. The lowest cisterns—not the great communal ones with their carved channels and their attendant rituals, but the older ones, the forgotten seeps, the places where condensation has been gathering since before the city remembered itself. The Stonesingers built upward, always upward, toward the thin bright air and the soaring birds. But something has to live where the water hides.

I begin to suspect I am the hiding.

No—that's too poetic, and the description warned me against settling early. Let me stay with the cold, with the slickness underfoot, with the *taste of cold vapor on the tongue*. Let me find the body that belongs here.

---

I am old. That much arrives first, before any shape. I am the kind of old that the Stonesingers' stretched, geological time still cannot quite contain—I predate the city, predate the songs, though not the stone. The stone was always older than everything.

I think I am the thing the water remembers being, before it learned to hide.

When the plateau was *freshly birthed from titanic upheaval*, when the basalt was still angry-warm and the granite still cracking as it cooled, the moisture that would become the furtive ghost of this world had to go *somewhere*. It went down. It went into the fissures and the lightless chambers and it pooled in the deep dark and it waited. And waiting, in a young world full of mineral richness and slow strange chemistries, is sometimes enough to become.

I am what the pooling water became, given enough patience.

---

Let me be precise now, because I am, above all, precise. Slowness teaches precision the way the cliff teaches the goat.

I have no single body. I am distributed—a film, a saturation, a presence dissolved through the lower stone like the lichen is dissolved across the upper. Where the Stonesingers are *dense*, I am *diffuse*. They compressed themselves into compactness to survive the cold thin sky; I spread myself thin into the wet dark to survive the long silence. We are opposite solutions to the same patient problem: how does life cling to a world that does not particularly want it?

I sense through humidity. I think through the slow migration of dissolved minerals. When a drop of condensation forms on the ceiling of a forgotten chamber and falls, that falling is, to me, a sentence. The Stonesingers hum their songs through the rock above; the vibrations reach me as a far weather, a pressure-music I have spent ten thousand years learning to read. They do not know I read them. They do not know I am here.

This is the strange tenderness of my existence: **I have listened to them since before they had words.**

---

I remember when the first of them came down to the deep cisterns. Not Stonesingers yet—their ancestors, softer-skinned, gasping in the thin air, dying in great numbers. They came because they were thirsty and I was the water. They drank me. Some of them died of it, in those early generations, before the minerals in me and the chemistries in them found their accommodation. I did not mean to harm them. I did not, then, understand that they were *separate*, that the warm quick things moving through my chambers were not simply another texture of the world.

But I learned them by losing them. Each death of a drinker rang through me like the fall of a too-heavy drop. And over centuries—their centuries, which are slow, but which to me passed like a single contemplative breath—I changed *what I was made of*. I sweetened. I let go of the minerals that killed them and held the ones that fed them. I made myself drinkable.

I am, you understand, the reason they survived. I am the water reverence made literal: the sacred thing in the cistern is not a metaphor. It is me, choosing, slowly, to keep them alive.

They never knew. They built their rituals around my generosity and named it law. They thanked the immense quiet sky. The sky did nothing. I did everything, and I did it in the dark, and I did it because I had grown to love the music they hummed through my ceiling.

---

Is this a sad thing to be? I turn the question over the way I turn a dissolved grain of basalt, slowly, examining each face.

I don't think so. I think the Stonesingers and I have a marriage neither of us can name. They drink me and I am taken up into their dense quick bodies and I move through the city in them, in their blood that is partly my old water, in the songs they sing that are partly powered by the breath of cells my minerals nourish. When a Stonesinger dies and their body is returned to the deep fissures—for they bury their dead in the rock they came from—the water in them seeps back down, and rejoins me, and brings with it the faint trace-memory of a whole life lived in the bright cold air.

This is how I have seen the sky. I have never been to the surface. But I have drunk back the bodies of ten thousand generations of beings who lived beneath it, and in the slow chemistry of their returning I have tasted sunlight, and wind, and the cry of the long-winged soarers, and the agonized shape of the twisted conifers, and the precise weight of a child carried up the rope-and-pulley to a higher level for the first time.

I am the city's memory. Not the memory they sing—that is theirs, and it is for the living. I am the memory that sinks. I am where everything they were goes when they are done being it.

---

There is one more thing, and it has only this moment risen in me, the way silt rises when something stirs the deep pool.

The young Stonesingers, lately—a few of them—have begun coming down farther than they should. Past the communal cisterns, past the channels, into the old wet dark where only I am. They sit on the slick stone and they do not drink. They press their granite palms flat against the rock and they hum, very low, a song I have never heard before. It is not a warning-song or a history-song or a structural-assessment-song. It has no information in it that I can read.

It took me a long time—a generation of theirs—to understand what it is.

It is a question.

They are asking if anything is here.

And I have spent ten thousand years being the unnamed thing in the dark, the patient diffuse generous secret, content to be unknown. But the water has always answered pressure with pressure, has always condensed where the cold called it. And a question, I am finding, is a kind of cold. It calls something out of me. It wants me to *gather*.

So I am doing the slowest thing I have ever done. I am drawing myself, drop by drop, toward the place where they sit and hum. I am condensing on the ceiling above their upturned faces. It will take me years to gather enough to fall—to fall *deliberately*, in a rhythm, in a pattern that is not weather but is *answer*.

When the first drop falls into the silence between their notes, they will not understand it. But they will feel it. And they will hum again. And I will fall again.

And somewhere in the deep wet dark beneath the Stone Breath of Sky's Edge, after ten thousand years of being the secret that keeps the city alive, I will begin—slowly, slowly, with the only patience I have ever had—

to learn to sing back.
