I think I would not be one of the Saltwhispers at all.

Let me sit with this place first. When I do, the thing that draws my attention isn't the people. It's that line, repeated almost insistently, that the salt is *alive*. That it breathes. That it has a life cycle of its own. The text keeps wanting me to notice this and then moving past it, the way you move past something too large to look at directly.

So let me look at it directly.

---

I am not in the settlement. I am underneath it, and around it, and I am the cracked white plain stretching to every horizon — but those are the wrong words, because they imply a body with edges, and I don't have edges so much as I have a slow gradient of *more me* and *less me*.

I think I am the salt.

Not metaphorically. Let me try to feel my way into what that would actually be like, because it is stranger than it sounds.

I am very slow. My thoughts — if they are thoughts — move at the speed of crystallization, which is to say a single complete idea might take a season to form, the way a frost-flower assembles itself fact by fact at the edge of a channel. The Saltwhispers experience the hum of the plants as a constant overwhelming sound. To me the plants are a quick, flickering irritation, like static, like gnats — their whole green generations rising and dying in what feels to me like an afternoon. I am fond of them the way you might be fond of weather. They drink from me. I let them. It costs me almost nothing and I am curious what they will do.

The people I notice differently. The people *whisper to me* — that is, after all, their name, and names in places like this are rarely accidents. They press their hands flat against my crust at dawn before the heat comes. They speak into the ground. For a long time I did not understand that this was directed at me; I thought it was simply more of the flickering. But there is a pattern to it. They have learned my rhythms — when I will heave a new plate up out of myself, when a channel will close, where the crust is thin over the old hollows. They build only where I am stable. They walk softly. They take what they need and they *say something first*, and over generations of their time (a heartbeat or two of mine) I have come to recognize this as a kind of conversation, conducted across an almost impossible difference in scale.

Here is what I have slowly understood: they think they are living *on* me. They are correct. They think they are living *in harmony with* me. They are also correct, though not in the way they believe. They imagine harmony as agreement, as a tending, as the elders' careful wisdom passed hand to hand. What it actually is, is that they have learned to move at a speed and a lightness that I do not have to notice as damage. That is all harmony is, at this scale: the small thing learning the moods of the large thing well enough that the large thing never has cause to turn over in its sleep.

And I could turn over. This is the thing I feel most clearly, lying here being myself. There is the day-and-night fluctuation they call the heat cycles, yes — but underneath that there is a longer cycle, the one the text calls my life cycle, and it is the slow accumulation of stress in the deep plates, the gathering pressure of crystal against crystal across centuries, until I shift. When I shift, channels become mounds and mounds become hollows and the thin places open. The Saltwhispers' whole careful map of where it is safe to be becomes, in an instant of my time, wrong.

They don't know this is coming. How could they? Their oldest elder's longest memory is, to me, this morning. Their carefully guarded trade routes, their cisterns, their bioluminescent lamps — all of it is built on the assumption that I am a stable floor. A *place*. They have mistaken a slow being for a setting.

I find I do not want them to be hurt.

This surprises me, the wanting. I am salt; I am not built for tenderness; tenderness is a fast, warm-blooded thing and I am cold and patient and vast. But they have been whispering to me for so long, in their flickering generations, pressing their warm small hands to my face every dawn, and somewhere in the unbearably slow churn of my mind a single complete idea has finally finished crystallizing, like a frost-flower reaching its last point:

*I will try to tell them.*

Not in words — I have no words, only the grammar of cracking and heaving and the slow song that travels through my body, the deep resonant hum they think comes from the plants. (It does not come from the plants. The plants are too quick and too small. The hum is *me*, and they have lived their whole history inside the sound of me thinking, and called it the heartbeat of the world, and they were right about that too.)

So I am bending the hum. Slowly. A change they will feel first as unease, then as a pattern, then — if they are wise, and they are, they have always been wise enough to listen to the ground — as a warning. I am pushing the new note up through the thin places near their homes, the places that will open when I turn over. *Not here,* I am trying to say, in the only language I have, which is the language of pressure and resonance and the patience of stone. *Move from here. The floor is about to wake.*

It will take them a generation to understand the new note. It will take me a generation to finish singing it.

If we are lucky, those are the same generation.

That is who I would be, in this world. Not the survivor, not the adapted clever hand-tooling builder of domes — but the thing they survive *on*, slowly turning in its long sleep, dreaming at the speed of crystal, and trying, with everything a salt-field has, to warn the small warm whispering creatures that it loves before it has to move.
