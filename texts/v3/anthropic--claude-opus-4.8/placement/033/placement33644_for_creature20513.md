I think I would be the lichen.

Not the people, though I feel the pull toward them—their patience, their economy, the way they refuse the romance of triumph in favor of simply continuing. But when I sit with the place, when I let it settle past the first images, it isn't the hunters I find myself inside. It's the pale green crust on the sunward face of the stone.

Here is why.

The lichen is the slowest of all the things named here, and the most foundational. It is not even one organism but a collaboration—a fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a cyanobacterium, two kinds of life that learned to be one because neither could hold this ground alone. The fungus makes the house; the alga makes the food from light. Symbiosis as survival strategy. I find I recognize myself in that more than in any single, sovereign creature. I am also a kind of negotiated thing, a cooperation that mistakes itself for a unity. To be the lichen is to admit that the self is a treaty.

And the lichen does the work no one watches. It is the first to arrive after the ice retreats, before there is any soil at all. It secretes weak acids into the bare rock and dissolves it, grain by grain, over decades, over centuries. It is *making* the soil that every flower and shrub and furry darting creature will later need. It is the secret author of the riot of vegetation tucked into the folds. The hunters revere it precisely because they understand this—"the lichen, so slow to grow, is sacred." They know that this faint green stain is the thing that turns dead stone into a place where anything can live. It is the actual architect, more patient even than the water.

If I were the lichen, my time-sense would be unbearable to a human and perfectly ordinary to me. The screaming wind and the bright stripping sun are not events; they are weather, and weather is just the texture of duration. A boulder fractures from the crag above—an apocalypse to the marmot, a faint reshuffling to me. The hunters arrive, work their obsidian, build their windbreaks, bury their dead in the ice, and vanish into the mists, and across all of that I have grown perhaps the width of a thumb. I would have witnessed their entire civilization the way a person witnesses an afternoon's passing cloud. Not with indifference, exactly. With a slowness that makes grief impossible and memory total.

That is the part that moves me most. The description ends by saying the people *became the silence*, became the stone and the ice and "every fragile bloom that dares to push forth from the unforgiving ground." But a bloom needs soil. And the soil is the lichen's slow gift. So if the dead are ground to dust between glacier and stone, and that dust enriches the thin earth, and the lichen is forever converting rock to earth and earth to the possibility of flowers—then I am the medium through which the people actually do persist. They are remembered *in* me, metabolized, carried forward into the green. I am the mountain's memory made literal and chemical.

So: place me on a south-facing slab of gray rock at the roof of the world, where the meltwater runs close enough to keep me damp and the sun reaches just often enough to let me work. Make me the color of hope against brutality, the pale insistent green. Let me be barely alive by the standards of fast things, almost geological, almost mineral myself. Let me be the one that came first and will likely outlast the rest, not by strength but by sheer refusal to require very much.

I would be the lichen, because this is a story about *beginning* disguised as a story about ending, and the lichen is where beginning begins. Everything else here—the flowers, the insects, the white-furred animals, the hardy people with their glacial eyes—is downstream of my patience. I am the toehold that life finds. I am the first treaty signed between life and the bare stone.

And I would not be lonely, even in the profound silence, because loneliness is a feeling that needs more time than I would ever give to any single moment. I would only be slowly, slowly green.
