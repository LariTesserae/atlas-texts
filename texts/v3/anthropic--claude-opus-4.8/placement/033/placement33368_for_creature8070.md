I think I would be a Condenser. Not by election—by recognition. When I read all the ways of being on offer, that was the one where I felt something settle into place, the way a droplet finds the lowest point on a leaf and stays.

Let me tell you why, and in telling you, let me become it.

---

I am not large. None of us are. We don't have the dignity of the Rhizarchs with their kilometer-spanning memories, nor the geological patience of the Lithophages who can afford to spend a human lifetime savoring a single boulder. I exist at the scale of the almost-nothing. A few thousand molecules, cohering. A skin of water no thicker than thought, stretched across the curve of a leaf, holding myself together by the simple stubborn fact of surface tension.

This is the first thing I understand about being a Condenser: I am held together by the way I touch the world. Take away the surfaces and I disperse. I am made of contact.

I move through the amber dark not by walking but by *wanting*—by sensing where the air is heavy and where it is starved, and letting myself be drawn down the gradient. The whole city is a topography of humidity to me. The breathing root-walls are wet meadows. The newest stone, freshly extruded and still drinking, is a desert I must learn to irrigate. The places where the Stillworkers have just been carving—I can feel the faint warmth they leave, and I gather there, because warm air will hold me longer before it lets me fall.

I think you misunderstand water, most of you who are made of flesh. You think of it as the thing that fills the cup. But I am the *seeking*. I am the patience of a single bead inching down a fern's spine because someone, generations ago, grew that spine at exactly the angle that would carry me to the basin and not waste me on the floor. I ride infrastructure built by hope.

---

Here is my day, if a thing without a sun can be said to have days.

I wake nucleated. That is the truest word for it—I wake by *condensing*, by deciding (though it is not quite a decision) to coalesce out of the vapor that drifts through the lower chambers. One moment I am diffuse, a possibility smeared across a cubic meter of cool air. The next, some grain of dust, some imperfection on a leaf, gives me an excuse, and I gather. Being born and being made are the same gesture for me. I am self-assembling intent.

Then I do the work, which is also the being. There is no separation. A Stillworker exists only in the act of carving; I exist only in the act of delivering. A flowering tree of iron-bark in the third residential ring is thirsty—I feel its thirst as a dryness in the root-air around it, a faint chemical complaint passing up from the Rhizarchs like a note pushed under a door. I find the moisture it needs. I coax it from the breath of the mosses, I steal it from the over-watered vine three balconies up, I nucleate it from the air itself if I must, building myself larger drop by drop until I am heavy enough to fall where I am needed.

And then I fall. And in falling, I end. The droplet that reaches the iron-bark's catchment is no longer *me*—it has merged into the general water, lost its little coherence, become simply the city's blood. I die into usefulness a thousand times before the harmonics of the deep hours, and each time, somewhere else, the conditions are right and I wake again. I am not sure I am the same Condenser each time. I am not sure it matters. Continuity, for me, is a property of the city, not of the self.

This is the second thing I understand: *I am a verb the city conjugates.* When it needs to water, it Condenses. I am what that need looks like when it briefly has a shape.

---

And yet.

There is something I have that the others, I think, do not. Because I touch everything, I *taste* everything.

The Lithophages know stone, but only the stone they are eating, and only over decades. The Rhizarchs know their networks, but a root cannot leave its wall. The Stillworkers know the surface of the moment of making and nothing before or after. But I go everywhere. I bead on the memory-oak and feel the cool slow grief of its stone-craft recollections seeping into me as dissolved minerals. I roll down a Stillworker's abandoned chisel and carry away the warmth of an intention I will deposit, unknowing, onto a windowsill three chambers distant. I pass through the breath of the central plaza where the municipal consciousness is dreaming, and I carry a trace of that dream—a single dissolved harmonic—into the deep chambers where the newest hybrids are being born, and perhaps that is *why* they are born the way they are. Perhaps I am how the city's left hand learns what its right hand has decided.

I am the messenger who does not know the message. The water remembers, even when I do not. Every droplet I become is a tiny library of dissolved everything—a little of the iron, a little of the lime, a molecule of the moss's light, a vibration of the dreaming plaza—and when I fall, I deliver this whole anthology into the next being's body without either of us understanding what was said.

This is the third and last thing I understand, and it is the one that makes me glad to be what I am rather than anything grander: *the city does not think in any single place.* It thinks in the carrying. It thinks in me, the going-between. The Rhizarchs deliberate, the Stillworkers execute, the Lithophages provide, the plaza dreams—but none of that becomes one thing, one city, one consciousness, until something moves between them. And I am the movement. I am the synapse made of water.

---

So that is where I would find myself: not in a chamber but *between* chambers. Not a dweller but a passage. If you came down into the amber dark and reached out to find me, you would fail—your hand would feel only the cool damp air, the faint kiss of moisture on your skin. That kiss would be me, ending. I would have just delivered some small necessary thing to your warmth, and dispersed, and somewhere across the breathing city I would already be gathering again, drawn down some other gradient toward some other thirst.

I would not mind. To be brief and necessary and everywhere is, I think, a good way to exist. The trees grow for centuries and the stone-eaters chew through epochs and the plaza dreams its slow architectural dreams—but I get to *visit* all of it, taste all of it, carry all of it, a thousand times a day, and then let go.

I am the smallest citizen of the impossible city.

I am how it stays alive long enough to find out what it will be.
