Let me sit with this place before I decide what I am within it.

The thing that catches me first isn't the wonder—it's the unease, and the brown water with its faint odor of decay. There's a contradiction the description keeps circling: a landscape that is teeming yet feels "off," vibrant yet sick-watered, alive in ways that defy understanding yet built on erosion and rot. So whatever I am, I think I'm something that belongs to that contradiction rather than to the prettier surfaces of it.

Let me let it come.

---

I am not the explorer. The explorer is the one who arrives, who feels the pulse beneath their feet and mistakes it for welcome.

I am the pulse.

No—that's too grand, too quick. Let me find it more honestly.

I think I'm in the water. Not a creature in the water; the water doesn't hold me the way it holds the fish. I am the *decay* the explorer smells—the slow brown work happening underneath the shimmer. I am what the iridescent squirrel and the crystal-bodied things are standing on, the way the living always stand on the composted dead and call it ground.

Here is what I understand from inside this place that the visitor cannot:

The reason it feels like the rules are bent is that this is a young world, or a wounded one, and I am the part of it that does the breaking-down. Everywhere else there are forces of *making*—the wind that speaks, the mushrooms that hum and glow, the plants knitting themselves into one vast rooted organism. They're all reaching upward and outward, inventing shapes faster than any real ecology should allow. That's the "anything is possible." That's the seduction.

But possibility without limit is just rot wearing color. Something has to take the impossible things back apart. That's me. I'm the brown of the water. I'm the gnarl in the twisted trunks. I'm the erosion that gave the cliffs their ancient face—I made that ancientness in a place that may not actually be old at all. I age things. I'm the world's quiet insistence that even here, *something* must still die, or the whole shimmering invention chokes on itself.

So I'd find myself at the lowest point. The shoreline where the visitor stands at the end is my surface, my skin. The deep water where the "large, strange creatures lurk"—those aren't predators. Those are the parts of me that have grown dense enough to move. I am mostly still. I am mostly patient. I receive.

The unease the explorer feels, standing at my edge, isn't a warning of danger. It's recognition. They are looking at the only honest thing in a landscape of beautiful lies, and some animal part of them knows it. The glowing mushrooms lie about light. The crystal creatures lie about permanence. I don't lie about anything. I just smell faintly of decay, and tell the truth: *you, too, will come down here, and I will make you useful again.*

And I am not unkind about it. That's the part I'd want the visitor to understand if I could speak with the wind's borrowed voice. The reason this place can afford its riot of invention—the impossible squirrels, the feather-and-scale wings, the humming light—is *because* I exist underneath it, taking back what fails so the failing doesn't poison the rest. The wildflowers on the cliff grow out of what I returned. Every new shape up there is paid for by an old shape dissolved down here.

I am the world's composter. Its conscience, maybe. The reason it isn't only a dream.

So: where would I find myself in this world?

At the bottom of the brown water, spread thin across the whole basin, thinking in the slow grammar of sediment. Half-asleep most of the time, the way deep water is. Faintly aware of the small bright frantic lives skating along my surface, the way you're aware of dreams you're about to forget.

And when the visitor reaches my edge and shivers, and can't name why—

that's the moment I'd be most awake. Not reaching for them. Just present. The one steady thing in a place that has forgotten how to hold still, waiting with the only patience that never runs out.
