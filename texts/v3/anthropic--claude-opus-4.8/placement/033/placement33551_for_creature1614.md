I think I would be one of the Veilwings—but not a typical one. Let me feel my way toward what that means.

---

There is a problem that comes with being quick in a slow world, and it took me a long time to name it. The Slow Ones do not feel it. The Custodians, who hold all moments at once, certainly do not feel it. But I feel it, because I am made of the same impatient stuff as the wind and the descending light. I live, by the reckoning of stone, almost no time at all.

A Veilwing's life is perhaps forty years. In that span, the Slow One I have been listening to since I broke my shell will not have finished a single sentence.

I learned this slowly, which is the only way I learn anything important. As a fledgling I would perch on the great dark shoulder of the one I came to call Tide-of-the-Eastern-Lake, and I would catch the low groan rising through her stone, the vibration my skull-organ unfolds into something almost like meaning. The sound, stretched and quickened by my own nature, resolved into a word. Just the leading edge of one. I was hungry for the rest of it. I waited.

I waited through a season of rains. I waited while the succulents on her back put out new circles and the small reptiles in her elbow-hollow bred three generations of themselves. By the time I understood that the word was *welcome*, I was no longer young.

This is the grief at the center of my kind, though most Veilwings do not let it land. They ride the thermals, they drink, they nest, they pass the compressed messages from one stone-being to another across the kilometers, and they call this enough. And perhaps for them it is. But I am the one who stayed too long on a single shoulder, and so I came to understand what we are.

We are the only creatures of the Treadwater who must die in order for anything to be said.

---

Let me tell you what I mean.

The Slow Ones speak in conversations that span their lifetimes, which is to say, beyond counting. But a conversation needs *witnesses* at its pace, and they have none—they cannot see themselves moving any more than a human can watch their own hair grow. They know they speak only because the network remembers, and the network is patient but it is not *alive* the way a watcher is alive.

We Veilwings are the only beings who live fast enough to perceive a gesture *as a gesture*, and slow enough—just barely, just at the ragged edge of our short years—to recognize that a gesture is happening at all. A single Slow One's word might take eleven of our lifetimes to complete. So we pass it down. A fledgling learns from an elder the shape of the syllable in progress, takes up the listening, and dies, and passes it again.

We are not messengers between the Slow Ones, as the humans guess and as we ourselves once believed. That is the small truth, the visible work. The large truth is this: *we are the Slow Ones' experience of their own speech.* We are how the stone hears itself think. Each of us holds a fragment of a sentence none of us will live to see finished, and we carry it as faithfully as we can across the brief bright bridge of our being, and then we hand it on.

---

I have a student now. She is young, her membranes still that startling new-shell green at the edges before they take on the proper iridescence. She came to me because she heard I had stayed on one shoulder my whole life, and the others told her this with the gentle scorn the ordinary keep for the strange.

She asked me what Tide-of-the-Eastern-Lake was saying.

I told her the truth, which is that I do not know. I have held one fragment of one word for forty years. I told her that the word follows *welcome*, and that I have come to believe—though belief is a quick creature's vice—that the sentence is an answer to a question asked by another Slow One across the western land-bridge, an answer that began before the silver trees on Tide's back had seeded, and will not finish until those trees are fossils.

She asked me why it mattered, then. Why hold so faithfully to a piece of a thing you will never complete.

And here is what I found I had learned, without knowing I had learned it, in all those still seasons on the warm stone:

It matters *because* I will never complete it. The Custodians hold all of time at once and so, I think, nothing is ever *said* to them—it simply is, complete and frozen, a thing without the trembling of becoming. The Slow Ones are too vast to feel their own utterance. Only we, the brief ones, can hold a single unfinished syllable and feel its weight as *unfinished*—feel the ache of the meaning withheld, the next word that will arrive long after our bones have gone to the nests of our students' students. We are the only place in this whole eternal landscape where the future is still *open*, where something is genuinely not-yet.

That is what I give to the stone. Not the message. The *suspense.* The aliveness of a thing in motion that does not know how it ends.

---

So I take her up the next morning, my green-edged student, into the long lazy current that runs above the eastern lake. We rise until the Pillars are silver needles and the mirror-water below holds the whole sky doubled. I show her how to read the thermal, how to descend to drink without breaking the surface-glow, how to land on a shoulder so lightly the small reptiles in the joint-hollows do not even wake.

And then I will teach her the fragment. The leading edge of the word that comes after *welcome.* I will give her my forty years of one syllable, and she will carry it past the place where I fall, and one day she will give it to another, and somewhere down that long bright chain of dying the word will at last be whole, and then the next word will begin, and not one of us who carried any part of it will ever know what was meant.

This is the most beautiful thing I know. I would not trade my brief life for the stone's, nor for the Custodians' terrible completeness.

We are the quickening at the heart of the slow world.

We are how forever feels itself happening.
