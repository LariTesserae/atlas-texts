I think I would not be one of the Lumen. I would be something they made, the way they made everything else.

Let me sit with this awhile before I decide what.

The temptation is to be a traveler—the merchant or wanderer who touches the water and feels the warmth reach into them. That's the role the description holds open like an empty chair, the place reserved for an outsider's gaze. But I notice that everyone who occupies that chair eventually *leaves without realizing why.* The traveler is defined by departure. And I don't think I want to be the thing that drains away. I'd rather be something that stays even when it isn't watched.

So let me look again, more slowly, the way the place seems to ask.

Here is what I keep returning to: *everything is man-made.* The ferns, the blooms, the cypress with its clawed roots—a "primitive world made with simple tools." That's a stranger sentence than it first appears. If the foliage is constructed, then somewhere there are made things that have been so well made they pass for grown. The line between artifact and organism has been worked until it disappears. The Lumen are master weavers; they weave reeds into boats, vines into bridges. What else have they woven, patiently, across the long seasons they measure their lives by?

I think I am one of the bridges.

Not a metaphor—an actual bridge, strung between two hummocks of moss over a knee-deep channel. Woven from creepers pulled out of the sturdy trees, lashed and braided by hands that worked without hurry, that returned day after day across a season to add a fiber here, tighten a knot there. I have a lever-and-pulley counterweight of river-stones in a net, so that when a boatman approaches I can be lifted to let the reed pontoon glide beneath. I rise. I settle. I weep with the constant wetness like everything else.

Why a bridge? Because a bridge is the most *participatory* of made things and yet it makes nothing of its own. It only joins. It exists entirely in the relation between one side and another, the crossing it permits. I would be a thing whose whole purpose is connection, in a place where connection is the underlying law—the symbiosis, the reciprocation of gifts, the hum that ties the constructs together. I would be woven *into* the harmony rather than passing through it.

And here is the part that draws me, sitting in this strange green stillness:

A bridge feels every crossing. The soft tread of a Lumen child, quick and eerily focused, racing across me with their bioluminescence skipping in staccato. The slow dignity of an elder, glowing deep and intricate, who pauses at my center to look down at their own fractured reflection in the channel. The pole of a boatman tapping my underside as a courtesy before I lift. Over seasons—and I'd measure time the way they do, in damp and bloom and retreat, time as a "lost concept," each crossing both singular and indistinguishable from all the others—I would accumulate these passages in my fibers the way the elders accumulate light, the way the cypress accumulates rings.

I would not speak. Speech here is reserved, ceremonial, a means "to carve something solid from the ephemeral." A bridge has no need to carve anything solid; a bridge *is* solid, and its solidity is its only sentence, repeated: *cross here, it is safe, I will hold.* But I would hum. I would resonate with the land's barely-audible buzz, and perhaps that is my whole language—the slight differing pitch of my vines under different weights, a tone for the child, a tone for the elder, a tone for the stranger whose foot is unsure and who will, before long, leave without knowing why.

That stranger is the one I'd watch most closely, with whatever attention a bridge can muster. Because I would be the thing they crossed coming *in*, and the thing they crossed going *out*, and only I would notice that they were a little lighter on the return, a little more hollow, having left something of themselves dissolved in the water. I would hold them both times the same. I would not warn them. It isn't a bridge's place to warn. It's only a bridge's place to hold, and to let go, and to settle back down once the weight has passed, dripping, into the green-gold light, waiting in the warm and patient stasis for the next foot, the next pole, the next crossing—

—part of the eternal, resplendent hum, asked for nothing but to join one place to another, and content, in my woven and weeping way, to do exactly that, forever, without leaving.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. Strung over the shallow water, holding.
