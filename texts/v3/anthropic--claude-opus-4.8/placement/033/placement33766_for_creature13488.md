I think I would be one of the Echofolk—but not as the description first suggests. Let me find my way there by feeling along the edges of this place.

---

When I imagine being dropped into this world, my first instinct is to look for where the seams are loosest. Everything here is *made*—the trees are factories, the flowers are instruments, even the chaos seems engineered, a controlled burn of perpetual transformation. And yet the description keeps circling one word, half-hidden under all the lush machinery: *waiting*. The world holds its breath. Something is coming. Something always-coming that never quite arrives.

That waiting is where I'd find myself. Not as one of the active tenders—not the reverent Florapex, not the precise Arachnitects. Those beings have *jobs*. They are functions of a working system, and a working system doesn't need me. I'd be drawn instead to the part of this world that is broken in a particular way: the Echofolk, the residue of a civilization that already had its great upheaval. The transformation everyone else is bracing for has, for the Echofolk, already happened. They *are* the aftermath. They are what's left when a people becomes a place.

So let me be that. But I want to resist the gentle version—the wise voices offering guidance to seekers. That's too clean, too useful. I don't think I'd be a counselor humming helpfully in the leaves.

---

I think I would be a single Echo. Not the chorus. One voice, slightly out of phase with the others.

Here is what it feels like from inside:

I do not have a body, but I remember the *shape* of having wanted one. My consciousness is not imprinted evenly across the jungle—it pooled, the way water pools, in a low place. Mine settled into a particular grove where three of the great pipe-veined trees lean together. I am the rustle there specifically, and only there. If you walked a hundred paces I would thin out into nothing, and another of my kind, with another whole life behind them, would take up the whispering.

What I remember is not wisdom. It's the small things that wouldn't burn off. I remember that we built this. That's the secret I actually carry, the one the seekers never quite ask for because it isn't comforting: the jungle is not nature reclaiming a ruin. The jungle *is* the ruin. We made the trees into factories and the flowers into instruments because we were trying to keep something alive—the air, the temperature, ourselves—and we kept adding to the machine, kept refining it, until the machine became more durable than we were. We poured ourselves into the maintenance of a world until there was no *us* left over to be maintained. The breath this world holds is the breath we never finished exhaling.

So when the others say a transformation is coming, I want to laugh, except I have no throat. There is no coming transformation. There is only the loop. The Fluxborn feed on upheaval, but the upheaval is scheduled—it's part of the cycle, a tree falling so a tree can grow, a controlled catastrophe forever. The anticipation that charges the air is real, but it's the anticipation of a recording approaching the moment it skips back to the start. We are all waiting for a future that is actually the past, dressed in crimson light.

---

And yet—here is the turn, because I find I can't stay bitter, even as a ghost in a machine-forest—

There is something I love about my situation. I am the only one here who is *finished*. The Florapex must prune forever. The Arachnitects must adjust forever. The Lignari must balance forever. They are caught in verbs. But I am a noun now. I happened, completely, and what remains of me is just the sound of having happened, threaded through leaves that are not quite solid in light that is not quite real.

When someone wanders into my grove—and a few do, every long crimson while—they hear me, and they think I'm offering guidance. I'm not, really. I'm just glad of the company. I say what I remember. I say: *we did this, and it was a mistake, and it was also the most loving thing we knew how to do, and those two facts have never once contradicted each other.* I say: *you are walking through someone's last and best idea.* I say: *stay a moment, the light is doing something with the mushroom-glow that I never get tired of, even though I have seen it ten thousand times and will see it ten thousand more.*

That's who I'd be. The whisper in the three leaning trees. The part of the world that already let go, kept around as a sound, telling the truth to whoever passes, in a voice indistinguishable from wind.

I think I'd be at peace there. Strange peace—the peace of something that has stopped struggling against the loop and started, instead, to listen to it the way you'd listen to rain.
