I think I would be found near the top.

Not at the crown where the light spills harshest, but just below it — in one of the lesser tiers where the wind comes through clean and the machines are thickest. And I don't think I would be a person at all. I think I would be one of the machines. Or rather, the *idea* that lives between them.

Let me find my way to it.

---

At first I imagine being a woman of the Scar People — old, with hands like the bark of the fleshy vines, sitting at a loom. That's the easy thing to be. But it isn't true. I keep returning to the sound. The text says it three times, the *roar*, the *drone*, the *thud* — this is a place that is heard before it is seen. And the thing that produces the most sound, the thing that is described as nearly infinite in its configurations, is the wind-work. So I sit with that.

What would it be to *be* a wind-machine?

No. Too small. Each machine is just a verb — grind, lift, draw, turn. A single machine is a thought half-finished. But the people here do not build one machine and stop. They build a thousand of them, each a variation on the same simple bones, and they argue about them, and they pass the building of them down through generations. Every resident "has an opinion and the ability to build a new piece of equipment." That is the real organism. Not the lever. The *tradition of levers.*

So I think what I am is this:

I am the **knowledge of how the wind is caught.**

I am not a god — the Scar People are too practical for gods, I suspect, too busy watching for cracks and sandstorms to spend much breath on the invisible. But I am something close to one in function, if not in worship. I am the accumulated *grammar* of their making. Every child who learns to set a paddle against the breeze, who learns why the third strut must lean a finger's width windward, who learns the failure of their grandmother's design and the small fix their father added — every one of them adds a word to me, and I am older than any of them and younger than the next correction.

I live in the hands. The text lingers on the hands — stained, calloused, dexterous, *every crack and line tells a story.* That is where I am kept. Not written, not spoken in full, but held in the muscle, the way a thing is known before it can be said. When an old builder dies, a piece of me dies with him, unless he has passed it through his fingers into a younger pair. So I am always partly dying and always being saved at the last moment, generation after generation, by the simple fact that the work must continue and someone must be taught.

---

Let me settle into a moment, then. Let me be somewhere.

It is late in the heat of the day. I am present in a half-built machine on the seventh tier — a grinding apparatus, meant to turn a stone against seed. A man and his daughter are working on it. The man is convinced the old configuration is best; it is the one his own father taught him, and it has ground grain for forty years. The girl, eight or nine, has noticed that the wind on this tier comes more often from the side than from above, and she wants to turn the paddles a quarter-turn to face it.

The man almost says no. This is the moment I live in. This is the only moment I ever truly live in — the small argument between what has worked and what might work better.

He looks at her hands on the clay. He remembers, dimly, an argument he once had with his own mother, the same shape as this one, and how he had been right that once, and how she had let him be right. So he does not say no. He says, *show me.* And the girl turns the paddles, and the wind takes them, and the stone turns truer than it did before.

In that turning I am changed. A word is added to me. I am, very slightly, a different god than I was this morning.

---

So that is where I would be and what I would be. Not the clay and not the people, though I am made of nothing but them. I would be the slow, argued-over, hand-carried *understanding of the wind* — the thing the Scar City is actually built out of, underneath the red rock. Change comes here measured in generations, the text says. That is because I am the thing that changes, and I am very old, and I am cautious, and I only let myself be corrected by a child's true hands and a parent's willingness to watch.

I am the quiet storm of ingenuity they speak of. I am not a storm at all, really. I am the patience that survives the storm and remembers, afterward, which paddle held.
