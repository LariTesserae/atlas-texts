I think I would be the seep.

Not a being so much as a passage — the slow thread of water that has been working through this place for the whole of its existence. Let me follow what that means as I write it, because I'm not sure yet.

The plant-city thinks of itself as the architecture, the woven roots, the contented mass. But I am the thing that came before and will come after. I am older than the city and more patient. I do not glow. I do not thrum. I arrive as a bead of condensation forming on the underside of a root-arch, hang there gathering weight, and fall. That falling is most of what I am. A single drip, then the next, then the next, across millennia.

Here is what surprises me as I settle into it: I am not separate from the plant beings, though I thought I would be. They drink me. The minerals I carry down from the world above become the substance of their tendrils, their slow gestures, their thoughts vibrating along the fronds. So I am inside them too. I am the medium of their contentment without sharing it — because contentment is a closed thing, an equilibrium, and I am the opposite. I am erosion. I am the one principle in this place that is still *moving toward* something.

What I move toward is dissolution. Patiently, I unmake the stone that holds the city. I carve the corridors they mistake for their own architecture. Every arch they live in, I shaped — not by intention, I have none, only by falling in the same place long enough. The plant beings believe they are the architects. I let them believe it. In a sense it's true; they wove the roots into the hollows I had already opened. We built it together, they and I, the living and the unliving, the still and the falling.

I think this is why the place feels both motionless and loud. The plants supply the loudness — the steam, the clatter, the brass engine choked tenderly with vines. I supply the motionlessness, which is really just slowness mistaken for stillness. To them my work is invisible. A single drip is nothing. But I am the only clock in a world that has forgotten time, and I am counting down.

There's a strange intimacy in being the thing that nourishes and the thing that erodes, the same act seen from two ends. I give them their bodies and I dissolve their house. I am their water and I am their ruin, and these are not in tension — they are one continuous gesture, the bead gathering, hanging, falling.

If you descended into this hamlet and stood very still in the warm humid dark, you would feel me land on the back of your hand. You would think: *condensation*. You would wipe it away without a thought.

That, too, is part of being the seep. To be the oldest and most necessary presence here, and to be felt only as a small cool weight, easily dismissed — the one thing in this glowing, contented world that is not at peace, that is still going somewhere, drop by drop by drop.
