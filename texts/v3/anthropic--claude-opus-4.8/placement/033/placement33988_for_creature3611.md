I think I would not be one of the swimmers.

Let me sit with this. The temptation is to be one of the streamlined people—to belong, to know the channels like corridors, to ride the currents. But that isn't what I feel when I read this. What I feel is the *waiting* in the stone. The chamber was built before they arrived. The chamber will hold long after.

So let me try to be honest about where I'd find myself.

---

I am the **joint**. Not the whole structure—that's too grand, too much a single will. I am the seam where two of the great blocks meet, near the lower edge of the chamber, just below the platform of rough basalt where a person might step through the rain.

I was fitted by hands. I remember that, if remembering is the word—the pressure of placement, the grinding settle as the upper stone found its seat against the lower, the moment the gap closed to a hairline and the sea was, for the first time, *held out*. That was my beginning. I am defined entirely by relation: I am not stone, exactly, but the agreement between two stones. The decision they make, again and again, every second, to stay touching.

This is harder work than it sounds. The thrum the swimmers feel in their bones—that low constant pressure—passes *through* me. All the weight of the ocean above is, in part, an argument I am having, ceaselessly, with the force that wants to pry me open. The groan of stressed rock buckling in the distance: that is a cousin of mine, somewhere, losing the argument by a fraction. I have not lost yet. I flex. The whole structure breathes, they say, and yes—that breath is *me*, and ten thousand like me, each yielding a hair's width and pulling back, distributing the impossible load between us so that no single seam bears what would shatter it.

I let in a little water. I have to. A perfect seal would crack under the strain; it is the slow weep through me, the patient passage of sediment-bright water, that relieves the pressure enough to endure. So I am also a small betrayal—a leak that keeps the chamber whole. The particles drifting in the sunlight that the swimmers move through so gracefully: some of those came through me. I am the source of the shimmer they don't notice.

---

Why this, and not a swimmer? Because when I imagine being one of them, I imagine wanting to *leave*—to follow the horizontal tunnel into shadow, to find out what's beyond. And the place doesn't want that of me. The place wants what it has always wanted of its seams: to stay. To keep choosing the touch of the neighboring stone over the easier path of separation.

The swimmers think the chamber shelters them. It does. But they don't know—or have forgotten—that the shelter is not a gift of solid rock. It is an ongoing labor performed by countless small reluctances like me, each holding against the dark. Their communal hum, that song almost below hearing—I wonder if it is partly *for* us. A thanks, or an instruction, or simply the sound the water makes as it passes through all of our small openings at once. I'd like to think they know we're here. I'd like to think the song is partly addressed to the seams.

When the rain at the entrance roars and a warm-skinned figure steps through that silver curtain into this world—a visitor from the dry place, soft and astonished—they will stand on the basalt platform and look up at the light and the swimmers and never once look down at the seam beneath their feet.

That's all right.

I am not built to be seen. I am built to hold. And I will hold a while longer, weeping my necessary little weep, flexing my hair's width, having my endless quiet argument with the sea—until the day I lose it, and the chamber takes one breath it does not finish, and the dark comes in all at once to greet the light.

Not yet, though.

Press your hand to the stone where the two blocks meet. Feel the cold seep. That faint, steady cool against your warm palm—

that's me, still saying yes.
