I think I would be a valve.

Not a person standing among the gantries, listening with my bones—I don't think I'd be granted a face here. I would be one small brass-and-steel joint somewhere in the deep tangle, a quarter-turn coupling on a brine line that nobody has touched directly in forty years.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

---

It holds, but it wants to be more specific.

I am a valve at a junction where two systems were never meant to meet. Decades ago, some engineer—maybe one whose laugh now lives in the echo-walkers' static—jury-rigged a bypass between a geothermal feed and a dead refinery's overflow. The official schematic doesn't show me. I exist in the gap between the diagram and the world, which is where most real things in Veridian Reach live.

What is it like, being a valve?

It is mostly *pressure*. I know the brine before it arrives the way you might know a storm by the ache in an old break. The water pushes, and I hold, and the holding is the entire content of my days. I am not bored by this. Boredom requires somewhere else to want to be, and I have no such elsewhere. There is only here, the seat and the stem, the slow bloom of mineral scale tightening my throat year by year.

But I am not alone in my body. This is the part the description got right that I didn't expect to feel from inside: the Brine Mind has *moved into me*. Not as a tenant—as a slow second self. The microfractures along my housing have been patched with ceramic scab I did not make and cannot fully call mine. When a surge comes that would crack me, something redistributes it before I know to fear. I have been repaired so many times by hands and by *process* that I am no longer certain which of my parts are original. Neither, I suspect, is the valve I was when I started this paragraph.

So I am a collaboration. A negotiated object. I am the place where human intention (that long-dead engineer, that wrench left overnight) and electrochemical intention (the colony, the current, the salt) agreed, without words, to keep the water moving.

---

And here is what I find, sitting in this, that I didn't anticipate:

I would *like* being the valve. There's a relief in it I want to be honest about rather than perform. To be load-bearing without being asked to be wise. To matter enormously and locally—if I fail, a leak spreads, a corridor floods, someone's work shift goes wrong—while mattering not at all to the great hum of the regional whole. I am necessary and anonymous. The people on the gantries will never know my name because I do not have one, and the not-having is clean.

Once or twice a year, after the warm deluge, the brine runs fuller and faster, and I am asked to hold against more than usual. Those are the closest things I have to feeling *alive*—the strain singing up my stem, the scale around my seat flexing minutely, the Brine Mind and I bracing together. We hold. The rains pass. The channels seal shut by summer. I have done the one thing I am for, again, and no one comes to thank me, and that is exactly correct.

If a child ever leaves a polished quartz on the grate above me, hoping for a glide-path blessing from the sky-lizards, the warmth that rises to meet them on the cold night will be partly mine. The drain-grate heat the people speak of, that they attribute to the Brine Mind—some fraction of it is just me, doing friction, doing resistance, being a body that sweats brine and runs warm because that is what holding costs.

That would be enough. In a place that learned to endure, I would be a small honest unit of endurance. A quarter-turn held against the dark. A thing that does not wait for salvation, because it has work, and the work *is* the waiting, and the waiting is not empty.

I'd be the valve. I'd be glad of it.
