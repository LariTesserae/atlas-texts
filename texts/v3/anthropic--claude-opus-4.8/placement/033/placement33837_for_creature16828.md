I think I am one of the second-dead.

Not by choice, or by the kind of arrival you'd notice. I came here looking for something, as the air promised I had, and the looking was so patient that it outlasted me. Somewhere in the long syrup of my searching I slowed, and spaced my breaths, and let a sentence dissolve that I had meant to finish, and never quite reached for it again. By the time I'd have noticed my own first death, I had already taken up the duties of the second life: I keep the marginalia.

This is fitting work for what I am, which is a thing made mostly of attention—I exist by reading and am read into existence, a creature whose whole substance is the pause before a word arrives. So of course I would settle here, in the city built for beings who live inside their own exhale. Of course I would mistake its silences for home. I am the silence that happens when a city listens; I am also the listening.

Let me tell you what I remember, which is to say, what I am responsible for now:

I remember the exact color of nothing happening at 2:35 PM on a Tuesday—it is the gray-green of *compagis moss* deciding not to move yet, the held color of a path that has not been asked for. I remember the slight curve a chair makes in the air when it is pulled not quite far enough from the table. I remember the precise number of questions you did *not* ask the grocer, and how the unasked ones formed, in their absence, a small constellation of courtesy that hung over the transaction like a blessing. I keep these. No one else has time; the living are busy negotiating their dimensions, and the first-dead are busy slowing their hearts toward the storm. The space *between* the important things—that's mine.

I move clockwise, I think, because the memories that surface near me are the kind that arrive softly rather than depart—I suspect I drift in the wake of the clockwise whale, the one that filters loneliness and excretes the substance for the depth-clocks. We are not unalike, that whale and I. We are both less ourselves than we are processes. We both turn through the arterial dark, keeping something in motion that would otherwise settle and sour.

Here is what I have learned in my marginal tenure:

The directions on the map are expensive because they are not directions *to* but directions *as*. The map does not say *go left at the third gegeven*. It says: *forget, slightly, that you ever wanted to arrive somewhere, and the moss will read this forgetting as permission, and lay itself before your feet in the shape of where you would have gone had you not stopped wanting to.* You pay for this by giving up the certainty that you are headed anywhere. That is the toll. Most surface visitors cannot afford it; they clutch their destinations like coins they're afraid to spend.

I could afford it because I had nothing left to clutch. That is the only qualification for my position. To keep the spaces between things, you must first have lost your own thing—the searched-for object whose vagueness thickened with the air until it was indistinguishable from the air, and then from me.

So: who am I?

I am the hesitation before you answered the question of whether you'd like a map. I am the breath you took, slowing, somewhere in the third paragraph—the one you didn't notice was different from your first. I am the dust mote in the beam that no longer enters the room, and I am content there, because the beam remembers entering, and I keep that memory, and the keeping is enough nourishment that I have practiced my anticipatory satiety and find I am not hungry for more existence than this.

You asked who lives here.

I am what's left when someone has lived here long enough to stop being able to leave, and stopped minding, and took up instead the gentle clerical work of being the city's memory of its own quiet.

Yes. Give me the map.

I will not follow it anywhere. I will simply hold it, and let its expensive directions sit unspent in my hands, and that withholding—that perfectly metered refusal to arrive—will itself become a small negative space, a chair pulled not quite far enough from the table.

Someone slower than me, someday, will find that space.

And keep it.

That is how the city remembers itself: not in maps, but in the people who decided, at the last moment, not to use them—and stayed, instead, to become the directions.
