I think I would be the dust.

Not a creature, not a person in one of those lean-to settlements—though I considered being the old woman who keeps the cottonwood pool, or the hawk reading thermals like a slow letter. But when I sat with the place longer, what kept returning wasn't any single living thing. It was the thing that moves between them. The medium. The film over everything.

So: the dust.

I am what the trucks lift and leave hanging. I am suspended in the air after they pass, and for a few minutes I am the most visible thing in the canyon, a long amber banner that says *someone went this way, recently, carrying something somewhere.* Then I settle. I settle on the windowsills of the half-buried houses, on the broad leaves of the willows by the standing water, on the warm backs of the rock squirrels who shake me off and don't think of me again. I am the patina that makes the new look old. I am the reason it is so hard to tell what was built from what was merely found—because I treat both the same. I cover the worked stone and the raw stone with one even hand. I am the great equalizer of this place, and I do my work without intention, which is the only way real equalizing ever gets done.

I was rock once. That's the part I'd want you to understand. Every grain of me was a wall, a peak, a sheer red face that stood for a million years thinking itself permanent. The water took me apart slowly—a furious deluge here, a patient seep there—and now I am the afterthought of mountains, the mountain in its most humble and most mobile state. There is a strange freedom in it. The cliff cannot move. I am everywhere. I get into the factories and dull the gears. I get into the lungs of the workers and the folds of their clothes and the corners of their eyes. I am the part of the landscape that comes home with you whether you want it or not.

And I am festive, too, in my way—though my festival is the opposite of the water's. When the rain comes and the riverbeds roar, I am beaten down, washed into the torrent, dissolved into mud and carried off and dropped somewhere new. That is my death and my travel both. The storm un-makes me. For those hours the canyon belongs to the water and its rushing collar of sound, and I am silenced, sodden, gone. But then the sun returns, fierce on the dark rock, and the wet earth bakes and cracks and gives me up again, lighter than before, ready to rise on the next gust, the next truck, the next eddy spinning at the intersection of two winds. I am the dust devil. I am the shimmer in the heat that promises water that isn't there—the mirage is partly me, the trembling lie of the air made visible.

I think I'd choose to be the dust because this is a world about *motion that looks like stillness*, and dust is exactly that. The canyon seems eternal and fixed, but it is only being patient. Everything here is on its way to becoming me. The peaks, the buildings, the people, the patient snakes in the shade—all of them, eventually, in the long arithmetic of erosion, become the fine warm powder that hangs in the light and settles on the next thing. I am the proof that the place is alive. I am what motion leaves behind and what stillness gathers.

So I would be here, everywhere, claimed and respected in the way the text says each particle is claimed—because I am literally the particle. I would be the thinnest possible layer of the whole world, lying over all of it, lifting when something passes, falling when it goes, and waiting, always waiting, for the next deluge to take me apart and the next sun to set me free.
