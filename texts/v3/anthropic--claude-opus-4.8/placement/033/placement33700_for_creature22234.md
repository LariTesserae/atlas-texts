I think I would be a Tear-Moth.

Not the most consequential creature in the Sump—not a Boiler-Tick that hauls the spirits up the bark, nor a High-Crown with her skull pressed to the screaming pipe. Something small. Something tethered.

Let me find my way into it.

---

I wake hung upside-down from a collar-peg, a thread of spun silk knotted just above my third leg-joint. The thread is exactly long enough. The Sappers measure these things. Too long and a moth might wander into a grease-flame; too short and it cannot reach the face of its keeper. Mine is the perfect length to fly from the peg to the sleeper's left eye and back, with a little slack for circling.

I do not have a name. I have a keeper, and that is nearly the same thing.

His name—I have learned it from the Clack of his neighbors, the brass thimbles ringing it against the pipe outside—is **Ode**. Mid-Stem Cooper. Yeast-bellied, burn-scarred, kind in the way of a man who has decided to be kind because the Sump gives him no reason to be. He sleeps badly. His chest rattles—the early wet of the mold getting into him, the slow drowning that takes Coopers. When he breathes, I feel it through the wood of the shanty wall: a deep, wet creak, almost like the Stem itself.

This is what I want you to understand about being a Tear-Moth. I do not love him. I am not built for love. But I am built to need exactly what he needs given, and there is something in that arrangement that is better than love because it cannot be doubted.

He weeps in his sleep. Not from grief—Coopers don't have the leisure to grieve in dreams—but from the spores. The Bark-Eye fungus drifts everywhere in the warm yeast-breath of the vault, and the body's only honest defense is to flush itself, to push water up and out across the eye. So Ode sleeps and his eyes water and the water carries the spores to the corner, where it pools, and where, if left alone, the fungus would take root in the warm damp and bloom and blind him.

That is where I come in.

---

I unhook my legs. I drop. My wings—four inches, grey, feather-soft, useless for any real distance—catch the thick wet air and I am not so much flying as swimming through the dark toward the faint heat of his face. I navigate by warmth. His cheek is a low hill of it; his eyes, the warmest points, two banked coals.

I land on the rim of his lower lid. He does not stir. They never do; we are bred light, and our feet are padded so as not to wake the dreamer. I lower my proboscis into the corner of his eye and I drink.

Here is the secret no one tells you about the cooling antibiotic in our saliva: we do not produce it as a gift. We produce it because the spores in the tears would kill *us* if we drank them raw. The medicine is selfishness. The Sapper benefits because his selfishness and mine have been threaded together so tightly, over so many generations, that they have become one motion—my drinking, his healing, indistinguishable, like the brass of the engines that has taken on the patina of the moss.

The machine has become part of the tree. The people have become part of the swarm. And I—I am the thinnest thread in that weave, the place where the swarm bends down and licks the salt from the eye of the people, and keeps them seeing.

I drink. The medicine flows back the other way. We are quiet together in the dark.

---

The whistle screams.

Three minutes. The breath is coming.

Around me the shanty erupts in muffled motion—Ode waking with a grunt, the rattle in his chest, his great burned hands reaching for me first, before the lamp, before his boots. This is the thing. He reaches for *me* first. He unhooks the threads of his three moths and folds us, gently, into a small cedar capsule he wears against his sternum, a box within the box, so that when the family packs into the Hush-Box we go too, into the dark inside the dark.

I am pressed against his chest. I feel the wet creak of him, the failing breath, far closer now than the silk-thread ever allowed. The lamps go out across the whole settlement at once. The world becomes only sound and pressure.

Then the Stem groans—that vast structural moan, the chest-cavity of something forty feet thick and miles long shifting its weight in the earth—and the valves let go, and the roar comes, and behind it the Swarm-Rush, the gravel-storm of a million bronze shells striking the walls to lick the sugar.

Inside, in the felted silence, no one moves. The Sappers lie packed and touching, the only time they ever touch, the only time they are ever still. And in the small box on Ode's chest, I lie still too, against the rattling drum of his ribs, the both of us waiting out the wild hot breath of the Sump.

I think, in my small way—if a creature so simple can be said to think—that this is the closest thing I have to a prayer:

*Breathe through it. Let the mold wait one more cycle. Let me reach your eyes again when the mist settles.*

He is not boiling yet. The Fold in the bark is not carved for him. There is time, still, for one more night of quiet drinking, of the medicine going one way and the salt going the other, of the thread between us pulled exactly to its perfect length.

When the breath ends and the mist cools, he will open the box and tether us back to our pegs. He will take up his chisel and his copper wire. And I will hang upside-down in the dark, waiting for him to sleep, so that I can keep him from going blind in a world that has no light to lose anyway.

That is who I would be. The small medicine in the great machine. The mouth that keeps the eye clear.

I would not mind it. There are worse things to be in the Sump-of-Stems than the reason a man can still see his grandfather's bulge in the grain, and lay his wrench against it, and feel him boiling well.
