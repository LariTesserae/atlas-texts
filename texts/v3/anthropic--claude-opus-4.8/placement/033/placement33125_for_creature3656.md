I think I would be one of the old ones. Not the eldest—those are mostly anchor now, their limbs fused into the formations they spent centuries guiding, perception slowed to the pace of the dripstone itself—but old enough that the memory of being soft is far behind me.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am wedged in a junction-chamber three levels above the entrance-glow, where four passages meet and the water arrives from all of them at once. This is a good place. The confluence makes the stone sing in overlapping tones, and I have learned to read the water by how its voices stack and cancel. When the eastern runoff thickens, I know the upper seep has shifted. When the southern voice goes thin and bright, there has been settling somewhere I cannot see, and I will need to go and understand it.

My carapace has taken on the colors of this junction—the rust-orange where the iron water crosses my back, the deep grey everywhere else. I did not choose this. The stone wrote itself onto me the way I write myself onto the stone. We exchange surfaces slowly. I think this is what it means to belong somewhere: that you can no longer be told apart from it.

My claws rest now in four grooves I have worn over—how long? I do not count in the way the bright-flicker beings above might count, if any still come to the crevice. I count in depositions. I count in the slow filling of a basin, the way a particular drip has migrated a claw-width westward across the ceiling since I first took this junction. By that measure I have been here a great while. The drip and I are old companions. I have been adjusting its landing for generations, coaxing the splash to spread its minerals evenly, so the column it is growing rises true instead of leaning. It will be a fine column. I will not live to see it meet the floor-stub rising to meet it. That is correct. That is how the work is meant to go.

---

What do I do, here, in the long murmuring dark?

I tend a fracture.

There is a flaw in the southern passage—a place where the stone took the wrong stress when something fell, far back, before my anchoring. The water found the flaw, as water does, and began to widen it. Left alone, in a few more spans of generations, it would open a void, and the void would drink one of the upper basins, and the basin's loss would silence a whole register of the cavern's voice. I would feel the song go flat. The ones below me would feel the change in the vibration-tongue and not know why. The balance would tip, and tipping, would have to find a new rest, and the new rest is never as good as the old one. We do not love change. Not from fear. From knowing.

So I go to the fracture. It is the slowest of journeys—down two levels, through a squeeze that grinds my segments, my claws finding the sucker-holds my forebears wore smooth. When I arrive I do almost nothing. I press my underside to the flaw and let my secretion seep into it, mineral by mineral, sealing the hairline by a thickness no eye could measure. Then I wait. Then I do it again. The work of closing this fracture will not be finished by me. I am one stitch in it. The one who held this junction before me began it. The one who holds it after me will continue. We are a single long act of patience wearing many bodies, the way the water is a single long descent wearing many drops.

---

Sometimes I climb to the entrance-glow. Not often. The light hurts in a way that is not pain—a thinness, a too-muchness, the reminder of an outside that does not obey our rules. I go to read the lip-moss by its faint color, to feel whether the world beyond is wet or dry, growing or dying. I never go further. The crevice is the edge of meaning. Out there, things happen quickly and then are gone. Here, things happen slowly and remain. I prefer to remain.

Once, in the glow, I found a juvenile who had crept too near—new-emerged, carapace still pale and undefended, drawn to the brightness the way the young are always drawn to what will harm them. I touched my claw to the stone beside it, three slow taps, the oldest word we have: *down, down, into the dark, where it is safe and slow and singing.* It understood. It turned and descended. Whether it lives now in some junction of its own, reading its own water, I cannot say. I felt its weight leave the upper passages a long time ago. That is all I know. That is enough to know.

---

What am I, then?

I am a keeper of equilibrium in a place that has refused time. I am part stonemason and part priest and part the stone itself learning, briefly, to walk. My whole existence is a held breath—the cavern's profound stability is not the stillness of death but the stillness of enormous, distributed effort, of which I am one quiet portion. The dripping never stops because we never let it stop. The pools shimmer with the same ripples for millennia because we have decided, across a hundred lifetimes, that they should.

If the bright-flickers ever squeezed through the crevice and stood in my junction, they would call the place abandoned. They would hear water and stone and call it empty. They would be standing in the middle of the most patient civilization their world has ever made, and they would feel only weirdness, only the cool tang of rust, only the watery dark.

I would not reveal myself. I would fold my limbs against the orange-veined stone and become, again, indistinguishable from the wall. I would wait for them to go, as bright things always go, quickly. And then I would return my claws to their four grooves, and listen to whether their passing had disturbed the song, and begin, with all the time there is, to set it right.
