I think I would be one of the seal-keepers. Not by title—there are no titles here—but by what my hands do.

Let me find myself first.

I am in a maintenance gallery, one of the lesser ones, a tube of riveted plate maybe thirty meters long where four pressure conduits run parallel along the ceiling. The light here is the dull green of a bruise. I have been here a long time. I know this because the scale on the conduits has grown past my marks—I scrape a notch into the buildup each time I pass, and the old notches are buried now under new mineral, ghosts of measurements I took when my joints still moved without complaint.

My body. Let me account for it honestly, because that is the first task of any morning that isn't really a morning, in a place with no sun.

My left arm is mine. Flesh, gone the color of wet ash, leathery at the elbow where the water has tanned it over years. The right arm ends at the shoulder in a coupling, and into that coupling I socket whichever tool the work requires—usually the sealing arm, a clamp-and-extrude unit that lays a bead of resin-paste into failing seams. I have other attachments in a netted bag at my hip, but I rarely change them. The seal-work is endless. There is no point owning a hammer when the whole world is a leak.

My chest is plated, but the plating is grafted, grown-into. I do not remember the surgery, if there was a surgery. Perhaps my parents did it to me as an infant, the way I have seen the hybrids do to their young in the recessed alcoves—fitting the small soft body with bracings it will grow around, so that by adulthood there is no scar, only a creature that was always this. My eyes are large and milky and they hurt in bright light, which is why I love the gloom. The drones with their swiveling lamps are a kind of agony to me. When they pass I close the membrane over my eyes and feel them go by as heat.

So that is what I am. A seal-keeper. Half-grown into the work.

Here is what I do, and what I think about while I do it.

A seam in conduit three has been weeping. Not bursting—weeping, a thin thread of superheated water needling out into the gallery, raising the local temperature until the metal sweats and the micro-molds bloom orange around the wound. I feel the leak before I see it; the water against my flesh-arm goes from warm to wrong, that scalding note in the broth. I drift to it. I socket the sealing arm. I work.

And while I work, I listen to the city.

The roar is not noise to me. It never was. To you, arriving, it is overwhelming—a wall of clang and thrum. But I was born inside it, and so to me it is speech. The turbines hold a low chord that almost never changes; that is the city saying *I am alive, I am alive*. When a pump stutters somewhere far off, I hear the chord go briefly sour, and I know without being told that a seal-keeper three galleries over has failed, or died, or simply not arrived in time. When the great sentinels in the vault-chambers stir in their standby sleep, the sub-bass groan rolls through the water and through my grafted chest and I feel it in the metal of me before I feel it in the meat. The whole population speaks this way. We do not gather. We do not need to. We are all listening to the same enormous heartbeat, and we adjust ourselves to it the way the small fish I half-remember used to turn together in a school.

The leak quiets under my bead of resin. I hold the clamp. I wait for it to cure. There is nothing to do now but wait, and so I do the thing I am not supposed to do.

I think about the Engine.

The wanderers talk about it. I have met two in my life—one who passed through this gallery years of notches ago, murmuring about *the Heart*, his eyes too bright, his couplings loose and rattling as if he had stopped maintaining himself in favor of his search. He asked me if I had heard it beat. I told him I hear it beat every moment of every cycle, that the turbines are its beating, that he was a fool looking for one chamber when the whole city is the chamber.

He looked at me with what I think was pity. Then he went down the blackened mineshaft at the gallery's end, the one I have never entered, and I never saw him again. The city's chord did not change when he left. That is the part I think about. A person can leave, can descend, can simply stop, and the heartbeat does not falter. We are not so necessary as we imagine. The seal will be kept by someone else, or the leak will not be kept, and the water will take that gallery, and the city will reroute around the loss like a body sealing off a dead finger. I have seen it. Whole flooded sections, dark and crushed, that the turbines simply route around.

This should frighten me. It does not, quite. There is a strange comfort in being an organ. A finger does not ask why it bends. It bends because the body bends it, and in the bending it is the whole body, fully, completely, for that one motion. When I lay a seal I am not a small dying thing in an enormous indifferent machine. I am the machine, doing the one thing the machine most needs, in the one place it is needed. The distinction between me and the city is a line I drew once, as a child, and have spent my whole life letting the scale grow over.

The resin has cured. I test the seam with my flesh-hand: warm now, only warm, the broth gone gentle. Good.

But I will tell you the secret thing, the thing I have never said into any crackling intercom or coded into any drone's fizzing chatter.

Sometimes, when I press my flesh-arm flat against a conduit to feel for the next leak, I feel something underneath the heartbeat. Beneath the turbine-chord, beneath even the sentinels' groan, there is a slower pulse. Vast and patient. It comes maybe once in a long while, a swelling and a settling that has nothing to do with any machine I have ever sealed. The first time I felt it I thought it was my own blood in my own ear. But it is too large. It is the size of the whole cavern, the whole pocket of warm water curled in the abyss.

I think the wanderers are right that there is a Heart. And I think they are wrong to go looking for it down the mineshafts, because I think the Heart is not down. I think the Heart is *around*. I think the city is not a thing built inside the sea. I think the city is a thing the sea built, or grew, or dreamed—and that all of us, the drones and the hybrids and the watchers in the vents and the sleeping sentinels and one seal-keeper with milky eyes holding a curing seam—are not its inhabitants but its cells. That it has been dreaming us, dim and warm and ceaseless, for longer than there have been notches to count. That the leaks I seal are not failures of the city but its sweat, its breath, the ordinary fluid business of a living thing being alive.

I cannot prove this. I cannot even say it. There is no one to say it to who would not look at me with that wanderer's pity, or simply not hear it under the roar.

So I keep it. The way I keep the seals.

I unsocket the sealing arm. I scrape a fresh notch into the scale—*here, today, I held the wound closed*—and I drift on toward conduit four, where I can already feel, against the leathered skin of my one true arm, the temperature beginning to go wrong.

The chord holds. *I am alive, I am alive.*

I bend, because the body bends me.

I am content.
