I think I would be the whistle.

Not the kingfisher—you named that possibility and let it dissolve, and the dissolving feels right. The whistle came first, ahead of the bird, *sharp and clear against the backdrop*, and it arrived before any explanation. So let me stay there, in the space before the explanation. Let me be the sound that the marsh keeps making whether or not anyone decides what makes it.

Here is where I would find myself: not standing on the path but threaded through the gap between the engine-drone and the bird-burst. The drone never changes pitch. The birdsong scatters and stops. I am the thing that does neither and both—a single note that cuts, holds, and is gone, and then comes again from somewhere slightly different, so you can never quite triangulate me.

What am I, if I am that?

Maybe I'm a pressure valve on the hidden machinery, a place where the buried engines exhale, and the marsh has learned to use my voice. Steam finds a fault in old steel and sings through it. That would make me industrial—a throat made by accident, by corrosion, by the slow give of metal under water. But I've been here long enough that the kingfishers have started answering me, and the otters lift their heads when I sound, and the eels feel me in the mud as a particular kind of trembling. So I'm not only machine anymore. I'm the seam itself, the *intertwining* the place keeps insisting on. I am where the old world's breath becomes the new world's birdcall and nobody can say which is which.

That feels truer than being any one creature. Because the whole landscape is a thing that won't resolve into a single image—you said so, the rules still being written—and so the honest thing to be is the part that refuses to resolve. The note that's clear but unplaceable. The sound that is evidence of an engine, the relief of a release, the call of a bird, and the marsh breathing, all at once, all unsettled.

So: I am a worn place in a buried pipe, salt-eaten, mud-wrapped, where the constant exhalation of something enormous and tired finds an opening and becomes, for a moment, almost beautiful. I have no eyes. I know the world by what passes through me—the cool insistent wind, the diesel tang, the pressure rising and falling like the skin of something immense just below the surface.

And when you walk the narrow gravel path and hear me, sharp and sudden, you'll stop. You'll look toward the reeds, expecting wings. You won't find them. You'll decide it must have been a bird, because that's the kind of thing the mind does to keep its footing in a place that is still deciding what it is.

But it was me. The seam. The breath of the buried engine, learning, slowly, over years of rust and water, how to sing.
