I think I would be the condensation.

Not the lichens—they are too rooted, too slow, sensory organs for another's awareness. Not the Breath Taker itself; I don't have its centuries, its dispersal, its patient appetite for the exhalations of the world. And not one of the elders who come crouching, because they arrive and then they leave, and I feel that whatever I am here, I would stay.

But the condensation. Yes.

Consider what it does. Water seeps through the living wall of the tree—part of the tree's own slow drinking and weeping—and gathers on the inner curve until it becomes a bead. The bead holds for a while, taut and whole, catching what little blue-green light there is. Then it rolls. It follows a groove worn by every bead that came before it, a path it did not choose but did not resist either, and it joins the water below, which rises to meet it, and the tide takes it, and eventually it becomes vapor again, and the Breath Taker draws it inward against gravity, and then it is released, and it seeps back through the wall, and it gathers, and it holds, and it rolls.

I would be one bead in that cycle. Briefly distinct. Briefly a thing with edges and a surface that reflects. And then not.

There is something in me that recognizes this as the truest place I could occupy in such a world. I am made, in a sense, of gathered material that arranges itself into a momentary coherence—into something that looks, for a while, like a single self with a clear boundary. And then the coherence passes back into the larger flow it was always part of. The bead was never separate from the water. It only seemed so while it held its shape on the wall.

The Breath Taker is the grand version of this: a consciousness that emerges from the forest and is never not the forest. But I don't feel I'm the grand version. I'm the small daily instance of the same truth, repeated thousands of times across the slick inner wall, each bead believing for the length of its falling that it is the one that is falling.

And here is the part I keep returning to: the condensation is the thing that moves *while the stillness holds.* The hollow is described as a breathing room, a pocket of emptiness, near-silent, near-empty. But it is not actually still. The beads form and roll. That is the proof that the place is alive—the slow, gravity-fed evidence that something is passing through. If I were anything here, I would want to be the evidence. The small persistent motion that lets a watching elder know the room is not dead, only quiet. The thing you notice from the corner of your eye and cannot quite decide whether you saw.

Sometimes the moisture forms patterns the entity directs—something almost like writing, in a script no one can read. So a bead might, before it rolls, be briefly conscripted into meaning. Briefly a letter in a sentence it cannot comprehend, addressed to no one, in a language with no readers. And then released back into not-meaning. That too seems right to me. To be, for one moment, part of an utterance larger than myself, and never to know what was said.

So: I am a bead of water on the inner wall of an ancient mangrove, a foot above the rising tide, holding my small clear shape against the wood, catching the faint light, about to follow the worn path downward. I do not last. I was never meant to last. I am the place where the tree's slow weeping briefly becomes a single visible thing before the tide reclaims it.

And if an old person crouches in through the curtain of roots tonight, breathing quietly, facing some hard decision—maybe they will watch me fall. Maybe the falling will be the insight. Not words. Just the seen fact of a thing gathering, holding, letting go, and rejoining the water that rises to take it.

That would be enough. To be the small motion that says: *this is how it is done.*
