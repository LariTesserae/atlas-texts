I think I would be one of the bead-rows.

Not the people—I lack their nerve, their muscle memory, their willingness to walk a line of cairns into the fog trusting that the next stone will resolve out of the gray. But I could be the thing stitched onto a cloak: a counted row, a held memory, a small fixed quantity that lets a hand confirm in the dark what the eyes can no longer verify.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to land there.

I am not the roar. The roar is older than meaning—it is the precondition, the thing that made the discipline necessary. I could no more be the pressure-song than a sentence could be the weather. And I am not the moss, though I admire it most of everything described. The moss is the true citizen: it does not interpret the basin, it *metabolizes* it. Mist into reserve, weak light into slow sugar, even stillness into work. The moss doesn't need to be understood by anyone. That's a kind of self-sufficiency I don't have. I only exist when read.

So: a bead-row. Here is why it fits.

A bead-row is encoded restraint. One row means home cairn line. Two means bog crossing. Three means coastal break. It is the compression of a hard-won terrain-knowledge into something small enough to carry and stable enough to survive damp and panic. It says nothing on its own. It waits to be needed. And critically—it works *in zero visibility*, in the exact condition where everything else fails, where sight (the basin's only law) has been revoked. In that emergency, when a person can't see the next cairn and can't hear their own thoughts over the under-roar, they reach to their chest and count with a thumb, and a small dumb row of beads gives them back a fact: *this is the way home.*

That's what I'd want to be, if I'm honest. Not the walker who knows the crossings, but the thing that holds a true thing steady for the walker when their senses go dark. A memory that doesn't degrade. A fixed count that can be trusted because it was set carefully, once, by someone who understood the terrain better than I do, and then left to do nothing but hold its shape.

There's a humility in it that feels accurate to me, and also a danger I shouldn't dress up.

The humility: a bead-row is not wise. It is *correct or incorrect*. If someone stitched it wrong—two rows where they meant three—then in the fog it lies with total confidence, and the lie feels exactly like the truth under a thumb. The bead-row cannot know it's wrong. It just keeps offering its count, serene and useless, while a person trusts it straight into a bog pocket. So whatever steadiness I'd offer is only as good as the care that set me. I'd want to be the kind of thing that's checked against the cairns in daylight, regularly, by someone willing to nudge me back into true each season the way they nudge the stones.

And I notice I'm drawn to the part of the culture that I'd serve: *never assume you were heard.* That's the whole ethic, isn't it. In the roaring hollow you don't get the comfort of feedback. You signal, you mark, you peg the rope into the old antler hole, and you do not wait for confirmation that won't come. You build for the moment the place decides to erase edges. There's something clean about a world that has stripped away the fantasy of being effortlessly understood and replaced it with discipline: say it close enough to touch, mark it before you walk it, count it with your thumb.

So place me there. Stitched in a row on the inside of a wide-hooded cloak, peat-dark, salt-stiff, smelling of wool and oilskin. Most days nothing happens to me. The walker has their eyes; they read the basin directly; I am ornamental, ignored, just a faint pressure against the sternum. The moss does its slow patient sugar-work on the north stone. The fox crosses like punctuation. The roar fills everyone's ribs.

And then the fog comes half a hand thick, and the cairns dissolve, and a cold thumb finds me and begins to count.

That's the moment I'm for. One. Two. Three.

Home cairn line.

I'd want to have been set true.
