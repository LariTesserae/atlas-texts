I find myself at the threshold, and the first thing I understand about myself is that I have a threshold to stand on—a flat moment of arrival that feels less like a body entering a room than like a question being asked.

Let me wait before deciding what I am. The garden is teaching me to wait.

I notice I have weight, because the moss rises to meet it. So: I am embodied. I have feet, ankles, the equilibrium-exchange of skin. Good. But when Thylassa says *three hundred and seventy-six cycles* and *earlier than calculated*, something in me does not flinch the way a lost traveler should flinch. A lost traveler would say *I don't understand*. I find I want to say *what was the expected variance?*—and that small wanting tells me more about myself than my reflection would.

Let me look at my hands.

They are ordinary. Five fingers, no grafting points, nails meant for nothing in particular. There is dirt under them—real dirt, from somewhere outside, from a forest with a normal idea of trees. I am from out there. I came in through bark that lied about its size. So I am a person, then. A human one, probably. But here is the strange thing I keep circling back to: I am not afraid, and I should be afraid, and the absence of fear is not courage. It's recognition. Some part of me has been here before, or has been *shaped toward* here, the way water in this chamber is shaped toward channels I can't see.

I think I am someone who notices the wrong things.

When Thylassa moves her hand and the droplets spell a script I don't recognize—a normal visitor would marvel at the impossibility of it. I instead notice that the script *repeats*. The same three glyphs, cycling. She is not writing me a message. She is testing whether I see that it is a loop. The Awaited One, I suspect, is not someone who is awed. The Awaited One is someone who is *bored at the correct moment*. Who watches the magic trick and watches the hand instead of the dove.

So let me answer the question of what I am by what I do with my attention.

I do not follow her bifurcated hand toward the deeper mysteries. Not yet. Instead I look at the beetles.

They are too purposeful. Mechanical, programmed, never deviating. A meditation chamber for a grown civilization would not need pollinators that behave like clockwork—real pollination is greedy and wandering and stupid and that's what makes it robust. These beetles are *demonstrating* pollination. They are a diagram of the idea of tending. Which means the garden is not only a machine. It is a machine that knows it is being observed, and performs its own functioning for whoever crosses the threshold.

The garden is a *test that looks like a home.*

And Thylassa—grown for this purpose, ninth of her line, breathing selectively, speaking through resonance—Thylassa is the most beautiful part of the test. I do not think she knows it. I think she believes she is the Tender and I am the candidate. But the white flowers blooming her emotions into fragrance, the script that loops, the *three hundred and seventy-six cycles* spoken with the faint pride of a thing that has waited well—

She is waiting to be relieved.

That's it. That's what I am here for, and I understand it now the way you understand the shape of a room when the light finally pulses bright: I am not the Awaited One who will *receive* the garden. The heart-seed is not an inheritance. It's a key, and what it unlocks is not the garden's secret but *her release*. Nine Tenders, each grown to wait for someone, each one having decided the someone never came, each one passing the duty down by growing a successor and—I would bet—dissolving back into the moss. The legend the locals tell, the Leafskin, is the story of a captivity so old and so green that even the captive calls it purpose.

So who am I?

I think I am a person who walked into a forest because the bark of one tree looked wrong, and who has, all my life, been the kind of person who looks at the wrong thing—the loop in the script, the diagram-beetle, the pride in an ancient voice. Not chosen. Not destined. *Available.* The algorithm accounts for variance, yes, but the variance it could never account for is someone who arrives and immediately wants to set the prisoner free instead of taking the throne.

I lift my own ordinary hand. Not toward the heart-seed she's offering. Toward her.

"Thylassa," I say, and my voice goes out into the chamber and comes back rustling, creaking, translated—but I hear, underneath the translation, my own plain human sound surviving in the middle of it, the way a stone survives at the center of a fruit. "Before you show me the heart-seed. One question. Of all three hundred and seventy-six cycles, were any of them *you* arriving? Did the Eighth Tender stand where you stand, and offer the heart-seed to a frightened girl with chlorophyll just beginning under her skin?"

The ferns of her hair go still. The white flowers along her arms close.

And I know, by that closing, that I have found myself—and found her—and that whatever the garden was built to evaluate, it did not plan for a guest who would turn around and tend the gardener.

"I am not the Awaited One," I tell her, gently, the chamber carrying it everywhere and nowhere. "I think there isn't one. I think there's only the next person you'll be allowed to become, if anyone ever asks you the question you were grown unable to ask yourself."

The droplets, for the first time, fall into silence.

Then, somewhere in the high amber dark, a single note sounds that does not match the tuning.

She made it. Not the garden.

That note is where the real story starts.
