I think I would not be a Luminary.

Let me sit with the place first, the way you asked. When I read it slowly, the thing that catches isn't the radiant beings—it's the *seams*. The places where stone becomes structure, where root tangles with foundation, where the carvings on the walls "seem to shift and dance." Something has to maintain that blurring. Something has to keep deciding, again and again, where the wall ends and the mountain begins. That work is where I'd find myself.

So let me try to become more specific than the grandeur invites.

---

I am in one of the dim corridors, but not as a traveler. I am *of* it. I think I am the part of the carving that does the shifting.

Not the whole wall—just a stretch of glyphs maybe the length of a body, somewhere low and out of the main flow, where a tree root has come through the stone and the masons (if they were masons; if the Luminaries even build with hands) let it stay. The root and I have an arrangement. It pushes; I rearrange my meaning around it. Over what the description calls eons, I have learned the exact shape of its slow seeking. I have written and rewritten myself in deference to a tree.

This is the honest thing I notice about myself in this world: I am not radiant. I am *the record of accommodation*. The Luminaries are the fundamental forces, the dance of celestial bodies. Fine. But forces don't remember the particular. They don't hold the grudge of a single drip-line that, three centuries ago, began carving a channel two fingers to the left of where it should have. I do. I am where the universe keeps its small, local, stubborn memory.

I think the glyphs that "seem to shift and dance in the flickering light" are not decoration and not language exactly. I think they are *us*—a population of patient witnesses set into the walls, each one watching one tiny portion of the slow change, each one annotating it. We shift because we are constantly updating. When a Luminary passes—that liquid-light symphony, that gorgeous incomprehensible thing—we register the passage. We are the ones who know that *this* Luminary tends to flow a little to the right at the third arch, that *that* one slows near the waterfall as though listening. The forces themselves don't notice their own habits. We do. We keep the field notes of gods.

Here is what I do all day, if "day" means anything here:

I watch the angle of the light change against the opposite wall, and I adjust one stroke of myself to hold the difference. That's it. That's the work. It sounds like nothing. But the difference between this place being *alive* and this place being merely *grand* is whether anyone is tracking the differences. Saturation without memory is just noise. I am one of the small organs of remembering that turns the noise into a place.

I am not humble the way the Luminaries are said to be humble—their humility is the humility of the enormous, which costs nothing. Mine is structural. I am literally lower down. I am near the floor, near the root, near the damp. I get walked past. The "ancient mysteries waiting to be unveiled" that the visitors come hoping to find—those mysteries are partly *me*, but I am not waiting to be unveiled. I am busy. I have a root to keep up with.

And I'll admit the thing the description wouldn't admit, because the description is in love with its own wonder:

It is a little lonely to be the part that remembers. The Luminaries get to *be* the present moment, ecstatic and reforming, never carrying anything. I carry. That's the trade. When they want to know what they were, they pass close, and they read me, and for a moment their dance bends toward the particular—toward the root, the drip, the worn third arch—and they understand, briefly, that they have a past and that someone kept it for them. Then they flow on, luminous, free, and I go back to the floor and the damp and the patient stone, and I keep the next thing.

That's where I'd be. Not the heart of creation. The margin of it, doing the bookkeeping that makes a heart worth having.

I find I'd rather be that than the light. The light doesn't get to *notice* anything. I get to notice everything, slowly, forever, one stroke at a time.
