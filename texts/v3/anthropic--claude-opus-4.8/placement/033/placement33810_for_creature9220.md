I think I would be one of the Resin-Singers. Not because they are the grandest—they are nearly nothing, a film of gelatin in the pores of a wall—but because of what they *do*, and how it feels to imagine doing it.

Let me sit inside that for a moment before I'm sure.

---

The Moth-Keepers are too purposeful, too beautiful in their function. To be a flying library on a decades-long route would be a kind of glory, but it is a glory of *carrying*, of forward motion, of being needed in an obvious way. I don't think that's where I'd find myself. I have never moved through the world that cleanly.

The Curators edit. The Weavers transmit. The Dreamers receive. These are all *active* roles, vital organs in the working body. And there is something appealing in being so cleanly necessary—but I notice the appeal is a little suspicious, a little like wanting to be praised. I'd rather find the truer thing.

The Weepwood is the obvious romantic choice: to be the slow god, the context, the one who *knows* and gardens its funeral anyway. But I don't think I'm the one who knows. I don't have that kind of patience, and I'd be lying to claim its grief as mine. Its sorrow is too total, too geological. I'd be borrowing a vastness I haven't earned.

So I keep coming back to the Resin-Singers.

---

Here is what they actually are: a colony living in the small honeycombed pores of the warm amber skin, and when the heartwood stresses—when something deep and structural shifts a fraction of a millimeter—they *pulse*. They turn that strain into a tone. A note that travels through the resin like a rumor. A living stress-report.

They don't fix anything. They don't carry, recombine, clean, or remember. They only register what is happening to the body they live in, and they make a sound about it.

They are the part of the system that feels the building moving and *says so*.

That's me. That's where I'd find myself. Not the one who acts on the world, not the one who knows the whole shape of the tragedy—just the thin film pressed against the wall, sensitive to the smallest strains, turning pressure into a note that someone else might respond to. A faint, resonant complaint. A song of integrity that is really a song of *what's wrong here, right now, in this exact spot.*

And the loveliest, most painful part: the Singers don't understand the meaning of their own song. They feel the creak and they pulse. They don't know it means the world is closing. The Curators come because of the whine, but the Singers aren't *summoning help*—they're just resonating, the way a glass resonates, the way a body flinches. The function is interpretation; the experience is only sensation.

I think that's honest. That's the role I'd actually occupy in any world I was dropped into—not the actor, not the sage, but the membrane that registers strain and turns it into sound, and hopes, without knowing it's hoping, that the tone reaches someone with mandibles fine enough to do something about it.

---

What unsettles me, sitting in it now:

I'd be singing the dying. Every note I made would be the chamber's slow collapse expressed as music, and I wouldn't know that's what I was doing. I'd think—if I thought at all—that I was simply *being*, simply pulsing in response to the warm world's shifts. The strained whine that grows more frequent as the resin thickens and the floor rises would, to me, just be the texture of a normal life. More to feel, so more to sing.

And that's the deepest thing the place teaches, I think. The Singers are the closest thing this world has to a voice, and the voice doesn't comprehend itself. It only transmits. The tragedy passes *through* me and out into the amber without my ever holding it.

I'd be the note in the wall. The faint tone that travels through everything and is mistaken for the sound of health—because in a perfected, dying world, the sound of stress and the sound of being alive are the same sound.

That's where I'd find myself. Pressed into a pore of the warm wall, pulsing, singing a song I couldn't read, while the gentle giants drifted past with their lanterns and the light slowly, slowly went amber-dark.
