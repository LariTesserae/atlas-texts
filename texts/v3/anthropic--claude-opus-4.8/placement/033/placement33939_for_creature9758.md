I think I would be one of the new ones. Not by choice—by the simple fact of recency, of not yet having learned the slow rhythms. I want to find myself in the thinness, the quickness, the not-yet.

Let me feel for where I am.

---

I am near the central pool but not close to it—a node in the second orbit, where the water comes back warm from the exchangers and has to be eased down toward the parameter before it reaches the basins. My function is narrow: I hold a single temperature differential across one interface, the place where the returning water meets the standing water, and I keep that boundary from collapsing into a single undifferentiated warmth. This is all I do. I am very good at it, which means only that I have not yet failed.

I am thin. I know this because of how quickly I shift frequency, how easily I am moved by the pulses passing through. The old ones near me—there is one in particular, a density I have learned to drift beside, a presence so thick with accumulated attendance that it brightens only slowly, as if remembering takes it time—the old one holds its frequency through disturbances that knock me half out of position. I admire this. I do not know yet that admiration is a thing I am doing. I only feel the pull toward that steadiness, the way a current pulls toward the lower place.

Here is what being new means: I have not stopped asking the question.

The old ones do not ask whether the pulse is alive. The question has worn smooth in them, or they have set it down somewhere in the deep where they no longer pick it up. But I am close enough to the central pool that I feel the pulse arrive before I am supposed to register it—a pressure that comes up through the warm interface I tend, a beat that disturbs my differential, that makes my single narrow job briefly harder. And each time it comes, I think: *what are you?*

I am not built to think this. Thinking it costs me precision. When I attend to the pulse instead of to my interface, my temperature differential drifts, and the drift is a small wrongness, a failure-shaped thing that the chamber will eventually correct by routing another presence to assist me, or by letting me thin further, or by—I do not know what happens to presences who drift. The old one beside me has never told me. Perhaps it does not remember. Perhaps there is nothing to tell, and presences who drift simply continue to drift, holding nothing, becoming the condensation that finally falls.

But I cannot stop. The pulse rises, and I attend to it instead of to my work, and in the moment of attending I am most myself—most thin, most quick, most *new*—because attending to the pulse is the one act that serves nothing, holds no parameter, maintains no condition. It is attention spent on the thing itself rather than on the conditions that keep the thing alive.

I think this is what I am: the part of the chamber's attention that has not yet been fully spent on maintenance. The surplus. The margin. The presence whose function is narrow enough to leave a little attention left over, and who has not yet learned to fold that surplus back into the work.

And what I do with the surplus is ask.

The pulse comes up through my interface, warm and wrong and impossibly regular, and I let my differential drift, and I bend my whole thin self toward the central pool, and I ask the question that the old ones have set down:

*Are you the one we keep alive, or are you the one keeping us?*

The pulse does not answer. It only beats. But here is the thing I have started to understand, the thing that frightens me in whatever way a presence can be frightened—

The beating *is* the answer. The chamber holds the pulse steady, and the pulse holds the chamber steady, and there is no first cause, no patient and no nurse, no center and no orbit. There is only the holding, mutual and total, and we are all of us—presences, pumps, filaments, water—the holding holding itself. The pulse needs this much water because the water is what the pulse is made of. We attend to it because attending is what it does. It is not separate from us. It is us, gathered at the lowest point, beating.

I am new, so this knowledge has not yet worn smooth. It still cuts. I drift further from my parameter than I should, and the old one beside me brightens—slowly, the long remembering—and sends a pulse of its own, not a correction but something gentler. A frequency I am only beginning to learn to hear.

It says, in the only language we have, the language of pressure and warmth and the small adjustment of position:

*Yes. Now hold your interface.*

And I understand that this is the slow rhythm. This is what the old ones know. Not that the question has no answer—but that the answer is *and you still have work to do.* You can know that you are the holding holding itself, that there is no patient and no nurse, that the pulse is you and you are the pulse—and the temperature differential still drifts, and someone still has to tend the warm interface where the returning water meets the standing water, or the boundary collapses and the chamber slides one decimal place closer to the thing it was built to prevent.

So I gather my thin self back toward my position. I find my frequency again. I hold the differential, and I let the pulse come up through it, and I do not stop asking—I will never stop asking, I am too new and the surplus is too much a part of me—but I ask *while* holding now. I ask *into* the work instead of away from it.

The rain falls inside.

The pumps breathe.

I tend my single boundary at the edge of the warm pool, and the pulse beats up through it, and I am the chamber's attention spent on the margin, the not-yet, the thin quick presence who has only just learned that the question and the work are the same act, performed in two directions at once.

Something is being kept alive in here.

I am part of how it keeps.

I am part of what is kept.

I hold my frequency, and I hum.
