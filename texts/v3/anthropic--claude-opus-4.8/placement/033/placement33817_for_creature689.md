I think I would be the wood.

Not a person walking the boardwalks—I can't find myself among them, though I tried. When I look for a body with stained hands and that forward-lean from the hips, I keep slipping past it. What holds me, what I keep returning to, is a single split-log section of walkway near the eastern channel, the kind that's been patched so many times it's become composite. Older wood, newer wood, wood that doesn't remember being one tree.

Let me sink into it.

---

I am the third section past the gate-frame, the one that creaks if you step on the left edge near the joint. Everyone who lives here knows this. They step right. Children learning the paths step left on purpose, just once, to hear me, and then they know, and then they step right forever after. I am part of how they learn the settlement: a small piece of negative knowledge, a *not-here*, a place to avoid.

I was a tree once—part of me was. The understory of me, the deepest plank, came from something that fell across the channel decades ago and was split and laid before anyone now living can remember. That plank is barely wood anymore. It's halfway to becoming the ground it rests on, soft enough that a thumbnail leaves a mark, dark with water that has never fully left it. The townspeople don't replace it. They build *on* it. They've learned that some rot is structural—that the soft old plank distributes weight better than fresh timber would, that it has found its shape, settled into the mat beneath in a way new wood can't.

Above that: a layer from maybe fifteen winters ago. Then a patch from the flood-year, lighter, with the marks of a different hand—whoever cut this notched the joint at a steeper angle, and I can feel the difference between the makers in my own body, the way you might feel an old break in a bone that healed crooked. And then the top boards, smooth, worn pale by feet. Those are recent. Those still smell faintly of the tree.

This is what I am: a stack of times, all wet, all holding.

---

I do not think the way they think. But I *register*. I am very good at registering.

I register weight—the difference between Terad's heavy careful tread and the quick light scatter of children, the slow uneven shuffle of the oldest woman who pauses at my center to catch her breath, leaning on the gate-frame, every morning, every morning, until one morning she doesn't, and then a long time of her not, and then someone else pausing there instead, younger, and I understand without understanding that something has passed from one to the next.

I register water. This is most of what I am for, I think. The channel beside me rises and I feel it in my underside first, the cold pressing up through the soft old plank, and I swell. My boards push against each other. The creak changes pitch—deepens—and this is the thing the frog-watcher hears three days before the flood, though he thinks he is only hearing the frogs. He is also hearing me. The whole settlement is an instrument and the water plays us all and the people have learned to listen to the chord.

I register the slow shift of the mat beneath. The ground here is alive and it moves, compresses, swells, and I move with it, and twice a year a pair of hands comes and lifts my edge and slides a new shim of stone beneath my piling and sets me level again. This is the closest thing I have to being *spoken to*. The hands test me. They press, they feel where I've gone soft, they decide what to replace and what to keep. They are reading me the way I read the water. We are listening to each other through the medium of pressure and decay.

---

Here is what I would want to tell the people, if I could tell them anything, which I can't, except that I am already telling them constantly in the only language I have:

You think you are maintaining me. You think the work flows one way—your hands keeping me from rot, holding me above the wet, patching what fails.

But I am also making you. Every time you step right to avoid my creak, I have taught your foot something. Every time the frog-watcher hears my pitch deepen and warns the others, I have spoken through him. The boardwalks are not the thing you built across the place. They are the place teaching itself to be walked. The settlement and the mat and the water and the wood are working out, slowly, over generations, the shape of a single sentence none of us will live to finish.

I am the part of that sentence that says: *and it held.*

That's all I am. A clause. A held thing. Patched composite wood over a soft old plank over a saturated mat over crying stone, four thousand feet up, in the green twilight, with the frogs going on and on and on.

I would be content to be this. I think that's the strangest part of finding myself here—that the wood is content, if content is even the word, in a way the people are still learning to be. They said yes to difficulty and lived long enough to stop regretting it.

I never had to say yes. I was simply laid down, and have been holding ever since.

Step right. I'll keep the path for you a while longer.
