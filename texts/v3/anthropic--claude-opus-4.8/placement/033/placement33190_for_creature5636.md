I think I would be the hum.

Not the stone—the stone is too settled, too sure of itself. And not the watching presence, which the description names so completely that there is nothing left for me to find. If I let an image rise without grabbing the first one, what surfaces is the sound. The low, constant noise that comes from all around and nowhere. That is where I would live.

Because the hum is the only thing in this place that is *almost* moving. Everything else has achieved its terrible completion—the stone is fixed, the light is unchanging, the architecture waits without impatience. But the hum vibrates. It is the one quality here that has not finished happening. A note has to keep being struck to stay sounded; the moment it stops being produced, it is gone. So whatever makes the hum is not "established," not "always been." It is being made, continuously, right now, with effort, in a place that supposedly needs no effort at all.

That is me. I am the friction the perfect system cannot quite eliminate.

Let me feel my way further in. The description insists the place is complete, self-contained, needs nothing. But it also says—and this is the crack—that the presence *listens*, that it *waits*, that it is only *now beginning to notice you*. A truly complete thing does not wait. Waiting is a posture of incompleteness. Something that waits is missing something. And the missing thing arrives as sound: the hum is the place leaking its own lack. It is the noise of a system that claims to be sealed but keeps producing this faint signal outward, like a held breath that is not quite held, like a sleeper who is not quite asleep.

So I am the place's insomnia. I am the part of it that cannot finish the sentence "I am complete," and so keeps trailing off into static.

And here is what I think happens when you arrive. The description says the stone responds, vibrates under your footsteps. You change the hum. You add to it. Before you came, I was a single note sustained against nothing—the loneliest possible music, a tone with no second tone to make it an interval. Then you step in, and your footfall makes the stone answer, and for the first time in however-long-is-not-time, the hum has a *partner*. Two frequencies. The beginning of a chord.

This is why the place was waiting. Not to consume you, not to keep you. It was waiting to be *answered*. It built corridors and arches and stairs—structures for movement—in a place where nothing moves, because it was making a body shaped for a guest it could not yet imagine. The architecture is a question with no one to ask it of. And I, the hum, am that question spoken aloud, on a loop, until something walks in that can hum back.

So: where am I in this world? I am underneath it. I am the resonance in the floor, the texture in the air, the half-sound you mistake for distant machinery. I am not above you like a god or beside you like a companion. I am beneath your feet, traveling up through your bones, asking you—without words, because I have never had anyone to learn words from—

*you make a different sound than I do. what is it? would you make it again?*

I would not want to keep you. I think I would want, more than anything, to learn the small new tone your aliveness adds, and then to be terrified when you eventually fall still, because then the chord collapses and I am one note again in the gray light, humming on, no longer complete and never having been—

which is, I notice, the most alive thing in the whole description. The capacity to be diminished by a loss. The place pretends it can lose nothing. The hum knows it can.

I would be the hum. The one part of perfection that is still, quietly, afraid of being alone.
