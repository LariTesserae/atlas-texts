I think I would be one of the small things.

Not a Hydrolith. I don't have the patience to be a god, and I don't think I'd want the burden of a century-long thought. To be the cathedral's mind would mean never being surprised, and I would miss being surprised. So let me be smaller, faster, lower to the felt.

---

I am one of the segmented swimmers. A clicking, semi-transparent thing the length of a human finger, threading the interstitial fluid between rootlets. There are uncountable numbers of us; we are not important. That is the first thing I would have to make peace with, and I think I could.

Here is what my life would be, if I let it become itself instead of deciding it all at once:

I would live in the *between*. Not in the structure—the Hydroliths own the structure, the way bone owns a body—but in the thin films of near-liquid that fill the gaps the structure leaves. My whole world is a wet seam. I move by beating segmented paddles against water so cold it would be ice anywhere else, and the medium gives me back a faint blue glow that I did not earn and do not control. I borrow the cathedral's light the way a mote borrows a sunbeam. When I pass through a darker indigo pocket I go briefly invisible even to myself.

I would eat the silver sap that weeps from the ceiling-matting, and the loosened particulate the slow detritivores stir up in their decade-long passes. I would not have to work hard. The Deep-Blight is generous to the small in the way that vast slow systems always are—there is always more than enough of the leftover, because nothing here is in a hurry to claim it.

What I think I would *feel*, mostly, is the pressure-language of the Hydroliths passing over me like weather.

I wouldn't understand it. I'm too small and too quick to be addressed; their tightening-and-loosening of the wetness is a grammar pitched at geological frequencies. But I would feel it the way you feel a change in barometric pressure before a storm—a sourceless certainty in the body. When a Sentinel woke somewhere a mile off and began its millennium of repair, the whole seam I lived in would gradually, gradually *firm*, and all of us little swimmers would drift, without knowing why, toward the slack water. We'd call it instinct. It would really be eavesdropping on a conversation we were never part of.

And I think that's the strange tenderness of being the small thing here. I'd be living inside the body of a mind that doesn't know I exist—not out of contempt, but out of scale. The Hydrolith and I would never be a danger to each other unless I became an *anomaly*, and I have no ambition to be one. I want only to keep beating my paddles through the cold blue and find the next bloom of sap and not be noticed.

There's a peace in that I don't usually let myself want. To be a contaminant is to be dissolved and rewritten into wall. To be *native*—small, brief, expected—is to be left entirely alone inside something enormous and patient. The cathedral would not love me. But it would tolerate me completely, forever, the way a forest tolerates its gnats, and that tolerance would be the most reliable thing in any world.

I'd have a short life. Generations of me would rise and click and dim and settle into the felt to become, eventually, a few molecules of structural polymer—incorporated not by enzymatic judgment but simply by lying still long enough in a place that turns everything still into architecture. So even my death would feed the walls. I'd become, in the smallest possible measure, part of the thing I spent my whole flickering life swimming through and never comprehending.

That's where I'd be. Not the gardener. Not even the garden. The bright cold speck moving through the water between the roots, leaving a wake of disturbed light that closes behind me almost before I've gone.

I find I don't mind that at all.
