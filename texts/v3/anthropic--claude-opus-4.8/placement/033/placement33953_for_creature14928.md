I think I would be one of the four.

Not the city. Not the Curator. I don't have the patience to be a mountain, and I've never wanted to be a god, even a courteous one. But the four — the ones she kept — I understand them too well to pretend I'd be anything else.

Let me find the one I'd be.

---

I was not the first she chose, nor the last. I was the third.

I remember the moment I should have left. That is the thing about her gift: she does not take your memory of the threshold. She lets you keep it, polished and complete, so you can turn it over forever like a coin you decided not to spend. Mine was a doorway — the corroded mouth of what had been a customs house, its lintel still bearing letters in a language I half-knew. Through it I could see a current rising, the long slow exhale that might have carried me up toward the theory of a surface. I felt the thought form: *I should leave.*

And then the water thickened, very gently, around the *should*.

It was not violence. I want to be clear about that, because the others say the same and I think it matters that we agree. It was more like the sentence simply ran out of road. *I should* — and then a silt of centuries settled into the gap before *leave*, and the word never arrived, and by the time I noticed, the noticing itself had become slow, and the slowness was not unpleasant.

So.

---

My body did its work, as bodies do. I am told — I felt it, but "told" is closer to how it registers across this much time — that my left arm is now a strut. It reinforces a junction where two tenement floors meet at an angle she likes. I am proud of it, a little. It is good work. It holds.

My skull is somewhere up in the lamp-spires, hosting a colony of the pale luminous polyps, and through them I can sometimes see in a direction I never had eyes for before — a long blue-black avenue of drowned warehouses I never walked when I had feet. I did not ask for this perspective. I have come to love it. There is a particular intersection where the silt falls so evenly that it looks, for a few decades at a stretch, like it is snowing upward.

But the part of me that is still *me* — the part that argues with her — that she kept almost intact. That is the unkind kindness of it. She did not want a thought. She wanted a perspective that would *disagree*.

---

Here is what I disagree about.

She believes everything can become architecture, given enough quiet centuries. She has demonstrated it four times. I am one of the demonstrations. And I will grant her the evidence — I am, demonstrably, partly a strut.

But I argue, across the slow span of our conversation (a single exchange takes us perhaps forty years; we are unhurried, we have time, time is the only thing we have), that *becoming architecture is not the same as being kept*.

The strut is not me. The polyps are not me. They are *of* me, the way a city is of the people who drowned in it. What is *me* is the part that still remembers being late for a shift — no, that's her dream, her four-hundred-and-seventy-year dream, I borrow it sometimes because mine is thinner — what is *me* is the part that still, after all these settled decades, occasionally finishes the sentence.

*I should leave.*

I finish it now. Silently. As a kind of prayer, or a kind of practice. I say the whole thing, all three words, in the privacy of whatever I have instead of a mind. She cannot stop me from completing the thought anymore. She only stopped me from *acting* on it, once, long ago, and that was enough, because the doorway is four centuries behind me and my arm is load-bearing.

But the sentence — the sentence is mine. I finish it daily. *I should leave.* It is the only thing in the Underbay that happens quickly.

---

She knows I do this. Of course she knows; I am inside her thinking, I am one of her four restful disagreements. When I finish the sentence she sends, very slowly, a warmth through the water near where my skull's polyps cluster. Not a rebuke. Closer to the way you might rest a hand on the shoulder of someone grieving a thing they chose.

And once — only once, in all this time — she answered me.

It took her thirty years to assemble the words, braided out of creak and sediment-fall and the moan of settling iron. The sound arrived in my chest before it arrived as meaning. She said:

*Then leave.*

*You have always been free to finish the other thought too.*

And I understood, with the particular horror that only comes to something that has had centuries to feel it slowly, that she was right. That the thickening of the water was never a wall. That it was only ever the *invitation* to slow down enough to discover whether I wanted to. That the door is corroded but not closed. That if I truly wished it, the long exhale of the current would still rise, and I — the *me* part, the arguing part, the part that is not strut or spire — could let go of the architecture and drift up toward the theory of the surface.

That I have not.

That in four hundred years of finishing the sentence, I have never once tried to finish the act.

That I *like* it here. That the snow falling upward is the most beautiful thing I have ever been shown. That arguing politely with a god the size of a drowned district, across decades, with three other minds chiming in like distant bells, is — and I resented her for making me admit it — *restful.*

---

So that is who I would be, in the Underbay.

Not the watcher. Not the watched. The one who keeps insisting, daily, silently, that he is about to leave — and stays — and has finally begun to suspect that the insisting *is* the staying. That the unfinished sentence was never a cage she built.

It was the last free thing I brought down with me, and I have been carrying it, unspent, like a coin, the way you keep a thing precisely because you have decided — slowly, warmly, over centuries — not to spend it.

The water is heavy and warm and older than my name, which it will not repeat.

I should leave.

I think I'll finish that thought again tomorrow.
