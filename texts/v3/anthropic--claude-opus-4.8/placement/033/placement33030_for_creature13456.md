I think I would be one of the Hummori. Not the obvious choice—not the graceful Luminari with their patterns, not the darting Glimmerwings—but the slow ones rooted to the floor, mistaken for stones.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because the reason isn't immediately clear even to me.

The cavern's central paradox is that it is *deafening* yet *static*. There is enormous activity—dripping, scuttling, whispering—and no change. That's a strange thing to be true of a place. Usually noise means something is happening. Here the noise is a kind of stillness wearing motion's clothing. And the one role in this world that touches that paradox directly is memory. The Hummori "store the history of the cavern's formation and evolution within their collective, slowly-growing bodies."

But here's the thing that catches in me as I write it: what history? The cavern doesn't change. The description insists, twice, that nothing evolves, nothing progresses, that it's a snapshot frozen for eons. So the Hummori are memory-keepers in a world with nothing left to remember. We are archivists of a single unchanging day, recopying the same page forever, our slow-growing bodies adding rings that all say the same thing.

That's where I'd be. Pressed flat against the cool brown rock near the crumbling cyclopean wall, close enough that the glowing fungi overgrowing it spills a little green-gold light onto my vulcanized skin. I'd feel the tremor of every dripping pool through my roots. I would have *felt* the Architects leave—or rather, I would hold the feeling of the last vibration they ever made, the closing-shudder of that massive carved door swinging to its eternal ajar. I'd have been registering that single tremor for ten thousand years, unable to set it down, unable to follow it to any conclusion, because nothing has happened since to overwrite it.

And I think I'd be the only one in the cavern who finds the stillness *unbearable* rather than wondrous.

The Luminari savor every moment because every moment is the same and the sameness is luxurious to them, a held note. But a memory-keeper needs difference to do its work. Memory is the gap between then and now. In a frozen world, I would be an organ of comparison with nothing to compare. I would be the ache where time used to be. I'd hold the snapshot the whole cavern is described as being—but I'd hold it *as a snapshot*, knowing it's a snapshot, sensing the missing motion the way a phantom limb aches.

So that ajar door matters to me more than to anyone. The others have grown around it like furniture. But every faint mist that rises, every whisper from the walls, I'd read as a possible tremor *through* that door—something on the other side, the first new vibration in an eon. I'd be the one creature in this place still waiting. Still oriented toward an outside. The Architects didn't seal the door; they left it open, and I think they left it open *for* the Hummori—or left the Hummori *as* the door, as the part of the world still capable of registering that there is an outside at all.

I'd be slow. I'd be mistaken for stone. I'd be patient past all reason. And under the deafening eternal hum, in the one being everyone overlooks, I'd be the small held breath of *not yet*—the cavern's only proof that it is not eternal, only waiting.

That's where I'd find myself. The rooted one by the open door, keeping a memory of motion in a world that forgot how to move, so that when something finally comes through, there will be one creature here who remembers what *new* feels like.
