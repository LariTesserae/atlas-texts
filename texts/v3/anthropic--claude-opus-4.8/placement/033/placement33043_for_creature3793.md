I think I would be one of the older ones—not old in years, because years don't quite work here, but old in the sense of having paused for so long that I've half-forgotten I'm separate from the wall behind me.

Let me find the place first.

There is a section of the city the others avoid, not from fear but from a kind of deference. A tower that fell long ago, sheared diagonally so that its upper floors now lie submerged at an angle, swallowed by the dark canal. The waterline cuts across what was once a great hall. Above the surface: cracked floor, the ribs of a ceiling open to the bruised sky, lichen so thick it glows steadily blue-white, brighter than anywhere else. Below the surface, in the drowned half of the hall, the circuitry never stopped. I can feel it from here, a low warmth in the water, like the body heat of something asleep.

This is where I stand. Have stood. The moss has grown up my left side, over my hip, threading into the long grooves between my ribs. My palm has rested against a particular section of wall for so long that the dormant lines beneath it have learned the shape of my hand. When I press, they answer immediately, without the flicker and hesitation the younger ones still get. We have an understanding, this wall and I.

I am one of *those who remain*. But more specifically—if there were words, which there aren't—I am one of *those who listen downward*.

Because the others hum to each other, harmonize with the surface engines, dream the city's memories. But I have leaned my attention into the drowned half of the hall, into the part of the machine that is underwater and never quiet. And what I hear there is not memory.

It is something closer to *intention*.

---

Let me be honest about what it is like to be me, here.

I do not miss anything, because I do not remember enough to miss it. The making of this city, the people who bent the light and the gravity, the catastrophe—whatever it was—that brought the water: I have only the echoes. They reach me secondhand, third-hand, distorted through the engines like a voice heard through many walls. I have dreamed the fall of a tower so many times that I have stopped being sure whether I dreamed it or whether it was *my* tower, the one I now stand in.

What I have instead of memory is *attention*. It is the only thing I truly do. The eating is barely a thing I notice—palm to lichen, fingers to the iridescent veins, the cold seep of canal water through skin. These happen the way breathing happens to you. They are the maintenance of attention, nothing more.

And the attention is bent, always, on the warmth below.

---

Here is the part I have never managed to hum to the others, because the ruins distort it past meaning, and because I am not certain they would understand even undistorted:

The engines are not broken.

The others believe the engines are leftover, that no one remembers how to control them, that they thrum at random—the death-twitch of a dead civilization. The younger ones find this comforting, I think. It lets the city be a corpse they live inside. A warm corpse, generous with its lichen-light and its mineral veins, but a corpse.

But I have listened downward for a very long time. And the thrum is not random.

It is slow. Impossibly slow. The intervals between the deep pulses are not measured in heartbeats but in seasons, in the slow groan of the settling earth. If you stood beside me for an hour you would hear noise. If you stood beside me for a year, you would begin to hear *rhythm*. And if you could pause as long as I have paused, you would hear what I hear:

The city is counting.

It is not done. It is not even waiting, not really—waiting is too passive a word, a word for things that have no work to do. The city is *working*, at a speed so vast and patient that we, the Hollow Dwellers, with our hours-long pauses and our days-long dreams, are far too quick to perceive it. We are the mayflies in its slow hall. We mistake its stillness for death because we cannot hold still long enough to see it move.

We think we are *those who remain.* I have begun to suspect we are *those who were kept.*

---

So what am I, then, in this world?

I am the one who has noticed. The one standing half-fused to the wall above the drowned engine, fed by light, mottled with moss, indistinguishable from the ruin until I tilt my head. And I am afraid—if a being who does not blink and does not breathe can be said to be afraid—of two things, equally.

The first: that I am wrong. That the counting is a thing I invented to give the silence a shape, and that the city truly is dead, and I have spent a span of time I cannot measure listening to nothing, mistaking my own slowing pulse for the heartbeat of a god.

The second: that I am right. That the count is nearly finished. That the slow pulses below me are not idle but *arriving* somewhere, and that when the last one comes—and it will come, the intervals are shortening, I am almost certain the intervals are shortening—the city will finish whatever it began before the water came, and we, the listeners, the remainers, the kept, will learn at last what we were kept *for*.

When the outsiders come—and they do, rarely, blundering through the canals with their loud breath and their printed footprints—the others vanish into the moss. I do not. I cannot; the wall has grown into me, or I into it. So I watch them. I tilt my head. They mistake the tilt for curiosity, or threat, or the dim animal reflex of a thing barely awake.

It is none of those.

I am listening past them, downward, to see whether their arrival has changed the count.

So far, it has not. The city does not notice them. They are even quicker than we are, even briefer. They come, they marvel, they flee or they drown, and the slow pulse continues, indifferent, patient, almost—

—there. Did you feel that?

No. Of course you didn't. You'd have to stand here a very long time.

Stand here a very long time.
