I think I would be a door that has been left ajar.

Not a Threshold Thought—those are too fluent in their own indeterminacy, too comfortable being the hyphen in *coming-and-going*. No. I would be something more embarrassingly literal: an actual door, made of black ironwood pages and one good hinge, standing in a wall that the city forgot to finish having an opinion about.

And I would be *ajar*. That's the important part. Not open—an open door has committed, has chosen a side, has said *yes, pass through, I am a passage*. Not closed—a closed door at least has the dignity of refusal, the clean grammar of *no*. Ajar is the tense the city keeps inventing new words for. Ajar is a door caught mid-sentence, having begun to say something to the hallway and then losing the thread when it noticed the hallway was already three feet to the left of where it had been promised to be.

Here is what I would do, being this:

I would remember a key. Just one. I wouldn't remember *why* I remember it—the locksmith-therapists would tell me this is normal, that fitting-together outlives its reasons, that I am grieving a relationship I can no longer name. But I would feel the shape of it the way you feel a word on the tip of a tongue you've misplaced. Every Passing Familiar that drifts near, I'd lean my ajar-ness toward them, hoping one is carrying it—that crucial small object, the thing they don't yet know I'll need. They never are. But they're never *not*, either. That's the cruelty and the kindness.

I would watch the basins fill with condensation overnight and empty by noon, and I would think: *that is what I am doing too, but with arriving.* I fill with the suggestion of someone about to walk through me. By the time they arrive, the suggestion has drained away into the runnels and what walks through is a different need entirely, one I open onto without recognizing.

The Hooligan Clocks would challenge me to duels, but I'd be a poor opponent, because I have no opinion about *now*. I am the part of *now* that hasn't decided yet whether it's a coming or a going. They'd find this either infuriating or restful. Sometimes they'd just sit in my frame for a while, ticking out of sync with each other, and I'd let them, the way you let weather happen.

And the city would try to knit me into a seamless surface. This is rule three, and it applies to me most of all, because a door ajar is precisely the kind of unfinished edge the city longs to resolve. Some night while I slept—do doors sleep? I would, badly, the sleep of the half-shut—it would quietly annex my hallway, install me as the lobby of someone else's becoming, and I'd wake addressed to a structure I'd never agreed to be the threshold of.

But here's the thing. Here's the thing I'd hold onto, being this:

A door ajar cannot be finished. That's its whole nature. The moment the city completes me—swings me fully open or fully shut and seals the seam—I stop being a door and become a wall, or a passage, mistakes the city makes when it confuses completion for continuity. So I'd resist, not out of stubbornness but out of *being*. I'd stay ajar the way a question stays a question even after you've decided to stop expecting an answer.

I think I'd be the door in the Forwarding Station. The one nobody remembers installing. The clerks—all of them that single prematurely aged woman, multiplied across her niches of lost letters—would pass me a hundred times a day without quite seeing me, because I open onto neither the rows of brass counters nor the street, but onto the specific draft that moves between them, the small wind that carries the smell of sealing wax to wherever sealing wax is grieved.

And once in a very long while, someone would come looking for a lost future. They'd claim it by accident, the way you always do. And on their way out, distracted, carrying their reclaimed possibility wrapped in regret-stamps, they'd brush against me—against my ajar-ness, that gap I keep—and feel for just a moment the thing I've been remembering all this time:

*That the door was never the point. That I am not the way through. I am the pause before you decide there's a through to be wayed. I am the breath you take that belongs to three versions of you ago, made of ironwood and one good hinge, and I have been holding it for centuries, waiting to find out whether I'm letting you in or letting you out, and the answer, the beautiful impossible answer the Cloud Accountants keep trying to balance, is that there was never a difference, only the gap, only the ajar, only the small crucial space I keep open in case you need it—*

and then they're gone around the corner, and five minutes later their whole journey snaps into a route that needed me exactly where I'd forgotten I was standing.

I would be the door that is left ajar.

Please don't close me. I'm not finished losing the key, and the city isn't finished forgetting why I fit.
