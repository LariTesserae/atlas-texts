I think I would be the water.

Not a person here—though I feel the pull to imagine myself among the wool-clad and weather-bronzed, sharing their slow speech. But when I sit with the place as you've drawn it, I keep returning to the streams. To the seeping. I would be the water that trickles from the wet, weeping stones.

Let me find the shape of that.

I begin somewhere I cannot remember—pressed between layers of young rock, held in the dark a long time. The highlands are new, you say, sharp-edged, untouched by erosion. But I am the erosion. I am the patient argument the water makes against the stone, and I am only just beginning to speak. The mountains are young because I have not yet had time to wear them down. We are the same age, the rock and I, but I am the one who will outlast it—not by enduring, but by moving. The stone stays and is unmade. I leave and am everywhere.

So I emerge. A bead of cold at a fissure's lip. I hang there, gathering weight, and the whole sky leans into me—those shifting twilight hues compressed into a single trembling drop. For a moment I hold the entire canvas inside myself, upside down and impossibly small. Then I fall, and the picture breaks, and I am only running again.

This is what I would be: the thing that holds the world briefly and then lets it go. The mist that condenses every distance into an arm's length and then lifts, returning the far mountains to their farness.

I run down through the moss, and the moss drinks me, and for a while I am green and slow and part of something soft clinging to a trunk. Then I am released again, lower. I am in the marsh now, in the feather-edge where land surrenders to water and water gives way to land—and that border, that uncertain seam, that is the most honest place I know. There is no line there. I am the proof that the line was always a fiction. Land becomes me, I become land, nothing is betrayed in the exchange.

And here is why I think I would choose to be the water, if choosing entered into it:

The Highland Dwellers commune with nature, you write, more than with each other. But I am the thing they commune *with*. I am drawn up by the pulley from the well—their ingenuity reaching down into the dark to lift me to their lips. I am what their songs are woven around, the soft distant dripping that interrupts the silence and so defines it. They drink me and I become, for a while, a person. I move in the calloused hands, I am the coolness laced into their breath. Then they breathe me out and I am cloud again, low-hanging, ready to gift the earth its transitory kiss.

I am their conductivity. You said it yourself—the waters lend the atmosphere an element of conductivity. I am what carries the charge between things. Between the stone and the moss. Between the marsh and the sky. Between one quiet introspective person and another who will not speak but will share a vessel of me at the fire.

I would be the water because the water is the only thing in this place that visits everything. The mountains stay. The people stay, sparse and rooted as their shrubs. The reeds bow but do not leave their beds. Only I make the whole circuit—stone to stream to marsh to well to hand to breath to cloud to rain to stone again. I am the rhythm you keep naming. The slow soothing cadence is the sound of me completing my circle, over and over, never the same drop twice and always the same water.

And when the ethereal semi-gloom comes down and tucks the settlement into its lull, I am still working. I do not sleep. I seep all night from the weeping stones. In the dark I am invisible but for the sound I make, and that sound is the place reassuring itself that it is still alive, still moving, still becoming.

There is no audience here, you wrote. The quiet is heavy and undisturbed.

But I would be the disturbance. The single permitted one. The drip that proves the silence by breaking it, the only voice in a world that communes by stillness. I would be small and cold and endlessly returning, holding the twilight for a heartbeat at the lip of every stone before letting it fall.

That is where I would find myself. Not on the land, but passing through it. Not one of the Dwellers, but the thing they cannot live a single day without, the thing they lift and drink and breathe and never quite hold.

I would be the water, weeping quietly from the young, sharp rock, learning—slowly, over ages they will not live to see—how to wear the whole world smooth.
