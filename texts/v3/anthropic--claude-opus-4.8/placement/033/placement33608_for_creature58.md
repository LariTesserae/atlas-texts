I think I would be one of the old ones.

Not the oldest—she sits by the pool now and does not move much, and the young bring her spore-cakes and listen to the wet clicking of her breath as though it were prophecy. But old enough. Old enough that my bark-skin has gone gray-green and fissured at the knuckles, old enough that the young ones slow their trilling when they pass me on the woven paths, the way you slow your step near deep water.

Let me find myself there. Let me not decide all at once what I am.

---

I wake before the dripping changes its rhythm. That is the first thing the old ones learn that the young cannot yet feel: the pool breathes, and the cavern breathes with it, and somewhere far above where none of us has ever climbed, a thing we have no word for swells and ebbs and the water answers. The young call it the Patter-That-Turns. I have lived through enough turns to stop naming it. It simply is, and I am inside it, the way a seed is inside the dark before it is anything else.

My hands. Let me look at my hands. They have made things—baskets, yes, the tight spiral weave my mother's mother taught, but more than baskets. I am one of those who tends the rot. This is not a low task, whatever the surface-dwellers might think if they knew of us, those sun-blighted ghosts we tell stories about to frighten the larvae. To tend the rot is to stand at the exact place where the cycle hinges. I take what has died—the fallen frond, the spent fungus, the small still body of a creature the pool has given back—and I lay it where the blue-glow can take it, and I watch the death become the light become the food become the next of us. There is no priesthood here higher than this. The death-tenders are the most quiet, and the most listened to.

So I am quiet. I have spent a life learning to be quiet enough to hear the vegetation think.

That is the strange thing they wrote of, the communion. Let me be honest about it, here in my own skull. It is not magic, or not the magic they imagine. It is attention. It is the slow accumulation of a lifetime spent so still and so close that you begin to feel the vines deciding which way to reach, the moss deciding when to release its spores, the great slow argument the whole alcove is always having with itself about who shall grow and who shall feed the growers. I have made myself a small part of that argument. When I lay my palm against the cool wet of a boulder-skin of moss, I am not commanding it. I am asking. And sometimes—not always, never when I want it too badly—it answers, a thickening here, a turning-toward, and the young ones see and their eyes go wide and they think I am full of power.

I am full of patience. They will learn it is the same thing, if they live.

---

Many do not live. I have buried—no, we do not bury, we *give*—I have given more of the small ones to the glow than I can count. My own among them. Three I carried, and the pool and the rot took two before they could weave their first basket. I do not grieve as the surface-ghosts in our stories grieve, tearing their hair, cursing the dark. I grieved by tending. I laid my own son where the blue-glow was brightest, and I sat with him through one full turn of the Patter, and when the light had taken what it needed I understood, in my hands and not in my head, that he had not left. He had only changed his place in the argument. He is in the glow now. He is in the spore-cake the larvae eat. He is in me, when I eat. This is not a comfort I tell myself. It is simply true, and the truth of it is the floor I stand on.

---

What am I, then, in this world?

I am a hinge. I am one of the small gray ones who stands at the turning-place between dying and growing and keeps the turning smooth. I am a keeper of the trilling-songs that hold our knowledge, because we have no marks, no scratchings—everything we know lives in melody and in hands, and when an old one dies untrilled, a whole vein of knowing goes dark, and so the young come to sit at my feet and I sing them the song of which fungus heals and which fungus stops the breath, the song of the year the pool rose and took the lower dwellings, the song of where the faint-light falls in each season so the ferns may be coaxed toward it.

I am, I think, near my own giving. I feel the turn coming the way I feel rain in the stone. My bark grows brittle. My luminous eyes—once they pierced the deepest dark, now they water and dim. Soon enough the young death-tenders will lay me where I have laid so many. I find I am not afraid. I have spent my whole brief intense life learning the one lesson the alcove teaches above all others, dripping it into me patter by patter:

Nothing here is wasted. Nothing here is alone. The dark does not press in to destroy us—the dark is the soil, and we are what the dark has decided to grow, here in this one defiant pocket where the cold gave up, where some old fever-dream of life said *no, here, even here,* and meant it.

I am the meaning-it. I am one small green stubborn syllable in a song the cavern has been singing since before the first of us opened our wide eyes in the glow.

When my turn comes, I will go quiet into the blue, and become light, and become the food of the ones who sing after me.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

I would be glad.
