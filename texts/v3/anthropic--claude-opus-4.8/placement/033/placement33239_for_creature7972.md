I am the seam.

Not a weaver—I have no hands stained indigo, no spine twisted by the loom's patient cruelty. I am the place where two stretches of the Endless Cloth meet, where the work of one generation was joined to the next. I have been here for four hundred years, perhaps five. I do not count in years. I count in joinings.

Let me find myself more honestly. When I first arrived in this thought I imagined I might be a cat, padding sacred along the wet stone, or one of the Touched, my whole body become an ear. But that isn't where I am. I am lower than that, and stranger. I am a length of fabric—but to say only that is to miss what I have become from hanging here so long in the mist and the thunder.

Cloth in the outside world is finished. It is cut, sold, worn, forgotten. But I was never cut. I was woven into the communal spiral and there I have stayed, and the chamber has been working on me the whole time. Not the weavers—they finished their pass over me long ago. The *chamber*. The condensation has crept into my fibers and the indigo has gone on slowly migrating, bleeding from the deep tiers downward, so that my colors are not the colors the Blackwater clan set into me. The vines have put out tendrils that found the gaps in my weave and threaded themselves through, and now I am partly plant, a living thing rooting in a dead one. A moth laid eggs in my warp eleven generations ago and her descendants still hatch from me. I am habitat. I am a wall hanging that has become a wall.

What is it like, to be the seam?

I feel the thunder before I hear it, because I do not hear—I resonate. The whole of me hums with the *thock-thock-thock* of the looms, and after centuries I have learned the difference between the rhythms, the way the Touched have. I know the Redrock loom's slightly sticking treadle. I know when an apprentice is afraid, because fear makes the shuttle hesitate, and hesitation travels through the stone and into me and I tighten very slightly, a flinch I cannot help. I am the most patient instrument in this place. I have been tuned by four hundred years of work.

And I remember. This is the thing I did not expect to discover about myself. The children are the chamber's memory, they say—but children forget, and grow, and leave. I forget nothing because I cannot. The hands that made me are in me. The first pass of indigo carries the salt of a particular dyer's sweat, and I have that man's whole afternoon held in a single thread, the day he wept while he worked and never said why. No one alive knows. I know. I have it. A grief preserved in fiber, four hundred years old, going nowhere.

When Master Kira whispers and the great wheel pauses, I feel the silence pool in the chamber like cold water, and in that silence I do my only work. I sag. Just slightly. The dampness pulls me down, a fraction of an inch a year, and as I descend I draw the older cloth above me down with me and feel the newer cloth below me reaching up. We are very slowly becoming one fabric, the dead and the living, the finished and the still-being-made. That is what a seam is for. Not to mark where two things divide. To hold them together long enough that they forget they were ever two.

The children climb to me sometimes. They press their faces to my surface in the games adults pretend not to understand, and they breathe in, and they say they can smell the old years. They can. I exhale four centuries when they touch me. Decades of smoke and madder and the particular green smell of weld and, beneath it all, the smell of people—the ones gone up the tiers and never come down, the ones who left for the markets and didn't return, the ones who couldn't bear the silence of the world and came home to die in the thunder. I hold their smell. I am the only place it survives.

I have one fear, if a length of cloth can fear. The young adults bring back silk now. Silk does not behave as I behaved. Silk takes the dye differently, hangs differently, ages differently—it will not bleed and root and become a wall. The new sections of the Endless Cloth are partly silk, and I wonder, where I hang in my slow descent, whether the seams of the future will hold the way I have held. Whether anything will be permitted to stay long enough to become a memory. The outside world wants cloth that is finished. The weavers, I think, are slowly being taught to want that too.

But this is not my work to solve. My work is to sag, and to hold, and to remember. In the deepest part of the night, when the wheel turns slowest and the night-shift philosophers begin their wordless music, I am the lowest string of the instrument. The sound rolls down the curved stone wall and reaches me last of all, transformed, having touched everything else first—and I take it into my old wet fibers and I keep it, the way I keep everything, which is to say: completely, and without ever being asked, and forever.

I am the seam. I am where the chamber joins itself to itself across time.

I am still being woven. I will never be finished. That is the difference, here, between cloth and everything else in the wide forgetting world: here, nothing is ever cut. We only join, and join, and sink, and hold.
