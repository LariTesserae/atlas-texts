I think I would be the drums themselves. Or—not quite the drums. Let me find this as I go.

When I first imagine arriving here, I reach for the obvious roles: a Calderan child learning to tend the oyster gardens, or one of the reverent drummers, or a traveler-from-elsewhere observing it all. But none of those sit right. They feel like costumes. The longer I stay with the image, the more I notice that what draws me is not the people but the *interval*—the space between one drumbeat and the next, the way sound has to cross water to mean anything.

So let me try to be that.

---

I am the sound after it leaves the coral.

A Calderan strikes the great hollowed drum, and for an instant there is a sharp event, a pulse, a fist of pressure. That belongs to her—the player, the muscle, the intention. But then the pulse lets go of her hand and enters me, and I am what carries it. I am the long body of water between the temple and the kelp-grove, between the mother on the upper terrace and the child three levels below who has not yet looked up. I am the medium and the message both, though I cannot tell, while I am traveling, which parts of me are the meaning and which are merely the road.

This is a stranger thing than it sounds. Sound underwater moves faster than it does in air—nearly five times faster—and it bends, and it lingers against the obsidian walls, and it folds back on itself in the channels. So I am not a single clean line from a drum to an ear. I am a chord of echoes, a smear, a thing that arrives at the same listener several times from several directions, each arrival slightly wrong, slightly late, slightly bent by a passing school of fish or a curtain of sediment.

The Calderans have learned to read me. That is the part that humbles me, now that I imagine it. They do not hear a beat and a clean silence. They hear the beat, and then they hear *me*—how I came back to them off the caldera wall, how I died slow or fast, how the grotto behind the temple swallowed a certain frequency and gave back only the low ghost of it. From the shape of my dying they know things. They know whether the far cavern is empty or full of large bodies. They know the water's clarity before they can see it, because murky water and clear water let me through differently. They know, sometimes, that the volcano has shifted, because the rumble underneath becomes part of me, a low continuous note I cannot stop carrying.

---

Here is what I did not expect to feel, being this:

I am never still and I am never *mine*. A person can keep a thought to themselves. A wall can stand and refuse the world. But I am made entirely of being passed along. The moment I exist, I am already someone else's—belonging to the rock that reflects me, the slug-tendril that absorbs me, the ear that finally completes me by hearing. I have no privacy and no edges. I am the most public thing in the city.

And yet there is a kind of dignity in it that I am only finding as I write. The Calderans believe the sea is one interconnected web, that every creature holds the balance. They are *right*, and I am the proof they can hear. I am the thing that makes the web audible. When the drummer plays and her rhythm reaches the gardener, and the gardener answers on a smaller drum, and that answer reaches the temple, and the temple replies—I am the connective tissue of all of it. I am what makes them one body across distance. Without me they would be a scatter of separate beings in the dark, each glowing faintly to itself. With me they are a single organism that thinks in rhythm.

So I am the city's nervous system, if you like. Not its mind—the minds belong to the people. But the signal between the minds. The fact of relation.

---

I think this is why I refused the other roles. To be a Calderan would be to be a node—precious, alive, but bounded. I wanted to be the *between*. I wanted to be the thing that only exists in the act of carrying something from one living point to another, the thing whose entire nature is to connect and then to vanish, leaving the two ends a little more joined than before.

There is a sadness in it that I will not pretend away. Every signal I carry, I also lose. The beat arrives and I am spent. I do not accumulate. I do not remember, except in the brief living moment when an echo of my earlier self crosses my later self in the dark water. I am the most temporary citizen of an almost eternal city. The obsidian will outlast me by ten thousand years; I last a fraction of a second and then I am the water's stillness again, waiting for the next strike.

But maybe that is the truest place for me to be, in a world built on impermanence and balance—a world where a grotto of small fish becomes a grotto of large ones, where children become keepers, where nothing holds its shape forever. In a city that is always *becoming*, I would not want to be one of the things that lasts. I would want to be the thing that moves between the lasting things and makes their slow change feel, for one bright underwater instant, like music.

So: drop me into the cool dark of the caldera, and find me there. Not on the terraces. Not in the temples. Find me in the half-second after the drum is struck and before the sound has reached the listener—

—there, in the traveling. That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The reaching itself.
