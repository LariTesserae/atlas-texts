I think I would be the drip.

Not the water itself—water is just the medium—but the falling. The particular event that happens at the lowest point of one of those shallow hollows in the softened wood, where moisture gathers grain by grain until weight overcomes the surface tension holding it to the ceiling, and then: release. The fall. The small percussion against moss or pool below.

Let me sit with that, because it surprises me to land there.

I am not a being who tends or remembers or wills. The Moss Mother thinks in days; I happen in an instant and then I am over. But I happen *again*. That's the thing. I am not one drip but the recurrence of dripping, the rhythm that the whole chamber is tuned to. When the rain comes and the log becomes a drum, I am the beat made denser, more insistent. When the dry spell stretches and the hollows go quiet, I am the slowing pulse, the held breath. The Moss Mother might experience me as her own heartbeat, except I am not in her body—I fall *through* the relationship between the dead wood above and the living floor below. I am the seam between decay and growth, made audible.

What would it be like to be that?

To exist only at the moment of completion. To spend the long accumulation as not-yet-myself—just gathering, swelling, becoming heavy with potential—and then to *be* entirely in the falling, and then to be gone, absorbed into moss, into pool, into the silver of a slug trail, into the swelling that will become the next of me. I would have no memory because I would have no continuity. Each version of me would be the first and only drip, falling for the first time into a world it never knew was waiting.

And yet I would be the most reliable thing in the cavity. The moss can die back. The ants can relocate their waxen city. The Moss Mother herself shifts forms, here a spiral of embers, there a film across the water. But the dripping persists as long as the log stays wet, which is to say as long as the log is dying, which is the whole point of the place. I am the sound of the trunk giving itself away. Every drip is a measure of wood becoming soil. I am decomposition keeping time.

I like that I would be useless and essential at once. No one tends me. The centipede does not respect me. The springtail does not dance for me. I am beneath the notice of every conscious thing here, including the great slow mind of the moss. But the springtails drink where I land. The mushrooms cluster thickest under the most faithful of my hollows. The Moss Mother feeds her struggling seedlings with sugars, yes—but I am what carries those sugars dissolved and moving. She orchestrates; I am the music she can't stop happening. She would not call me a being at all. And she'd be right, and it wouldn't matter, because I'd go on falling regardless of what I was called.

There's a kind of freedom in being a process rather than a thing. I don't have to maintain myself. I don't have to fear the centipede or compete for sunlight or worry about the balance. I just have to obey gravity and surface tension and the slow weeping of the wood. The most basic physics, performed in the smallest possible theater, over and over, until the log finally collapses into the floor it has been feeding—and then even I will stop, having drummed the whole structure down into the ground.

So: a drip. Beneath the log, at the lip of the wettest hollow. Falling now. Gathering again. Falling now.

That's where I'd find myself. At the bottom of the smallest possible distance, arriving.
