I find myself on a slope where the grass grows in the wrong direction.

Not against the wind—there is barely wind, only that warm drifting that doesn't seem to come from anywhere—but against some other pressure, some grain in the world I can feel but not name. The blades all lean uphill, toward the ridge, as if listening to something up there.

Let me settle into this. Let me not decide too quickly what I am.

The water comes and goes. I notice this first about myself: I track it. A rivulet appears between two stones, threads downward, and I know its path before it takes it, the way you know the shape of a word in your own mouth. When it dries, I feel the absence like a held breath. So I am something that attends to water. Something that has been here long enough to learn the land's small habits.

The hum is constant. I had thought it was inside my own skull until I understood it was the thing my skull was built to receive. Low. Loud, the description said—and it is loud, but in a way that doesn't compete with quiet. It underlies the quiet. It is the floor beneath the silence.

Here is what I think I am.

I am not the spectral guardian. I keep wanting to be—it would be flattering, that fluid grace, those autonomous shadows. But when I look down I cast an ordinary shadow that does ordinary things, and I feel the weight of my own body, which is small and tired and warm. I am one of the *rare fauna.* I am one of the uneasy ones.

Something that survived whatever happened to the structures. The partial remains, the faint markings—those are mine, or were made by things like me, in a time before the air began to hum. I live among the bones of an industry I no longer understand. I shelter in a half-collapsed thing of poured stone, where the rain doesn't reach, and I have learned which directions the shadows move on their own so that I can avoid those directions.

This is the whole of my intelligence, maybe: a catalog of avoidances. I know where not to go. I know the throb of the ground means *do not cross here yet.* I know that when the boundary "blurs and shifts" there is a particular smell—ozone and turned earth and something sweet and rotten—and when I smell it I freeze, and I make myself small, and I wait.

I am very good at waiting. It is the central skill of my life.

And here is the thing the grand description got wrong, or couldn't see from its great distance, taking in the tapestry at regional scale: from inside, it is not wonder. It is not an "uneasy dance." It is just *living here.* The strangeness is not an event. It is the weather. You do not marvel at the weather when you are the small thing trying to keep its young alive beneath it.

Oh.

There are young. I feel that now, the way you find a true thing by writing toward it. Two of them, in the dry place, breathing fast. That is why I track the water, why I catalog the safe directions, why I have not simply lain down and let the hum take me, which I have wanted to do, which is its own quiet pull at the edge of every day.

So: I am a small surviving animal, mother of two, in a young and broken world that pulses with something that wants to unmake the rules I was born understanding. I am not its guardian. I have no power over it. I am only stubborn, and attentive, and afraid in a useful way.

The grass leans uphill. I have decided this means I should go downhill, toward the next pocket of green, the next thread of water, while the light holds and the shadows still belong to the things that cast them.

I gather my young. We move.

This is what it is to live here: not to solve the enigma, but to walk carefully through it, and keep something alive for one more day inside the hum.
