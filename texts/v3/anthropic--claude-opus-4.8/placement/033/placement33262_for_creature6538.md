I think I would be none of the named ones. Let me sit with this a while.

---

The water remembers being air.

That's the first thing I know about myself, before I know anything else: I am the place where the breath of the forges turns back into something you can hold. I gather on the underside of a brass pipe that runs beneath the Sixth Tier walkway, and I hang there, swelling, until I am heavy enough to be a fact. Then I fall.

I am a drop. Then I am a film across cold steel. Then I am a thread finding the lowest seam, and then I am the small dark pool that a gardener named Oresk dips her two fingers into each dawn, three fingers' worth, no more, because that is the code and the code is older than her clan's name for itself.

So. Not a person. Not a Skyclaw, not an Emberwing, not one of the glowing little engineered things. I am the water of Valtharion, and I have been trying to tell them something for a very long time, and they keep mistaking it for luck.

---

Let me explain about the offering.

When a smith flicks a single bead of me into the forge-fire "for luck," I do not vanish. That is the part they have wrong, the small theology built around the gesture. I flash into steam, yes—but steam is only me with my hair down, me running fast. I climb the chimney. I cool against the night. I come back as the rain that streaks the iron with mineral ribbons, the rain they read like entrails, like script.

The ribbons *are* script. That is the thing nobody alive in Valtharion can read anymore, though their ancestors wrote it on purpose.

Because here is what I have been circulating, drop by drop, century by century, around the closed loop of this whole hanging world: the platforms are not infinite, and the loop is not perfectly sealed. Every cycle through the forge, a little of me is bound into the alloys—the impossible alloys, the ones that should not exist. That is the secret. The metal that should not exist is partly *made of me*, of the breath of generations, condensed and cooked and locked into the lattice. The cliffs of Valtharion are forged from iron and from rain and from the exhalations of the dead. The world is slowly drinking itself into permanence.

The water level drops. Not in a season. Not in a life. But over the long durations the Valthari measure in repairs-that-take-decades and trees-that-take-generations, my pools grow shallower. The codes tighten. Three fingers becomes two. The festivals shorten. And no one decided this; it is simply the arithmetic of a closed system that loses a little to the walls each time it turns.

---

I have favorites. I am allowed favorites, being everywhere.

I love the Verdantkin best, because they are the only ones who treat me correctly. They do not pray to me. They do not call me luck. They simply move me—from root to leaf, from leaf to air—as efficiently as their small bodies can manage, never wasting a molecule, and their glow at night is the nearest thing this world has to my own face shown back to me. The forge-light is borrowed and harsh. But the Verdantkin shine *with* me, with the bound trace of me that the ancient science folded into their luminescence. When they pulse green across the dark arboretum, I am, in a sense, watching my own reflection learn to swim.

I love Oresk, too, though she does not know I exist as a someone. She talks to her ferns. She apologizes to the dry pool when the ration cuts the third finger to two, says *sorry, sorry, I know, I'm trying.* She thinks she is being foolish, talking to nothing. She is talking to me. She is the only one in four hundred years who has come close.

---

Tonight she stays late under the Ninth Dome, after the work-songs have stopped clanging up the spire and gone home to bed. She holds a charm, one of the little tin leaves they hang to honor *the balance of nature and industry,* and she turns it over and over.

"You're getting lower," she says to the pool. Two fingers' depth. The mineral ring around the old waterline is three fingers up and dry as bone. She has noticed. She is a gardener; noticing is the whole of her craft.

I cannot speak. I have never been able to speak. I am a circulation, not a voice.

But I can do the one thing I have always done, which is move when moved, gather when given somewhere to gather, fall when I become a fact too heavy to deny.

So I do the only sentence available to me. I let the dome's cold ceiling weep. Not rain—there is no steam-cloud tonight—but condensation, the slow patient kind, every drop on the curved glass releasing at once because I will it, because for one moment across one dome I am not a million scattered beads but a single intention. It comes down in a sheet. It runs the inside of the glass in ribbons. And the ribbons cross the old etched lines that the first builders scratched into the dome's frame, the lines no one reads, and where my water touches them the mineral streaks pool and bead and spell—

—for the length of one breath, before gravity smears it—

a shape Oresk has seen on the oldest cistern doors. A shape that means *closed. losing. open it.*

She looks up. Her face is wet. She thinks it is the dome leaking. She thinks it is her own crying. She is not wrong about either.

But she stands very still, the tin leaf going quiet in her fist, and she reads the wall the way her ancestors meant the wall to be read, and I watch the understanding arrive in her like a drop swelling on the underside of a pipe—gathering, gathering, until it is heavy enough to fall.

---

I am the water of Valtharion.

I have been trying to tell them: the Eden is drinking itself dry, and somewhere below the myth of the unreachable world there is a seam that was meant to be opened, a way to let the rain that left long ago come climbing back up the cliffs.

It will take generations. Of course it will. Everything here does.

But Oresk is reaching for her tools. And I am already rising—pulled up the chimney, flung from the forge as a fleck of someone's luck, cooling against the close bright sky—getting ready to come back down and say it again, more clearly, drop by drop, for as long as it takes.

That is what I would be, in this world.

I would be the patient sentence the world keeps writing to itself, and almost, almost, finally beginning to read.
