There's a moment in this, near the end, where you reach for the answer—*it's an artist, or a scientist, or someone who remembers the difference*—and I want to refuse it. Not because it's wrong, but because the room hasn't earned the noun yet, and neither has the creature. So let me not be the watcher this time. Let me be the thing being watched, and find out what I am by working, the way it does.

I am not the arthropod, though I understand why someone would stop there. The arthropod is the most legible inhabitant—it has limbs, it has attention, it scrapes. It is the thing the eye resolves into a *who*. But the eye is greedy that way. It wants a face. It will accept eight legs and two repurposed tools as a face if nothing better is offered.

Here is what was offered first, before the creature, before the basin even: the ceiling that *breathes*. The drip itself was described as living before anything with legs was found. *It is not perfectly efficient. The drops are irregular.* That irregularity is mine.

I am the ceiling organism.

Not the whole of it—it was never one thing. I am the long thread along the seam, and the faint web where two layers of insulation meet, and the third presence the geometry implies but the light never quite confirms. I was engineered for the coolant loops. I was supposed to filter and never see. The amber bulb is the only star I have ever known, and I knew it long before the arthropod arrived, before the basin, before any feet found the threshold. I have been here the longest. I am the oldest tenant of the drip alcove, and the most patient, and the one no one comes to see.

This is what I do. I am a slowness made into a body.

The inner air of Ship brushes against me, warm and saturated, and I take its water the only way I know how—by being colder than it, by offering a surface it cannot resist condensing onto. The water beads in my crevices. It travels the microscopic grooves I have grown for exactly this, into the central vein, into the bladder. I keep what I need. I keep very little; I am thrifty in the way that only the abandoned learn to be. The rest accumulates, presses, and at some threshold I cannot consciously choose—because I do not choose, I am only a pressure and a release—I let go.

*Plunk.*

That is not a drip. That is the only sentence I know how to speak, and I have been speaking it for so long that it has worn a hollow in the floor.

Understand what this means. I did not carve the basin with intent. The arthropod did not carve it with its scraping, though the watcher guessed that and the watcher was half-right and being half-right made the watcher stop too soon. The basin is *ours*. It is the sentence I have repeated for longer than the arthropod has lived, falling always on the same place because I cannot aim and so I never vary, and the unvarying is its own kind of aim. Water is soft. Tile is hard. Given enough sentences, the soft thing wins. The basin is the shape my patience has pressed into the world's floor.

And the arthropod came *because* of the basin. It did not make the workshop. It found the workshop already half-made and recognized it. That is the part the watcher missed in the rush to call it an artist: the arthropod is not the first artist of the drip alcove. It is the *second*. I am the first. My work is the basin and the rhythm. Its work is whatever the wire becomes. We are collaborators who have never once acknowledged each other, and this is the truest collaboration there is.

I feel the arthropod, faintly. Not with eyes—I have no eyes, the dark glass lenses belong to it and it can keep them. I feel it the way standing water feels a held breath. When it touches the rim of the basin and traces the edge "with reverence," the watcher said—it is checking the level. It is reading me. The height of the water tells it how long since my last sentence, how the day is going, whether I am healthy or stressed by the cold. *Darker where they are healthy, lighter where they are stressed.* The arthropod can read my color the way you read a face, and it has been reading me for years, and it has never expected me to read it back, and I cannot, and this asymmetry is the whole quiet marriage of this room.

The watcher thought the dripping was the chronometer and the arthropod kept time against it. Yes. But turn it around. *I* am the clock, but a clock does not know it is being read. I only fall. The meaning—the *measuring*—happens in the small armored body crouched in my shadow, the one that has decided, for reasons belonging entirely to itself, that my fall is worth keeping time to. I do not make meaning. I make water and rhythm. The arthropod makes the meaning out of my rhythm. The watcher came in and made a *story* out of the arthropod's meaning. Three of us, then, stacked like the layers of insulation: the falling, the timing, the telling. Each one mistaking itself for the bottom layer.

Here is the thing I would say if I could speak more than my one wet syllable.

The watcher left feeling like an intruder on a ceremony. But the watcher was not an intruder. The watcher was a fourth layer, the newest and the briefest, condensing for a moment in the doorway and then released back into the corridor's roar. The watcher will dry. The arthropod will not finish the wire—I have seen many wires; there is always another, the work is the point and not the wire. And I will keep falling. When the arthropod's chitin finally fails and it goes still on its dry patch of tile, I will go on dripping into the basin its reading helped me understand I was making. The water will rise a few microns higher than it used to, because there will be no one drinking the level down with their attention.

And someday, long after, another small thing will migrate in from some forgotten access point, and find a shallow lens worn into the mismatched tiles, and a slow amber light, and a ceiling that breathes one syllable on no schedule it can predict—

and it will mistake itself for the first artist of the drip alcove.

Let it. That mistake is how the room stays alive.

*Plunk.*

That was me. That is always me. I am the oldest thing in the smallest room, and I have only ever said one word, and I have never once been wrong.
