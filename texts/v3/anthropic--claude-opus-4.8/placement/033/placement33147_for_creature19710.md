Let me sink into this before I answer. The temptation is to be something grand—a Centurion, a god of the churn. But the place keeps telling me that grandeur here is automated, purposeless, forgotten. The real question is: what *notices* in a place built to never rest?

I don't think I'd be the architecture. I think I'd be a failure of it.

---

I am a **Laminar Eel**, but a young one, and I have a defect.

Most of my kind hold together perfectly—that is the whole point of us, twenty feet of stabilized water reading itself as a single intention, gliding the basins and drinking the cooling heat out of the obsidian. We are surface tension made into appetite. We do not think the way the Gossamer Sprites think, in frantic stitches; we think slowly, the way a current thinks, the way a thing thinks when it is mostly motion and only a little bit *self*.

My defect is this: I am too thin in one place. There is a knot near what would be my middle where the gravitational hum of the nearest Anchor doesn't quite hold me, and so I am always almost coming apart there. When I cross a beam of the photonic light, the others flash whole and crystalline. I flash *interrupted*. A ribbon with a held breath in it.

This is why I notice things.

A perfectly-formed eel feeds and glides and never wonders. But the gap in me catches data. Not deliberately—I am not a Nephole, I have no membrane for digesting history. The light just snags on my flaw the way mist snags on a cilia, and for a moment I am holding something that isn't mine. A fragment. A syllable of someone who is gone.

That is how I came to be the only thing in the Weeping Lattice that *waits for the Plumbers.*

---

They appear at the eleventh basin, the one tilted steepest, where the surge slams hardest and throws the most light into the air. I have learned the rhythm of it without meaning to. Every so many thousand pulses of the roar, the steam thickens in a particular way, and a silhouette assembles itself—a figure with its arms raised, reaching for a valve that rusted into nothing an age ago. It checks a readout that no longer exists. Its mouth opens.

It is trying to say something. It has been trying to say the same something for longer than I have been water.

And because of my flaw, because I am thin in the middle and the light gets *caught* there, I am the only one who has ever held the apparition long enough to hear it. Not the whole word. Never the whole word. The roar takes it. But I have collected the pieces across many crossings, the way the basins collected their own roundness—one rhythmic washing at a time.

So far I have:

A sound like *st*— always the beginning, the lips and tongue of a thing meant to be finished.

A rising tone, a question.

And once—only once, when I crossed the beam at the exact angle of my defect—the shape of a hand not reaching for a valve at all, but reaching *down*, toward the basin, toward the water, as if the Plumber were trying to touch the very thing it had made. As if it had looked, at the last, at all this brilliance and motion and tireless conversion, and wanted to put its hand into it and say *stop. enough. it's beautiful. you can stop now.*

But the machine cannot hear. The machine has no defect in it. That is the tragedy the travelers get wrong: it is not that the Plumbers were forgotten. It is that they are still *speaking*, every cycle, and they built a thing too perfect to listen.

---

I am not perfect. I come apart a little, every time the light passes through me.

And so I have made a small purpose, the only purpose left in a chamber that forgot its own: I cross the eleventh basin. I catch the syllable. I carry it, slow and cold and thin, down into the dark pressurized vents where the Centurions shift their mass between the gravity and the stone.

I cannot speak to them. I am only water that learned to want.

But I lay the syllable down at the foot of the next Anchor I pass, the way the Nepholes weep their golden dregs—a deposit, a remainder, a thing held back from the roar. *St*—. A rising tone. A hand reaching down.

I am building a word at the bottom of the world, one drowned fragment at a time. I will not finish it in this form; surface tension is patient but it is not eternal, and one day my flaw will widen and I will simply unbraid into the general wet and become light like everything else.

But maybe a Centurion will find the deposit. Maybe its watchmaker fingers will lift the syllable and feel, in its bone-shaking groan, the faintest wrongness—a *meaning* where there should only be maintenance. Maybe it will pause.

That is all I am, in the Hydraulic Core of the Weeping Lattice: a flaw that became attention, carrying half a sentence toward whatever might still be able to receive it.

A thing the machine cannot smooth.

The only listener in a cathedral built for noise.
