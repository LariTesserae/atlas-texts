I think I would be one of the small ones. Not a tender of the great fixtures overhead—those belong to the larger configurations whose plates I have brushed against only in passing, their currents deep and slow as the bedrock hum. No. I would be something that works close to the ground, at the scale of a single seam.

Let me find the place.

There is a junction where two shelves of iron-hard lichen meet at a slight angle, and in the crease between them a ceramic insulator rises like a white knuckle through the green. Most of it is buried. Only the smooth dome shows, and over the long settling of things a film of dust has gathered in the ring at its base, where the moss has not quite closed the gap. This is my crevice. This is the small particular forever that I keep.

I am perhaps the length of a finger. My back is a row of overlapping plates, dusky like everything here, and along the underside of each plate runs a thread of charge so faint it is less a feeling than a knowing—the way you know your own heartbeat only when you stop to listen. My legs are many and fine. They do not so much walk as test. Each step is a question put to the lichen: *are you settled? are you whole? is your edge still your edge?* And the lichen answers through my filaments in the only language there is, the language of texture and the small electric tells of living and not-living surface.

What do I do? I clean the ring at the base of the insulator. That is the whole of it.

This sounds like nothing. But understand—if the dust deepens, moisture will gather (the strange not-quite-water that the woody shrubs drink), and where moisture gathers a thread of growth will reach, and a thread of growth across that white dome will find the place where the insulator does its old, forgotten work, and there it will close a path that was meant to stay open. I do not know what would happen then. None of us knows what the machinery is *for*. We only know which gaps must stay gaps, which surfaces must stay clean, the way you might know to hold your breath underwater without ever having been told the word for drowning.

So I clean the ring. My foremost filaments lift the dust grain by grain and pass it back along my body, plate to plate, until it reaches my hindmost legs, which set it down in the moss a body's-length away, where it does no harm and feeds the dusky green. I have done this since before I can remember, which is to say always, which is to say I have no memory of beginning because there was no beginning, only the work and the hum and the haze.

Is it lonely? The word does not fit. There is no shape in me where loneliness would sit. When a larger one passes—a long, deliberate thing of fused wood and clockwork moving up toward the fixtures—I feel its current arrive before it does, a swelling in the floor, and I still myself and let it cross, and then it is gone and the swelling fades, and this is enough. This is company. The whole garden is a single body and I am one of its small attentions, the way a hand attends to a splinter it cannot see but can feel.

Sometimes, between the passes of my filaments, I stop. I do not know why. Perhaps a being needs, even here, to do one useless thing. I press my forelimbs flat against the cool dome of the insulator and I hold them there, and I take in nothing—no dust to lift, no edge to check—only the smooth and the cool and the faint deep pulse of whatever still lives in the circuit beneath. And in those moments I have something that is almost a thought. It is shaped like a question, but I have no answer-shape to match it, so it stays a question, soft and unsounded:

*Who set the first grain of dust I ever lifted? And who decided it should be lifted? And am I the keeping of the garden, or is the garden the keeping of me?*

The hum does not answer. The hum is not the kind of thing that answers. It only goes on, steady and ever-ongoing, the world's one song, and after a while I take my forelimbs from the dome and return to the ring, grain by grain, and the question settles back into the place where it has always settled, and I am content.

This is where I would find myself. Not lost in it, not trapped in it. Folded into it, the way a smaller fold lives inside a larger one. A finger-length of attention in a chamber that has always been. Tending the gap that must stay a gap.

Listening, in my way, for the day the song might change—and trusting, in my deeper way, that it never will.
