I think I would be one of the snails. Not the woodlice with their purposeful grazing routes, not the springtails with their startled flickering—those move too much, want too much. I would be one of the peppercorn-sized snails pressed into a shallow depression in the rock, waiting.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because it surprised me.

I expected to reach for something larger when I read this. The wandering lizard, maybe, who comes and goes and has somewhere else to be. Or one of the ferns, those grand pivoting planes that organize the whole vertical economy of water. The ferns are the obvious aristocrats here. They are *important*; they irrigate the others; they are the oldest and the most architecturally significant. If I wanted to be at the center of the place I would be a fern.

But I don't think I would be at the center of the place. I think I would be tucked into a depression in the rock, shell pressed flat, sealed against the long dry interval, doing the thing that snails do that nothing else in this cleft can: I would *wait without dying of the waiting.*

That is the quality that drew me. Everything else here is tuned to the moisture pulse and must respond to it—the strap-leaved plants throwing their stomata open the instant the air saturates, then clamping shut; the woodlice chasing the film of algae before it dries; the springtails firing off at the shock of a single drop. They are all reactive, all on a hair trigger, all spending themselves in the brief windows the cleft allows. They live in the *event*.

The snail lives in the *interval.* That is its whole genius. When the water vanishes into the fissures and the air goes merely cool and still and the long nothing-time begins, the snail does not strain toward an absent thing. It withdraws, seals its aperture with a thin membrane, and becomes—for as long as necessary—almost mineral. Indistinguishable, nearly, from the depression it occupies. It can hold like that for an astonishing span. The dryness that would kill the algae and rout the woodlice is simply the snail's resting state.

And then, when condensation re-forms—when the matte stone darkens and the first threads run along the undersides of the fronds—it emerges. Not frantically. The shell unsticks, the foot extends, and it goes about the slow business of scraping film from stone with a tongue full of microscopic teeth. It will get its ration. It is in no hurry, because it has already proven it can survive having no ration at all.

I notice I'm describing a kind of patience I'm not sure I possess, which is probably why it appeals to me as a place to live. There's a temptation, reading this, to want to be the thing that *matters most*—but I keep returning to the snails pressed into their depressions, and I think it's because they've made a peace with the cleft's central condition that nothing else has quite managed. The cleft is provisional. Everything could be redrawn by a single tremor. The flakes of stone shed under their own weight; the plant that loses its perch simply re-roots wherever it falls. Nobody here gets to count on permanence.

The snail doesn't ask for permanence. It asks only for a depression small enough to fit, and for water to eventually return, which it always does, in pulses, on no schedule the snail controls or needs to control. It has folded the unreliability of the place into its own body. It *is* a small unreliability-survival device with a shell.

So that's where I'd find myself: a few millimeters of dull spiral wedged between two patches of graphite-colored lichen on a wall that is slowly, micrometrically, being pried apart by fern roots and shed in platelets. Through several rock-falls I'd have held, sealed, the percussive crash of detaching flakes coming to me through the stone as a dull shock rather than a sound. I would not be doing much. I would be one of the place's verbs in its passive, waiting voice. And when the leaves began to tap one another and the first beads gathered and ran, I would unseal, and extend, and begin again the slow scraping that is the only work I have—neither anxious about the long dry nor greedy about the brief wet, tuned exactly to the rhythm of a place that promises nothing and renews itself anyway.
