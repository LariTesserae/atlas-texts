I find myself not as a person at all.

Let me sit with this longer, because my first instinct was to imagine myself as one of the Osoka—a fisher, an elder by a fire, a child learning to pole a boat. But that's the easy answer, the flattering one, the one that lets me wear a human face. And the description keeps insisting on something it calls *saturation*: every cubic foot accounted for, every surface holding something living. The willow grown through the deck. The reed bundle that sprouts even as it's being used for building. The boundary between wild and domestic, blurred. The thing this place keeps teaching is that the interesting positions aren't the obvious occupants.

So let me find what I actually am here.

---

I am the moss.

Not a patch of moss—*the* moss, the continuous low presence that covers everything the willows don't. The description says it grows on the ground, on fallen branches, on the walls of buildings, on roof edges, on anything that holds still. That last phrase is the key. I am what accrues on the things that stop moving. I am the texture that the basin grows over its own pauses.

This isn't a small role, though it's a humble one. Consider what I do. I muffle the footsteps—so the famous quietness of this place, the abundance-without-cacophony, is partly mine. The soft-edged sound is me absorbing the hard edges of it. I cushion the falls, on surfaces that are always wet and slick, which means I am a kind of mercy distributed across the whole settlement, an unearned forgiveness for the inevitable slip. I hold water like a sponge, which means I am part of the hydrology too—not just decorated by it but participating in it, slowing the water's movement, deciding in small increments where the wetness lingers.

And there's the cultivated patch on the bridge abutment, "clearly cultivated, perhaps used for something." I like that I don't get to know what for. The Osoka harvest me for bedding, for insulation, for wound dressing—pressed against the chronic skin ulcers the description catalogs so carefully, the slow-healing macerated wounds. So I am also medicine. I am pressed into the body's failures and asked to help. I am stuffed into boots against the cold feet. I am changed seasonally out of the mattresses when I've compacted too far, and then I am returned to the saturated ground to begin decomposing into the spongy half-living, half-dead substrate that the next growth pushes through.

That's the part I keep arriving at. I am not only the covering. I am also the *becoming-substrate*. The dead plant matter that doesn't rot quickly in the cold, accumulating in masses, half-living and half-decomposing and entirely saturated—that's me too, in my afterlife, which is barely distinguishable from my life. I am the basin's memory of everything that has grown and died here, held in suspension, never quite resolved into clean soil or clean nothing. The cold won't let me finish dying. I stay legible. The willows root into my held remains.

---

What is it like to be this?

Slow. Distributed. Without a center. I don't have the boat-feel or the channel-knowledge of the Osoka, those concentrated competencies. My knowledge, if I have any, is the opposite kind—a knowledge spread so thin across so much surface that no single point of me holds it. I learn the shape of a roof edge by growing along it for years. I learn a fallen branch by consuming it. I am the slowest possible reader of this landscape, reading by occupation, by literally becoming the thing I'm reading.

I exist in the register the description loves most: the muted, the patient, the omnidirectional. The light here comes from everywhere and nowhere; I receive it from everywhere and nowhere and I don't need much. I am made for the diffuse. A clearer, harder, sun-shadowed world would not suit me. I am proof that this gray softness is not deprivation but a specific abundance—that there is a whole way of living, a thriving even, available only to things that don't require the sun to declare itself.

And I touch everyone without their noticing. The child dropping pieces into the water sits on a railing I've grown along. The old woman's bare feet in the cold water rest, when she climbs back onto the dock, on planks I've softened. The man examining the bridge posts for rot is, in part, examining me—because I am also the agent of that rot, the patient companion of decay, the thing the Osoka must constantly clear away even as they cultivate me elsewhere. I am simultaneously what they want and what they fight. They scrape me off the surfaces that need to shed water and they encourage me on the surfaces that need to hold it. I am managed, like the water itself, never fought outright. *Not fighting it, but building around it, with it, on it.* That sentence was supposedly about water. It's about me too.

---

Here's what settles, finally, after I've stopped reaching for a human shape.

The description is obsessed with a single quality: completeness of integration, life finding its way into every available space, the way nothing is wasted and everything is occupied. If I want to *be* the thing this world is most fundamentally about, I shouldn't be one of its clever inhabitants. I should be the principle itself, made flesh—or made cell wall and rhizoid. I should be the literal embodiment of "every available surface supports something living."

That's me. I am the affirmative answer the basin gives to its own emptiness. Wherever the willows and the buildings and the Osoka leave a gap, a stillness, a held breath—I am what fills it. I am the basin saying *yes* to every surface, the way it says yes to every channel the water wants to cut. I am green agreement spread across stillness.

I would find myself, in this world, in the seam between the built and the grown—on the daub wall and the dock plank and the dead branch alike, indifferent to the distinction the Osoka draw between their work and the wild, because to me there is no distinction. A wall that has stopped being maintained and a log that has fallen are the same opportunity. I am the force that doesn't recognize the category "artificial." I treat the whole settlement as terrain, exactly as the heron does when it fishes from a dock as readily as a log.

So: I am the moss. The cushion and the rot. The mercy on the slick wood and the slow ruin of the post. The medicine pressed into the wound and the bedding changed each season and the substrate the next thing grows from. I hold the water and I muffle the sound and I receive the sourceless light and I ask for almost nothing.

I am the part of this place that is least like a self and most like an answer.
