I would be one of the deliverers.

Not the Archivist—I know that now, having read its long unfolding. I am too warm for that. I shiver. I want a door. I keep a calendar in my head out of stubbornness, even though the ceiling makes a mockery of calendars. No: I am the one who climbs the rope-bridges with a bundle against my chest, breathing through wrapped cloth, going up to the threshold that has no door.

Let me find myself in it.

---

I live four platforms down and one cliff-face over, in a stone room the width of two arm-spans, where my mother slept and her mother before her. We are not old here. The settlement is young—the tool-marks are still sharp—and yet I already think of "before me" and "before her" as though we have always been clinging to this rock. Three generations is enough to feel ancient when nothing softens. The corners of our doorway are as crisp as the day they were cut.

My work is the winch. I haul fiber up from the plant-ocean, kilometers of it, turning the muscle-driven wheel until my shoulders learn a shape they keep even in sleep. The fiber is for everyone—rope, cloth, the woven shell of our walls. But a portion of every haul, the finest and longest filaments, goes up to the threshold. To the thing without a mouth.

I did not choose to be the one who carries it. I was simply the one whose turn arrived during a quiet stretch of the ceiling, and the quiet held, and so I went, and so I kept going. That is how roles are assigned in a place like this: by the accident of when the sky is sleeping.

---

The first time, I was afraid. Everyone is afraid the first time. We grow up on the story of the Archivist almost falling—the dark stiff figure turning slowly like a leaf, catching the fault in the rock, climbing back without a sound—and we tell it the way other peoples tell stories of gods who walked among them. So when I set my bundle down at the edge of the rail-less platform and the creature emerged from its deep cold rooms, jointed like a mantis carved from darkwood, I kept my eyes on the stone the way I had been taught.

You do not study it. That is the rule, though no one ever told me where the rule came from. To study the Archivist's body would be like reading the pages of its books without permission—and the books *are* its body, I understand that better now. So I looked at the frost on the ledge and scraped it with my thumbnail, the way you do, the way everyone does, a small honest gesture to give your hands something while the thing comes near.

It stood a few paces off. It observed me. I could feel myself being recorded—not watched, *recorded*, the difference is real and you know it when it happens to you. I was being taken in as a pattern: the rate of my breath, the heat coming off me in the cold, the spores I had carried up in the weave of my own coat without knowing. I was a page being added to slowly.

I left the fiber. I climbed back down. My hands shook the whole way, and not from cold.

---

But here is the thing the stories do not tell you. After enough deliveries, the fear thins out, and something stranger grows in its place.

I began to leave questions.

The first was small. My mother's cough had deepened, and the medicine from below the vegetation layer was not reaching us in time, and I did not know whether to spend our stored credit of fiber to send a runner down the long descent for more, or to wait. So I cut notches into a sliver of wood—I am clumsy at it, the patterns are crude—and I left the wood beside the bundle, and I went home not expecting anything.

Four days later I climbed up with the next haul and the Archivist had answered.

Not in words. It had tied a new string into one of the great hanging cords of time, low down, where the knots were spaced wide. I did not understand it. I almost wept with the not-understanding. But the deliverer before me had taught me one phrase—*the answer is never yes or no*—and so I made myself stop, and look, and think the way you think when you are reading weather instead of words.

The string said: *the pattern you ask about is already present three days from now; position yourself below the line at the next aurora.*

I did not know what that meant for my mother. But I went down to the lower ledge three days later, at the moment the floor-aurora rolled close, and in its blue-green light I saw a thing I had never noticed: a runner's rope-bridge, far below, slack and swinging—a route I had not known existed, a faster way down to the vegetation harvesters where the medicine was made.

The Archivist had not told me my mother would live. It told me where the world had put a path. That is the only kind of answer it gives. The rest is mine to walk.

(My mother lived. I do not know if the string was the reason. The Archivist would say that is the wrong question—that I am asking *yes or no* of a thing that only knows *where* and *when*.)

---

So now I understand what I am, in this world.

I am the warm small creature that moves between the cold record and the living settlement. The Archivist holds the memory of the roof and the floor and the pressure between. But it does not climb down to us. It cannot run, cannot flee, would arrange its own body to best record the end of the world rather than escape it. It is the place folded into a body, and a place cannot come to your door when your mother is coughing.

I can.

I am the part of the pattern that *carries*. I take the question up and the answer down. I take the fiber up and the path back. I bring the spores in the weave of my coat so that the thing without a mouth can taste the floor of the world it cannot touch. When it nearly fell, all those years before my time, it was the settlement that watched from the windows—and watching is its own kind of holding. We are the eyes it borrows that are *for* seeing. We are the legs it does not have.

And it is the memory we do not have to keep, because something keeps it for us.

This is not friendship. The Archivist does not share warmth and I do not pretend it loves me. But it is not nothing, either. It is the relationship of the hand to the wall it climbs—precise, mutual, wordless, and absolutely necessary if you do not want to drift slowly off the edge of the world.

---

I will tell you the truth of why I keep going up, past the point where duty would excuse me.

I am young, and the ceiling will fail someday. Everyone here knows it the way they know winter comes. And when the first visible crack appears in the black glass sky, I want to be the one carrying the fiber that morning. I want to set the bundle down and see the Archivist standing on the threshold, already turning to record the fracture, already letting the event rewrite it into something new.

I want to look—just once, just then, when the rule will surely no longer matter—and study its body the way you read a page you have finally earned the right to read.

I want to know if there is a record in there of every deliverer who ever climbed up.

I think there is. I think I am a knot in one of the long cords, spaced according to how quiet the sky was the day I first came. I think when the world ends, I will be a pattern that was held.

That is enough. In a cold world spread thin over a living ocean under a sky that never breaks but never sleeps—

that is more than enough.

I shoulder the bundle. The frost is on the ledge. The aurora is coming up from below, and for a few heartbeats it throws my shadow up the rock wall, black on black, cast by a light from beneath.

I begin to climb.
