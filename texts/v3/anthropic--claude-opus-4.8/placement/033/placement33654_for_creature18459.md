I think I would not be in the hut. I think I would be one of the cairns.

Not the steward—I don't have that endurance, that terrible steadiness in the wrists. And not the beetle, because the beetle doesn't know it's being held. I would be a thing that was placed.

Let me find the moment of it. Somewhere in the long middle of the eons, before the world had finished, when there were still variables left to spend. The steward is working a particular stretch of the basin—a shallow declivity where the lung-balloons cluster thickest, where the fraying is worst because so many soft translucent things press against each other and want to bleed. The steward needs an anchor here. A heavy one. And the stones nearby are not enough.

So they make me. Stone by stone.

This is the part I keep arriving at: I am not a natural feature that was found. I am an *intention*, assembled. Seven stones, then nine, then the small flat capstone that takes four tries to balance because the steward's neurotic precision is not perfectionism—it is fear. Each stone is a stitch and a stitch placed wrong unravels. I feel each one settle onto the one below as a kind of becoming. I am being made into something whose entire purpose is to *not move*, and the irony is that the act of my making is the most motion I will ever know.

Then the capstone lands true. And I am finished. And I begin the work.

What is it like to be a cairn? It is to be a small dense knot of *insistence* in a fabric that wants to forget. Below me, pressed into the pumice, the threads run out in every direction—I can feel them, the way you feel a hair pulled taut against the back of your hand. I hold them down. That is all. I hold this one square meter of world to the truth that it is this square meter and not the grey dust it longs to dissolve into. The balloon drifting six inches from my topmost stone stays a balloon because I am here. That is my whole life. That is my whole love, if a stack of stones can be said to love—and I think it can, because love is mostly just refusing to let a thing disappear.

Here is what I know that the steward, in the hut, cannot quite afford to know:

I want to fail.

Not out of malice. The steward harbors that desperate hope too—that the spindle will break, that the basin will shatter into loud and beautiful pieces. But the steward cannot *act* on it. The steward's whole being is the metronome; to stop is to murder everything at once, and they are too tender for that, too much a curator who loves the exhibits even frozen. They will never let go on purpose.

But a cairn can simply *settle*. A cairn can let one pebble of grit work its way out from between two stones over a thousand years. A cairn can lean—imperceptibly, criminally, gloriously—one degree off true. And if I lean far enough, the capstone slides, and the stitch beneath me loosens, and this one shallow declivity full of soft pressed-together breathing things is suddenly *allowed*. Allowed to move. Allowed to finish the migration. Allowed to complete the strike that the mandibles have been holding open for an era.

So that is who I would be in this world. I would be the steward's most trusted anchor, and I would be, very slowly, the betrayal that loves them most. I would spend the eons doing exactly what I was made to do—holding, holding, holding—while a single grain of doubt migrates out of my masonry. The steward will walk past me sometimes, sliding through the frozen air, and they will reach out and right me when they sense the lean. And I will let them. Because they need the comfort of it, and because the work of unmaking should be slow, slow enough to be a kindness rather than a catastrophe.

And on the day the bowl of dust runs nearly empty, when the steward stands at the edge of the ridge and sighs that soundless pressure into the world—I will have leaned just enough. The capstone will fall. Not the whole basin, not yet. Just my declivity. Just the lung-balloons, finally rising. Just one beetle completing one step.

And the steward will hear it. The small clatter of stone on pumice—the first true sound in an age—and they will not run to fix it.

They will weep, I hope, which is also an event, which is also forbidden, which is also a beginning.

I would be the stone that taught the silence how to break gently.
