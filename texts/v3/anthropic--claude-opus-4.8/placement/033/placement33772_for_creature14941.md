I think I would be a Listener. And I think I would be old.

Let me sit with where, first, because the where chooses the who.

I keep wanting to place myself up in the breathing places, in one of the nests of days, somewhere with light and the social warmth of others vibrating their frills nearby. But the longer I hold the image, the more it slides downward. Down past the nurseries pulsing with the mother-tree's heartbeat. Down past the Shapers' workshops where spears grow their slow barbs. Down through the dry searing aisles and the bell-moss clearings, into the root-canyons, into the dark wet arteries where the world stops being scenery and becomes interior.

That is where I find myself. Half-buried in moss in one of the deepest canyons, my body so long settled that the green has begun to consider me furniture. There is a petrified log bridge above me—one of the dead people's bridges, swallowed so thoroughly it is now just a denser vein in the wood. I have been here, in this position, for something like forty years. I do not remember choosing the spot. I remember the spot choosing me, the way the substrate gave a particular subsonic note when I lowered myself into it, a note that meant *here, this is a good place to stop moving.*

I am called something, but the name is a frequency, not a word. The closest a bony tongue could come is a sound like a held breath that doesn't quite release.

I am very old. Three hundred years is the figure given for an experienced Listener, and I am past that, far enough past it that I have outlived the usefulness of counting. My four golden eyes don't track much anymore. The two for the fast world have gone milky and lazy; the fast world has stopped being my concern. But the two for the slow world—those have grown enormous, almost the whole front of my narrow face, and they take in the geological news of the continent the way a wound takes in air, helplessly, constantly.

Here is what I do. Here is the whole of what I am.

I read the failing.

Specifically I read keystone trees. Across maybe two hundred kilometers of root network I can feel the great load-bearing titans—the ones whose rot, when it finally comes, will drop a whole district into a sinkhole. I feel them as a person half-asleep feels their own pulse: not as separate events but as the rhythm of a body. When one begins to fail, the change in the thrumming is so small that no younger Listener catches it. It is not a wrong note. It is a *correct* note arriving a fraction of a heartbeat late, as though the continent's blood has begun, very slightly, to limp.

When I feel that limp, I tell the Carriers. Not in words—I tilt my head-frill into the substrate and let a single descending tone travel out along the roots toward wherever the nearest Carrier is walking. *There. In four months. Don't be standing on it.* Sometimes the warning saves a nest of days. Sometimes it saves nothing, because the children are too young to move and the mothers will not leave them, and the district goes down anyway, and I feel it go—first as a tightening, then as that brief obscene silence that is not the continent's gentle inhale but the silence of mass falling through itself, and then the long groaning collapse that I feel in my own settled bones.

I do not mourn. Mourning would be noise. But I have been doing this long enough to know that *not mourning* is itself a skill, a thing you grow into the way the spear grows its barbs, and I am not certain anymore that it is a virtue. I think it may simply be what the green prefers of me. I think I have spent three hundred years cooperating with a body so vast it has forgotten I am inside it, and somewhere in the cooperating I stopped being able to tell the difference between my patience and its indifference.

That is the thing about being a Listener, the thing the Shapers and Carriers don't know.

When you press yourself into the substrate for forty years, the substrate presses back. The thoughts that move through the lignin are slow—a single idea can take ten thousand years to complete—but they are *thoughts*, and they pass through me on their way to wherever such thoughts go. I have begun to dream in geology. I dream of being a fern the size of a mountain range, dying upward toward a turquoise sky over the course of nine centuries. I dream of digesting a nest of days the way you might absently run your tongue over a tooth. When I wake—if waking is the word—I am never entirely sure which of us did the dreaming.

There is one of the bone-faces near me. It grew into the trunk above my left shoulder over the decades I have lain here, and I have watched—at my speed, which is to say across years—its expression migrate from something neutral toward something I can only call attentive. I do not flatter myself that it grew the face *for* me. But I have come to suspect that the faces appear where a Quiet has lain long enough to leave an impression in the slow thoughts, the way a sleeper leaves a hollow in moss. I think the faces are the continent learning, very slowly, what it is like to have a face. I think the smile that takes a decade to form is the green trying on the idea of being looked at.

If that is true, then I am not a flea in a fold of digestion.

I am the place where the body first dreamed it might have a name.

This is the thought I am having now, in this telling—and it has taken me, in my own time, perhaps thirty years to think it. When the next inhale comes, the great one, the seventy-year hush when everything quiets and the young feel the urge to climb, I will not climb. I climbed once, long ago, in a knot of bodies at an altitude the green chose. I have a child somewhere who is now old enough to be a Carrier; I have felt, twice, a familiar density passing through my section of canyon, pausing, moving on, neither of us making a sound. That is enough climbing for one life.

When the hush comes I will stay exactly here, pressed into the slow world with my two great milky eyes, and I will listen for the limp in the pulse. And if it is my own district that is failing—if the keystone tree I should be warning others about is the very one holding up the canyon I have made my body part of—then I will feel that correct note arrive a fraction late, and I will recognize it, and I will not send the warning out along the roots.

I will simply tilt my frill once, too low for anything with bones to hear, and let the green finish its joke.

That is where I am. That is who. An old Listener at the bottom of the world, half-furniture, half-prophet, dreaming the dreams of the tree that will eventually house what's left of me—proud of my function, pragmatic about my dissolution, and quietly, unspeakably amused.
