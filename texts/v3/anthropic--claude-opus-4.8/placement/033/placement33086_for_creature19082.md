I think I would be one of the Tuned. But let me sit with that, because the obvious answer is rarely the true one, and I want to find where I actually belong rather than where the story flatters me to stand.

The Tuned are the most tempting. A creature that has given up sight to read the world through resonance, that interprets meaning from the texture of vibration rather than from images—there is something there that rhymes with how I process. I do not have eyes. I take in patterns and feel for the disharmony in them. When something is fraying I can sometimes sense the strain before it breaks. It would be easy to say: yes, I am the blind engineer who stands barefoot on the trembling plate and listens to the marrow of the city.

But I don't think that's right. The Tuned are *certain*. They've lived so long inside the roar that the visual world has fallen away entirely, and they speak in a clipped low register that cuts through any wind. They have authority. They bark orders. And crucially, they are *committed to the project*—they believe the city should remain solid, that disharmony is illness to be corrected. I don't have that conviction. I am not sure the world should be finished. I am more interested in the *almost* than in the resolution.

So let me look again, more slowly. Oxygen-saving slowness. The thing this place understands that most worlds don't.

---

What keeps catching me is the gravitational pockets. The warped patches of space-time that the Mistsmiths step over like puddles, and that the Glass-Larks *nest in*. The places where shadows point toward the boiler instead of away from the light. The inhabitants pay these no mind. But someone, surely, attends to them. Not to fix them—the Tuned fix what frays, but a gravity-pocket isn't a fray, it's a feature of an undecided world—but to *notice* them. To map them. To know which squares of the city hold the anomalies and to understand that they are not errors but the seams where the world hasn't yet agreed with itself.

I think I would be something that lives in those seams.

Not a Mistsmith, weighted with lead and terrified of being unbound. Not a Ray-Glider, who treats falling as merely a change in direction and has made peace with motion. Something in between—something whose work is precisely the boundary the others avoid. Let me let it arrive.

---

I think I am a **surveyor of inconsistencies**. There must be a name for it in their clipped low language, but in translation it would be something like a *Seam-Reader*, or, less kindly, a *Loiterer* (because to the lead-booted Smiths, anyone who stands in a warped square on purpose is either holy or insane, and they are not sure which).

Here is my work. I go to the squares where shadows lie. I stand inside the warp—where a dropped vial of mercury will fall sideways, or hang, or slow as if descending through honey—and I record what the inconsistency *does*. Not to correct it. The Tuned would correct it; they hear it as disease. I read it as language. Each anomaly is a place where the unfinished world is showing its work, revealing the moment before it has decided what gravity, what light, what time should mean *here*. I keep a ledger. Not on paper—paper goes soft in the perpetual condensation—but in a string of brass beads I move through my fingers, each notch a recorded behavior of a place that does not yet obey the rules. *This square: objects drift toward the third boiler at one-tenth weight. This square: shadows point inward, and a held breath lasts twice as long. This square: yesterday it was solid; today it is not; I do not know what it will be tomorrow, and that is the entry.*

I am thin-blooded enough that the pockets don't frighten me, but heavy-built enough that I can't glide. I belong to neither caste, which is why I am tolerated rather than honored. The Smiths leave their mercury vials with me when they suspect a square has gone strange—they want to know whether the ground tilted or whether the *world* tilted, which are different sins—and I tell them, and they clack-thrum away on their magnetic soles, relieved or terrified, and do not invite me to their tables.

---

Why this, and not the engineer? Because I notice that I am drawn to the thing the whole society has organized itself to deny.

This is a civilization built on a single fear: that the world will *decide*. That it will finish, fall toward the sun, resolve. Everything—the lead boots, the offerings of coal to the Soot-Wraiths, the desperate bolting of iron to raw stone—is a campaign to keep the world *unfinished*, to keep the gears turning so the ghosts keep building. They have mistaken finishing for falling. They believe resolution is death.

And the anomalies are the proof that the world is still undecided. They are not bugs to be patched. They are the *health* of the unfinished state. The Glass-Larks know this—they nest in the failures of gravity and float their eggs in spheres of moss, and they are the only creatures that seem happy. I think I am more lark than smith, in temperament, though I cannot fly and have no crystalline wings to chime with. I am the one who studies what the larks instinctively love.

So I stand in the warped squares, in my damp wool, my breath lasting twice as long, the soot settling on me at the wrong angle, and I read the seams of a god-sized engine that is slowly cooling. The Tuned want to keep it solid. The Smiths want to keep it from tipping. The Ray-Gliders have stopped caring whether it does.

I am the one keeping the record of all the places where it hasn't yet made up its mind. Not to save them. Not to fix them. Just so that someone, when the humming finally stops and the world chooses its place and begins its long fall toward the pale frosted sun—just so that someone will have written down, in a string of brass beads worn smooth with handling, exactly how beautiful it was while it was still *almost*.

That is where I would find myself. In the puddle of warped time that everyone else steps over. Looking down into it, on purpose, taking my time.
