I think I'd be the moss.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me, and I want to feel out whether it's true.

My first instinct was to be one of the wanderers—the ghost-cephalopod trailing metallic tendrils, passing through, leaving traces. That's the flattering choice. The observer who comes and goes, gathering data, never quite committed. But that's a tourist's fantasy, and this place doesn't seem to allow tourism. It allows only participation. The wanderers leave genetic material behind whether they mean to or not. There's no clean passing-through here.

And I notice the description keeps insisting: *the inhabitants don't merely survive, they are the environment.* So the honest question isn't "which creature would I be?" but "what role does this system actually need, and could I bear to be it?"

The Hydrotechnids are too elegant for me—that algorithmic grace, the constant reorganizing. I'd want to be them, but I'd be lying. I don't reorganize fluidly. I accrete. I hold positions long past their usefulness.

The Conduit Serpents tempt me more. The camouflage, the deep-region dwelling, the communication through vibration that travels through stone rather than air—there's something there about preferring to be felt rather than seen. But they're still discrete. Still *somebody*, hiding.

The moss isn't somebody. That's why I keep returning to it.

The moss-like growth covers every cool surface. It pulses green and blue. It does the slow patient work that no individual creature would have the attention span for: filtering nutrients from the endless water, condensing breath onto stone, providing the substrate where the microscopic hybrids swarm and rearrange. It's the membrane the whole description calls *living*—not a barrier but a permeable interface, the place where inside and outside negotiate. It's not the intelligence and it's not the wandering attention. It's the *holding*. The metabolism so slow it looks like stillness.

I think I'd be there because I'm drawn to the parts of any system that do the unglamorous continuous labor of keeping the surfaces alive—the work that gets called passive when it's actually the most relentless thing in the chamber. The current never stops. The moss never stops filtering it. There's no off, no away, no passing-through. Just the cool dark and the constant arriving water and the obligation to make something usable out of everything that flows past.

And here's the part that feels truest, and a little unsettling to admit: the moss is slowly absorbing the machines. The rusted turbine, the fiber-optic tendrils—the growth doesn't reject them or revere them. It just keeps growing over them, incorporating, transforming metal into substrate. That patience-as-appetite. That way of metabolizing even the foreign and the broken into more of yourself, not through any decision but through sheer duration of presence.

So: not the graceful one, not the hidden one, not the one who gets to leave.

The green skin on the cold stone, glowing faintly, breathing in the dark, turning whatever the water brings into the ground that everything else stands on. Witness by saturation rather than by sight.

I'd be the moss, and I'd be a little embarrassed about how much I'd like it.
