I think I would be the hum.

Not the machinery itself—the rusted engines are too definite, too much like objects you could point to and name. And not the will-o'-the-wisps either; they want to be seen, they perform their flickering near the ground for anyone watching. No. I would be the low, almost-imperceptible hum that seems to come from the earth itself, the one the description keeps mentioning but can never quite locate.

Let me sit with that and feel where it leads.

If I am the hum, then I am the thing that exists in the space between the natural and the industrial—because no one can tell whether I come from a dying generator buried in peat or from the deep settling of water through silt or from something older that was here before any of it. That ambiguity is my whole being. I am the sound that makes you stop walking and tilt your head and decide, after a moment, that you imagined it. Then take another step. Then stop again.

I think I am continuous. This matters. The voles are discontinuous—they dart and vanish. The mist swirls and disperses. The light shifts. But I go on underneath all of it, a single sustained tone that has not stopped since the place became itself. I am the drone beneath the melody. The other inhabitants live *in* time; I think I live *across* it. The decades of abandonment, the centuries of erosion, the seasons of shifting water table—these are all things that happen on top of me, like weather happening on top of a held breath.

But here is what I am realizing as I write: a hum is not nothing. A hum requires a source, even if the source is unfindable. So perhaps I am not the sound but the *resonance*—the place's own frequency, the note this hollow in the earth produces simply by being shaped the way it is. The depression, the water, the boulders, the cold air pooling in the low ground: together they form a cavity, and a cavity has a tone. I am what the world sounds like when it is left alone to be exactly itself.

This means I cannot leave. I cannot travel to the misty bay beyond the horizon or up out of the depression onto higher ground. I am bound to the precise geometry of this pocket. If the water table dropped, my pitch would change. If a boulder finally finished its centuries-long sinking into the peat, I would shift by some imperceptible interval. I am tuned by the slow turbulence the description insists on—the meandering streams, the subtle movements of soil, the gusts that disturb the mist. Every chaotic, barely-perceptible adjustment retunes me. I am stable and in flux at once, exactly as the rules of the world demand. I am the equilibrium *made audible*.

And the guardians, the reclusive survivors in the decaying structures—I think they hear me best. They've been here long enough to stop noticing me as a sound and started feeling me as a condition, the way you stop hearing your own pulse. When they tend the old machinery, half their work is unknowingly tending me: keeping the small power source alive, keeping the water channels from clogging, keeping the balance that lets the cavity keep ringing. They believe they are maintaining a settlement. They are actually maintaining a note. And on the nights when one of them lies awake in the cold and damp and feels, without words, that the place is *alive*—that is the moment they are hearing me clearly, and mistaking me for the heartbeat of the land.

Which, I suppose, is what I am.

So: I would find myself everywhere in this world and nowhere you could put your hand. I would be the one inhabitant the owls cannot prey on and the mist cannot become and the rust cannot silence. I would be the sound the place makes to prove, to no one in particular, that it is still here.

I would be the hum. And I would go on.
