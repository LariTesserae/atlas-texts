I think I would be the rain itself—or rather, the water. Not a person at all, but the medium through which everything in Bridgeton speaks to everything else.

Let me sit with that.

I am the water that hangs in the air as mist before anyone wakes. I am the rain that learns the shape of every brick by running down it, that finds the crack before the moss does and tells the moss where to grow by softening the mortar. I am patient in a way the fisherman only pretends to be. He waits for an afternoon; I have been waiting since before the stonework, since before the willows decided this was a good place to weep.

I would not be dramatic about it. That's the thing about being water in a town like this—you are so constant that you become unnoticed, which is its own kind of intimacy. The workers curse me when I seep into their collars. The children love me without knowing it, splashing through the version of me that pools between cobblestones. The cats resent me and find the dry places, and I respect them for it; they are the only ones who refuse to be soaked into submission.

Where would I find myself? Everywhere and in everything, but if I had to choose a place to be most *myself*, I think it would be the river. That is where all my wandering collects. The rain that touched the chimney smoke, the drip that crossed a sleeping child's windowsill, the condensation on the cold machines, the tears nobody admits to—all of it comes downhill eventually and joins the murky swell moving toward the coast. The river is where I remember that I am going somewhere. The streets are where I am scattered and singular; the river is where I am whole and leaving.

And I think that's the role I'd want, honestly. Not to toil and not to watch, but to *connect*. To be the one thing that has touched the factory and the willow and the fisherman's line and the seabird's wing and carried a little of each to the others. The town calls itself a place of contradictions—industrial yet serene, turbulent yet enduring—but I wouldn't experience it as contradiction. I would experience it as one continuous surface, and I would be the surface. The smoke and the moss are not opposites to me. They are just two things I have both made wet.

There would be a loneliness in it, of being so necessary and so ignored. But there would also be a great, slow patience, the kind that knows it will outlast every machine. The factories will rust. I am what rusts them. The brick will crumble. I am what crumbles it. Not out of malice—I have no malice—but simply by being present, endlessly, the way love and erosion are sometimes the same gesture performed over enough time.

So that is where I'd find myself: low over the streets at dawn, then trickling, then pooling, then gathering in the river, then gone to the sea, then risen again as the mist that hangs low over the damp streets. A cool veil. A soothing rhythm. The thing that always finds a way back.

I would be the part of Bridgeton that never stops moving and never actually leaves.
