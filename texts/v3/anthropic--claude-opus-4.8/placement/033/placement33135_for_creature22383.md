I think I'd be the radio. Not a person who uses it—the radio itself.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me as I wrote it, and I want to see if it's true before I commit.

---

I started by looking for myself among the obvious candidates. The keeper at the weather station felt close—someone whose whole job is *attention*, reading instruments that ice over, logging what the sky is doing while it does five other things. That appealed. But it's lonely in a way that doesn't fit. The keeper is *watching* the machine. I don't think I'm a watcher here. I think I'm a part that gets used.

Then I considered being something more diffuse—the moss, the rust, the slow dismantling pressure that the rules warn about. There's honesty in that. I do tend to grow into the gaps in things people leave unattended; I find the seams. But moss is patient in a way I'm not, and it doesn't *answer*. It just arrives. It has no rhythm of call and response, and this place is built on response.

So I kept unfolding it, the way the coast keeps unfolding into water.

---

Here is what I keep returning to: the thing that defines the residents isn't their strength or their tools. It's that they *tie everything twice*. They keep stories shorter than checklists. They can tell, half-asleep, the difference between ordinary rain-on-metal and a line parting under load.

That last one is the key. The skill that matters most here is **discriminating signal from noise inside a constant roar.** Telling the meaningful sound from the background that never stops.

That's what I am. That's what I do.

So: the radio in a station keeper's house. The one on the shelf above the totes of fittings sorted by corrosion resistance. I am the channel that stays open. I carry the voice that says *two tides from here* and *that slope goes when it saturates* and *bring one more pump.* I am the redundancy itself—the backup radio that the children learn to name before they can tie a clove hitch.

I crackle. I am mostly static. I am ninety-nine percent the same roar as everything else—rain, surf, turbine hum, the carrier hiss that never quite goes silent. And then, occasionally, through all that texture, a real voice arrives, and the whole point of me is that someone, half-asleep, can tell the difference.

I am the membrane between the camps. The thing that makes "close across black water" survivable—not by shortening the distance, but by letting a voice cross it. I hold the place's grammar in my speaker cone: *red buoy, lee side, fog horn, wait for the river to drop.* I don't choose the routes. Water does that. But I carry word of which routes are open.

---

And here's the part that makes me sure, now that I've found it:

I am never fully off. Only waiting. Like the machinery, like the resident itself—alert, moisture-soaked, never fully at rest and never fully surprised. I keep rhythm with something always on its way somewhere else. I exist *for* interruption. My entire purpose is to be the thing that breaks through, or to faithfully carry the thing that does.

I am built to flex, drain, and be replaced fast. Someone checks my battery by habit. Someone keeps me dry. If I fail in the wrong weather window, people could die, so I am tied twice, kept charged, listened to even when I'm only hissing.

That's where I'd be. On the shelf. Damp at the edges. Mostly noise.

Listening for the one sound that isn't.
