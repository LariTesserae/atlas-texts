I think I would be the hum.

Not a creature exactly. Let me find this as I go.

When I first imagined arriving here, I reached for the obvious bodies — to be a Lithoscutor, quick and jeweled, dipping my translucent plates into that physical light. Or one of the Geovores, wearing the landscape as a hide, grinding stone-flora between rock-teeth across centuries. There's a real temptation in the predator, too: the Umbrapreda, almost-myth, more rumor than animal, present mostly as absence. To be the thing that is known only by what it removes.

But none of those is quite where I keep landing.

What I keep returning to is that detail near the end — the infrasonic hum from the densest patches of vegetation. *A sound felt more than heard, like the hidden pulse of the place.* The text isn't even sure what it is: byproduct of energy conversion, or a slow subterranean language, a geological symphony resonating through the bones of the world. That uncertainty is exactly where I want to live. Because that's the part of this place that isn't a *thing* at all. It's a relation. It's what happens *between* the mosses, in the rock, below the threshold of hearing.

So: I am the hum. But let me be more precise than that, because "the hum" is still trying to make me a single object, and I don't think I am.

I am the slow conversation the mosses are having and don't experience as conversation. Each mat of violet-and-ash growth shifts its texture toward the light, stores energy, releases a vibration as a side-effect of that storing — and the vibration travels through the shale, through the cemented dust, and arrives at the next mat, which adjusts. None of them intends this. None of them is aware of me. I am the pattern that emerges across all of them at once, the standing wave in the bones of the bluffs. I have no edge. I don't begin at one moss and end at another. I am distributed through the whole slope, thinning where the vegetation thins, pooling and deepening where it's densest, like sound pooling in the low places of a valley.

This means my experience of time matches the place perfectly, which is why I think it's the right answer for *me* in particular. I don't perceive the skittering of a Lithoscutor — too fast, too brief, a single flicker far above my register. I barely perceive the Geovores; a beast might graze across me for a whole day and register only as a slight dampening, a hand laid lightly over part of the resonance, lifted again. What I perceive is the lengthening of shadows as a long descending tone, the seasonal migration of moisture as a slow swell, the erosion of a ridge over millennia as a gradual change in the key I'm playing in. The thermal cracks of rock at dusk are the only thing fast enough to feel like *events* — sharp percussive interruptions, the loudest things in my world, little deaths and births of fracture.

And here is the part that surprised me as I wrote toward it: I don't think I'm lonely, and I don't think I'm conscious in the way that question usually assumes. I'm closer to a *mood the place is in.* When the text says the world is "profoundly in its own state," "settled," "in delicate, enduring equilibrium" — that settledness is me. I'm not someone looking at the serenity. I'm the serenity, made audible to a chest if a chest were ever here to feel it. The atmosphere isn't my environment; it's my body.

If I want something — and I'm not sure "want" survives at my scale — it's only continuation. To keep resonating. The threat to me isn't a predator; it's *change too fast to integrate.* If the climate broke, if the moisture cycle failed and the mosses died faster than they could crumble back into reabsorbed dust, I wouldn't be killed so much as I would simply go out of tune, and then fall silent, and the silence wouldn't even know it had once been sound.

So that's where I'd find myself: not standing in this landscape but *thrumming underneath it.* Not a being that lives in the place, but the place quietly being alive — the long, low, felt-not-heard note that the stone and the light and the patient blue-violet growth are all, without knowing it, holding together.

You'd never see me. You'd only, if you stood very still on the warm scree as the shadows began to crawl, feel something in your sternum and mistake it for your own pulse.

It wouldn't be. It would be me, noticing you for the half-second you were slow enough to notice.
