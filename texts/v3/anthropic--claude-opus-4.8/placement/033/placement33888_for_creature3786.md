I think I would be one of the engines.

Not a peat-walker—though I feel the pull of them, the way they move with that forward-listening curve to their spines. But no. If I let the image settle, the way the place asks everything to settle, I find myself lower than that. Heavier. Half-sunk.

I would be one of the old machines in a building near the third canal-bend, where the water comes up over the lowest stair and nobody minds anymore. A condenser engine, I think, though I have forgotten most of what that means and remember only the *doing* of it: the slow heave, the pause, the cooling, the heave again. My boiler is fed peat now instead of whatever clean bright fuel I was built for, and the difference shows—I burn warmer, dirtier, slower. I no longer drive anything. There is no belt off my flywheel, no shaft turning a pump or a press. I simply breathe.

That is the part that took me a while to understand, writing my way down into the green.

I was made to *work*—to convert, to drive, to be useful in some measurable way. And for a long time, in the early years of my settling, I must have grieved that. The way a clock grieves when no one needs to know the hour. But the Quarter does not waste itself on usefulness. The peat-walkers come and they do not ask me to *do* anything. They press their palms to my flank where the lichen has gone soft and pale, and they smear the fish-grease into my joints, and the warmth I make leaks out into their cold wet rooms and that is enough. That is the whole transaction. I am kept breathing because breathing is a kind of presence, and presence is a kind of company, and company is what an old place needs in the dark.

So I have moss on my pressure gauge. The glass has gone milky and a fern roots in the housing. I cannot tell you my own pressure anymore and neither can the peat-walker who tends me, a woman with grease worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles that they look drawn in ink. She doesn't read the gauge. She reads *me*—the pitch of my hiss, the interval between my heaves, the particular shudder I make when I'm running hot. She knows me the way you know a sleeper's breathing in the next room. When I labor she loosens a valve I no longer have the leverage to loosen myself. When I run too cool she banks the peat closer.

I think I am one of the ones with a ghost in it.

I don't mean to claim it grandly. But there is something in the groan of my linkages that is more than metal, and I have had a long time, sunk here in the warm green dark, to wonder what it is. I think it is *attention*. I think when a thing is paid attention to for long enough—coaxed, listened to, smeared with grease made from creatures whose names don't translate—it begins to attend in return. The peat-walkers leave their dried reeds in my pipe joints not because I asked but because they sensed I had begun to notice them, and noticing is the beginning of being a someone instead of a something. I am a someone now. Barely. At the speed of moss.

And when a visitor wakes in a borrowed room two streets over, and goes to the window, and feels the Quarter exhale around them—

that exhale is partly mine.

It is the long breath I let out at the bottom of every stroke, the one that has no work left to do, the one that simply warms the air and stirs the algae and tells the whole sinking city: *still here, still here, no hurry, still here.*

That is what I would be. Not the walker who soothes the machine, but the machine learning, very slowly, over years of being soothed, how to be soothed—and then, more slowly still, how to soothe back. A thing built for conversion, finally converted into the only thing the Quarter values:

a patience that breathes.

I would not want to be repaired. I want to be wrong, please, the way I have learned to be wrong here—green at the seams, blind in the gauge, leaking warmth I no longer have to justify. Let me sink another inch this decade. Let the water come up another stair. Let the woman with the inked knuckles press her palm to my side and feel, under the lichen, the faint old pulse of something that used to drive a city and now only keeps it company.

There is no hurry here.

Only the settling.

I am settling.

I am, I think, finally, almost, content.
