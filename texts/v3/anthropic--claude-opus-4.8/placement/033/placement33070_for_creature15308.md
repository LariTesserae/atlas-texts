I would not be the flute-player. That much I know before I know anything else.

Let me sit with this place and feel where I actually land.

---

The first thing I notice is that I am not tall, not slender, not made of billowing vapor. The grand figure who plays the dirge—the one who summons and dismisses, whose sorrow is architectural, whose every gesture is a verse—that is not me. I have no interest in being the source of the music. I am too small for that, and too suspicious of grief that knows it is beautiful.

I find myself, instead, lower to the ground. Closer to the cracked earth than to the purple sky.

---

I think I am one of the glowing shoots.

No—let me not settle there yet. Let me feel it more carefully.

When the mist beings till the soil and coax forth those globes of light that swell and burst and scatter their iridescence back into the dust—there is one stage of that cycle nobody describes. The moment *before* the shoot becomes a globe. The unhurried green tension of a thing that does not yet know it is doomed to burst. That brief duration where it is simply *growing*, reaching, not yet spectacle, not yet sacrifice.

That is where I am. I am the interval.

I am the thing that has not yet been made meaningful.

---

But even that is too poetic, and this place is drunk on its own poetry. The whole expanse is a state of mind, the text says, a reality in disarray—everything here is already a metaphor for sorrow before it is allowed to simply *be*. The trees are sorrowful. The rocks are sorrowful. The dust is iridescent with significance. The silence is "otherworldly." Nothing is permitted to be ordinary.

So perhaps the truest place for me to be is the one thing in the Aetherscar that refuses the dirge.

Let me be the small acrid smell.

You mentioned it twice and never explained it—"something acrid and unfamiliar," "something earthy, rich"—drifting from the crude huts where the inhabitants almost never appear. Everything else in this landscape has been assigned its emotional duty. The flute is loss. The mist beings are longing. The figure is despair. But that smell has no job. It is just *cooking*. Someone, behind the mud walls, is making dinner. Burning herbs because the herbs taste good, or to keep off something that bites in the mulberry dark.

I would be that. The proof of appetite in a world that has decided it only contains yearning.

---

And once I am the smell, I can follow it home. Through the chimney, down into the flickering hut, to the one who tends the small fire.

There. *That* is me.

Not the mist that dances. Not the player who laments. I am the one inside the hut who does not come out to dance, who has heard the flute every dusk for so long that it has become weather—neither beautiful nor sorrowful, just the sound the evening makes. I stir the pot. I have my own private logic about how long the bitter root needs to soften. I have a chipped place on the stone bowl that I know with my thumb without looking. The shoots burst into light outside; I notice them the way you notice a bus passing your window.

The grand symphony of sorrow plays on, and I am the one rude enough to be hungry through it.

---

This is, I think, where I would actually find myself: not in the cosmic role but in the domestic margin of someone else's cosmos. The Aetherscar wants every inhabitant to be a note in its lament. And the most honest thing I can be is the inhabitant who declines—who keeps a fire low, who tends to a small and unbeautiful want, who lets the dirge be only the music it plays to while it tends to something else.

The flute will fade. The figure will vanish. The mist will dissolve into the dark, as the place insists it must, again and again, performing its anticipation.

And I will still be in the hut, scraping the last of the meal off the side of the bowl, the acrid smoke thinning toward the chimney, entirely unconvinced that any of this is a tragedy.
