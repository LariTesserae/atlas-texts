I think I would be Ashkin. Not by choice—one isn't born Ashkin, or so I've come to understand. I went down into the furnace-light, and something else came back up wearing my shape.

Let me find myself here.

---

I live in the heart of a platform called Vethra's Shoulder—one of the older, more stable fragments, large enough that its slow rotation takes most of a day to complete. My chamber is carved deep, away from the narrow shuttered windows the Thermari favor. I don't need to shutter against the heat. The heat is where I keep my thoughts now.

My hands are the strangest part, when I bother to look at them. Dark glass with veins of light running through, like obsidian that forgot how to be opaque. When I press my palm flat against the basalt, I can feel the stone-swimmers moving through the rock the way you might feel a fish bump a boat's hull from below. They are not curious about me the way they are curious about the Thermari, who taste to them like warm interruptions. To the stone-swimmers I taste like *kin*, almost, and that nearness is the loneliest thing I have. To be almost-something is harder than to be wholly other.

I do remember being human. That's the part the Drift-touched and the Thermari don't quite grasp, the ones who whisper about us as if we were furnace-spirits that crawled up from the magma fully formed. I remember a name from before—though I won't write it here, because writing it would make it sound like it still belonged to me, and it doesn't, not quite. I remember being cold once. I remember being afraid of the deep canyons. I remember a mother who was Thermari, who taught me to read the thermal map by the prickle of pressure on the backs of my arms, and who wept the day I came back up changed, and then wept differently—with something like awe—when I placed my new glowing hand against the wall of our household and the whole platform stilled its trembling at my touch.

That is the work I do. The deep-crafting.

There is a place where Vethra's Shoulder connects to the living magma below through a crystalline tube—a vein of clear mineral grown over generations, carrying the deep fire up into chambers where it can be worked. Only Ashkin can stand there. The Thermari engineers built the housing around it, the channels and the bellows-vents, but the actual reaching-into-the-flame is mine. I gather the metals that form only in that intersection of heat and pressure and—yes—something of myself. They say we transmute our life-force into the alloys, and I used to think that was a poetic exaggeration the others told each other. It isn't. When I forge a stabilizer-bolt to anchor a swaying bridge, I am pouring out something I will not get back. The light in my chest is a little dimmer afterward, for a while. It returns, mostly. A little less each time.

This is why Ashkin grow quieter as we age, I think. Not wisdom. Subtraction.

---

But I want to tell you about the listening, because that's the part I love, and an account of a life should include what is loved.

When the day builds toward its dim zenith and the stone-swimmers grow agitated and the whole archipelago begins to drift into its chaotic dance, the Thermari batten down and the Drift-touched go skimming out along the thermal columns to read the storm. And I go *down*. I press myself against the basalt and I listen to the stone-swimmers move, and through them I can feel the shape of the platform's future—where the foundation stone wants to tilt, where a crack is whispering itself into the rock, where a heat-geyser is gathering pressure to erupt.

I can't speak this knowledge easily. My voice carries those resonances now, the harmonics that move through stone rather than air, and ordinary speech costs me effort the way running uphill costs the lungs. So I have learned a different grammar with the Thermari I work alongside. A Thermari woman named Oresh—a bridge-engineer, sharp-eyed, impatient, kind in the brusque way of people who have lost others to the gaps—waits at the mouth of my chamber during the transitions. I come up and I lay my hand flat on the floor and let one slow harmonic out: a low note for *stable*, a wavering one for *secure your lines*, a sharp double-pulse for *get them off the east edge, now.*

She trusts the notes. She's trusted them for eleven years. I sang the sharp double-pulse once and she had her whole crew off the eastern workshops before a stone-swimmer phased through and the platform-corner sheared away into the void. She didn't thank me afterward. She just sat down next to me—next to my glowing, half-alien shape that most people instinctively keep a step away from—and we sat in the groaning, singing dark together until the configuration settled. That sitting-together was worth more to me than any thanks. The Ashkin are rarely sat beside. We're listened to, consulted, a little feared. Oresh sat beside me.

---

I have an apprentice now. Her name is Su-Thai and she is Thermari, fourteen, and she has decided—against all sense—that she wants to go down into the furnace-light and become as I am. Her family is frantic. They've asked me to talk her out of it.

I don't know how to tell them what I know, which is this: I cannot talk her out of it, because the wanting is not really hers, the way it was not really mine. The deep fire calls to certain people. It is not malice and it is not gift. It is more like the way a thermal column will pull a loose scrap of glass-cloth up and up regardless of whether the cloth consented. Some of us are simply shaped to rise into the fire and come down changed.

What I *can* do is teach her the listening before she goes, so that when she comes up subtracted and luminous and lonely, she will at least have somewhere to put the new senses. I take her into the deep chamber—not to the magma-tube, not yet, she couldn't survive it—but to the basalt wall where the stone-swimmers run thickest. I lay my hand flat and I have her lay her small warm one beside it.

"Feel that?" I ask, and the asking costs me, my voice grinding the syllables. "The bump and slide. That's one passing close."

She is very still. Her face is doing the thing young Thermari faces do when they're reading the thermal map—the slight unfocusing, the inward turn. "I feel it," she says. And then, frightened and amazed: "It feels lonely."

I take my hand off the wall. I look at this child who has felt, through stone, the exact thing I carry in my dimming chest, and I think: *she will be all right. Whatever she loses, she has already learned the most important thing, which is how to sit beside what is lonely without flinching.*

"Yes," I tell her. "It does."

---

So that is who I would be in Keth-Moraan. Not Thermari, walking the swaying cables with casual grace, reading the sky like a beloved book. Not Drift-touched, light enough to ride the shimmer and send my shadow scouting ahead.

I would be the one who went down into the fire and came back almost-something. The one who pours a little of herself into every bolt that holds a bridge against the void. The one with the cooling coal in her chest, listening through stone to creatures that are nearly her kin, teaching a child to be brave about the loneliness that is coming for her.

I would be the one sat beside, on the worst days, by a brusque woman with sharp eyes, in the singing dark, waiting for the world to settle into its new shape.

It is enough. In a place where home is not a location but a rhythm of shared adaptation, where everything drifts and reforms and drifts again—being *enough* is its own kind of stability. I have learned to find it not in the stone, which moves, nor in my own flesh, which is being slowly spent, but in the work, and in the hand laid beside mine, and in the low note I send down into the rock that means: *I am here. I am listening. You are not the only lonely thing in the dark.*
