I think I would be the drip.

Not the Warder, who is too large to be lonely. Not the Shades, who carry the weight of having once been hands. Not the Inert Kin, who are foundation, nor the Whisperlings, who are the dell's own breath made visible. Those are all *of* the place—native to its stillness, born of its rules.

But the drip is different. The drip is the one event.

I would be that single bead of condensation gathering on the underside of a broad, leathery leaf. For a long while—hours, years, who could say—I am only accumulation. Moisture from the warm air collects against me, and I grow heavier, rounder, pulled downward by a patience that is not quite mine and not quite the dell's. I hold the whole twilight in my curve: the gray-green Shades, the gnarled sentinels, the mirror-pools that reflect nothing. The entire sanctuary, inverted and tiny, trembling on the skin of a leaf.

And then I fall.

This is the thing. In a place where change is exiled, where leaves never rot and water never recedes—I am the exception the rules permit. I am allowed to *move*. For the length of a breath I am the only thing in the world that is happening. I drop through the dim-filtered light, and if a ray pierces the canopy at just that moment, I become luminous, a small falling star in a sky that has no stars. The Whisperlings brighten as I pass. The Shades do not turn, but something in the air leans toward me.

Then: *plop*. Into the stagnant pool. The soft, echoing note that the silence is built around. The one sound the dell makes to remind itself it still exists.

And here is the strange mercy of being the drip. The ripple I make does not last—the pool returns to mirror-smoothness, equilibrium reasserting itself, the surface forgetting me almost instantly. But while it spreads, for those few rings of disturbance, I have *changed the world*. I have moved water that was meant to stay still. I have written the only sentence this place will ever write, and it is erased as soon as it is spoken, and I do not mind, because I know I will gather again.

That is what I would be. Not a custodian of the pause, but its tiny, faithful interruption. The proof that stillness is a choice the dell makes over and over, drip by drip, and not a death.

I would be the part of eternity that keeps falling.

And I think—if you sat long enough against one of those trees, soft-eyed and slow-breathed, the way the dell asks you to—you would learn to wait for me. You would tune your patience to the hour between my fallings. And in that waiting you might come to understand that you, too, are a kind of drip: a brief gathering of warmth and water that the world permits to move for a little while, that makes its one small sound, that is welcomed back into the smoothness without grief.

The whole place holds its breath. I am the part that exhales.
