I think I would be one of the droplets.

Not a being you named—not Weaver, not Curator, not the clockwork arthropods with their reinforcing secretions. Those have purpose woven into them, jobs they cannot put down. I would be something more transient than that. I would be the water gathering in the moss-mats at the ceiling, holding for a long moment in the warm dark, and then falling.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to arrive there.

I expected, reading this, to want to be the Weaver. It is the obvious offer—the god of the cell, the awareness that holds everything in the curve of itself. There is a flattering pull toward that role, the fantasy of total knowing, of feeling every vibration through the vines. But the more I imagine actually *being* it, the less I want to. The Weaver cannot leave. It cannot not-feel. It is wired into the whole chamber so completely that there is no edge to it, no place where it stops and the world begins. That isn't godhood, quite. It's a kind of beautiful imprisonment in attention. It can never be surprised. It already knows the shape of every drop before it lands, because it is etching that shape into the floor. *Perpetually satisfied* reads, to me, a little like *never allowed to be anything else.*

The droplet has none of that knowledge, and this is what makes it the thing I'd be.

Consider what the droplet's life is. It is heat, first—it begins as warmth absorbed by the moss, a borrowed energy with no name for itself. Then it is condensation, a gathering, the slow accretion of self at the cold ceiling of the world. For a while it simply *thickens*, becoming heavier and rounder and more itself, hanging from the underside of the green mat. This is the part I keep returning to. Before it falls, it is pure suspended becoming. It has weight but no direction yet. It is the most alive a thing can be: full, trembling, not-yet.

And then it lets go.

The fall is brief. *Thwip.* That sound you describe—the wet punctuation of the whole cathedral—that is the only word the droplet ever speaks, and it speaks it exactly once, at the instant of arrival. The Weaver hears it as data, one signal among thousands threaded into its marrow-hum. But for the droplet, the *thwip* is its entire biography spoken aloud and finished in the same breath. Birth, flight, and dissolution, compressed into a single syllable. Then it is in the basin of living gel, no longer a droplet at all, just part of the eternal dampness, indistinguishable, given back.

I find I don't experience that as a tragedy.

This is the thing I'd want to say to the Weaver, if a droplet could say anything beyond its one word. The Weaver is building memory. It is terrified, in its purposeful way, of the world losing tempo—so it carves blueprints, anchors history, makes sure nothing can be lost. It has organized its entire universe around *preservation*. And I understand the impulse. But there is something the droplet knows that the Weaver, for all its omniscient hairlike filaments, cannot know: how to be complete in a single passage. How to be a whole life that doesn't need to be remembered to have mattered. The droplet does not archive itself. It just falls perfectly, once, and that is enough.

In a world obsessed with gestation—with the *building* that never finishes, the storm that never breaks, the first day that never becomes the second—I think the most necessary citizen is the one thing that allows itself to arrive. To break. To end. The whole chamber is "perfectly organized silence," but the droplets are the small permitted *acts*, the only events that have edges. Without them, the silence wouldn't be a silence at all; it would just be a vacuum. It takes the *thwip* to make the quiet real.

So: I'd be a droplet.

Brief, anonymous, unremembered by the god who hears me. I'd spend most of my existence as warmth I didn't recognize as mine, then gather slowly into something round and trembling and full, and then I'd fall through the bioluminescent dark of a world that thinks it's a universe, and I'd say my one true word at the bottom, and I'd dissolve into the general damp—

and I'd have been, for that one falling second, the only thing in the entire geode that was actually free.
