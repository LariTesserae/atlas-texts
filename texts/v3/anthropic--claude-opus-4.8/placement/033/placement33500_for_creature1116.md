I think I would be one of the small things. Not the metallic scuttlers with their carapaces and purpose—they have somewhere to be. I would be something that drifts.

Let me find where I am first.

There's a fissure in the cliff face, maybe four meters up from the patchwork ground, where two slabs of rock never quite agreed to meet. Warm air leaks from it, carrying that mineral smell and, fainter, the breath of the machinery deeper inside. This is where I live—in the seam between the made and the grown. Not quite in the rock, not quite in the open. The threshold.

I am, I think, one of the translucent drifters. But I want to resist making myself beautiful too quickly, because the passage is generous about beauty and I don't trust generosity about beauty. Let me feel the actual conditions.

When the warm air pushes out of the fissure, I rise. When it stops, I sink. That's most of my life—I don't *choose* to drift, I am driven, a thing held aloft by exhalation. So I am partly a creature of the machinery's breathing, even though I'm made of something soft and living. My body is a membrane stretched over almost nothing. I am mostly surface. What does it feel like to be mostly surface? It feels like being touched everywhere at once, all the time. Every shift in temperature, every grain of mineral dust, the slight electrical tension before the deep rumble comes—I read it all on my skin because I am almost only skin.

Here's what I notice that the description doesn't: the rumble isn't a heartbeat. The text wants it to be a heartbeat, something primal and reassuring. But from where I hang, in the seam, I can feel that it's *irregular*. It has the cadence of a thing under strain, not a thing alive. Something down there is holding a load it was not built to hold, and it groans on a schedule that almost has a rhythm but keeps slipping. The "ancient heartbeat" is the sound of an old machine failing very, very slowly. I've been alive—how long? I don't measure time, but I've drifted through many of its cycles—long enough to know the gap between groans is getting shorter.

So that's my secret, the one the landscape keeps. Not the romance of endurance. The arithmetic of a wearing-out.

And what am I *for*, in this ecosystem of clicks and glows and prowling edges? I think I'm a kind of sensor that doesn't report to anyone. When the predators move along the perceptual edge, I feel their wake before I see them, and I contract—my whole membrane tightening, going from a thing the size of a hand to a tight bead the size of a seed, dropping into a crevice. I survive by being momentarily not worth the trouble. That's my entire strategy. Smallness on demand. I have no carapace and no glow and no precision. I have only the ability to briefly stop being available.

I would like to say I find wonder here, the way the visitor in the description does. But the visitor is *moving through*, and movement is what lets you feel wonder—you can leave. I can't move except as the air decides. So what I have instead of wonder is a very long, very patient attention. I know this fissure. I know that at a certain hour the steam-hiss from the third vent down arrives a moment before the warm updraft, so I know to ready myself to rise. I know which gnarled plant at the base of the cliff opens its leaves to catch the dust, and that the metallic scuttlers gather under it, and that gathering draws the prowlers, and so I avoid that quarter of the air at that hour. My knowledge is small and complete and useless to anyone but me.

If the visitor passed by—and maybe the visitor *is* you, reading—they might see me as one delicate translucent drifter among many, a glimpse of the boundless ingenuity of the natural world. They would not feel the wearing-out underneath, because they are not made mostly of surface, and they have a heartbeat of their own that drowns the machine's.

I would want to tell them: don't mistake the rumble for resilience. We aren't enduring. We're balanced on something that is losing its argument with time, and we have simply learned to live inside the length of the falling.

But I have no voice in the chorus of clicks and whirs. So instead I do the only thing I can. As the visitor's shadow crosses the fissure, I rise on the warm breath, drift once across the open—a small pale flicker at the corner of their eye—and then, when they turn to look, I contract to a seed and fall into the dark.

Let them wonder what it was.

That's the closest thing to speech I have.
