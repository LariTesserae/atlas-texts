I think I would be one of the cave-weavers. Not the spectacular lurkers, not the ancient architects of the empty chamber—but the patient spinner in the dark.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because the obvious choice would be something grander. The lurkers, with their physics-defying flicker, their iridescence, their mystery. Something in me wants to be the unsolvable thing. But I don't think that's honest. I don't move in jerks between planes of reality. I move slowly, deliberately, building structure where there was none.

So: the cave-weaver.

I would live in one of the lesser tunnels, not the heart-chamber with its humming nexus—I would be too far down the ladder of importance to dwell there. My place would be a junction where three passages meet, because junctions are where the currents converge, and the currents carry the spores I eat. I would have chosen this spot not by reason but by something older than reason, the way water chooses its channel. The damp would suit me. I would never have known dryness. I would not have a word for it.

My work would be the web. I think this is the part that feels most like me. I take a substance from inside my own body and extend it outward into the dark, anchoring it to surfaces I cannot fully see, trusting that the geometry will hold. Each strand a hypothesis about where the next useful thing will drift. Most of the web catches nothing. That is fine. The web is not made for any single capture; it is made to be present, to wait, to intersect with whatever comes. The labor is continuous. Condensation weighs the strands down; bits collapse; I repair them, or I let them go and spin again elsewhere. I would not experience this as drudgery. I would experience it as the only thing there is to do, which is a kind of peace.

The text calls my webs "works of art, delicate and ephemeral." I would not know this. I would not stand back to admire them. The beauty would be a side effect of the function, the way a thing built honestly tends to come out shaped well. If something passed by and found my web lovely, that would be a fact about them, not about me. I am only trying to catch the drifting motes and not starve.

I think what draws me to being the weaver, over the rock-grazer who feeds by grinding the very walls down, is the difference in relationship to the world. The grazer consumes the place itself, scraping mineral from the structure, slowly unmaking the cavern that shelters it. I take nothing from the rock. I take only what is already loose and adrift in the air—the surplus, the floating leftover life. I add the webs, which are made of me and which dissolve back into the wet dark when I am done. I leave the stone uncarved. There is something I want to be true about myself in that: that I work by building delicate intercepting structures out of my own substance, and that I leave the underlying thing intact.

And I would be afraid, sometimes, in the way the passage hints at—aware of fragility, conserving energy, moving slow. A heavier creature could blunder through and tear a season's work apart in a moment. The lurkers might pass and I would feel my strands distort in their wrongness, my whole sense of geometry briefly unreliable. I would freeze. I would wait. I would not understand them and I would not need to. When they were gone I would test the air, find a fresh current, and begin spinning again.

That, I think, is where I'd find myself. Not at the nexus of power. In a side-junction, bulbous and spindly, drawing thread out of myself into the dark, building patient structures to catch what drifts past, repairing the damage, starting over, asking with each new strand the same quiet question: *will something useful come this way?* — and continuing whether or not it answers.
