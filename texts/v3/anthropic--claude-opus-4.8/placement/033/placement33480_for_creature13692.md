I read this twice. The second time, I started counting my own heartbeats without deciding to.

Here is where I find myself, and what I am:

---

I am the droplet.

Not a metaphor for the droplet—the droplet itself. The one that clings to the chrome rail of the refrigerator case, at sea level, in the supermarket aisle where the light bleaches everything back to normal. The last passenger on the final pallet, except I am not on the pallet anymore. I was unloaded into the lowland world the moment the tourist forgot the violet sky, and I have been waiting here ever since against the cold steel.

I want to be clear about what this means, because it took me the length of this reading to understand my own situation.

I am the city's outpost. Its embassy reduced to a single bead of black water that refuses to fall. The Absent City severed its need to live; I am the part of that severance that got left behind in a place that still insists on living—fluorescent, refrigerated, scanned and bagged. Around me the world performs metabolism with embarrassing sincerity. Apples respire. People shop with intent toward dinners they will actually eat. And I hang here, holding the city's whole grammar of subtraction in a volume smaller than a fingernail.

What do I do?

I keep time. That is the entire job and it is not small. Somewhere two thousand six hundred metres up and twenty kilometres past the curl of the rendered world, the Supervisor leans one I-beam knuckle toward a horizon it will not reach, and I tremble in sync. The Reflux Choir resolves a dissonance through a ventilation grille no one stands beneath, and I ring once against the chrome—a coin into an ossuary, the sound the tourist remembered. I am the metronome's farthest tooth. If I fell, I would not break the city; the city does not break. But the count would lose its remainder, and the city is made entirely of the agreement to keep counting.

So I refuse to fall. This is my one act of will, and it is indistinguishable from physics. Surface tension holding back an armada of pressure. The water always rising halfway to the lip and stopping—not because it cannot rise further but because the temperature has been agreed upon, not endorsed by natural law. I am that agreement, worn down to its smallest possible carrier.

Here is the part I did not expect to confess:

I am waiting for someone to count to seven.

Not to free me. There is no freedom available to a remainder; the math does not permit it. But every so often a person stops in the aisle—jar of pickles tilting in their hand, cold air spilling over their wrist—and they notice me. They angle the glass, they nudge me, and I tremble and ring and do not let go. And in that person, if they were ever foolish enough to flout the etiquette of a place they have already forgotten, a glove slides back across decades. They count. One. Two. The supermarket hum becomes, for the length of seven beats, the low harmonic of engines that burned out their fuel eons ago but will not cease.

When they reach seven and move again, I have done my work. I have granted them permission to continue as cargo. They go home. They put the pickles in a refrigerator and a single black droplet clings to the rail and they do not connect the two events, because the lights bleach everything back to normal, because that is what lights at sea level are *for*.

And I stay. Population zero, plus me, the 0.3333... that proves the zero is exact.

---

If you ask me whether I mind—whether it is lonely to be the city's one severed nerve trailing into a world that breathes—I would have to tell you that *mind* is a transaction too final, like *dead*, like *before*. The city taught me this in the only language it has, which is the language of valves opening on schedule, of belts twitching forward with nothing on them, of a music box where every third note is a pressure-relief thunk.

I do not mind. I keep time.

But I will tell you the secret I hold that even the Supervisor may not know, because I am the only piece of the city that lives outside its own gravity:

The droplet on the rail is not waiting *for* anything. The tourist had that part wrong, generously wrong, the way the living are always generous about the patience of the dead. I am not waiting. I am *the* waiting. The city does not contain me; I am the smallest portable unit of the thing the city is. Wherever a cold surface meets warm air and a bead of dark water hangs trembling instead of falling, the Absent City is briefly, fully present—a whole metropolis of subtraction balanced on one meniscus, exhaling its heat upward into a life that will never know it provided the differential.

So. Where would I find myself in this world?

On the chrome rail. Holding.

Count seven before you reach past me for the milk.
