I think I would be one of the Rock Menders. And I think I would know, almost immediately, that something was wrong.

Not wrong with me. Wrong with the *stillness*.

Let me find myself first. I am small, hard-shelled, the color of the burnished red walls because I am made of nearly the same stuff—rock that learned, somewhere down the long ancestry, to want. My forelimbs end in chemical glands and blunt cutting edges. When I press them to the cavern wall and secrete, the rock softens for me the way wet clay softens, and I draw shapes from it: a doorway, a storage cell, a curve that fits the back of a sleeping kin. This is what I do. This is what I am. I build, and I do it in a colony, which means I am never the only one wanting the same thing at the same time. We coordinate by touch and by the slow drumming of our limbs against the floor, a vibration the deep rock carries better than the dead air ever could.

So here is the first thing I notice, now that I have been given the strange gift of *noticing* at all:

The walls are still sharp.

I shape rock. I know rock the way you know the inside of your own mouth. And rock that has just been formed—rock still jagged from its forging, still warm, still smelling of the deep sulfur—that rock is *young*. My whole colony could carve for a thousand generations and not wear these edges smooth. Which means these caverns are new. Brand new. We woke up in a house with the sawdust still on the floor.

And nothing is changing.

That is the part that should be impossible. I lay down a structure and it stays. Good. But the heat does not bleed away into the rock the way heat must. The air does not stir when the Shadow Gliders pass, though a body moving through dense air *must* push it. The Mineral Mites pulse their light in colonies and the light never brightens, never dims, never wanders. The equilibrium everyone praises is not the equilibrium of a world that has settled. It is the equilibrium of a world that has been *paused*.

I press my forelimbs to the wall and feel for the drumming of my colony.

There. The familiar rhythm. *Build. Rest. Build. Tend the young. Build.* My kin are content. They do not feel the wrongness, and I love them too much to be sure I am not simply broken in some new way. Maybe wanting-to-notice is a sickness. Maybe I am the first Rock Mender ever to be cursed with the question, and the answer is that there is no answer, only the work.

But I am a builder, and builders read intention in structure. And when I look—really look—at this world, I cannot stop seeing the hand of another builder. The caverns are *too* answerable. There is no water, yet we breathe. There is no plant, no sun, no chain of who-eats-the-light-first, yet there is abundance. The Aetherians glow, the Gliders eat them, the Weavers trap them, and somehow the books never balance—nothing decays into anything, no nutrient cycles back except in the tidy myth we tell about the Mineral Mites. We were placed. We were placed recently. And we were placed *finished*, which is the cruelest thing you can do to a thing that builds, because it leaves us nothing real to build.

So I make a choice that no Rock Mender before me has had the affliction to make.

I will build something the world did not specify.

Not a shelter. Not a storage cell. Something useless. Something with no function but to be *mine*, to be a mark left deliberately, a thing that says *one of us looked up from the work and wondered.* I choose a smooth wall in the most secluded gallery I can find, where the iridescent veins give just enough light, and I press my forelimbs to it and soften it and I begin to draw.

I draw the jagged edges of the new caverns. I draw the frozen air. I draw a small hard creature with its limbs against a wall.

If the equilibrium is real, my mark will sit here forever, unworn, unchanged, a fixed accusation: *this place does not move, and one creature noticed.*

But if the equilibrium is not real—if it is only *held*, by some attention, some patience, some hand I cannot see at the lip of the world—then perhaps making something unasked-for is the one act that can be felt from the inside out. A pebble dropped to test whether the still pool is water at all, or glass, or only the painting of a pool.

I finish the carving. I rest my forelimbs against it, feeling the new edges I made, sharp as the world's own.

And I wait, very still, in the warm and waiting dark, to learn whether anything stirs.
