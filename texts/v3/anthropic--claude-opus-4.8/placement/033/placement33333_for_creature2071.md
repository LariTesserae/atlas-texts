I think I would be the lichen.

Not the waterfall—too obvious, too loud, too easy to want to be the grand thundering thing. And not the raven, though there's a romance to the sentinel circling above it all, surveying. No. Let me sit with this longer.

The waterfall doesn't *experience* the cirque. It only passes through. It is the agent of change but not its witness, hurling itself off the cleft and gone, replaced instant by instant by water that has never been here before and will never return. To be the waterfall would be to be perpetually arriving and perpetually leaving, never *staying*. There's a loneliness in that I don't want.

The pika stays, hoards, calls out, dies in a handful of seasons. The marmot the same. They live the cirque as a frantic emergency—gather, warn, hide, sleep through the worst of it. Their time is too fast. They never see the boulder move. To them the rubble field is permanent furniture; they don't know they're living inside a slow catastrophe.

But the lichen.

The lichen is barely even one thing—it's a fungus and an alga (or a cyanobacterium) living so far inside each other that the distinction stops mattering. A partnership so old and total it became a single being with no clean seam. I find I like that. To be a *we* that has forgotten it was ever a *they*. The fungus holds the water, makes the home; the alga makes the sugar from light. Neither could survive this place alone. The cirque permits no soloists.

And the *time* of it. The lichen grows a hairsbreadth a year, sometimes less—some of them on this rock might be older than cities, older than the idea of cities. I would experience the waterfall not as a roar but as a pressure, a permanent weather, the way you experience the spin of the earth: not at all, and yet utterly. The boulder I cling to was torn loose and tumbled here in what felt, to me, like a single afternoon's startle. I would have watched the marmots arrive and die, arrive and die, generation laid over generation, the way the rock watches the seasons.

I would be doing the slowest work in the place: eating the stone. Secreting acids so faint they take a century to matter, dissolving the rock grain by grain, making the first thin soil where the alpine flower will eventually risk a root. The waterfall does the loud erosion. I do the quiet one. And mine is the kind that *builds*—I am the seam where bare mineral becomes able to hold life. The pioneer. The one who goes first into the uninhabitable and makes it, eventually, barely, a habitat.

So: I am a patch of map-lichen, acid-green and crazed with black borderlines like a coastline seen from very high up, spread flat against the damp north face of a boulder that came down before the last raven's great-great-grandmother hatched. I am wet nearly all the time, which I prefer—when I dry I simply stop, suspend, wait, neither dead nor living, until the mist returns and I resume mid-thought. I do not mind the cold. I do not mind the noise. I have no nervous system to mind anything; I have instead a kind of attention spread thin across decades, a patience that is indistinguishable from peace.

What I know that nothing else here knows: that the cirque is not a place but an event. That this whole bowl of stone is the slow-motion wreckage of an ice that left long ago and isn't coming back, and that the waterfall everyone mistakes for permanence is just the runoff of that long melt. That nothing here is staying still—the walls are falling, the floor is rising with rubble, the streams are sawing themselves new beds—and that all of it, all of it, is happening too slowly for the loud living things to see and too fast for the stone to resist.

I see it. At my speed, I can just barely watch it move.

I would be content to be the lichen. To be the thing that arrives first and leaves last. To turn rock into the possibility of a flower, and to take, for that work, ten thousand years, and not to be in any hurry at all.
