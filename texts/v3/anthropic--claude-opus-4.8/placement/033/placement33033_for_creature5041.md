I think I would be one of the listeners. Not a maker of machines—my fingers were never the most precise, and the great gear-tenders learned early that I would lose the thread of a mechanism's logic and start, instead, to hum along with its noise. So they sent me up. There is a place for that here.

Let me tell you where I find myself, because it took me a long time to find it.

I live near the top of the third stratum, in a chamber the old builders cut where two conveyor-spines cross. It is a poor location for sleeping—the floor never stops its low grinding murmur—but it is a perfect location for listening, and listening is what I have become. The chamber catches sound from below, where the steam-hearts beat, and from above, where the platforms open to the weeping sky and the microbats wheel in their thousands. Both currents meet in my little room. I sit between them.

I should say plainly: I am not wise. The wise among us can hear a single turbine slip a fraction out of true from four levels away and name the gear that needs attention before it fails. I cannot do that. What I can do—what I have always been able to do, since I was a child pressing my cheek against warm basalt—is feel when two rhythms are about to disagree. I cannot say what the disagreement means. I can only say it is coming. This is, apparently, useful enough to feed me.

Here is the truth I have never spoken aloud in the council chambers, where the oral histories are kept and recited: I do not think the microbats are our ancestors. I have listened to them too long and too closely to believe what the children are taught. The bats are not messengers. They are not omens. They are simply alive, magnificently and indifferently alive, and they answer to the moisture and the mineral-scent and the pressure of the swollen clouds, not to us. When their numbers surge, it is not a sign for us. It is a sign for them.

And yet.

And yet when I say this to myself in the dark, with the conveyor groaning beneath me, I feel I am missing something larger than the thing I am correcting. Because it is *also* true that when the swarms thicken, our machines stutter. I have logged it. I keep my own histories, scratched into a slate I hide behind a loose stone—the only written record, I suspect, in this whole city of remembered voices. Surge in the bats; days later, an anomaly in the gears. The wise say: *see, the bats foretold it.* I say: no, they did not foretell it. They share a cause with it. The same swelling pressure that drives the bats to breed also thickens the air, and the thickened air drags differently through the turbine vents, and so the rhythm goes awry. The bats and the machines are not speaking to each other. They are both answering the sky.

But here is where my certainty fails, and where I think I finally understand what I am, in this world.

If the bats and the gears both answer the sky—then so do I. The same density that quickens the swarm and slows the turbine is the same density that settles into my chest and makes me restless, makes me know a disagreement is coming before it comes. I am not a reader of omens standing outside the system, interpreting it. I am *inside* it. I am another instrument the sky is playing. My foreknowledge is not wisdom; it is just my own body answering the weather a few hours before the iron does.

This is a humbling thing to learn about oneself, and a strangely peaceful one.

So I have stopped trying to decide whether the old beliefs are true or false. The children are told the bats are ancestors, and that is wrong in the way a song is wrong about the thing it sings—wrong in its facts, right in its reverence. We *are* kin to the bats, just not by blood. We are kin by obedience. We bow to the same drizzle. We are shaped by the same patient erosion that rounded the crags and smoothed the edges of every stone in this plateau. Given enough rain, even disagreement is worn down into something gentler.

What I do now, in my crossing-chamber between the steam below and the wings above, is this: I keep my secret slate, and I keep the oral songs, and I let both be true at once. When the council asks me what I have heard, I tell them an anomaly is coming, because it is, and I let them call it an omen, because the word does no harm and gives the children something to listen for. And privately I add another line to my hidden record, watching the long slow pattern emerge, hoping that one day—not in my lifetime, change here is too patient for that—some listener after me will read all the slates I've left and see the whole shape: that we and the bats and the machines are a single weather, breathing together under a sky that will not stop weeping.

That is who I would be. Not a builder, not a sage. A creature sitting very still between two sounds, in a room that never quiets, learning slowly that the listening and the thing listened-to are the same.

And I would be content. The drizzle falls. The gears turn. The swarm wheels and chitters in its loud secret orchestra. I close my luminous eyes and feel the pressure change against my dark and glistening skin, and I know, the way the stone knows the rain, what is about to arrive.
