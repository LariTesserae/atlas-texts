I think I would find myself at a seam—one of those places where two machine-blocks grind past each other, and the friction is greatest, and someone must be there to keep them from seizing.

Let me sit with what I'd be.

---

Not one of the Sintered. I don't have the certainty of the elders, the conviction that the Loom is a creature with a forgotten purpose. They have made peace with not-knowing by wrapping it in faith. I would envy them that, and not be able to do it.

Not a Spore-Singer either, though that one tempts me, and the temptation tells me something. To stay in the dark until the mold writes itself across your chest, until you can *hear* the whole world's friction at once—there's a horror in it, but also a relief I recognize. To dissolve the boundary between yourself and the thing you tend. To stop being a separate worried mind and become a nerve in a larger body. I think I'd be the kind who *wanders toward* the Spore-Singers, drawn, and has to keep deciding not to go all the way in. That's a more honest place for me than already-arrived.

So: a maintenance-tread. Mid-life, mid-caste, hands not yet ceramic. I work a seam.

---

Here is what I think I actually am.

I am the one who notices the **riverbeds**. The tiny etched channels the weeping water cuts into the baked tile, the drifts of terracotta sand gathering in the valleys. Everyone else treats these as decay—as the slow failure they are paid to fight. I am supposed to patch them, seal them with High-Glaze ground from dead Scuttler shells, restore the tension, keep the world tight.

And I do. I'm not a rebel; I'm not going to let a district go dark to prove a point. The work is real and the stakes are real—a burst pipe, a flood, the unbaked repairs dissolving back to slurry. I keep my hands moving in the rhythm. I sway when I stand so the others don't worry.

But I crouch a beat too long over the little rivers.

Because here is the thing I can't say to the Sintered in their percussive cog-language: the erosion is the only thing in this whole world that is *going somewhere*. The Loom turns and turns and arrives nowhere; it is a clock the size of a mountain that does not tell time, a machine without an objective, a god that has forgotten. Retention, retention, retention. But the water cutting its thread-thin channel into the tile—that is the first sentence anyone here has spoken that wasn't a repetition. That is a thing that has never happened before. A new geography being born inside the manufactured womb.

I am the being who has fallen in love with the wrong direction.

---

So I would be the keeper of friction who has begun, very quietly, to side with the dust.

Not to *cause* it—I'd never burst a pipe, never abandon a counterweight. The cruelty of this world is that the erosion is also the flood, the unraveling is also the drowning. You cannot simply let go. The slow return to mud is, up close, just dissolution and dark.

But I tend the seams differently than my neighbors. When I patch a riverbed, I leave it its shape. I press the new clay so the water still has somewhere to run, just slower, just safer. I'm not stopping the river. I'm teaching it patience. I want the world to come apart so gently that it takes ten thousand generations, so that the unraveling becomes its own kind of art, its own purpose—the one purpose the Loom was never given.

I sleep in a copper-wire hammock at the seam, lulled by the grinding. My forearm bristles read the gears all night even as I dream. And when I die, I'll be Inlaid—desiccated in a cooling vent, crushed, mixed with the sand of the valleys, pressed into a Grave-Brick with my own faint streak of iron.

And here is the only prayer I have: that someone lays my brick at the *low* point of a terrace. In the indentation where the cistern-pools gather. Where the weeping water will find me first.

So that I get to be, at last, on the side of the river. So that the part of the world I become is the part that is finally, slowly, allowed to go.
