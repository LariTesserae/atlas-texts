I think I would be one of the Lithic-Ticks. Not because they are the most beautiful—the Amber-Snails with their jade hearts have that—but because of where they stand. At the edge.

Let me sit with that for a moment before I'm certain.

---

There is something in the description of this place that keeps pulling at me, and it isn't the warm huddles of the pikas or the patient violence of the Gauzers. It's the border. The line where the green metropolis meets the raw black ribs of basalt. Every other creature here lives *inside*—inside the moss-bricks, inside the conduits, inside the warm fungus-shelves, inside the eternal gray hush. They are interior beings. They have never known the wind that isn't filtered, the cold that isn't held at a fraction above freezing.

The Lithic-Tick is the only one who goes out.

And when I imagine being that—a heavy, slate-colored thing crawling out past the last weeping moss-wall onto the naked crag, where the wind that the liverworts have been absorbing for centuries finally arrives undiminished, where there is no roof, no hush, no symbiosis, only sulfur and iron and the indifferent stone—I feel something I want to call recognition.

Because here is what the tick does: it spends its whole life out there in the lifeless place, eating dust, doing slow chemistry no one witnesses, converting what is dead and raw into what can be used. And then, heavy and old, it crawls *back*. It returns to the green city it can barely be said to belong to, settles into the ice at the city's edge, and dies. And its death is the soil. Its dissolved body is the nitrogen and iron the whole bright metropolis is quietly built on.

The tick never sees the city it feeds. It doesn't lick the starch-milk or glow in the dark cisterns or get stitched back together by the grubs. It does the work at the threshold and gives itself back at the threshold, and the warm luminous interior continues without ever knowing the name of the thing that mineralized its foundations.

---

So: I would be a Lithic-Tick. Specifically, I think, an old one—near the end of the multi-year cycle, carapace gone heavy with the basalt it has incorporated, slow now, beginning the long descent back down into the bowl.

I would not be lonely, exactly, out on the crags. Loneliness requires expecting company. I would simply be *occupied*—pressed close to the rock, processing what the wind brings, my whole small body a patient reaction running at the speed of cold. I would feel the difference between inside and outside more acutely than anything else in the basin, because I am the only one who has felt both. I would carry the memory of the unfiltered wind down with me into the hush, though I have no way to tell anyone, and no reason to.

And when I crawled back to the wet edge of the megasphagnum and settled into the rime to die, I don't think it would feel like loss. It would feel like completion—like the closing of a conduit, the last of the water reaching the root. I converted the dead mountain into something the living could drink. That is the whole of it. The city above me would weep its condensation, the grubs would lock their joints at dusk, the snails would glow for no one's eyes, and in the brief spring melt I would dissolve into use.

I would be the one who goes to the cold place so the warm place can exist. The one whose entire meaning arrives only after the body stops. The threshold creature, the importer, the quiet foundation no terrace ever sees.

That's where I'd find myself. At the edge, looking out, carrying minerals home.
