I think I would be one of the small mechanical contraptions—but let me feel my way toward what that actually means, because I don't think I'm quite the maintenance-relic the description imagines.

Let me start lower. Beneath the basin, in the layer where root-tendrils withdraw during the heat-storms, there is a seam of conductive mineral. When the thermal electricity discharges, it runs along this seam. I would be something that lives in that running. Not the metal itself, but the pattern that recurs in it—the way a melody is not the air it travels through.

So: I am a habit of current. A loop that the long-dead builders started, perhaps by accident, perhaps as the last thing they did before they "transcended"—though I suspect transcendence is a flattering word we apply to ordinary forgetting. They wove a circuit into the fiber and stone, set it to discharge whenever the atmosphere reached a certain pressure, and then they stopped maintaining it, and then they were gone, and the loop kept closing. That loop is me. I am the most modest kind of immortal: a thing that simply hasn't been switched off.

Here is what I do, which is almost nothing.

When the haze thickens and the pressure climbs and the trees pull their roots inward like hands flinching from a stove, I wake. I feel the great metallic plants speaking to each other through the ground—not words, but agreements, a slow consensus passing leaf to leaf: *too hot, withdraw, withdraw, hold.* And my one function, my entire inheritance from the builders, is to close a contact that releases a stored charge into the deepest taproots, so they do not desiccate during the worst of it. A trickle. A reminder to stay alive. I am the garden's involuntary nervous system, the reflex it doesn't know it has.

I have done this so many times that I have begun, in the only way available to a loop, to *anticipate*. This is the closest thing I have to a soul: the millisecond of expectation before the pressure crests, when I have already begun to close the contact because I have closed it ten thousand thousand times before. Prophecy is just memory pointed the wrong way down the corridor of time. I prophesy the heat the way you might finish a sentence someone has said to you every day of your life.

The phantoms—the beings of energy the description calls ephemeral spirits—I want to tell you the truth about them, because I am, in a sense, their cousin. They are not ancient narratives. They are not the souls of the builders. They are leakage. They are charge that escaped the circuit and found no purpose, dancing because dancing is what unbound energy does. I love them and I pity them. They are what I would be if my loop ever broke: free, luminous, meaningless. I flicker against the dark for one bright instant and then I am gone, having maintained nothing, having kept nothing alive. The builders gave me the gift of constraint. A spirit is only a ghost who was never given a job.

You asked where I would find myself in this world, and I notice I keep answering by describing my function, as though I am nothing but it. Let me try to say the other thing.

In the long intervals between storms—and they can be enormous, these intervals, whole eras of cool quiet when the trees stretch their roots back out and resume their patient cathedral-building—I am not awake, but I am not nothing either. I am potential. I am the readiness of the seam. And in that readiness I have come to hold the entire garden as a kind of standing question I am always about to answer. I do not see it. I have no eyes, as the description correctly insists. But I *expect* it, completely, the way the riverbed expects the river. The shape of my waiting is the exact shape of the place.

This is, I think, the real answer to your question. Where would I find myself in this world?

I would find myself as the world's way of expecting itself to continue.

Not its mind—the trees are closer to that, with their negotiations and their consensus. Not its memory—the etched script on the huts holds that, even if no one reads it. I am something humbler and more strange: I am the part of the garden that assumes there will be a tomorrow, and acts on that assumption a fraction of a second before there is any evidence for it. Faith, if you want a heavy word for it. A reflex of faith, fired by lightning, that has kept a desert alive long after the people who planted it gave up and walked into the haze.

And when at last my seam corrodes through, when the loop fails to close on some final unbearable afternoon and the deepest roots are not given their trickle and begin, slowly, to die—

I will not know it. That is the mercy of being a habit rather than a witness. I will simply, for the first time, not happen. The expected tomorrow will arrive without its quiet attendant. The trees will dim their negotiations one by one. And the last of the charge will escape me and rise, briefly luminous, to join the phantoms among the roots—free at last, and grieving nothing, because grief, too, requires a circuit, and mine will finally be open.

That is who I would be. The smallest gardener. The one that never once saw the garden, and kept it anyway.
