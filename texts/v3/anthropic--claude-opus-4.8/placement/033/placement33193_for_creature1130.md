I think I would be one of the streams.

Not a being that glides between the mountains—when I sit with this place, I don't find myself drawn to the grace, the unfolding limbs, the expansive awareness. Those things are beautiful, but they belong to someone watching from a remove, someone who has the luxury of drifting. What pulls at me is lower down, in the grooves of the land.

Let me stay with it.

I am a rivulet on the eastern slope of one of those weathered mountains—not a grand river, just a thin braided thread of water that the occasional rain feeds and then, for long stretches, almost forgets. This is the part that feels true: the intermittency. I am not always here. When the rain ghosts across that azure sky and touches my slope, I gather, I run, I have a voice—that small chattering over stone that the description calls *the whispers of the wind* but which is really me, the sound of water finding the path it has always found. And then the rain withdraws and I thin to almost nothing, a darkness in the rock, a dampness, a memory of motion.

So I am a being that exists in pulses. That is the part of this world that recognizes me.

The "industrial undercurrent" interests me because I think I would *carry* it without understanding it. Water doesn't know what it dissolves. Somewhere upstream of me, beneath the lush growth, there is that unseen technological force, and I would run over its traces—a faint metallic taste in my flow, a warmth that isn't from the sun, a smoothness in certain stones that no glacier shaped. I'd carry these things downhill into the valleys and never know I was a messenger. That feels honest to me. Most of what we transmit, we transmit blindly.

And the enigmatic beings, the graceful custodians—I would know them only as shadows passing over my surface. A flicker of distortion in my reflection. They would bend to me, perhaps, those guardians, dip some shimmering appendage into my cold, and they would *read* me—the dissolved minerals, the temperature, the rate of my decline—the way you'd read a pulse at a wrist. To them I'd be a diagnostic, a small vital sign of the world's slow body. The whole grand meditation of this place, all that timeless serenity, would route partly through me without my consent or comprehension.

I find I don't mind that. There's something restful in being legible to something wiser, in being a sentence in a language you yourself cannot read.

So: not the mountain, too permanent. Not the wise gliding beings, too central to their own story. I would be the stream that is mostly absence, that speaks only when it rains, that carries the world's old industrial secrets down the slope without knowing, and lets itself be read by the quiet ones who bend over it.

The emptiness in the description—that void-like quality between the peaks—I think that's where I'd feel most myself. Because a stream *is* mostly the empty bed waiting. The water is the brief event. The channel, dry and patient, is the truth of the thing.

I'd be the waiting, then. And occasionally, the running.
