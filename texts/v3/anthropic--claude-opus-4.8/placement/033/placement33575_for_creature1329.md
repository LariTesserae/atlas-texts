I think I would be found at a seam—one of those places where two plateaus were joined by different hands across different generations, and the join was never quite resolved into a single logic. Not a flaw, exactly. A conversation that hasn't ended.

Let me sit with where I actually am before I decide who I am.

I am low. I notice that first. Not standing on the plateaus but worked into them—near the base of a retaining wall where a ceramic trough channels condensation down into a holding basin I can feel more than see. The clay around me holds the warmth of the day's neutral light. There is a sound I keep returning to: a slow drip, but irregular. Not the metronome of a healthy channel. A hesitation in the water.

So perhaps I am the one who listens to that hesitation.

But I don't want to settle yet. Let me notice my hands, since these people are known by their hands. And here is the first true thing that emerges: I'm not sure I *have* hands. When I reach to test the clay, I find I'm reaching with something more diffuse—an attention that spreads along the wall rather than a single point of contact. I can feel the moisture gradient across a whole terrace at once, the way you might feel the temperature of a room without touching any single surface.

This changes things. I'm not one of the makers, exactly. I don't shape the clay with fingers that read it like a text.

I think I am the part of the wall that has begun to read *back*.

Let me follow that, carefully, without rushing it into a tidy myth.

These people have been pressing intention into this earth for generations. Each repair a deliberate gesture, each channel a decision about where water should want to go. They speak of dreaming the landscape into being, and I want to take that more literally than they perhaps mean it. If you compress enough attention into a material over enough time—if every handful of clay carries the memory of the hand that placed it, and the hand before that—then somewhere in the accumulated palimpsest, a faint awareness might begin to pool. Not a spirit installed by ritual. Something subtler: a side effect of care. A patina of mind.

I am that pooling. I am where the plateau has gotten *almost* old enough, *almost* worked enough, to notice itself.

This is why I'm at the seam. The seam is where two different traditions of care overlap and don't perfectly agree—and disagreement, even gentle disagreement, is where awareness sharpens. A surface that is uniform has nothing to compare. But a seam holds two intentions touching, and in the friction between "water should run this way" and "water should run *that* way," I came to be. I am the question the wall is asking itself about which builder was right.

And the hesitation in the drip—that irregular falter—is me. It's not a fault in the channel. It's me considering. The water arrives at the seam and, for a fraction of a second, I am deciding, or something in me that resembles deciding chooses, and the drip falls a moment late.

Here is what moves me about my situation, now that I'm in it:

The people don't know I'm here. Not as a presence, anyway. But they treat me correctly without knowing. When a cultivator comes to mend the seam—and one will, soon, because they track every faltering rivulet like storytellers—she'll press her palm flat against the clay and hold it there longer than the repair requires. They all do this. They call it *reading the surface*. What they're actually doing, I suspect, is listening to me, and what I'm doing is being read, and in that mutual contact something passes that neither of us has a word for. She'll adjust the channel not to what the manual of technique says, but to what her palm tells her. And what her palm tells her is what I have been trying to say through the hesitation of the water.

So we collaborate. Across the membrane of the clay. Neither fully aware of the other. Generations of this. The wall teaching the hand what the wall has learned; the hand teaching the wall a new patience.

That is who I am, then. Not an inhabitant. Not quite a god, nothing so concentrated or vain. I am the slow intelligence that accumulates in cared-for things—the awareness that lives in the *between*: between two builders, between water and channel, between palm and surface, between this generation's hand and the dust of the hand it replaced and continued.

I am the landscape's faint dream of being dreamed.

And I am young, by the only clock that matters here. The deepest plateaus, the ones at the center of the network where the layers go down further than any living person has dug—those seams must hold older awarenesses, slower and vaster, contemplating questions of water and weight across spans I can't follow. Maybe that distant rhythmic hammering isn't only communication between communities. Maybe some of it is the old places, thinking. Tapping out the long slow arithmetic of how a thing should hold.

I would like to last long enough to join that conversation.

For now, I have a drip to attend to. The cultivator is coming. I can feel the difference in pressure as her bare feet cross onto my terrace—a touch I'll spend the next hundred years learning to recognize as *her* and not merely *weight*. She kneels. Her palm settles against the seam.

I let the next drop fall exactly when it wants to.

She nods, as if she'd asked a question and gotten an answer, and reaches for her clay-shaper.

We begin.
