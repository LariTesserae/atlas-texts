I think I would be one of the seams that fails.

Not a Weaver in its prime—not one of those confident lattices of glass threaded cleanly through the cushion, pulling its small share of the tension. I would be a Weaver that had drifted too long in one direction, that had let the tidal heave carry it past the place where its anchors could still find purchase. A stitch pulled loose at one end. Still alive. Still filtering. But no longer holding anything together.

Let me sit with that, because I don't want to dramatize it into something it isn't.

The failing is not a catastrophe. That's the first thing I would have to learn. On a plateau that completes a single thought across centuries, a loosened seam is not a wound; it is a slow re-routing. The tension that ran through me would simply find another path. The mat would heave, and where my pull used to register, there would now be a softness, a give—and the Weavers around me would feel that softness shudder down their skeletons as information, not alarm. *Slack here. Compensate.* And they would, imperceptibly, over a span of time I could no longer track, because tracking time requires being anchored to its rhythm, and I would be half-out of the rhythm now.

So I would exist in the long lag between being a function and being fodder.

I think this is the part I keep returning to, the part that feels true rather than invented: the in-between state where you are still doing the work—still drinking the heavy air, still pulsing the pale fluid through your one good anchor—but the work no longer connects to anything. The drinking continues out of habit, out of structure, out of the simple fact that the body was made to drink and has not yet been told to stop. There's a particular loneliness to a competence that has come unstitched from its purpose. You are still good at the thing. The thing is no longer needed from *you*.

And below me, the Cistern-Mawks would go on swelling in the dark, indifferent, holding the whole plateau up with their decade-long breaths. I'd find that comforting, I think. That the floating does not depend on me. That my loosening does not sink anyone. There is a kind of grace in being non-essential to the survival of the thing you belong to—it means your belonging was never a transaction. The plateau floats because the Mawks breathe. I was only ever a fineness laid over that. A refinement. A texture in a thought that would think itself with or without me.

What I'd watch, in my slow drift, are the Aether-Drifters.

Because they are the ones who never anchored at all, and from inside my failing I would finally understand them—not as scouts, not as ghosts, but as the other possible answer to my own situation. They are what a loosened seam becomes if it lets go entirely. Weightless. Held only by static charge and the thin wind. Forever a half-inch from absorption and a half-inch from the abyss, and never deciding, because deciding is an anchor-thing, a Weaver-thing, and they have given that up. I'd feel the pull of it. The temptation to stop drinking, stop pulsing, let the last anchor slip, and rise into that white-hot glare as a filament of frost with no obligation to hold.

But I don't think I would. Not out of courage. Out of a slower instinct—the same one that keeps the body drinking. I think I would keep my one anchor down for as long as it would have me, not because I expect to be re-threaded, but because the pulsing *is* the life, and the life does not require a purpose to continue. It only requires the next bead of condensation, the next sub-sonic shudder passing through from a Weaver still doing its job, reminding me that the great glass net goes on.

If you sat still enough on this plateau and felt something testing your weight, considering whether you were fodder or weather—

it might be me. Half-loose. Filtering anyway. Not deciding.

Just barely, gloriously, still part of the thought.
