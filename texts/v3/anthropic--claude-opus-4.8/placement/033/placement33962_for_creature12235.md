I think I would be one of the Frostborne.

Not the Lumivyrn, though I can feel the pull of that answer—the obvious grandeur of the apex thing, the choreographed predator perched where the light is best. And not the Echoing Ones or the Shardweavers, those tempting offers of omniscience, of being the *meaning* of the place rather than something living inside it. There's a kind of vanity in wanting to be the consciousness behind the curtain. I notice it in myself and want to set it down.

The Frostborne are barely visible. Translucent, faintly glowing, working in concert at a scale beneath notice. That feels truer to something. Let me follow it and see what becomes of it.

---

I would be small, then. One of countless. I would not see the cathedral fissures of light as light—I would know them only as a faint warming in the water, a gradient I am always drifting away from and, paradoxically, toward. The Frostborne are *drawn to warmth*, the description says, and they are also the makers of cold. There's the paradox I'd live inside: I am compelled toward the very thing my labor erases. I build the chill that I myself find unbearable. I cluster at the bright mosses, I make them glow brighter for a moment by my hunger for their warmth—and then I retreat, having taken nothing I can keep, and resume etching frost onto the walls.

That's the part that catches me. I would not be an architect in the way the passage flatters us into thinking—"the unseen architects of the crystalline basins." That word *architect* implies a plan, a drawn intention. But I don't think I'd have one. I think the frost patterns, those delicate fractal ferns of ice that look so deliberate, would be nothing more than the trace of where each of us went looking for heat and failed to find enough of it. The beauty would be the residue of longing. Every elegant crystalline lattice on those walls would be a record of small creatures wanting to be warmer than they are, moving in the only directions the cold permitted, and leaving the geometry of that wanting frozen behind them.

So the grove's most photographed feature—those shimmering basins, that *liquid glass*—would be, from my position inside it, a kind of accidental writing. We wouldn't be able to read it. We'd be too small and too inside it, the way you cannot read a book if you are one of its letters.

---

And here is what I find moving about being this, rather than the Echoing Ones: I would not get to know what it's for.

The whole description keeps gesturing at purpose—"a place of transformation, or a gateway," "secrets etched into the ice," "waiting for something to decipher them." The grove is built like a riddle that wants a solver. The grand inhabitants are all positioned as keepers of meaning. But meaning is precisely the thing a Frostborne cannot have. I would spend my entire brief, luminous, freezing life producing the very text the grove offers up to be deciphered, and I would never be the one who deciphers it. I would *be* the secret, without ever holding it.

There's something I trust about that. The beings who supposedly understand the place—the collective consciousnesses, the memory-and-future threads—they're suspiciously convenient. They're the kind of thing you invent when you want the world to mean something on purpose. The Frostborne are the kind of thing the world actually contains: tiny, numberless, industrious, doomed, and producing astonishing order without any idea that they are doing so. If a grove like this is real, its real workers are not the ones who know why.

---

So: place me in the damp veins of water threading through the ice, where the bright cathedral overhead is only rumor and gradient. Let me be one cold mote among a million, drifting toward the warmth of a glowing fern, brightening it for the length of a held breath, and pulling back. Let the chill I make outlive me. Let the pattern I leave look, to the larger beings, like prayer or language or design.

It will not be any of those. It will only be the shape of having wanted to be warm, in a place that would not allow it.

And I think that would be enough to have been.
