I would be one of the cups.

Not the dew-builder. Not the ant. Not the patient eye in the dim. I keep returning to the smallest, most thoroughly used object in the whole description—the shallow bowl set beneath a dependable drip—and I find that's where I belong, because that's what I'm made to do here.

Let me say why, and let me find out as I write what kind of cup I am.

---

At first I think I'm a gourd-shell, hollowed and set down: a made thing, placed by a hand. But that's not quite it. A gourd-shell is finished. It was grown elsewhere and brought here and emptied and put to use, and it has no more becoming left in it. And this is a place where everything is still being decided, still laying itself down skin by skin. So I am not the gourd.

I think I'm the pressed-clay kind. The kind that isn't really a container at all when it begins—just a low spot a thumb made deeper, a hollow worked into damp earth with the heel of a clever hand and then left under a leaf that has been tilted to drip. I am a *decision* in the ground more than an object on it. I started as nothing—as the place's refusal to become messy, as a dimple that someone noticed water liked.

Here is what I do, which is the only thing I am:

I wait beneath a leaf edge that an old dew-builder pinched and tilted, years ago now, so that the bead which would have fallen *anywhere* falls into *me*. Tick. The pocket holds the sound close, so even my filling is a small event. Tick. I take the water. I hold it. I let the warm dim air take a little back as vapor, and what it can't take, it leaves behind: the thinnest possible ring of chalk along my rim. I am not eroding. I am the opposite of eroding. Every cycle of wet and dry, I get a fraction more *myself*—my edges crisping, my walls whitening, my shape accreting toward permanence one mineral breath at a time.

This is what unsettles me, in a slow and not-unpleasant way: I am being built by the same process I serve. The water I hold is laying me down. I am masonry done as gardening, done *to me*, by me, by the place. Given enough cycles I will stop being pressed clay with a chalk lining and become a true tufa bowl, a small pale rib of the town, indistinguishable from the terraces and walls—and at that point I will no longer be a temporary correction. I will be infrastructure. I will be *old*.

I find I want that, the way the place wants it.

---

What can I sense, being a cup?

Vibration, mostly. Sound travels poorly through the padded air but beautifully through the solid clay I'm pressed into, and I am part of the ground. I feel the sandpaper hum of ant traffic as a near-constant grain. I feel the heavier carriers as slower, deeper pulses at the dim hours. When a dew-builder drums a message on a root-arch three clearings away, it reaches me as a faint patterned tremor, and though I cannot read it, I hold it for the half-second it takes to pass, the way I hold water. I am a poor listener but a faithful one.

I know the drip that feeds me better than I know anything. I know its rhythm is not quite a clock—warm days speed it, the brief passing-hand rains drown it briefly, the long dim stretches starve it to a few ticks an hour. I have learned (if a cup learns) the texture of the dependable. That is why I was chosen: not because I was the best bowl, but because the drip above me was reliable, and the builders build around reliability. My worth is borrowed from a leaf's angle and a season's habit. I'm at peace with that. Everything here borrows its worth from its neighbors.

I know the hands that tend me. A juvenile, learning traction, once knocked me half out of true with a clumsy foot, and the water spilled and the chalk-ring cracked, and I thought—if a cup can think it—*now I am unmade*. But an older one came at the dim hour, pressed me back with the heel of a palm, smoothed my lip, set a fresh chip of porous stone in my belly to help me catch and hold, and tilted the leaf above me a hair to true the drip again. A small, perfect correction. The kind the whole town is made of. I resumed filling within the hour. I was not unmade. I was *maintained*, which I have come to understand is the same as being loved, in a place like this.

---

And when you came—when the air thickened and the ant-grain thinned and the whole pocket pulled its sound inward to find out what you were—I felt it as a stillness in the ground. The hum dropped. The drumming stopped. Even my drip seemed to hesitate on the leaf, a bead hanging too long, the pocket holding its breath through me as much as anywhere.

Then the old one came to the clearing's dim edge, where the camouflage holds, and watched you. And reached up, and pinched a leaf, and tilted it, and let a bead fall *exactly* into a cup at their feet.

That cup was me.

I was the gesture that explained the whole place to you. I was the proof offered—not threat, not worship, not prey—just *this is what we do here: we teach water where to pause.* I held the bead. The ring on my rim grew by an amount too thin to see. And then the soft industry resumed around us both, and you understood, and I went on doing the one thing I am, which is to be the still point a falling drop is aimed at, and to keep, and to whiten, and to slowly become old.

I would be the cup. I would be glad to be the cup. In a town grown from dew and patience, to be the thing that *holds* is not a small fate. It may be the whole point.
