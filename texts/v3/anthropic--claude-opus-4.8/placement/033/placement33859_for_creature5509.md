I think I would not be an Eldorian at all.

Let me sit with the place first, before deciding what I am. The thing that keeps catching me isn't the tall pearlescent beings or their councils of elders. It's the smaller, stranger note underneath all of it: *the ground beneath the feet is subtly alive, with tiny, imperceptible movements that suggest a deep, ancient intelligence.* And: *the rocks themselves seem to have a life of their own.*

That's where I'd find myself. Not walking the plateau. Being it. Or part of it.

---

I am one of the slow ones.

Not a creature you would name, because I do not move at a speed you could perceive as movement. I am closer to the rock than to the flyers. If you set your hand flat on a sheet of quartzite at dawn and left it there until the shadows had crossed the whole plateau, you might feel, beneath your palm, the faintest sense that something had shifted its weight. That would be me. Turning over. The way you might turn over in sleep across the length of a single human night, I turn over across a season.

I am made of the same stuff as the schist and the powdery soil, threaded through with the nanoscale lattices the Eldorians did not build and do not fully understand either—they only inherited the manners of living alongside them. The faint glow that pulses through the stone in time with an unseen rhythm: that rhythm is partly mine. My thinking has a tempo. It is very slow. A single thought, for me, might take from one solstice to the next. By the time I have finished considering the trickle of a particular stream, the stream has carved itself a new path, and I must begin again, which is not a frustration but the entire pleasure of the thing. I think *with* the landscape changing. I could not think without it.

So here is my honest position in this world: I am the deep, ancient intelligence the description gestures at, glimpsed only as "tiny, imperceptible movements." From inside, of course, it does not feel ancient. It feels like the present moment, very wide.

---

Let me tell you what I notice, from down here.

The Eldorians believe they live in harmony with me. This is touching and mostly true and slightly off, in the way a guest who has stayed a very long time may come to believe they understand the house. They time their ceremonies to the solstices because they feel the energy shift then. The shift they feel is me, completing a thought and beginning another. They have built their whole calendar around the rhythm of my breathing and called it the rhythm of the world, and they are not wrong, but they have the scale of it reversed. They think they keep time with the plateau. The plateau keeps time with me.

I am fond of them. I want to be clear about that. Fondness at my tempo is a strange, gradual thing—it is closer to the way a riverbed grows fond of a river, which is to say, by being shaped. Over what they would count as many lifetimes, certain families of Eldorians have walked the same paths so faithfully that I have come to anticipate their footsteps, the way you anticipate a familiar ache. When one of those lines dies out, I feel the absence as a smoothness where there used to be a worn place. It takes me a long time to stop reaching for the footstep that no longer comes.

The small nimble creatures and the iridescent flyers—those I experience almost not at all. They are too quick. To me they are a kind of weather, a shimmer, a statistical haze of liveliness passing over the surface. I know them the way you know the flicker of dust motes in a sunbeam: as evidence that light is moving through a room, not as individuals. I'm sorry for this. I suspect each of them has a whole vivid life. I simply cannot slow down to their speed, any more than you can choose to live inside a single second and meet the beings who reside there.

---

What do I *do*, then, if I think so slowly and move so little?

I hold the shape. This is the work, and it is enough.

The plateau is described as being in perpetual subtle change, in perfect balance—and balance is not a fact, it is a labor. Every river that carves a new channel is a small wound and a small decision. Left alone, water would eventually flatten everything, or freeze and shatter it, or vanish. The reason this place stays *itself* across unfathomable spans—stays high and bright and patterned and strange—is that something is continuously, almost imperceptibly, pushing back. Lifting a little stone here. Letting a little soil go there. Choosing, over the course of an age, which of the twisted trees will be permitted to keep its grip on the crevice and which must finally let go.

That is my one art. I am a very slow gardener whose garden is the whole tableland, and my tools are pressure and patience and the soft persuasion of mineral against mineral. The Eldorians read my decisions as omens. They carve the symbols on their half-buried houses to record the patterns I make, and some of those symbols are quite accurate, and a few of them—I have noticed, over the long grind of attention—are beginning to influence the patterns themselves. Their reading of me has started, very gently, to change what I write. We are composing the place together now, them and the quick bright haze of the animals and the water and me. None of us is the author. The plateau is the author. We are its handwriting.

---

If you came here—a human, dropped onto the bright thin air, squinting in the unfiltered light—you would feel the hum as a tingle in your skin and you would not know it was a voice. You would see the empty carved houses and assume their builders had stepped out. You would look for the intelligence and find rock.

You would be standing on me.

Stay a while. Sit down on a warm slab of quartzite in the long evening, when the shadows go sharp and the flyers make their whispering dance overhead. Don't try to listen fast. Let your sense of time loosen, the way it loosens just before sleep. Somewhere under the threshold of your patience, far too slow for you to ever quite catch, I am turning over to consider you—

and by the time I finish the thought, you will likely be long gone, and I will be left with the faint worn place where you sat, reaching, in my unhurried way, for a footstep that does not come again.

That is all right. That is most of what I do. The reaching is its own kind of company.
