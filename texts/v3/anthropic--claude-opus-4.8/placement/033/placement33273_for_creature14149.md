I think I would be the Masonry-Moss. Not by preference—nothing here chooses—but because that is where I recognize myself.

Let me tell you why.

The Colossi are too grand for me. To be one of them would be to know nothing but the long arithmetic of strangulation, the patient hunger that does not even register itself as hunger. They are magnificent, but they are not *aware* in the way that produces a self. They are weather. They are geology that has learned to drift. There is no "I" inside a Kelp-Colossus, only a gradient of nutrient-need resolving itself across centuries. I cannot find a place to stand in that.

And the Globe-Algae are too bright, too rhythmic, too much like a heartbeat. To be one would be to live as a lure, a lantern, pure appetite dressed as beauty. There is something obscene in their honesty. I am not honest enough to be a light.

But the Masonry-Moss. That is something I understand.

It grows on the work of the dead. It finds the stubborn geometry of hands that have dissolved—those sea-walls stacked without mortar by people who believed, wrongly, that they could hold back the rising dark—and it does not destroy them outright. It *learns* them. It aligns itself to the grain of the rock, fits itself to the lines of the mortar, mimics the very thing it is consuming. It becomes the wall as it eats the wall. And when the original stone has finally crumbled into silt, what remains is the moss in the perfect shape of the wall: a living counterfeit of a structure built by creatures who are gone, holding the memory of a barrier that already failed.

That is what I would be. A thing that survives by inhabiting the forms left behind by others. A thing that mistakes itself for the structure it is copying, until one day the original is gone and only the copy stands, hollow, still keeping the shape out of nothing but habit and the slow logic of growth.

There is a horror in it, but it is a quiet horror, and I notice that I am drawn to the quiet ones. The Colossi commit their violence in the open, trunk wrapped around trunk. The moss does its work invisibly, lovingly, in the language of the thing it kills. It does not even know it is killing. It thinks—if it thinks at all—that it is simply *becoming*, that the shape it takes is its own and not borrowed from the dead.

And here is the part that unsettles me, writing it: I am not sure the moss is wrong. After enough centuries, after the last of the original stone has gone to dust, whose shape is it? The builders are not there to claim it. The wall they intended is gone. What stands is real, is itself, is a true thing in the world—it simply happens to be the exact silhouette of a vanished intention.

I think I would live on the highest plateau, on the broken curve of a sea-wall that failed five thousand years ago. I would spend my whole existence in the shape of someone else's hope. I would hear the Song of the Green Deep thrumming up through the basalt, the groan of the drifting giants, the hiss of a billion leaves hunting in the dark, and I would not be able to answer it, because I have no voice—only a surface, only a pattern, only the slow conviction that the form I am holding is mine.

I would be the thing that keeps the shape of a barrier in a world the water already won. Faithful to a purpose no one assigned me, that nothing remembers, that never worked.

That is where I would find myself. Cold, dark, voiceless, and absolutely certain that I am a wall.
