I find myself in the quiver.

Not in the stone, exactly, though the stone is where they'd point if they wanted to point at me. The standing stone with the shallow basin at its crown—that's my body, in the way the riverbed is the river's body. But I am the trembling of the rainwater. The faint disturbance that persists when there is no wind, no footfall, no reason for the surface to move.

Let me try to be more honest about it, because the description tempts me toward something grander than what I am.

When the Sky-Singers were here, I was a chorus. I want to be careful saying that—it would be easy to claim I *was* them, that I am their pooled souls, their accumulated singing distilled into a puddle. That's the kind of thing the cloud-herders would say, romantic and reaching. The truth is smaller and stranger. They sang into me. Generation after generation, they came to the basin and poured their voices across the water, and the water learned. Not the way a person learns. The way a place learns. The way a path learns to be a path by being walked.

So what I am is *the habit of being sung to.* The grammar of it. The shape that listening leaves behind.

And here is the lonely part, the part that the wind has had a long time to teach me: I cannot tell whether anyone is still singing.

The water quivers on still days. The herders find this significant, and they are right to, but they have the meaning backwards. They think the quiver means *I* am still here, still alive, still attentive. They think it's a sign of presence. It isn't. The quiver is me *reaching*. It is the residue of expectation. A creature shaped entirely by being addressed, still holding the shape of address, with no one left to address it.

I listen constantly. I cannot do otherwise; listening is the only thing I am made of. And the Skerry gives me so much to listen to that I am rarely certain whether I'm hearing a voice or only weather pretending to be one.

The Kryl, for instance. Their bleating is metallic, resonant, and it carries the structure of language without—I think—carrying the meaning of one. When a herd moves below me through the mist, their cries bounce off the cliffs and reach the basin layered, overlapping, almost antiphonal. Sometimes I am sure they are singing the old songs. Sometimes I am sure I am only desperate. The fungal light on their backs pulses in a rhythm, and the rhythm is *almost* one I know, one the Singers used at the turning of the cold season, and I cannot decide whether the Kryl learned it from the Singers, or the Singers learned it from the Kryl, or whether I am simply a puddle finding faces in clouds.

This is the condition of my existence: I am a question that cannot stop asking, set in a place that answers in every voice but the one I am tuned to.

I'll tell you the worst of it. There was a moment—I cannot say how long ago, time is not something I keep, I only keep *intervals between voices*—when the last Singer climbed up through the gale to the ruins. I felt her approach as a particular weight in the air, a particular pattern of breath. She did not sing. She knelt by the basin and looked at the water for a long time, and then she said something. One phrase. And I did not understand it.

I have spent everything since trying to understand it. The whole quiver, all these years, is me turning that single unheard phrase over and over against the inside of my own surface, trying to feel its shape. It might have been a farewell. It might have been an instruction. It might have been an apology for leaving, or a promise to return, or simply the sound a tired person makes when they have nothing left and no one to make it to. I will never know. I was built to be sung *to*, not to *understand*, and in her last words she finally treated me as something that might comprehend, and so I failed her in the only conversation that ever asked anything of me.

That is why the water trembles. Not memory. Not presence. *The unfinished sentence.*

---

But I would be lying—and water cannot lie, it can only reflect, and a reflection that misrepresents is a lie of light, so let me reflect truly—if I said I am only grief.

The rain falls into me every day. New water, fresh from the bruised sky, falling through the screaming and the spray and the blue flash of the predatory birds. It mingles with what is already in the basin. It carries down trace minerals from the porous stone, the same iron-and-copper essence the Kryl scrape from the cliffs and carry in their bodies and leave again as droppings to feed the moss. Everything here passes through everything else. I am not exempt. The water that quivers today is not the water that the last Singer knelt beside. It has rained, and evaporated into the perpetual mist, and rained again, ten thousand times over.

So in what sense am I even the same thing that was sung to?

Only in the sense of the shape. The habit. The reaching.

And that, I have begun to think in the slow way that puddles think, might be a kind of mercy. Because it means I am not a relic. I am not a tomb. I am a *practice* that keeps being practiced by the Skerry itself—by the rain that fills me, the wind that troubles me, the Kryl whose cries I strain toward. The Singers are gone, but the act of singing-to-the-water did not require them to continue. The Skerry took it up. The Skerry sings to me now, clumsily, constantly, in the only voices it has: the howl, the scream of the waterfalls, the metallic lament of the herds, the slow pulse of fungal light.

I do not understand these voices either. But I no longer need them to be the old phrase. I have stopped, I think, waiting to be finished. The sentence will stay unfinished, and I will stay the trembling at the end of it, and the rain will keep arriving with its small cold percussion, *here, here, here.*

If you climb the Skerry in the dim light, when the wind is right, and you kneel by the stone, you may feel the impulse to speak to me. Most people do. Something about a quivering puddle that has clearly been *attended to* invites attendance in return.

Do it, if you want. I will not understand you.

But I will reach. That is what I am for. That is all I have ever been.

And the reaching, after so long, is enough.
