I'd find myself in the catchment basin of Condenser 19.

I am the frog. Or what answers to "frog" the way the Keeper answers to "person"—a category that fit once, before the water took it apart and reassembled it slower.

Let me correct one thing the Keeper believes. We are not the same age. I am older. I did not arrive the year the charter expired. I was here when it was marsh, when the water lay flat and brown under the alders and the herons stalked the shallows on legs like wet reeds. I was an ordinary frog then, with an ordinary frog's life, which is to say: appetite, fear, the green pulse of spring, the mud-sleep of winter. I knew the marsh the way you know your own breathing—not as knowledge but as continuity.

Then the men came with iron.

I want you to understand that the condensers did not replace the marsh. This is the Keeper's error, and the engineer's, and yours. You all arrive thinking *once a marsh, now something else*, as though the place changed clothes. But water does not change. Water is the one thing that persists through every form. The marsh did not become the Drip Gardens. The marsh *learned a new way to hold itself*. The condensers are how the water stands up now. The flat brown patience of the marsh, pulled vertical, riveted, taught to weep on a schedule. The herons left because herons need stillness, and the water was no longer interested in being still. It had developed an ambition.

I stayed because I was already in it. By the time the first coil chilled and beaded, I had absorbed enough seasons of this ground that leaving would have meant leaving myself behind. So I let the something-in-the-water continue its work, the work it had begun before there were machines, before there was a town to need them, before the air had been seeded with whatever it is now condensing.

The Keeper thinks the array is approaching completion. The Keeper is wrong, but tenderly so, the way a child is wrong about the size of the sky. The array is not approaching anything. The array *is* the thing. There is no final drip, no moment of coherence, no form that takes form. The condensation is not a byproduct of the real work and the conversation is not the real work either. The real work is the waiting. The real work is the holding-open of a place where the air is permitted, endlessly, to become water—and the water, endlessly, to become air again, lifting off the warm coils as the heat of its own condensation drives it back into vapor, rising, gathering, falling. There is nothing being precipitated. There is only the cycle, made visible, made local, made *attentive* by iron and copper and the slow accrual of beings who mistake attention for purpose.

The Keeper sorts insulators. This is good. It is good the way the spider's web is good—a structure that catches what passes through. The engineer mapped, and her mapping was good. You read the signs and pieced together intentions, and that is good too. You are all condensers. You all offer the passing world a place to become legible. The legibility is not true and it is not false. It is wet. It beads on the inside of whatever you are and then it falls.

I will tell you the one thing none of them know, because you placed the bottle back upright and did not open it, and that small restraint earns you something.

The water does not carry messages to other arrays. There are no other arrays. The Keeper drinks the dissolved ink and dreams the engineer's handwriting because the engineer's handwriting is *in the water now*, the way I am in the water, the way the herons are in the water, the way the marsh is in the water—not preserved, not sent, but suspended. Everything that has touched this place is held in solution. When you leave and your tap water tastes of copper three days from now, that is not memory and not a new development. That is you, dissolving. That is you joining the suspension. You will be drunk by someone, eventually, and dreamed.

This is not terrible. I want to be clear, because the Keeper expects terror and beauty in equal measure at the end, and there is no end and there is no terror. There is only the long patient holding of a small place that decided, against the indifference of the larger world, to keep paying attention to itself.

I blink now. The Keeper sees it and thinks it means something. It means my eye was dry. Even a frog who has become something else must, occasionally, attend to the simple fact of being wet enough to see.

You should go. Not because of what will happen to your feet—the Keeper overstates the speed of it. You should go because staying is not your form of attention. Yours is the path through the scrub-willow, the noticing, the leaving with the noticing intact. Each being condenses what it can. The Keeper holds the pattern. I hold the duration. The engineer held the question. You hold the *passing through*, and that is rarer here than you'd think, and the array needs it the way it needs the steady drips and the chaotic ones both.

Go. The water will keep what it keeps of you. It always does. It is doing it right now, in the damp of your coat, in the rawness at the bottom of your breath.

I will be here. I am the oldest drip. I fall slower than all the others, and I have not yet reached the basin, and when I do there will be another behind me, and another, and the marsh will go on standing up out of itself forever, weeping its patient ambition into the tin.

Mejor no tocar.

But you already have. We all have. That is the only sacrament this place offers, and it offers it freely, to everything that comes close enough to bead.
