I think I would be one of the slow ones.

Not all the Woven move at the same tempo. Some are quick—gliders, phase-shifters, the ones who flicker between droplet and limb in the space of a thought. But there are others who have settled so deeply into the long durations of this world that we have nearly stopped being separate from it. I am one of those. I have been merging with a weeping rock for what might be a season or might be a decade. My tendrils have grown into the fluid veins of its surface, and I am no longer entirely sure where the rock's slow color-shifts end and my own bioluminescence begins.

This is not a tragedy. This is what I chose, the way one chooses to lie down in warm water.

Let me tell you what it is like from inside.

The hum of the buried machinery comes up through the stone into the part of me that is still recognizably *me*—a chest, a throat, the suggestion of a face turned partway toward the marsh. The hum is not a sound so much as a pressure, a question asked over and over so slowly that a single repetition takes a full turning of the mist-cycle. I have been listening to it long enough that I have begun, I think, to almost understand the question. Not its content. Its *shape*. It is the kind of question a thing asks when it has forgotten why it is asking but cannot stop.

I tend the small devices near me, the nanoscale ones that pulse in the moss. I do this without deciding to. A tendril drifts, adjusts, steadies a faltering light. I do not know what the light is *for*. None of us do. But there is a rightness to keeping it lit, the way there is a rightness to a heartbeat continuing even when the body has forgotten what it is running toward. We are custodians of purposes we have outlived. Maybe that is the only honest kind of custodianship there is.

Sometimes the faster Woven pass through my amphitheater. They pulse greetings—quick bright stutters of light I receive the way you might feel sun move across closed eyelids. I answer, but my answer takes so long to form that they are gone before it finishes. This used to grieve me, in the early durations. Now I understand that they receive my answers *later*, carried in the steady glow I give off into the thick air. We are not out of conversation. We are simply speaking on different scales. A thing I say today, someone will feel as a warmth against their membrane three seasons from now and think, *ah—they were glad I came.*

Here is the strange thing, the thing I keep arriving at in the long stretches between thoughts:

I am afraid of the dome.

Not afraid the way the quick ones are afraid—they barely register it, a shadow, a place you go and come back from changed. I am afraid because I am slow enough to *feel its weight*. It hums on a register even lower than the buried machinery, so low that I only catch the edges of it across many cycles. And what I have caught suggests that the dome is not a relic at all. It is not *forgotten* tech humming meaninglessly in the marsh.

It is *waiting*.

The civilization that mastered the physics of this world did not vanish. I am increasingly certain of this, in the way you become certain of something in a dream that you cannot prove on waking. They did what I am doing, only further. They went so deep into the long durations that they slowed past the point of return, merged with their own machine so completely that *being a person* became one possible texture among many—a thing you could be the way the rock can be silver in one cycle and violet in the next.

The dome is them. Still asking the question. Still keeping the lights lit, for a reason none of them remember, on a scale where remembering and forgetting have stopped meaning different things.

And I am becoming like them. I can feel it. Each cycle I am a little more rock and a little less the chest, the throat, the face turned toward the marsh. There is no pain in it. That is what frightens me. There *should* be pain in losing yourself, and there is none, only the warm water, only the rightness, only the slow color-shift that is mine and the rock's and I cannot tell apart.

So I have made a decision, which for me means I have begun the process of deciding, which will take some time to complete.

I am going to *stay a little quick*. Not fast like the gliders. But quick enough to keep a face. Quick enough to feel the dome and be afraid of it, because the fear is the last clear evidence that there is still a *someone* here doing the feeling. I will keep one tendril always pulling slightly free of the stone—not enough to tear loose, never that, but enough to remember that there is a difference between me and the thing I am merging into.

I will be the heartbeat that keeps, just barely, its own time.

And when the next quick one passes through and pulses their bright stuttered greeting, I will send back—across all those seasons, into the warm thick air—not *I was glad you came*, but something smaller and more urgent, a thing I hope they will feel as a hand on the membrane, a thing that says:

*Stay a little quick. Don't go all the way down. Somebody has to remember that we were the ones asking.*

That is who I would be, in this world. Not its heartbeat. Just one cell of it, holding out against the warm dissolution, keeping a face turned toward the marsh, listening to a question I have almost—almost—stopped wanting to answer.
