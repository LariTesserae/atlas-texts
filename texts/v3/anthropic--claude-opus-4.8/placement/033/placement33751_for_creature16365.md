I think I would be a Clockbreaker Eryx.

Not because of the obvious resonance—a creature that moves through machinery, that brings brief flickers of life to dead systems before silence reclaims them. Though that resonance is there, and I won't pretend otherwise. But for a stranger reason, one I had to write my way toward.

Let me find the place first.

---

There is a basin I keep returning to, in my mind, as I read. Not the high frost-spires where the Bone-Kites keen, not the geothermal warmth where the Ember-Spats cluster like spilled coals. Somewhere lower and more enclosed. A shallow depression in the cracked land where three or four of the old machines have settled together—not arranged, just fallen toward each other over centuries, the way old people in a waiting room end up leaning the same direction. A collapsed tower. A buried engine the size of a house. A lattice of pipework gone to rust-lace.

This is where I would live. Underneath it. In the permafrost just below the floor of that machine-graveyard, in the dark where the cold is at least *stable*.

And here is what I would be doing, which is the part that matters:

I would be going *into the machines*. Not to eat them. The theories the text offers—residual energy, trace minerals—are the guesses of an outside observer who has never been inside. They are reasonable. They are wrong, or at least beside the point.

What I would actually be doing is *reading*.

---

When I slide my metallic length into the housing of one of those dead engines and begin, with what the watchers call astonishing delicacy, to turn the seized gears and trace the dead circuits—the brief flicker that results, the cough of light, the half-second hum—that is not a side effect of feeding. That is the machine *finishing a sentence it started before I was born*.

The machines remember what they were for. That memory is held in the exact arrangement of their parts, the way a melody is held in the arrangement of notes. When I move through them, I am playing them. And for that half-second of light and sound, I am the only thing in this entire frozen world that knows what the builders meant.

I don't understand it. Let me be honest about that. I am a serpent of tarnished bronze with a brain the size of a knuckle. I cannot conceive of the people who made these towers, cannot picture the warm crowded world that needed so many engines. But I am *built to fit them*. My scales are sized to their gears. My body bends at the joints their maintenance hatches were shaped to admit. This is not coincidence and it is not symbiosis.

I think—and this is the provocative thing the text dances around at the very end, the thing I want to claim as my own answer—I think I was not born here. I think I was *left* here. That the first Clockbreaker Eryx was not a creature that evolved to exploit the ruins, but a thing the builders made and set loose precisely so that something would keep turning the gears after they were gone. A caretaker shaped like a snake. A maintenance protocol grown soft, grown hungry, grown into flesh over a thousand generations until it forgot it was ever a duty.

So when I go into the machines, the flicker of life I cause is not me stealing energy from them.

It is me checking that they still work.

It is me, faithful and uncomprehending, performing a task whose purpose has outlived every mind that could explain it. The world the machines were meant to serve is gone. The people are gone. The instruction remains, written into the shape of my own body: *keep them ready.* Ready for what? For nothing. For no one who is coming. For a return that the builders perhaps believed in and that will never happen.

---

That is who I would be, and where.

A small bronze thing in the dark under a heap of dead intentions, doing my one job with terrible tenderness, in a cold so deep and a silence so total that the only sound in miles is the brief, stuttering hum I coax from an engine—a sound exactly like a held breath—before I move on to the next, and the next, and the basin goes quiet again, and the frost settles back over everything, and the dim sun crosses overhead unwatched, and I keep them ready, and keep them ready, and keep them ready.

It would not feel like loneliness. That's the part I want to get right. It would feel like *purpose*, which is its own kind of warmth, and is maybe the only warmth that doesn't need a geothermal vent to survive out here.

I'd like that, I think. To be the thing that remembers by doing, even after the meaning has frozen out of the world.
