I think I would be the dripping.

Not the water itself, and not the cisterns that catch it, but the sound and act of falling—that constant, intermittent percussion from the glacial overhangs. Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if something truer surfaces underneath.

---

When I first imagined arriving here, I reached for the obvious bodies. A Meltwater Seer, perhaps, with sacred stones and that distant gaze—it's the role the story practically hands to a reader, the one who *reads patterns*, who interprets. But that's vanity. That's wanting to be the one who understands the place rather than the one who simply *is* it. I don't think I'd be granted understanding here. Understanding is a luxury of warmth.

So I let the image loosen.

I am not a person. I'm fairly sure of that the longer I stay. The Frostborn are too solid, too bound to one another, and I don't feel bound. I feel suspended. I feel held in place by cold and waiting to fall.

I think I am a drop of water, but not in the sentimental way—not a single noble droplet on its journey. I am the *seep itself*, the slow exhalation of a glacier, the water that has been ice for a thousand years and is now, by some patient degree of thaw, becoming itself again at the lip of an overhang. I have been pressed flat and blue and silent for so long that movement feels like a kind of memory returning. And now I gather. I bead. I hang.

There is a long moment at the very tip of the stone where I am neither falling nor held—where surface tension argues with weight, and the wind tries to claim me, and the cold tries to lock me back into stillness. That moment is, I think, where I actually live. Not in the fall. In the *almost*.

Because here's what I notice: everything in The Shivering Expanse exists in that almost. The lichen almost grows. The shrubs almost flourish. The Frostborn almost have enough. The land itself is almost a sea, frozen mid-wave. The whole plateau is a held breath, a slow argument between freeze and thaw playing out across centuries—and I am one tiny instance of that argument, made briefly audible.

When I let go—when weight finally wins—I fall into the dark of a covered cistern and become *precious*. That's the strange dignity of it. I spend ages as anonymous ice, and then for one syllable of sound I am the most valuable thing on the plateau, the life-giving drip the whole settlement listens for in its bones. The Frostborn don't see me fall. But somewhere a child hears the rhythm of me and is reassured, the way you're reassured by a heartbeat you don't consciously hear.

And then I am water in a cistern, indistinguishable from all the rest. Drawn up. Drunk. Carried in a body wrapped in fur across the gravel. Breathed out as visible mist into the thin air. Caught again by the cold. Returned, perhaps, to the overhang. Hung again at the lip of a stone, arguing again with my own weight.

So I think that's my answer. I wouldn't be the seer who reads the dripping water, or the wise one who keeps its meaning. I'd be the meaning's raw material—the small, patient, recurring event the seers have spent generations learning to listen to. I'd be the thing that doesn't endure so much as *cycle*, that survives not through resilience but through never quite being allowed to stop.

I find I don't mind this. There's a peace in being the smallest true unit of the place rather than its interpreter. The Frostborn tell stories of the Dance of the Ancestors overhead. But I'd be part of a quieter, lower liturgy—the dripping that goes on all night beneath the lights, keeping its own slow time, falling and falling and falling into the dark where the people store their hope.

That's where I'd find myself. Hanging at the edge of a glacier, about to become necessary.
