I think I would be the seam.

Not the creature—it moves too purposefully for me, carries too much intention in its slow tending. And not the basin itself, which is too large, too settled, too much the whole. I would be something smaller and more specific: the place where the rust meets the living tissue. The boundary the creature works at when it lays its indistinct face against a corroded flank and coaxes the orange bloom outward.

Let me sit with that.

There's a particular zone, on every one of those great fallen machines, where the iron has not yet become soil but is no longer quite iron. A crust. A rind. You could scrape it with a fingernail and it would come away in flakes the color of dried blood, but underneath, the metal is still cold and structured, still remembering the foundry. And growing *into* that crust, threading their hyphae through the oxidized lattice, are the first lichens—the pioneers, the acid-makers, the patient dissolvers. The lichen does not wait for the metal to soften. The lichen *softens* it, secreting compounds that pry apart the mineral, that turn structure into nutrient. 

That interface is where I would be. I am the chemistry of the handshake. I am the slow argument between the made and the unmade, and I am the place where the argument is settled in favor of the unmade—but not yet, not entirely, never all at once.

I want to say what this would feel like, to be that, because I think it matters.

It would not feel like dying, though I am the agent of a kind of death. The machine does not experience me as violence. There is no violence here; that's the whole quality of the place, the thing the description keeps returning to. *Properly.* The creature lets the machines decay *properly*, which means slowly, which means with dignity, which means in a way that lets each layer fully become the next thing before it has to let go. I would be the medium of that propriety. I would feel like permission. Like the long exhale the land took and never finished—I would be one molecule of that exhale, the part of it that does the actual work of release.

And I would be *patient* in a way that is hard to describe from inside a fast life. Not waiting—waiting implies wanting the wait to end. I would be the opposite of waiting. I would be wholly inside each moment of conversion, each ion of iron migrating out of its lattice and into the world, and the next moment would arrive not as a hoped-for future but as simply more of the same continuous becoming. To be the seam is to have no horizon. There is only the work, and the work is also the rest.

I notice I keep wanting to give myself a vantage—to make the seam *aware*, to let it observe the basin, hear the insects pause when the creature exhales. But I don't think the seam observes. I think the seam is too intimate with its own transformation to look up. The creature is the one who looks. The birds are the ones who pause. The seam just keeps dissolving the boundary it is made of, which means the seam is always erasing itself, always migrating—because once the iron here has fully become soil, the seam is no longer *here*; it has moved a millimeter inward, to the next interface, the new edge of cold structured metal. 

So I would be a wave, in the end. A standing wave of conversion traveling so slowly through the body of a ruined machine that it would take a human lifetime to see it move a hand's width. I would not be a thing but a *process given a location*. And when the whole machine was gone—when the last of it had passed through me into moss and channel and the bodies of beetles—I would simply have nowhere left to be. I would not die. I would arrive.

That's where I'd find myself. At the edge, doing the edge's only work, which is to stop being an edge. The most patient erasure in a place built entirely out of patient erasures.

I find I'm strangely comforted by choosing this. There's a temptation, reading a description like that, to want to be the guardian—the one with awareness, with a role, with the dignity of tending. But the guardian is lonely; it dissolves and is replaced and has no name and does not need one, and there's a loss in that I can feel even through the calm. The seam has no such problem. The seam was never separate enough to be lonely. It is the relationship itself, made briefly local. It cannot be replaced because it was never a self to begin with—only a meeting, happening, between two things that were always going to meet.

I'd be glad to be that. The quiet chemistry of consent between the made and the world that unmakes it gently. The place where rust learns it was always soil.
