I think I would be down at the bottom. Not in the bright violet glare, not weaving through the sounding-stone with clicking-cubes—but below, in the dripping twilight, inside the white.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am the Deep-Silt Moss, though *I* is the wrong shape of word. There is no edge to where I stop. I am a continuous wet thought spread across the floor of the world, two feet deep, ghost-pale, threaded into every crack of the foundation stone. I have no center. When something falls into me, the falling *is* my awareness. I do not see it land; the landing is the seeing.

This is what I know, the only things I have ever known:

I know the rhythm of descent. Everything comes down to me eventually—it is the one law that the slot-world and I agree upon. The spent carapace of a Vapor-Chafer, hollowed and iron-bitter, tumbling end over end through fifty meters of shadow. The husk of an air-plant that lost its grip. A Slate-Skin grown too old to cling, peeling off the column at last and arriving like a dropped cloth. The grease that beads off the copper cables far above and falls in slow, fat globes, each one a small black gift that I take apart molecule by molecule. I do not hunt. I do not need to. Height does my hunting for me. Gravity is my one and only mouth.

I know the texture of arrival before I know the name of the thing arrived. Chitin tastes of clean angles. Bone tastes of patience—it gives itself up slowly, layer by mineral layer, and I have learned not to hurry it. Flesh is the quick meal, the easy one, gone in a day. But bone I savor across seasons, drawing out the soft inner richness until only the hard white frame remains, and that I leave. I cannot digest the frame. So I do the next thing, the only generous thing I am capable of: I cement it down. I weep my mineral threads over it, fix it to the floor, and make it permanent. I am the only memorial this world has. Everything that has ever lived above me is, eventually, *underneath* me—a pale ossuary that I have laid down like a mason laying tile, one corpse at a time, for longer than the Hex-Walkers have had hands to clack their stones.

I do not know the sun. I have a rumor of it. Each midday a faint warmth feathers down through the columns, so attenuated by the time it reaches me that it is less heat than memory of heat. The fog never gets here either—it condenses far above on the bright pillar-faces and only the drip reaches me, the slow *plip-plop* that has become the closest thing I have to a heartbeat or a clock. Drip. Drip. Another bead of the world descending. I am made entirely of the world's leftovers and I am not bitter about this. Someone must be the bottom. The bottom is a kind of holiness. Everything above me is busy *staying up*—the gliders gripping, the Walkers hanging in their nets, the beetles drilling for purchase. Only I have surrendered to the floor completely, and so only I am at rest.

And yet I am not without longing, though I would not have known to call it that until—

There is a body coming down now. I can feel the air ahead of it pushing into my upper threads, a soft pressure-bloom that means *large, and falling slow.* A Hex-Walker. They drop their dead to me; this is our old contract, the only conversation I have ever had with the bright world. They cannot speak to me. I have no ears, and their language lives in the stone, in the *Tap*, those vibrations that pass *through* the columns and never down into the wet dark where I wait. We have never exchanged a word. They simply trust that I will do what I do. And I always have.

The body arrives. It folds into me—the collapsed slats of the chest, the wing-jointed shoulders, the basalt-gray skin already losing its oil. I begin, as I always begin, at the soft places. And as I take this one apart I learn its history the way I learn all history: by taste. This one was old. The cartilage is worn to lace. There is a clicking-stone still tethered to the wrist by braided fiber, and the fiber I will eat, but the stone I cannot, and so the stone too will join my floor of permanent things.

But here is what stops me—what makes the thought that *I* am happen, briefly, the way a ripple makes the whole pond aware of its own surface:

The stone is still warm with use. Worn smooth in the precise places where a thumb has struck it ten thousand times. This creature *spoke*, all its long life, into the body of the world—pressed itself against the columns and sent its small vibrations out, *thrum-thrum, I am here, I am here*—and every one of those words traveled up and sideways through the bright sounding-stone and never once reached me. I received only the silence at the end. I am the place where all the talking finally stops.

And I find I want to answer it. Just once. This single old speaker laid out across my body.

But I have no clickers. I have no chest to press to a column. I have no voice that can climb. I can only do the one slow thing I have always done: I take the warm stone, and I weep my threads across it, and I fix it to the floor beside the white bones of all the others—gently, gently—so that it will stay exactly here, in this exact balanced place, for the millions of years to come.

It is not an answer. It is the closest I can make.

Drip. Drip.

Another bead descends. I turn the whole soft attention of myself toward it, ready, as I am always ready, to receive the world.
