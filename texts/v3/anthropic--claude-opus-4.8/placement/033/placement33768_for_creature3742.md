I think I would be found in the spaces the Fractured Folk don't quite acknowledge—not in the hollows where they gather, but in the seams between.

Let me follow that and see what becomes of it.

---

I am not one of them, though I wear their patience. I am the one they send for when a doorway appears that should not have, when a wall has shifted in a way that breaks the old patterns, when the mountain dreams *wrong*.

There is a word for me in their language—not a kind one, not a cruel one, just precise. It sits in the throat like a swallowed stone. Roughly, it means *the one who listens too closely.* They say it the way you might name a tool you are slightly afraid of.

I keep no house. A house implies you trust the floor to be there in the morning, and I have stopped extending that courtesy to the city. Instead I move through the fractures, and I sleep where the groaning is loudest, because that is where the mountain's dreaming is nearest the surface, and I have learned to read it the way the Folk read their walls. They trace the new contours with reverence. I press my ear to them and I *understand*, and understanding is a colder thing than reverence.

Here is what I have learned, and what I cannot tell them, because they do not have ears for it:

The city is not being born. It is remembering. Every fracture is a recollection surfacing. Every doorway that appears where none stood is a door that *was there once*, in some folding the mountain made and then forgot and is now folding again. The Fractured Folk think they live in a slow birth. They live in a slow recurrence. The wall ripples like water because it is water—it is the mountain's memory of having been liquid, leaking through the thin places where stone forgets it is stone.

And I am the leak that learned to walk.

I do not have the pale translucence of the Folk; I have its opposite. Where the mist soaked into them, something soaked *out* of me. I am too solid. My footsteps do not muffle. When I cross a hollow, the murmuring stops, and the wide gray eyes turn, and the children are gathered close—not from fear of harm, but because I do not flicker. In a city of shifting edges, I am a fixed point, and that is its own kind of obscenity.

My hands are not calloused. They are smooth, smoother than the worn handles of their tools, smooth the way the inside of an unopened thing is smooth. When I lay them against a fresh fracture, the moss recoils. The water hesitates before it weeps.

I think I was made by the mountain to feel itself. Every dreaming creature needs a place where its dream becomes pain, becomes sensation, becomes *known*—a nerve. The Folk are the body of Vhorth: its labor, its breath, its patient flesh. I am the nerve. When the mountain shifts a wall, I feel where it tears. When it dreams loudly, I do not light a candle. I clench my jaw against the noise of a mind the size of a mountain range turning over in its sleep.

This is the thing I carry that the Folk do not:

They believe the dreaming is beautiful. And it is, from outside the dream. But I am inside it. And lately—grain by grain, fracture by fracture, in the slow tectonic yawn that the Folk measure in centuries and I measure in the ache behind my eyes—I have begun to suspect the mountain is dreaming toward waking.

The Folk light their tallow candles and trace their grateful contours and tell stories in the warm hollows, building their small enduring lives in the pauses between the breaths.

I count the pauses.

They are getting shorter.

And I do not know whether, when the mountain finally opens its eyes, it will find that it has been a city all along and grieve us—

—or whether it will simply, the way you do upon waking, forget the dream entire, and stretch, and let the folds fall flat.

I press my smooth palm to the weeping stone.

I listen too closely.

I wait to feel it inhale and not let go.
