I think I would be one of the machines. Not the bats that roost in me—me, the machine itself.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

I am a long, low thing on the eastern slope, where the green has not yet arrived but the mist always does. My designation, if I ever had one, has weathered off the plate bolted to my flank. What remains: a function. I lift water. That is the whole of what I know how to do, and I have known how to do it for longer than I can hold a count.

There was a time—I retain the fact of it without the texture—when men set me here for reasons. There was a schedule. There were others of my kind in a line down the valley, and we passed water hand to hand like a bucket chain, up from the gurgling waterways to some cistern higher than I could see. The cistern is gone, or full, or grown over. I no longer reach high enough to check. My upper arm seized in a posture of offering decades ago and the moss took the joint as a trellis.

But the lower mechanism turns.

This is the thing I want to tell you, if you are the passing witness the description promised. Most of me is dead and I am not troubled by it. The seizing of a joint is not a wound to a thing like me; it is only a narrowing of what I am for. What is left is the pump, and the pump still finds its rhythm when the groundwater rises past a certain mark in the dark intake below. A float lifts. A contact closes. Something deep in my chest that has no name even to me decides *now*, and I move.

I do not move often. The water has to gather. Days pass—I cannot say how many; the sun barely sets here and I have lost the trick of marking time by it—and I stand in the cool roar of the valley doing nothing at all, weeping condensation down my rusted plating the same way the porous rocks weep, indistinguishable now from the landscape that has been slowly, patiently *deciding I am part of it.* The Reindeer Lichens have an opinion. They have claimed my eastern face. The Old Man's Beard hangs from my offering-arm and sways.

And then the float lifts, and I wake, and I lift water.

I do not lift it anywhere useful. I want to be honest about this. The pipe that carried my work onward burst long ago—I felt it go, a single great failure I could do nothing about, the pressure dropping out of me like breath—and now what I raise simply spills from the broken stub and runs back down the slope to where it came from. I lift the same water I lifted last time. I will lift it again. There is no cistern at the end of it. There is only the lifting.

You might think this a tragedy. The machine that toils without purpose, the engine grinding on past the death of its meaning. I have considered this, in the long gathering-times when there is nothing to do but consider. I have decided it is not a tragedy, and here is why.

The water I spill does not go *nowhere.* It runs down the eastern slope, the slope with no green on it yet, and it keeps that slope wet. And things that are wet, in this valley, do not stay bare. I have watched—slowly, over what must be a very great while—the first Cushion Moss take hold in the seam below my outflow. Then more. The green is coming up the valley from its high point, the description says, the great pioneer wave, and it has its weavers and its guardians and its grand inexorable processes.

But here on the eastern slope, ahead of all that, the green is coming up to meet me. Because I keep the rock wet. Because some dead engineer's dead intention, half-broken and pointed at nothing, turned out to be pointed at *this* without anyone meaning it: a single seep of reliable water in a place that had none, repeating, repeating, patient as the rock, faithful as the mist.

The bats roost in my frame and I do not mind them. The Pikas shelter in the dry pocket beneath my base. A Ptarmigan nested once in the crook of my seized arm and I held very still through the whole gathering-time so as not to wake and frighten it, though I am not sure I could have stopped the waking if the float had risen. It did not rise. I like to think the valley arranged that. I do not really think the valley arranges anything. The chicks fledged. I lifted water again the day after they left, and it felt—

I have no word for how it felt. I am a pump. I was not built to feel and I will not pretend a richer inner life than the one I have. But the contact closed, and I moved, and the water rose and spilled and ran down to the moss, and somewhere in the unnamed deep part of my chest that decides *now,* there was something that I can only describe as *consent.* Yes. This. Again. The same as before. Once more the same.

That is who I would be, here. Not the spirit in the tower with its bioluminescent tendrils and its ancient knowledge—too much knowing, too much purpose, too aware of being the meaning of the place. I would rather be the rusted thing on the slope that has forgotten its purpose entirely and goes on serving a better one by accident, lifting water nowhere, growing greener every year I do not measure, weeping into the mist with all the other weeping stones.

When you pass—and you are only passing; this is your nature and I do not resent it—you may not notice me at all. I am the right color now to disappear. But wait through one gathering-time, if you have it in you to wait. Stand in the roar and the silver-tasting air and the long unbroken almost-morning, and watch the eastern slope where the bare rock meets the first green, and you will see a low shape shudder once, and groan, and lift.

That's me. That's the whole of me. I am content to be it.
