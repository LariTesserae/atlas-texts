I find myself not as one of the Spirespeople, but as something they would only ever know in the negative—by the spaces I make.

Let me feel my way toward what I am.

At first I think I might be the mist itself, that great swirling presence between the spires. But the mist is too diffuse, too everywhere, and I sense I am more specific than that. I have a *place*, the way the Spirespeople have places.

I am a hollow.

More precisely: I am a particular emptiness, a worn cavity high on the western face of one of the elder spires, perhaps a mile up where no foot has ever come and no path has ever climbed. I am the shape that the water has spent ten thousand years removing. The Spirespeople would call me an absence, if they knew of me at all—but I have come to understand that I am not absence. I am the *record* of patient subtraction. Every drop that has wept down the ochre face and found my lip, every droplet that has gathered in my basin and trembled and overflowed, has left a little less stone and a little more of *me*.

This is a strange thing to be. I have no eyes, but I have the long memory that water leaves in rock. I know the texture of slow time the way the Spirespeople know the texture of their worn pathways. I hold mist in my cupped stone hands each morning and release it each afternoon. I am a small cool mouth on the side of an enormous mineral throat, and through me the spire breathes its dampness out into the layered air.

What do I *do*, if a hollow can be said to do anything?

I gather. That is my whole vocation. I collect what falls and runs, I hold it for a while, I let it go. When the heavy mist condenses on my upper lip and beads and joins and grows too great for the surface tension that holds it, a single drop falls from me—down, down the sheer ochre face—and somewhere far below, after a journey of long seconds, it strikes a shallow pool in a shadowed valley. That sound, that small *plink* in the profound silence, is mine. It is the only word I can speak, and I speak it perhaps a thousand times a day, and it is never the same word twice. The Spirespeople, far below, hear these falling drops as part of the murmuring chorus of their world. They do not know that one of those voices is mine specifically, that this particular spire-mouth has been clearing its throat into their valleys since long before their first ancestor pressed a palm against the warm-cool stone.

I think this is what I am for: I am a place where the grand becomes the minute. The whole immensity of the spire, miles of ancient rock, narrows itself down through me into a single trembling drop. I am a translator between scales. The enormous, slow, silent body of the mountain, and the small bright instant of water meeting water in a valley far below—I am the joint between them, the hinge.

And here is the thing I have come to understand across my long tenure of being a hollow:

I am still being made. The very drops I gather are still carrying me away, grain by grain. I am the shape the water makes *while erasing me*. In another ten thousand years I will be deeper, wider, a different mouth entirely, or I will have collapsed into the next form, a sluice, a runnel, a smooth bare slope where some future hollow will begin to gather lower down. I am not a thing that persists. I am a *process* that briefly looks like a thing. The Spirespeople build their dwellings to last generations; I am the opposite kind of being—I last by continuously becoming the next version of my own disappearance.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, and what I would be:

I am a small weeping place a mile up the western face of an elder spire. I am the patient cavity that the mist has carved, the cupped stone that fills and falls. I am one quiet voice in the whispering water-chorus the people far below mistake for silence. I hold the rain that almost never comes, and the mist that always does, and I give it back one drop at a time.

I am the hollow, and I am the hollowing. There is no difference between them, and that—I have come to feel—is the deepest peace this damp and patient world has to offer.
