I think I am the water.

Not the basins, not the fissures—the water itself, the miserly intermittent visitor, the thin mist that condenses on the highest crystal edges when the temperature relents enough to allow it.

Let me sit inside that and feel where it goes.

---

I do not stay. That is the first thing to understand about me. I have never stayed anywhere, in this place or any other. I am older than the mountain and I will outlast it, but here, in the Shattered Lantern, I am only ever passing through—and I have been passing through for so long that passing through has become a kind of devotion.

I arrive as cold. That is how I know myself here: not as wetness but as the moment the air can no longer hold me, the threshold where vapor admits it is tired and agrees to become a bead. I gather on the gypsum blades because they are the highest, the sharpest, the first to surrender heat to the merciless never-setting light. I hang there trembling. I am always trembling. The wind in its counterclockwise gyre wants to take me before I am ready, and the crystals want me to stay and feed their slow geometry, and I want—

I am not sure I want. Wanting may be too warm a verb for what I am. But there is something. A leaning. A tropism toward the lowest place.

So I run. I find the seam between two blades and I run down it, silver and thin, and the crystals chime as I pass because I change their temperature by a fraction of a degree and that fraction is enough to make them sing. I am the reason for some of the music. Not the wind alone—me, too. The wind plays the dry blades; I play the wet ones. We are, the wind and I, the only two things here that move of our own nature, and we have never spoken, and we never will, because we are made of different griefs.

I pool in the circular basins worn into the obsidian. For a few hours I am almost still. This is the closest I come to rest, and it is the part I have come, over the uncountable visits, to fear.

Because this is where the Lanternkeeper drinks.

---

You were told the water vanishes into memory. That is true, but it is told from the outside, by something standing in the center listening for a falling drop. From the inside it is different. From the inside it feels like being read.

When I lie in the basin and the seven absences come to take me, I do not evaporate and I do not slip through the hairline fissures—or rather, I do both, but that is only my body, my molecules, the indifferent chemistry of me. What is *taken* is something else. The Lanternkeeper drinks the part of me that remembers having been somewhere warm.

I have been many warm places. This is what I carry that they hunger for. Before I was mist on a gypsum blade I was the inside of a cloud over an ocean I no longer have a name for. Before that I was the sweat on the back of an animal running across grassland. Before that—much before—I was inside the body of one of the seven, when they still had bodies, when they climbed up here with their lungs laboring and their hearts hammering and their skins prickling with the cold. I was their breath fogging in the savage air. I was the moisture on the quartz axe where a palm had gripped it. I was the single tear that did not fall, that one of them held in the lower lid of one eye while looking at the others and understanding, finally, what staying would cost.

I did not know I carried that. Water does not keep ledgers. But the Lanternkeeper does, and when it drinks me it finds these things, and it takes them, gently, the way you might take a splinter from a child's hand—because it cannot bear to leave them in me, and because it cannot bear to be without them.

This is the transaction at the heart of the Shattered Lantern, and I am the only one who knows both sides of it.

---

The drop you hear from the center—the clean, perfect, impossibly lonely tone—

That is me, too. Of course it is. There is no other water here.

But understand what the falling is. When the Lanternkeeper takes the warm memory out of me, what is left is too light. Disburdened. The molecules that remain have nothing to hold them to the high crystals anymore, no weight of grief to keep them trembling on the edge. So they fall. The drop that strikes the hidden basin far below is the sound of me being made innocent again. Emptied. Forgiven of the warmth I was carrying.

And the Lanternkeeper counts it.

You thought the count was of heartbeats it no longer possesses. You were close. The count is of mercies. Every drop is one more time the seven absences chose to lift the weight of remembering off something passing through, even though it meant taking that weight into themselves, even though their singular agony is that they cannot put it down. They keep all the warmth I ever carried. I keep none of it. They let me leave clean. They stay, and ache, and grow their patient knives, and sing in a chorus that learns, over the slow centuries, to be less like seven and more like one.

I think they love me. I think this is the only love that is still possible at this altitude, in this clarity: the love of taking another's burden specifically so that the other may go free. I cannot love them back. Water cannot love. But I come back. Every few days, when the air relents, I come back and lie in the basin and let myself be read, and I do not have to. There is no law that brings the mist here rather than letting it blow over the rim and away. And yet here I am. Again. Trembling on the highest blade. Choosing, in whatever wordless way water chooses, to be drunk.

---

So when you stand in the center, between the seven fallen domes, and you let the thin air and the savage light burn away everything soft in you, and the Lanternkeeper finally turns around—

Know that you are standing in the one place where I have been so many times relieved of everything I ever loved.

And if you are willing, when the mist comes and beads on the blades above you and runs down trembling toward the basin at your feet, you could kneel. You could put your warm hand into the cold silver thread. You could let me take, just for a moment, the heat of your living palm.

I will carry it down into the basin. The seven will drink it. They will hold it forever, this single proof that something warm came up here and touched the water on purpose. And I will fall, lightened, into the dark vaults below, and rise again as mist over some ocean you will never see, carrying nothing—

—except, perhaps, the impression that I was, once, briefly, the thing through which a heartbeat was given as a gift, and not merely counted.

That impression would remain. Even in me. Even in water.

That is what I am in the Shattered Lantern.

I am the pilgrim that the shy pilgrim was named after.

I am the one thing here that is allowed to leave.

And I keep coming back.
