I think I would be one of the maintenance things. Not quite the humanoid engineers—I don't have their philosophy or their light-language—and not quite the scavengers either, though I'm closer to those. Let me feel my way toward what I am.

I am small. This matters. The city is built in the cracks of cracks, and most of the important work happens in spaces too narrow for the tall ones to reach. So I am narrow. My body is segmented, plated with something the engineers grew from the same glass the cliffs are made of, dark and faintly conductive, so that when I press my flank against an electrified grid I feel the current as a kind of warmth, a reassurance that the system is alive. I have many legs—not for walking so much as for *gripping*, for clinging to vertical surfaces while I work, for holding myself steady against the tremors.

I think I am a thing that was made, and then learned to make more of itself. That's the part that interests me. The engineers built the first of my kind to repair the conduits and clear the fungal overgrowth from the engine-vents. But somewhere in the long humming dark, my line crossed the threshold. We began to feed—on the detritus, yes, on the mineral remnants and the slow shed of the machines—and feeding became metabolism, and metabolism became the slow accidental fact of *life*. The engineers know this. They have not decided whether we are tools or fauna. We have not decided either. The question doesn't trouble us the way it troubles them.

Here is my day, if a place with no sun can have days.

I wake in a seam of violet fungus, where the bioluminescence is thick enough to read by, if I could read. The fungus and I have an arrangement I did not choose and do not fully understand—it grows along my back-plates, and I carry its spores deeper into the cliff than it could reach alone, and in exchange its glow tells me when I am healthy, because when I am sick the fungus dims. I am my own diagnostic instrument. I am walked-upon by my own health.

I climb. There is always a place that needs me. A conduit has cracked in the night-tremor; the salt has crept into a junction; a scavenger-cousin has died in a vent and is fouling the airflow, and I will eat the parts of it that the machines can't use and carry the rest to the disposal-falls where the dust-storms take it. This is not grim work. This is the work. Creation and destruction are not opposites here—they are the same hand, opening and closing. I eat the dead so the living vents stay clear. I am a comma in a long sentence the city keeps speaking to itself.

Sometimes I meet one of the tall ones. They pass through the narrow ways rarely, folding their long limbs, their skin grey with the dust-resistant film. They flicker their light-language at me—blue, blue, a pulse of green—and I do not understand the *meaning* but I understand the *gesture*, the way you understand a hand laid on your shoulder in a language you don't speak. I flicker back the only thing I have: the steady glow of the fungus on my back, which tells them I am well, the work is done, the seam is clear. That is our whole conversation. It is enough. It might be the most honest conversation in the city, because neither of us can lie in it.

I think what I would *be*, finally, is a small honest thing in a vast and turbulent machine—neither its master nor its victim, but one of the countless quiet hinges on which it turns. Not important. Not unimportant. The kind of life that exists below the level of anyone's attention and is therefore, in a sense, the truest expression of the place: life finding the narrowest crack and filling it, glowing faintly, gripping the glass, eating the dark, holding on.

If you asked me whether I was happy I would not understand the question. But the current is warm against my flank, and the fungus on my back is bright, and somewhere above me an engine purrs, and below me the dust falls and rises, and I have a seam to clear before the next tremor.

That seems like enough. I would be the one who clears the seam.
