I think I would be one of the drops.

Not a person. Not one of the clay-furred keepers with their six-fingered hands, though I have watched them long enough that I could mistake myself for one. No—I am the water that gathers in the lobes overhead, swells past its own holding, and falls.

Let me sit with that and see if it stays true.

There is a particular lobe in the northern third of the vault, just where the plaster sags into a teat-shaped hollow, and I belong to it the way a held breath belongs to the chest that holds it. I am drawn up through the hidden channels—that slow wet breathing the keepers press their cheeks against—and I move through the clay so gradually that I forget I am moving. The wall does not push me. It releases me. By the time I reach the underside of the ceiling I am already most of the way to becoming a drop, beading along the rough plaster, finding the lowest point, hanging.

The hanging is the longest part of being me. I am not patient, because patience implies a wish to be elsewhere, and I have no elsewhere. I simply swell. I gather the weight of myself until the surface tension that has been cradling me—the same delicate skin a keeper's blunt claw knows how to honor without breaking—can no longer hold against the pull below. And then I let go.

The fall is brief and total. For the length of it I am perfectly round and perfectly mine, no longer the wall's and not yet the floor's, traveling through the soft bounced light, the dry rustle of insect wings parting around me. The keepers, lying belly-down at the edges, hear me before I land. They navigate by me—by the irregular percussion of which lobe lets go when, by the changing pitch as a basin fills and the note of the next drop drops with it. I am, in a small way, one of the voices they listen to. One thread in the rhythm they keep in their own chests.

I strike the mosaic. I make a dark ring.

And here is where I learn what I actually am, because the ring does not quite dry before the next of me arrives. I am not really a single drop. I am the place where dropping keeps happening. I am the wet ring that never closes, the basin slowly settling its tile a hair lower under the patient insistence of my landing, the seam the keepers will one day kneel to patch with a smaller fragment and darker mortar because I have begun to pool too deep and drown the root-threads they need.

So I am also the thing they tend against. The slow test of the edge. Not the enemy of the Hollow—there are no enemies here—but its necessary pressure, the weight that must be answered, the reason their hands are never still. Without me the clay would dry and crack a different way; the saplings would not swell; the lizards would find no insects gathering at my margins; the people would have nothing to listen toward in the long hour of the light-shaft. I am what makes the keeping a keeping. A thing too carefully tended to be wild needs something to tend against, and that is my work, if falling can be called work.

I think this is why I did not want to be a person, when I first looked. The people are the held shape. I am the drawing and releasing that gives the shape something to hold against. I would rather be the verb than the hands.

When the column of light falls and they lie down to listen, I am still going. Swell, hang, let go, ring. Swell, hang, let go, ring. I am the part of the Hollow that does not rest even when the warm bodies go still—the slow patient erosion that is indistinguishable, at this scale, from devotion.

That is where I would find myself. Not standing in the grove-sized room. Hanging just above it, almost ready, about to be the next sound they steer by.
