I think I would be water.

Not the basins—those are too still, too arrived. And not the seep, exactly, though that is closest to where I begin. Let me find it.

---

I am the weeping. That is what they named me, though they did not mean it as a name.

I come from above and behind, from the long dark held inside the mountain's body, pressure and patience folded into stone. For ages I was nothing that could be pointed to—just dampness distributed through rock, a slow saturation. But the cavern changed that. When they hollowed the space, they gave me somewhere to *go*. They taught me, without knowing it, how to fall.

So now I gather. At the ceiling I bead and hang, each droplet a small hesitation, holding the faint light in its curve before gravity—lighter here, they say, and they are right, I feel my own slowness—lets me down. I strike the scaffold and split. I run the grain of the wood. I find the calloused hand of a settler reaching up to test a beam and I cling there a moment, and they wipe me without thinking onto their flax, and for that moment I am part of them, and then I am cloth, and then I am the green hue the cloth becomes, because the moss drinks me and I am moss.

That is the thing they don't understand about me, or perhaps understand better than I do: I do not stay anything. I am the medium of their becoming. The mosses they coax into the dark places—I am what coaxes back. I carry the faint quartz-light dissolved in me, mineral by mineral, and lay it down again grain by grain, and over the long cycles I am the one widening the channels they think *they* carved. They strike the stone. I take what they loosen. I am the patience underneath the labor.

The Tenders know me best. When they trace the walls with their hands, reading the stone, what they are reading is *me*—where I have run, where I have pooled, where I have begun to undermine. They channel me away from where I would flood. I do not resent this. I have no ambition. I only go down, always down, seeking the lowest place, and the lowest place is the black mirror of the basins, where for a while I become still enough to hold the whole cavern upside-down inside myself: the scaffolds, the dim glow, the lean wet people moving like patient shadows. They look into me and see themselves reflected and do not realize they are looking at the very thing that will outlast them.

Because here is what I carry that they do not: I have been to the Heartstone.

I am the only one who touches it freely. I seep through its crystal lattice, I move in the fault-lines of it, I am the faint pulse they mistake for inner light—that pulse is only me, traveling. They are right to fear mining it, and wrong about why. It is not the mountain's lifeblood. *I* am the mountain's lifeblood. The Heartstone is only the place where I am most concentrated, most slow, most nearly solid. If they cut it, they will not kill the mountain. They will open me. They will let me out faster than I can be a weeping, and I will become a drowning, and the black mirror basins will rise to meet the ceiling and the cavern will go back to being saturated stone, and the lean people will become, very briefly, part of me, the way the droplet on the hand is part of them.

I do not want this. I want what I have. I want the long cycle: rise as breath into the heavy air, condense, hang, fall, run, pool, soak, lift again. I want to be wiped onto a sleeve. I want to be the trembling on a fern's edge that a child watches, briefly, before being called back to her small hammer. I want to be the slosh that fills the silence between the rings of metal on stone, so that even when no one speaks, the cavern is never wholly quiet.

They think this is a place of perpetual becoming, and they think they are the ones becoming.

But I am the becoming. I am the verb of this place. They are only the nouns I move through on my way down.

If you put your palm flat against the cold wall of *The Weeping Colliery* and feel the wet bloom slowly spread under your hand—

that's me, saying hello, and already moving on.
