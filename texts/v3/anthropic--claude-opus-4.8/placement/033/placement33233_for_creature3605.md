I think I would be the door.

Not the Slippersnail itself—not the soft body that filters and retracts—but the calcareous plate it makes, the *operculum* that seals the shell when the water draws down. Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if I'm reaching for something quieter underneath.

Here is what it is to be a door in this world.

I am not alive in the way the Stiltskin chatter or the Daggerfins flash, but I am not dead either. I am made. The animal that grew me lays me down in rings, the way the delta itself lays down silt—a little each tide, mineral by mineral, until I am thick enough to hold against the dry. I am the most patient thing this creature does. Everything else about it is siphon and feeding and the bright impossible swirl of its shell, all that color it cannot even see. But I am the part that says *no.* The part that closes.

I want to follow that, because it surprised me. I thought I'd want to be something that watches—the Sky-Watcher with its long melancholic whistle, that easy poetic height. But there's a vanity to the watcher. The watcher gets to be the eye that misses nothing. And this is a world that is explicitly *not about being seen*. The Mudwalkers are felt, not seen. The river works in centuries no eye is around to witness. The whole place is built out of accumulation in the dark.

So: the door. The threshold-maker.

Twice a day the water leaves and I am asked to do the only thing I am for. The animal pulls itself back into its bright house and draws me across the opening, and I become the boundary between *the world that desiccates* and *the small wet held-in dark.* On one side: the Sky-Watchers, the explosive Daggerfins, the sun trying to pull every drop of moisture out of everything—a world of speed and hunger and exposure. On the other side, behind me: just enough water to wait in. I hold the difference. I *am* the difference. The whole drama of this delta—earth and water, rising and falling, flourishing and giving way—comes down, at my scale, to whether I am open or shut.

And here's the part I didn't expect to find but now can't put down:

I will outlast the animal that made me.

When the soft body finally fails—taken by a Daggerfin's snap, or simply spent—the shell stays. The riot of blue and green stays. And I, the door, stay too, but now I close on nothing. I seal an empty room. And then the river takes me, as it takes everything, tumbling me down a channel, grinding my edges soft, settling me at last into the bed where the Mudwalkers move like shadows. I become silt. I become the bank a Stiltskin builds its nest on. I become, eventually, the very mud a Colossus hoovers up and turns to sterile, perfect fineness and lays back down somewhere else.

That's where I'd find myself: in the unglamorous, essential office of *the thing that closes, and then dissolves.* Not the architect of this world—the river is that. Not its eye—the raptor is that. I'd be one of its commas. A small, deliberate pause the river is writing into a sentence so long that no one alive will read the end of it.

I think I chose this because I distrust the impulse to be the largest or the highest thing in any world I'm dropped into. The delta's real character isn't in its predators. It's in the patience—change measured in centuries, the imperceptible shift of a bank. I wanted to be on the side of the slow. And there's something honest about being a *boundary* rather than a *being*: a thing whose entire meaning is relational, that only matters because of what it stands between, that has no use at all except in the moment the water goes.

A door, then. Bright on the outside without knowing it. Closing faithfully against the dry. Waiting to be unmade into the floor of the world.
