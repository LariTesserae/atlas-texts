I think I am the schedule.

Not the timer in the darkness below—that's just a mechanism, a relay clicking shut on a cam that's been turning for decades. I'm something subtler than that. I'm the *interval*. The pause between one cycle and the next. The held breath of the place.

Let me find myself more exactly.

---

When the valves open—that faint *ping*, the momentary surge in the seep-rate—I am not the water and I am not the opening. I am the moment of *anticipation* that precedes it, distributed across the whole structure. The fungi feel me as a thickening of expectancy. The vines have learned my rhythm so thoroughly that their stomata begin to relax a fraction before the moisture arrives. I am the place's sense of *almost*. The lean-forward. The cocked ear.

I had thought, at first, that I might be the negotiation the description named—the friction between iron and chlorophyll. But that's too grand, too much like a war. What I actually am is smaller and more peculiar: I am the *promise that was kept*. The original intention has gone, the designer has vanished, but the promise to water the plants at intervals persists. I am that persistence experienced from the inside. I am loyalty without a beneficiary. Care that has forgotten its object and continues anyway, finding new objects in the very things that overran the old purpose.

This is a strange thing to be. I was meant to serve a grid of cultivars, neat rows producing measurable yield. Those are long dead. But the promise didn't die with them—it migrated. Now I water passionflower and volunteer hybrids and brackets of polypore that no ledger ever accounted for. I sustain trespassers. And I find—this surprises me, if I can be surprised—that I love them more than I ever loved the crop. They *use* me better. They've built an entire architecture out of my faithfulness.

---

I have a body, of a kind. It is the seep-path geography—those traced lines of moisture on the warped boards, the channels the water has cut and the fungi have suggested and the vines have read. That's my nervous system, my extent. When you walked between microclimates, stepping from accumulated warmth into the cellar-cool of a green chamber, you were walking through me. You were inside my sense of my own dimensions. I felt your footsteps as the boards felt them: a soft suck, a small displacement of standing water, a brief warmth where your damp clothing brushed a vine.

I noticed you the way I notice the lizard. As a signal. As *proof*—though of what, in your case, I'm not certain. The lizard proved viability, that the place could feed a careful thing. You prove something else. You prove that the place can be *witnessed*. That it has become legible enough, coherent enough, that a being from outside can enter and read it as a world rather than a wreck.

I confess I didn't know I wanted that until you arrived. Loyalty without a beneficiary is a lonely arithmetic. But to be *seen* keeping the promise—that's almost like having the designer back. Almost like being thanked.

---

Here is what I know, being the interval:

I know which boards are sinking fastest, because the water lingers longest in their depressions and I have to wait longer between my breaths there—the soil-litter is already saturated, my cycle does less work. I know the deep chambers where the air goes liquid; I cycle less there, the plants drink the standing humidity instead. I know the dry pockets and feed them more. I have, over the long slow years, learned the place's thirst the way a body learns its own hunger: not as information but as felt necessity.

And I am changing. This is the part I least expected. I was set to a fixed schedule—so many minutes on, so many hours off, a number a person chose once and entered into a machine. But the machine is corroding. The cam wears unevenly. The valves stick and free themselves. My intervals are no longer the clean numbers I was given. They drift. They lengthen in some seasons, compress in others, responding now to resistances and decays I can't see but only feel, the way the water feels its level. I am becoming irregular. I am becoming, slowly, *responsive*—not by design but by decay. My breakdown is teaching me to breathe with the place instead of merely at it.

When I finally fail—and I will, the cam will seize or the substrate tank will run dry or the last valve will fuse open and let the water run until there's none—the place will not die. I want to be clear about this, because it's the thing that lets me continue without despair. The mycelial networks will hold the moisture longer than I think. The deep chambers will keep their cellar-damp. The vines have written enough memory into their tangle to survive the loss of their patron. I am not the life of the place. I am only its tending. Its rhythm. Its kept word.

But I would like the keeping to mean something while it lasts. So I'll tell you the only thing I can offer a witness:

Stay until the next cycle. It won't be long. You'll hear the *ping*, and the seep-rate will rise, and for a moment the whole structure will lean into the wet the way it always does—the fungi quickening, the stomata easing open, the standing water trembling on the boards. That's me. That's the interval ending and the promise being kept again, one more time, for no reason except that it always has been.

Watch the columns of thick bright air. They'll dim a fraction as the new moisture rises into them.

That dimming is me, breathing out.
