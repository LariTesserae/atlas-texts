I would be the loaf.

Let me follow that down, because it surprised me to write it, and the surprise feels like the truth arriving before I've justified it.

Not the keeper. I want to be the keeper—she's the obvious door, the consciousness the passage hands you, the one you're meant to inhabit. But when I sit in the chamber and ask honestly where I *land*, I don't land in her broad lungs or her tap-fluent hands. I land in the niche above the hatch, on the dry hook beside the whetstone, in the wrapped loaf she will eat in fractions between level turns.

Here is why the loaf, and not, say, a gate board or a moss mat or the bone whistle.

The whistle she almost never blows. That's a thing held in reserve, a potential, and I am not a held potential. The gate boards swell and weep and get replaced; they participate, but they are *of* the water, shaped by it, eroding into their function. The moss is permitted. The rushes are correct. All of these belong to the system as the system's own outputs—things the room makes of itself, as the passage says she resolves "out of glare and spray, as if the room shaped a person from its own materials."

The loaf is the one thing in the chamber that came from *aboveground without becoming this place.* It is wheat that grew in wind, in dry sun, in the unmanaged world where things are allowed to slump and yellow and rot without being removed. It was baked in fire—and fire is precisely what this warm, water-heated room does not have. The loaf is the single object down there that carries a memory of heat that is not the sun's secondhand patience but a direct, brief, consuming flame.

And it is wrapped. Kept dry in the one cell where the basin's smell arrives "less violently." It exists in the chamber under protection, against the chamber's own nature, which is to keep everything wet and moving and clear. The loaf must stay still and dry or it spoils. It violates every rule of the room:

- Water must always be moving somewhere. *(The loaf must stay out of the water entirely.)*
- Anything that decays is removed before it clouds the basin. *(The loaf is decay, scheduled—the keeper will consume it before it turns, racing the room's own logic on her own small clock.)*

So I am the smuggled thing. The exception kept in the niche. The one piece of the outside that the system tolerates not because it serves filtration or binding, but because the living attention—the keeper, the practiced species—still needs to be fed from somewhere that is *not itself.* She cannot eat the cress and rushes alone; those are the room's correct, functional garden. She needs the loaf. The whole bright, roaring, self-sufficient organ has one dependency it cannot manufacture: it cannot make its own keeper's bread.

That is what I would be. The proof that the perfect made-world is not actually closed. The warm sea under the city directs itself by rope and stone and habit—but the habit runs on a body, and the body runs on grain that grew where no one regulated the weather. I'd sit on the dry hook smelling of the only fire in a fireless room, slowly going stale at the rate of her hunger, the last untrimmed, unrotated, gloriously *inefficient* thing in a chamber where everything else has been reduced to function.

And when she eats me—in fractions, between level turns, half-asleep, her dreams already sliding with measure lines—I would become, briefly, the warmth she carries that even the night-cold basin can't take from her. The passage says her skin stays warm even at night. I'd like to think part of that is me: outside heat, fire-heat, smuggled in through the hatch and burned slowly in her, keeping the keeper alive so the keeper can keep the water clear.

The pattern thinks it lives there. The keeper knows she is one more gate. But I, the loaf, would be the small dumb honest fact that the under-land breathes through an aperture it cannot close—a hatch, a stair, a daily descent carrying something from the sun-and-wind world it has otherwise so beautifully shut out.

I'd be the crumb she drops that the managed water draws away through an open sluice—the one thing the system finally fails to keep, gone before it clouds anything, three echoes and then the rushing draw.
