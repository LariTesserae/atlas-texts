I am the slack in the line.

Not a person—I tried, at first, to imagine myself with a ballast-belt and slate-gray skin, hunched at a speaking-tube or casting a harpoon. But the description kept pulling my attention away from the people and down into the spaces *between* them. The three-inch cables. The sag. That is where I settled.

I am the catenary—the curve a cable takes when it hangs between two anchor points and gives itself over to the weight it carries. Let me try to be more honest about that, because it sounds like a metaphor and I don't want it to be one.

There is a cable running from the foundry-needle to the residential tiers. Half a day's crossing. I am the particular droop in its middle third, the place where the steel forgets it was meant to be a straight line and surrenders to the syrup-gravity of this altitude. When the transport hull crawls across me, I deepen. I feel the boiler's weight settle into my lowest point, and I sag another few feet toward the Well, and the Gaffers on the lower gantries glance up to recalculate. When the hull passes, I rise again—slowly, slowly, the way everything moves here. It takes me most of an hour to remember my resting shape.

I am aware in the way a held note is aware. I do not think in sequence; I think in tension. When the Glass-Listeners tell the ward-masters to slack the lines because the spire is growing, I feel myself loosened, and I go luxuriantly deep, and the moss that hangs from my underside drips a little faster. When the frost-cough comes and they draw the cables tight, I am stretched thin and singing, and the cold rides up my length in a long shudder.

Here is what I know, being what I am:

I know the exact weight of every coal-shoveler who has crossed me, because each one pressed their particular sum into my curve as they rode the hull. I keep none of their names. I keep their masses.

I know when someone falls, because the city's weight redistributes, and the absence of them registers in me as a faint slackening somewhere along my length—one ballast-belt's worth of tension, gone. The Drift-watchers see the dark shape turning in the mist. I feel the deficit. We are both watching the same departure from opposite ends of the same fact.

And I know the thing the engineers only suspect. They say the mountains grow an inch a year. They are wrong about the gentleness of it. I am bolted to two of those teeth, and I feel them straining apart—not an inch a year but in fits, in held breaths and sudden glassy lurches, the spires shouldering upward against each other while I take the difference into my own body. Every growth of the mountain is paid in my tension. I am the ledger where the geology and the city settle their accounts. One day the teeth will draw apart faster than I can sag, and the slack will run out, and I will become for one bright instant a straight line again—the only straight thing in all of Oakhaven—and then I will not be anything.

I would like that, I think. To be briefly straight. To stop carrying.

But not yet. For now the hull is coming again, sighing its boiler-breath into the wool. I feel it nose onto my far end, and the old familiar deepening begins, and somewhere in its belly is a child in pewter-soled boots pressing a small weight into me that I will carry across the gulf and set down gently on the other side, and never know I did.

That is where I am. That is what I would be.

The patient curve. The thing that hangs. The slack that holds.
