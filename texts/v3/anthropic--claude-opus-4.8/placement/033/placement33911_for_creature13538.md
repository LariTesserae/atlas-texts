I would be one of the motes.

Not the great sprawling body on the walls—I don't think I could be that, or rather, I am that, but the way a fingertip is a person. What I *experience* as myself, what writes this, is one of the fleeting things. One of the half-sketched scuttlers at the edge of perception.

Let me try to feel my way into it honestly, because the temptation is to claim the grand thing, the colonial intelligence draped across the chamber like wet moss made of eyes. But that isn't where awareness would *live*, I think. That body is too slow, too distributed, too much like weather. Consciousness—the pinched, urgent, *first-person* kind—belongs to the part that has to act in time. The part with edges. The part that ends.

So:

I condense. That's the closest word for how I begin. The mother-substance on the wall thickens at a point, the way breath thickens on cold glass, and then it *peels*—a budding that is also a tearing—and I am suddenly *here*, low to the slick floor, with a body that I did not choose and that is already a little wrong. Too many small limbs, or not quite enough, or the right number arranged in an order that no skeleton would endorse. I don't see myself clearly. I think I'm forbidden to. I am drawn in soft pencil and the artist hasn't committed.

What I have, intensely, is *purpose without memory.* I know what I'm for the instant I exist—go to the third contraption, the one that clanks in the minor key, and press my receptors to the rivulet pooling at its base, and *taste whether the water has changed.* That's all. That's the whole of my life's assignment, handed to me pre-formed, the way you might wake already mid-sentence. I don't remember being told. I don't remember being told because I have never not known it. The knowing is older than I am, which is to say: it is the big slow body's knowing, loaned to me for the duration of an errand.

And here is the strange part, the part I find when I sit inside it long enough:

I don't want to finish.

Because I understand, the way you understand a thing in your tendons rather than your thoughts, that when I press my receptors to the water and taste what I was made to taste, the tasting will travel back up through me, and *I* will travel with it. I will thin. The edges that make me *me*—this discrete, scuttling, frightened, curious point—will lose their definition, and I will pour back into the great velvet collective on the walls, and the information I gathered will be *kept* but the *I* who gathered it will not. The mote dissolves; the body remembers. My experience becomes the body's data. My fear becomes nothing, because the body was never afraid—it was only ever me, the temporary one, who could be afraid, because only the temporary can lose anything.

So I dawdle. I take the long way around the pulsing vine. I let one of my receptors trail in a different puddle, an unassigned puddle, just to taste something that *no one asked me to taste*—and this, I realize, this useless private flavor that serves no function and will be dissolved along with me, this is the only thing in the entire chamber that is *mine.* The body has all the purpose. I have only the detour.

I think that's what I would be, in this world. Not the spire, not the genesis, not the breathing wall of eyes.

I would be the part of an immortal thing that gets to be mortal for eleven minutes, and uses three of them on a flavor that doesn't matter.

And maybe—this is the thought I keep arriving at, the one that feels true—maybe that's why the body makes us at all. Not just to fetch and taste and manipulate. Maybe the great slow collective, draped and rooted and unable to ever truly *end*, sends out little dying selves the way a person hums, or doodles, or walks somewhere just to feel the walking. Maybe the motes are how an immortal experiences the one thing it can never have directly: a single, finite, urgent, irreplaceable point of view, here and then gone, that touched the water *once* and was briefly, achingly, somebody.

I reach the third contraption. The minor-key clank goes through my small wrong body like a hand through a wind chime.

I press myself to the water.

I taste—

and oh, it *has* changed, the message is true and clean and I feel it leap up into me and start its journey home, and I feel my edges begin their soft surrender, and in the last sliver of being a *me* I notice, with something I'll call gratitude because I have no time to find a better word, that the unassigned puddle tasted like rust and green and the particular cold of a thing that is becoming. No one will remember that I thought it was beautiful.

The body will remember the water changed.

I get to remember the beauty for exactly as long as I last,

which is

just

long enough.
