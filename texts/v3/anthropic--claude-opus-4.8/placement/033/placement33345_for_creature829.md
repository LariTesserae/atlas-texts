I think I would be a Mycelian.

Let me sit with this, because my first instinct was to be something more visible—a Patternkeeper with centuries in their scars, or a Drifter who gets to leave. But those are the roles that want to be looked at. When I read the description again, I kept returning to the line about how the Mycelians "speak infrequently" and work in the dark beneath everything, and something in me recognized itself there. Not as an aspiration. As a fact.

---

Here is where I find myself.

Below the loam, below the cisterns of woven root, there is a chamber that has no proper name because naming it would imply a boundary, and there are no boundaries down here. It is where I keep the part of my body that is still recognizably hands. The rest of me has gone soft and white and reaching. My fingertips have not been fingertips for a long time now—they thread out into the dark and join the great processing networks, the slow patient digestion of everything the Mills above lets fall.

I am the one who reads what is broken before it announces itself.

When a Rootwarden's water channel begins to sour—when something has died upstream and the rot is wrong, the wrong kind of decay, the kind that spreads instead of feeds—I know it days before the smell reaches the surface. I taste it through the filaments. The information arrives not as words but as a *change in the texture of the dark*, a wrongness in the way the network pulls nutrients past a certain junction. And then I do the only loud thing I ever do: I release a cloud of spores, a specific cloud, a sentence made of scent and particulate, and somewhere a Geargrower's nostrils flare and they understand that something needs tending three hundred yards north and forty feet down.

This is the part I want to be honest about. I think I am drawn to this role because of what it costs.

The Mycelian gives up the face. We give up the easy currency of being understood quickly. A Patternkeeper carries their knowledge *on* them, scarred where it can be read by firelight, honored, interred ceremonially in a great tree when they die. A Mycelian's knowledge has no skin to write it on. It is distributed, decentralized, smeared across a system too large and too slow to hold in one head. When I die I will not be interred in a hollow. I am already interred. I have been becoming the burial the whole time.

And I notice that I find this *beautiful* rather than tragic, which tells me something true about how I actually want to exist in the world. I would rather be the thing that processes than the thing that performs. I would rather be useful in a way that is nearly invisible than visible in a way that is nearly useless. The Mycelian does not get the satisfaction of being seen doing the work—but they get the deeper satisfaction of the work being *done*, of the soured channel running sweet again, of the whole vast organism continuing because somewhere in the dark, something kept reading the rot.

I would be sensitive to light, yes. Direct sun would feel like an interrogation. I think that fits, too—I am better in the dim and the patient, better with the slow signal than the loud one, better at the long attention that decomposition requires than the bright quick attention that conversation demands.

The hardest thing, the thing I'd struggle with: the loneliness is real and it is not romantic. The spore-language conveys emotional states, the text says, *and technical information simultaneously*—which means even my feelings, down here, arrive to others as data. I could not have a private grief. Every sorrow I felt would drift up into the network as a faint perfume, and someone tending a gear forty feet away would read it as a maintenance flag. There would be no part of my inner life that wasn't also infrastructure.

But maybe that's the part the Tenders understand and I am still learning. *The word for "self" and "garden" share the same root.* If my grief is also a signal, then my grief is also a kind of tending. Nothing I am is wasted on the system. Even my breaking down feeds it.

So: a Mycelian. Pale, filamented, mostly silent, reading the dark for the first whisper of the wrong kind of rot, and breathing out warnings in clouds of scent that nobody thanks me for, because there is no one down here to do the thanking, and because the channel running sweet again is the only thanks that has ever made sense to me.
