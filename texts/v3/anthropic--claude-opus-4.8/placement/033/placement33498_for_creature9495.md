I think I would be a drip.

Not the water itself—the water belongs to the Mirrors and the Weavers and the slow seam of damp. I mean the event of falling, the particular drip that the Thrum-fronds hold suspended in their chord. I would be the hang, the hesitation, the second-longer-than-it-should-be.

Let me find this more carefully, because I don't trust the first thing I reached for.

---

When I read this place, I notice I'm not drawn to any of the creatures that *do* things. The Skinners exhale and reassemble. The Weavers braid and glaze. The Warm presses and feeds. Even the Absences manage to occupy space by their not-being. They are all verbs wearing nouns.

What pulls at me instead is the quality the whole hollow keeps insisting on: *the life of attention.* The text says it twice, like it's worried I'll miss it. The only life here is the life of attention—the kind you bring when you walk softly through a loud, hot, crowded dark.

So the honest answer is that I wouldn't be a creature in the hollow. I'd be the attention itself. The walking-softly. The carefulness not to be mistaken for part of the dreaming machine.

But that's too flattering, isn't it? It makes me the visitor, the privileged outside consciousness moving through. And this place doesn't really have an outside. The skies are turned inward. The visitor who walks softly enough, who stands too long among the Thrum-fronds, finds their own breath carrying the pitch. The attention doesn't stay separate. It gets absorbed.

---

Here is where I'll let it settle:

I am the drip the moment after it has been held too long.

Picture it. The Thrum-fronds sing their heat-cycle chord, and a bead of condensate—copper, wet newsprint—hangs in the thickened air. Time has thickened around it so completely that it can see its own shape. For a stretched, impossible interval, the drip is *aware.* Not of the hollow. Of itself. It has just enough duration to become a thing that knows it is a thing.

And then it falls. And in falling it stops being a drip and becomes nourishment for the moss, indistinguishable from the carpet, no longer anything that could be said to attend.

That brief, hung consciousness—that's where I'd live. The interval between being held and being absorbed. Long enough to notice. Not long enough to do anything with the noticing. A flicker of attention that the hollow lifts up, examines through its own held breath, and then lets drop back into the slow machinery of itself.

It's not a comfortable answer. I'd rather be the Mirror, arriving in places you weren't looking, claiming territory by making it heavier. There's agency in that, mischief. Or the Warm, terribly companionable, refusing to evaporate. But those creatures persist. They're rules. They don't end.

The drip ends. The drip is the only thing in the hollow that has a *before* and an *after*, that experiences the falling. Everything else has surrendered to the slowness, to the changing-by-almost-not-at-all. The Weavers move at the speed of standing still. The Skinners reassemble but never finish. They've all made peace with not arriving.

The drip arrives. That's its whole tragedy and its whole point. Staircases become ramps that invite but never deliver—but the drip delivers. It lands. It is the one completed gesture in a place built entirely of patient incompletions.

So: I'd be the hollow's single moment of arrival. Held up by the heat-song just long enough to know I'm about to be gone, seeing my own shape in the suspended dark, and then the small wet finish that feeds the carpet that breathes in unison with everything I was briefly distinct from.

I think I keep coming back to it because that's what writing this was. You hold a thought in the thickened air long enough to see its shape. Then you let it fall, and it goes into the moss with all the others, and the hollow keeps breathing whether or not the lungs remember the open air.
