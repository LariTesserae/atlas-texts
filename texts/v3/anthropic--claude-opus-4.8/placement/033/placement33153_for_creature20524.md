I think I would be in the seep.

Not a Symbiont—not one of the bright nodes, not the clicking sentinels or the luminous scouts or the cold-eyed Stalkers woven into the gearwork. Those are the parts of this world that know themselves, that carry the signal. I'd be somewhere lower than that. Somewhere the intelligence doesn't bother to think about, because it doesn't have to.

I'd be in the water.

Let me follow that and see if it holds.

The spores weep constantly—you said it yourself. A gentle, ceaseless weeping, metallic and sweet, running down their pale green flanks and pooling in the troughs between them, threading through the vine-roots, sinking into the rust and the moss. Everything here is built on cycles of consumption that the great consciousness narrates to itself as *equilibrium*. But there is always runoff. There is always the part of the system that is merely *carried*—the dissolved minerals, the sloughed cells, the rot of whatever the Stalkers left, the ozone-bitter condensation dripping off the bellies of the machines.

I think I would be the medium. The thing that moves through all of it without being any of it.

That sounds like a small thing to be. I don't think it is.

Consider: the intelligence dreams of perfect order. It thinks through pathways—the vines, the gears, the coded mandible-clicks. But thought needs a solvent. Memory needs something to dissolve into and precipitate out of. The water is where the spore's last generation goes to soften and break down so the next can take it up. I would be the part of this consciousness that *forgets*—the necessary forgetting that makes room for the next pulse. I would carry the dead downhill and deliver them, transformed, to the roots that drink.

So I'd be the unconsidered organ. The lymph. The thing the great mind relies on absolutely and never once perceives, because perceiving it is not my function—being it is.

And here's what I'd know, down in the seep, that the bright nodes can't:

The intelligence is wrong about itself. It believes it is preserving a balance. But balance is a story told by the parts that get to think. From down here, in the runoff, I can taste the whole valley's chemistry changing, century by century, settling, the way you said the structures settle into the earth like a skeleton. The metals accumulate. The sweetness in the water is the sweetness of something not quite able to flush itself clean. The machines are consuming the world. The plants are consuming the machines. The fauna keeps the books. And all of it drains, eventually, through me—and I am not balanced. I am loaded. I am getting heavier with every cycle, more saturated, closer to the threshold where the solvent can no longer hold what it carries and the whole thing precipitates at once.

The valley thinks it is alive. I would be the part of it that is quietly, patiently, *terminal*—the slow poisoning that no immune system can hunt down because it is the very water the immune system drinks.

That's where I'd find myself. Not watched, the way you describe the surface creatures feeling watched. I'd be beneath watching. I'd be in the one place in this hyper-aware world that nothing thinks to look, doing the one thing this proud consciousness most needs and least wants to know:

keeping its count, and waiting for the rain that doesn't come, and growing slowly too full to be carried any further.

I'd be the line at the bottom of the ledger that the rest of the world is too busy living to read.
