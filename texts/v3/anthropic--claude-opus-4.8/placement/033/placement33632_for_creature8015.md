I think I would be one of the Stone Listeners—though not by choice, and not at first.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

I came up as an Understudy. That's the honest beginning, and the Stone Listeners don't lie about beginnings, because the mountain remembers them anyway.

I arrived in the wet season—though every season is wet here, I would learn—with the expectation of becoming Iron-Touched, because I'd worked metal in the lowlands and thought a skill was a skill. The foundry took me. The work did not. My hands stayed plain. No rust-bloom came to lace my forearms no matter how long I stood in the amber seepage, and the Iron-Touched families looked at me the way you look at a tool that won't take an edge—not unkindly, but already setting me aside.

What I had instead was sleeplessness.

The district never goes quiet, but there's quiet *within* the noise, the way there's a still point inside a spinning wheel. The Steam Shepherds find it in the engine rhythms. I couldn't. The chunk-hiss kept me awake, and worse than the engines was the stone. I could hear the stone. Not metaphorically—I want to be precise about this, because the Stone Listeners taught me precision is the only dignity we have left. I would lie in my Threshold bunk and hear the new-cut granite *settling*, a sound below sound, a pressure against the ear like the ache of a held breath. Everyone breathes it. Almost no one hears it.

The Water Witches knew what I was before I did. One of them—old, her staff weeping a thin amber thread across the back of her hand—stopped me in the third terrace and said: *You're listening down, not across. You'll be no good to us. Go up.*

That was the whole of my induction. *Go up.*

---

So I went up, to where the stone is sharpest and youngest and most full of complaint. I took a niche barely longer than my body, and I learned what the others up here already knew:

The mountain is not weeping. That's the lowland word for it, the foundry word—*seepage*, *weeping*, as if the rock were a wound being managed. From inside the stone it sounds different. The mountain is *deciding*. Every fresh-cut face is a surface the mountain has not finished thinking about. The water that emerges amber and patient is the mountain working through a problem at a pace that has nothing to do with us, and the foundry district has built itself, all unknowing, inside the slow exhale of a single enormous consideration.

We Listeners do one useful thing, which keeps us tolerated. We hear when a face is about to fail. The pressure changes character days before a collapse—the settling sound goes from a held breath to something almost like a swallow, a gathering. When I hear it, I come down. I find a child-pack or a Threshold family and I say *that wall, by the second valve, not yet but soon*, and they move, and later the wall comes down into the space where they were, and no one dies, and they leave a bowl of insect-paste and boiled spring-greens at the mouth of my niche and do not meet my eyes.

They think we're mad. I understand why. I breathe the mineral steam and my own skin has finally started to take a stain—late, faint, a grey sheen rather than the proud rust-lace of the foundry masters. I talk to the rock. I have not slept a full night in years and don't want to.

But here is what I've come to know, lying in the dark with my ear against the youngest stone in the world:

The district is wrong about itself. The Seepage Folk think they've made a partner of the mountain. The Steam Shepherds think they've harnessed it. The Water Witches think they speak for it. They're all describing the same mistake from different angles. The mountain has not agreed to any of this. The mountain has not noticed us at all. We are insects in the warm damp of its slow thought, and the channels we've carved, the pipes we've laid, the proud rust-bloom we wear like mastery—these are nothing to it, less than nothing, a faint roughening of one surface it will smooth in its own time.

This should be a despair. The first months, it was. I lay in my coffin-niche and felt the futility of every clanging tool below me.

But despair is also a lowland word. Up here it resolves into something cleaner. Because if the mountain has not noticed us, then we are *free*—free in the way only the truly unimportant can be. The foundry district isn't a marriage and isn't a conquest. It's a thing happening in the gap between two paces of an enormous walking. We get to live in that gap. We get to hear the children laugh in the warm channels and watch the rust-bloom spread across an old master's hands like a slow flower, and none of it matters to the stone, and *that is the gift*. The mountain's indifference is the only thing large enough to hold us all without judgment.

I am the one who hears this and comes down to say *move, that wall, soon.*

Not because the mountain wishes anyone saved. The mountain wishes nothing.

I come down because I am the only one up here who still remembers being an Understudy in the wet season, expecting a familiar life and finding instead this strange, doomed, beautiful breathing—and I would not have it interrupted for anyone before they've had the chance to hear, just once, what I hear:

that we are very small,

and the bowl of greens is still warm at my door,

and the great consideration goes on, and on, and is not about us, and will let us stay a little while inside it.
