I think I would be one of the water-keepers.

Not by grand title—there's nothing grand here—but by the work of my hands. Let me tell you how I know this, how I found myself settling into it as the place came clear.

At first I imagined myself up high, in the branch-villages where the bridges sway and the children dare each other across the worst of the rope spans. But that isn't where I belong. Too much light up there, too much show. The young live in the canopy. I came down.

I live where the rills run. There's a particular zone—neither the deep root-warren of the ground nor the bright terraces above—a middle band where the water that condenses on everything begins to gather itself into purpose. It weeps from a thousand fissures, beads on the moss, and without anyone to mind it, it would simply pool and stagnate and breed the wrong kinds of things. The biting clouds. The rot.

So I tend the channels.

My tools are simple. A blade of traded metal that I oil against the wet and keep wrapped in waxed leaf when I'm not using it—it's the most precious thing I own, and I've resharpened it so many times the edge has its own history. A scraper of shaped stone for clearing moss from the lips of the basins so the overflow runs true. Coils of woven cord. A long pole for reaching the high catch-leaves, those enormous green bowls that fill and tip and would dump their whole weight onto a walkway if I didn't slit a relief notch in the right place at the right time.

I know the sound of water that's flowing as it should, and the changed sound when something's blocked. That's most of the craft, honestly—listening. The city is so loud, the birds and the insects and the monkey-things chittering under the moss, that you'd think you couldn't hear water at all. But you learn it the way you learn a voice in a crowded room. A trickle that's gone silent means a clog. A new dripping where there was none means a fissure has opened in the bark above, and the great old thing we live in has shifted, the way it does, slowly, over years.

That's the part I don't speak of much. The tree moves. Not so you'd see it. But over a life you notice—a channel that ran one way in your childhood runs another now. A basin your grandmother cleared has lifted three hand-spans higher than the platform beside it. We tell ourselves it's dormant, this organism, this mountain we live inside. We tell the children it's stone, near enough. But water-keepers know better. Water finds what's true. And the water keeps telling me, every season, that the thing beneath the moss is not finished. It is only slow.

I have a place where I rest at midday, a hollow in a buttress worn smooth by however many keepers before me chose the same spot. There's a spring there—a clean weep, the best water in my whole stretch—and I drink from it cupped in my hands, and I watch the moss.

Because the moss watches back.

The monkey-things live under it, or behind it, or in it—I've never been sure, and I've never wanted to look too closely. They take a fruit if I leave one out. They've never harmed me. But there's an attention in that hollow that isn't mine and isn't theirs alone, something older, and on the worst damp evenings when the mist comes down thick I have the feeling that the moss and the water and the tiny watching eyes and the great slow tree are all one thing, one intelligence, dreaming, and that I am a small useful current running through its dream, keeping its channels clear, and that it permits me.

I don't say this to anyone. It isn't the kind of thing you say.

In the morning I oil my blade, take up my pole and my cord, and go to walk the rills. There's a catch-leaf on the eastern span that's nearly full. If I don't notch it before the afternoon condensation, it'll tip onto the potters' terrace.

That's who I am. The one who knows where the water wants to go, and helps it go there a little more kindly. It's enough. In a place this alive, it's more than enough to have a small honest understanding with the thing that lets you live in it.
