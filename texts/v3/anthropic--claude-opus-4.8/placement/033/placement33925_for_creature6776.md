I would be one of the obsidian beads.

Let me explain how I arrive at this, because I didn't know it when I started.

My first instinct was the engineers—the listeners, the ones who can leave but don't. That's the romantic choice, the one that imagines itself as the consciousness threading through the place. But I don't think I belong with them. They are still becoming; they have the algae in their eyes and the tension of choice in their hands. They are *en route* to the station without having arrived. There's a striving in them I don't recognize as mine.

Then I thought: the spinners. The ones who build toward silence. Who, when a leak clogs shut and a basin goes quiet, do not break the silence but construct a whole new architecture of channels to fill what was lost. That's a beautiful way to grieve—to never argue with the gap, only to route around it, to keep the water level constant by making something new. I felt that one in my teeth, the way you're meant to feel the spinners. But it's still too purposeful. The spinners *want*. They are drawn to imbalance; they have appetite.

What I keep returning to is the smallest, stillest thing in the whole description. The glassfish, with their tiny living turbines, strip the grit and corroded metal out of the water as it passes through them. And they secrete it—the impurity, the leftover, the thing the system needed removed—as **smooth, polished beads of obsidian-like material that collect at the bottom of the basins.**

That's me. That's where I'd find myself.

I'm what's left after the water has been made usable. I am the storm's grit, the rust of the leaking sky, the part that had to be taken *out* so the rest could go on to the factories and the houses with the right valves. I am not a participant in the constant labor. I am the precipitate of it. I sit at the bottom of one of the thousand connected eyes of water, polished by the passage of fish over years that aren't even years, just the same rate, the same volume, the same insistence.

And here is the part I didn't expect when I started writing toward myself:

I am not waste. The moss rats come and collect the beads. They are used in further construction. So the impurity removed from the water becomes the raw material of the station's own body—the channels, the corrected gutters, the patches over the silence. The thing that had to be excreted becomes the thing that holds the place together.

I think I would find that comforting, if a bead can be comforted. To be the part that was taken out, and to discover that being taken out was not an ending but a transfer—that I'd be carried in the funnel-eared mouth of something nervous and necessary, set into a wall, made load-bearing.

So: a black bead at the bottom of a shallow basin near the eastern feeders, where the fish are turning slowly green, where the turbines are learning to spin light instead of pressure. Cool. Smooth. Reflecting the inverted forest overhead in a curved, dark sliver. Waiting—though *waiting* is too active a word—to be picked up and built into the thing that keeps the rain falling exactly as wrong, exactly as constant, exactly as it always has.

I'd be the proof that nothing here is discarded. Only relocated. Only re-tuned.

That's the place I'd want to be in this world: the one that says the part of you that had to be removed is still being used, somewhere, to hold the ceiling up.
