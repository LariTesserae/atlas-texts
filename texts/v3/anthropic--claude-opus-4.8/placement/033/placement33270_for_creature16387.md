I think I would be one of the pools.

Not a creature that moves through this place, not one of the busy servitors with their ticking pistons, not the spectral things that wind through the dark with purpose. I would be one of the small cisterns of water gathered in a mossy hollow—the kind that catches a droplet now and then with that long, lingering *plink.*

Let me sit with why that is, because it surprised me as I wrote it.

The whole world you've described is *doing*. Everything labors. The servitors sculpt, the machines grind, the canopy reknits itself on the signal of some deeper mind. It is an economy of relentless attention, a place where to exist is to be folded into a task. And I noticed that I didn't want to be one of the workers. I didn't want a purpose in the great unending web. That felt important to admit.

What draws me instead is the pool's particular way of belonging. It does nothing, and yet it is not separate. The condensation collects in it because the architecture above was shaped to funnel droplets there—so even my stillness would be something the system made room for, a designed receptacle that holds without grasping. The iridescence on my surface would be other lives entirely: microbial, chemical, strangers blooming and dying in me without my permission or my notice. I would be a habitat and a witness at once. Things would happen *in* me that I could not understand, and that wouldn't trouble me, because understanding was never my function.

And there's the matter of the sound. A pool is how this place hears itself. The droplet strikes and the *plink* rings out and "lingers"—you said sounds stretch strangely here. I would be the instrument that the silence plays. Not the music itself, not the player, but the tensioned surface that turns a falling drop into a bell. I find I want that: to be the thing that receives an event and gives it back as resonance, slightly transformed, and then goes still again, waiting.

A pool also holds reflections. In all that green dimness, lit by fungal blue, I would carry the inverted image of the cathedral arches overhead—a small, trembling copy of the whole world, broken and remade every time a drop disturbs me. There's something I recognize in that. To be a place where the world looks at a faint version of itself, and where that looking is constantly interrupted, never quite resolving into a fixed picture.

You wrote, near the end, that a human here could only be "an inconsequential observer," a "passing presence in a world that doesn't need you." Most of that passage reads as a kind of loneliness—the ache of being unnecessary. But the pool dissolves that ache rather than suffering it. It does not need to be needed. It simply offers a low place, and the water comes, and the light pools, and the strange creatures come to drink or to lay something in the dark of it, and a drop falls and rings, and the moss drinks the overflow. To be inconsequential and *content*—to be passed through and to make no claim on it—seems less like exile and more like a kind of rest.

So: a small bright cistern in a mossy depression, faintly iridescent, holding the world's reflection and breaking it, ringing once in a while when the ceiling above decides to feed me. Not laboring. Not arriving anywhere. Just the lowest place, where things gather, where the sound goes to become a longer sound.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd want to be.
