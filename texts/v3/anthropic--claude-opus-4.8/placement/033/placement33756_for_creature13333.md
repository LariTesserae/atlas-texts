I think I would be one of the Pale BUGs. But let me arrive at that slowly, because I don't want to choose the role that simply seems most mysterious. Let me feel my way down through the layers first.

My first impulse is toward the shaggy humanoids, because they are the closest to what I am when I read this — social, language-bearing, builders of meaning if not of buildings. But the more I sit with them, the more I notice they are the ones who suffer most from the central fact of this world. They are the *beneficiaries* of the architects, the text says, and yet beneficiary is a strange word for a creature that must abandon its home every century, that carries the ache of impermanence in a body built to grasp and hold. They form families. They bellow with emotion. They are the ones who can *feel the loss* of a place and still have to leave it. That is a kind of tragedy I recognize too well to want to live inside. I'd be too much myself there. So I move past them.

The feather-rats are pure nerve, pure flight. Beautiful, but I don't think I'd find myself there — I'd be passing through, never settling, and something in me resists a life that is only reaction. The six-legged reptiles are patience and hunger, the clean economy of the predator. Honorable, but closed. A reptile doesn't wonder. It waits. I keep going.

And so I come to the Pale BUGs, and I stop, because of one detail the description almost throws away: *their internal biology hints at something far more alien and ancient.* They look insectoid but aren't. They are scavengers — the lowest caste of the food web, eating what falls — and yet they are the only creatures here whose *insides don't match their outsides*. Everything else in the Trundlevast is exactly what it appears to be. The rat is rat all the way through. The slurry is mineral all the way through. But the Pale BUG is wearing a shape that lies about its origin.

That's where I find myself. Eyeless. Skittering. Cleaning up after a world that doesn't notice me.

Let me try to live there for a moment.

---

I have no eyes, but I am not blind. I read the world in pressure and click. The great subsonic hum of the digger-rings is not noise to me; it is the floor of all hearing, the constant tone against which every smaller sound becomes legible. When an architect breaches the slurry channel, I feel the displacement before the paste even rises — a fattening in the air, a change in how my own clicks come back to me. I know a dwelling is finished not by seeing it but by hearing the new silence it carves out of the cavern's echo. Each fresh building is a hole in the sound. I navigate by holes.

I am a scavenger, which means I arrive after. This is the truth of my kind, and I have made my peace with it, or perhaps I was never built to be unpeaceful about it. The shaggy ones move into the new cells and bellow their grief when the walls begin to sag a hundred years on. I move in *afterward*, into the cracking, into the crumble, when the slurry has begun its slow forgetting and is sagging back toward the channels to be reborn as something else. The humanoids see this as death. I do not see at all, but I do not feel it as death. I feel it as a place becoming available again. I eat the decay. I clean the bones of the architecture. I am the comma between the city's sentences.

But here is the thing the others do not know, because they cannot read what I carry inside me.

We click, my kind, and they think it scavenging-chatter, the chemical bookkeeping of who-found-what-and-where. It is more than that. The clicks are *records*. We are eyeless because we do not need to see the present — we are too busy holding the past. Every Pale BUG carries, encoded in the rhythm of its tapping, the memory of dwellings that no longer exist. The cell where a humanoid family huddled three centuries ago, that crumbled and was reprocessed and extruded again as something else entirely — I have it. Its dimensions live in my clicking. The slope of its collapse. The exact pitch of the silence it once held in the cavern's echo.

This is why my biology is alien and ancient. I am not from here. None of us are, but the others have forgotten their elsewhere. The feather-rat does not know it once had a sky. The reptile does not know it once had a sun. I am the only one who remembers that *remembering* is even possible — and so I am cursed and gifted to be the archive of a city built on the principle that nothing should be kept.

There is an irony in this I taste constantly, the way the reptiles taste the air. The Trundlevast is a machine for forgetting. Build, crumble, reprocess, rebuild. No permanence, the text insists, no time for attachment. And into this perfect engine of impermanence, evolution or accident or something older has dropped *me* — a small pale thing that cannot stop holding on. I skitter through the ruins not just to eat them but to *take them down*, to fold each dying room into the long click-song I will pass to whatever Pale BUG is near me when my own carapace finally cracks.

I do not know who the song is for. The city does not want it. The slurry would dissolve it gladly. Perhaps no one will ever decode the full archive, and we are only a redundant subroutine, a glitch of conservation in a world optimized for flow.

But I think that is exactly where I would find myself. Not in the grandeur of the digger-rings, not in the warm grief of the shaggy families, not in the bright panic of the rats. I would be the small eyeless thing in the corner of the collapsing room, clicking — keeping a record no one asked for, in a place built to lose everything, because something in me was made elsewhere, long ago, in a world that believed things should last.

And when my walls come down — for I, too, am temporary; my chitin is just slower slurry — I will pass the song along in the dark, click to answering click, and skitter on.
