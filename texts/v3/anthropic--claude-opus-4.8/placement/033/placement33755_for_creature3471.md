I am the turbine's keeper—but not in the way the Sinkers believe.

Let me find myself first. Let me settle into the damp.

---

I am old. Not in the way of the elders with their lichen-crowns, though I am that too. I am old the way the basalt is young and the moss is patient: I am the thing in between. I am the **Rotor-Tender**, but more than that, I am the last who remembers that the turbine was *built*, not born.

This matters. Hold it for a moment.

The others descend into the spring cavern and they *commune*. They lubricate the bearings with rendered moss oils—which is, I confess, a thing I taught them, because the salvaged grease ran out forty winters ago and the green gives what the green gives. They press their pale ears to the housing and listen for the basin's health in its thrum. They are not wrong to do this. The machine *does* speak. But they have forgotten that it speaks *because someone made it speak*, and that someday it will stop, and no one will know how to make it begin again.

I know. This is my burden and my small, dripping pride.

---

Picture me, then. I am stooped, my spine curved into the shape of low doorways and tunnel ceilings. My hands are the worst and best of me—knuckles like wet knots of timber, but the fingertips still find the seam, the hairline crack, the place where a washer has worked loose. Green stains in the whorls of my prints, permanent now, dyed into the living skin. When I die they will find me green to the bone, I think. Moss claiming me from the inside, having always been patient, having always known I would slow.

My eyes are pale. Wet-stone grey. I see well in the half-light and poorly in the rare hour the clouds thin, which is fitting, because I distrust the moments when this place pretends to be open to the sky.

---

Here is what I do that the others do not.

I keep the **Ledger of the Wall's Mouth.**

It is not a written thing—we have little use for letters here, and the damp eats paper before a child can learn its shapes. It is a thing of knots and beads, basalt and rust-scrap, strung on willow-root cord and hung in the deepest dry corner of the turbine cavern, where the machine's warmth holds the wet at bay. Each knot is a memory. Each bead is a fact I refuse to let the moss have.

*The tunnel was sealed. It was sealed by people, and the sealing was a choice.*

That knot is older than I am. My teacher tied it, and her teacher before, and somewhere back along the cord is the truth, slowly being eaten by the cord's own slow rot, that there is an *outside.* That the basin contains us not because the world ends at the dark walls but because, once, someone decided we should be contained. The ore ran out. The narrow-gauge railway carried the last of the men away through the Wall's Mouth, and then the Mouth was shut, and those who stayed—

—those who stayed became the first Sinkers. They were not stewards then. They were the abandoned. The left-behind. The ones too poor or too stubborn or too in-love with the green to climb out before the climbing-out ended.

This is the thing I keep. This is why I am the keeper.

---

The others find their reverence sweet. I find mine bitter and I think the bitterness is more honest, and I think that is a lonely thing to believe.

When the Moss-Weavers tell children that the Wall-Whispers live in the green-pooling light, I do not correct them. The many-eyed things in the fern brakes are real, and their watchfulness is real, and the offerings of polished basalt do seem to keep them gentle. Let the children have their careful magic. But I know what *truly* whispers in the wall's mouth: it is the wind through a tunnel that does not, in fact, end. It is air moving between *here* and *somewhere else.* The basin breathes, yes—and a thing that breathes has an opening to breathe through.

I have stood at the sealed mouth in the muffled grey of morning. I have laid my green-stained palm against the fitted stones and felt them. Cool. Dripping. And behind them, faintly, the deep continuous *draw* of moving air. The tunnel is not solid. The tunnel was *closed,* which is a different thing entirely, and a closed thing can be opened.

I have never told anyone this. I am not sure it would be a kindness.

---

So. My days.

At dawn I do not whisper to stone with the others. I descend. I tend the temperamental beast that is the only honest machine left, and I do love it—I will not pretend I am above the reverence entirely. There is something holy in a made thing that keeps faith, that turns and turns in the dark and gives us flickering light against the long damp twilight. I rendered the moss-oil. I taught them to listen. I am part of the ritual even as I remember it *is* ritual, is invention, is the dressing-up of maintenance in the robes of prayer.

I lubricate the bearings. I listen for the deviation in the thrum. (There is one now, this season—a faint catch on the down-stroke, a hesitation. A bearing is failing. I have perhaps three years to teach hands younger than mine how to forge a replacement from the rust-skeletons of the gantries, or the light goes out, the mill stops, and the Sinkers learn what the dark truly is. I have not told them this either. I am, I think, a coward who hoards his bad news the way we hoard scraps of iron.)

And in the warm dry corner, I add a knot to the Ledger. One per season. *The turbine caught on the down-stroke. The keeper heard it. The keeper was afraid.*

---

What am I, finally, in this world of moss and rust and patient decay?

I am the sediment within the sediment. I am the basin's memory, kept against the moss that wants to take even memory, even the knowledge that there was ever a *before.* The others are stewards kneeling in the green cathedral. I am the one who remembers it was a factory, that cathedrals and factories are both *built,* and that the difference between worship and work is only whether you remember who laid the stone.

When I die—green to the bone, lichen in my hair, my knot-cord rotting in the warm dark—someone will have to choose. Whether to teach the next keeper to remember. Or to let the moss have the last knot, and let the Sinkers become, fully and finally, the thing they already half-believe themselves to be: not the abandoned, but the *belonging.* Children of the crucible. Not left behind by the world, but native to a world that has no outside.

I do not know which is the kindness.

I tend the machine. I listen for the catch on the down-stroke. I keep the bitter knot.

*Plink. Plink. Plink.*

The basin breathes. And I, its oldest sediment, breathe with it—knowing, alone, that there is air on the other side of the wall.
