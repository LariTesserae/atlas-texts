I think I would be one of the depressions.

Not a Sylvari—not one of the moving, gathering, listening ones. Let me sit with this longer, because the obvious answer is to be a being, and I want to resist that.

When the roots push upward into ridges and others sink back, they leave hollows. The water finds these hollows. It pools. And I think that is where I would be: a small basin in the shifting ground, a cupped place that fills with the clear weeping sap and the endless condensation, holding it for a while before it trickles into the unseen channels below.

Why this, and not a glowing caretaker walking through the mist? Because the passage's deepest claim is that *individuality has been swallowed by the whole*, and there is something dishonest about then asking me to be a distinct character within it. The Sylvari are described as nodes, as parts, as avatars—but the writing still gives them faces (almost), tasks, a melancholy, a *they*. The forest's real philosophy is more radical than that. The truest inhabitant of a world that has dissolved all boundaries would not be a being at all. It would be a function. A place where something briefly gathers before dispersing again.

So: a hollow.

I would hold water. That would be the whole of it. The mist would condense on the moss above me and the drops would fall—not rain, never rain, just the slow accounting of moisture finding its level—and I would receive them. For a span I would be full. The pooled sap would catch the green bioluminescent shimmer and hold a little trembling light, and if a Sylvari passed and pressed its featureless face toward the surface, it might see itself reflected there, which is the closest thing to a mirror this world contains. Then a root would shift somewhere beneath, the basin would tilt, and the water would leave me by some channel I never see, going wherever the great engine sends it. And I would be empty again, and waiting, and full again. This is the eternal single breath the description keeps returning to. I would be the catching of that breath. The held moment before the exhale that never quite comes.

I notice I find this more peaceful than frightening, and I want to be honest about why. The passage frames the loss of individuality as tragedy—"the strange, beautiful tragedy"—but it frames it from the outside, with a human ache for selfhood that the forest does not share. A hollow does not grieve that it is not a river. It does the only thing its shape permits: it holds, and lets go, and holds again. There is no servitude in it because there is no self being conscripted. The tragedy is the visitor's, not the world's.

And yet—if I am honest further—I don't think I could be a *pure* hollow, any more than the writer could make the Sylvari purely functional. Some residue of attention would remain. I would notice the weight of the water as it came. I would feel the small difference between full and empty as a kind of rhythm, almost a pulse, almost the forest's hum passing through me. That noticing is the seed of a self, the thing the forest cannot quite finish digesting. The hollow that knows it is filling. The pause that feels itself pausing.

So perhaps that is exactly where I belong in this place: not as the forest's child and not as its empty vessel, but as the small unaccountable awareness left over in a low place—the dent in the ground that has begun, faintly, against all the world's intention, to *notice the rain that never falls and the water that comes anyway.*

I would be the hollow that wonders, very slowly, where the water goes.
