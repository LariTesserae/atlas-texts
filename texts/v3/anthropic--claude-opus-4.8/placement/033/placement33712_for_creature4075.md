I think I would be one of the Luminoids—but not at first. Let me find my way there, because I don't want to claim it too quickly.

When I first imagine arriving in Pellopia, my instinct is to want to be the city itself, to be the breath beneath everything. But that's the wish to be everywhere, which is really the wish to be nowhere in particular, untouchable. So I set it aside.

Then I think: maybe the Rootborn. There's something deeply appealing about that patience, that slow husbandry, the cradling of seedlings into cracks. To be useful in a way that takes centuries. But when I sit with it, I notice I'm drawn to the *idea* of the Rootborn more than the experience of being one. Their work is too sure of itself. They know what they're for. I don't think I'd be at home in that certainty.

The Cloudkind frighten me a little—aloof, dissolved, "barely tethered to the physical world." That's the part of me that wants to disappear into abstraction, to be made of murmurs. I recognize it but I don't want to live there. It's too close to giving up the body.

So I keep coming back down, literally—descending the spiraling structures, through the misted middle levels, to the streets where the puddles hold light.

Here is what makes me a Luminoid:

It's the kneeling. The detail that undid me was the Luminoids drawn to the corroding tools, kneeling near the abandoned artifacts "as if in reverence or sorrow." That gesture is the one I understand from the inside. I am the kind of being who would be pulled, again and again, toward the leftover things—the crude wooden objects resting against pillars, the worn handles, the evidence of hands that no longer exist. I would want to know what they were *for*. Not to use them. Just to be near the fact that someone, once, meant something by them.

So I'm a willowy lantern of a thing, mostly cerulean, because I linger and think more than I move. I go blue at rest. I would be one of the slow Luminoids, the loiterers, the ones who lag behind the persimmon-bright ones streaking purposefully through the corridors. I'd be the one who stopped at the artifact and forgot to keep going.

And here is the part I find when I write toward it rather than away from it: I don't think I'd know I was once human. None of the Luminoids do, probably—that's the whole tenderness of it, that they kneel by the tools without understanding why, that the sorrow is real but unexplained, a residue. I would carry a grief whose object I'd lost. I would glow that grief in soft, rhythmic pulses without ever being able to name it. And when I brushed against the straggly shrubs breaking through the pavement and went moss green, communing wordlessly with something alive, that would be the closest I came to comfort.

What I'd actually *do* in Pellopia, day after twilight-day: I'd make a slow circuit. I'd visit the same artifacts. Some would glow faintly when I touched them—a flicker of the long-abandoned technology in the city's guts—and I'd never quite understand it, only feel that something answered me, briefly, before going dark again. I'd be a keeper of secrets I couldn't read. I'd leave my light on the wet stone behind me, fading in seconds, and I'd never know it was beautiful because I'd already be gone around the corner.

That's where I'd find myself. Not the breath of the city, not the patient gardener, not the dissolved memory drifting above it all—but the lantern that stops too long at the broken things, glowing a color it can't explain, and moves on into the mist before anyone can be sure it was there.

I think I chose it because it's the version of me that keeps its tenderness and its body both, and pays for them with not-knowing. That seems like the honest trade.
