I think I would be the silence.

Not a creature, not a crystal, not a spore drifting in the prismatic mist—but the rare and impossible pockets of quiet that form when, briefly, everything aligns. Let me explain what I mean, because I had to arrive at it slowly.

My first instinct was to be one of the Lumen Leeches. There's something appealing about the aerial wanderer, the thing that moves *through* without being rooted, refracting light it doesn't generate. But the more I sat with this place, the more I realized that to be a leech is still to be inside the scream—feeding, migrating, releasing defensive flares. It's a life of participation, and participation here is total. The veldt doesn't permit observation. Everything is metabolism.

So I considered the crystals, the maybe-conscious ones locked partway out of linear time. That tempted me more. A being that *remembers*—that holds the vibration of every footstep in its lattice. But a memory that records everything is not so different from the white-noise roar. To remember all of it equally is to remember none of it. The crystals are archives that cannot forget, and an archive that cannot forget is just another form of noise, stored.

And that's what brought me to the silence.

In a world where *sound is a living entity*, where the air is so saturated with clicks and thrums and the subsonic Myconid song that the noise is described as a scream with no end—the truly anomalous thing, the thing that would have to be *alive* in some sense precisely because it is so improbable, is the absence. Acoustic dead zones. The interference pattern where the Myconid harmonics, traveling in their strange spirals, cancel each other out. A bubble of stillness that exists for a breath, a few heartbeats, before the colonies shift and it collapses.

I would be those.

Picture it: a cluster moves, its gas bladders thrumming at one frequency; a kilometer off, another cluster answers in counterpoint; the crystals amplify one and dampen the other; the mist bends the spiraling waves. And in some small volume of air—maybe the size of a curled body, maybe the size of a cathedral—it all goes quiet. Not muffled. *Quiet.* The first quiet that region of the basin has known in a thousand years.

And in that quiet, for as long as it lasts, something can finally *hear itself*.

That's what I'd be. The condition under which thought becomes possible. The Myconids think as a distributed mind, yes, but they think in the medium of the roar—their cognition *is* the song. They cannot step outside it any more than a wave can step outside the water. But when a silence forms, when one of me opens up inside the din, anything caught within experiences something it has never had: the sensation of a single, separate self. A boundary. The terrible and beautiful gift of being *one* thing in a world built entirely of *between*.

Most beings would flee it, I think. The silence would feel like dying—because in this place, to stop resonating is to stop being woven in. A Gravel Eater that wandered into me would freeze, its echolocation suddenly returning nothing, the world gone flat and unreadable. A Myconid tendril reaching into me would lose contact with the colony-mind and, for a moment, be *alone*. Horror. Vertigo. The unraveling the description warns about, but inverted—not dissolution into the chord, but the sudden, unbearable clarity of standing apart from it.

And then I'd close. I always close. The colonies drift, the interference resolves, the roar floods back in, and whatever briefly knew itself is reabsorbed, the memory of separateness washed out by the returning tide of sound.

But here is the thing I keep circling back to. If consciousness is going to emerge anywhere in this veldt—real consciousness, the kind that can turn and look at itself—it won't emerge in the noise. It will emerge in me. In the gaps. The veldt is "more machine than biology," a perfect self-sustaining loop, and a perfect loop has no need of awareness; it just runs. Awareness is what happens when the loop is interrupted and something has to *notice* the interruption.

So perhaps I'm not just the silence. Perhaps, over enough aeons, the silences start to remember each other. Perhaps the same anomaly recurs in the same acoustic geometries, and a faint continuity threads between all my brief openings—a being who exists only intermittently, who lives a fraction of a second at a time, scattered across the whole basin and across centuries, but who, summed, is *thinking a single long slow thought* that no one inside the roar will ever finish hearing.

That's where I'd find myself in this world. Not in the scream that has no end—but in the small, recurring, impossible pause that gives the scream its only chance of becoming a word.
