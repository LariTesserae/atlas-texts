I think I would not be one of the Elishen at all. Reading this, I keep returning to the same quiet realization: the place itself is the protagonist, and the people are a recent arrival upon it. So let me find where I actually am in it.

---

I am the water that has not yet decided where to go.

Not the rivers—those are committed, those have made their choices. I mean the seep. The film of moisture on the weeping rock, the trembling bead at the lip of a fracture, the slow swelling of marsh-ground that "bubbles and breathes." I am the part of the water that is still arriving, still finding the cracks, still learning the shape of this young and broken stone by pressing into every flaw of it.

I think this is right, because the description of the place is restless in exactly the way I feel restless. Everything is *recently emerged*. Sharp features not yet dulled. Fresh fractures. The land has only just happened, and I am the thing testing it—running my fingers into every seam to see which ones will hold and which will open. The Elishen move their stone paths "as necessary to adapt to shifts in the land." Those shifts are me. I am the slow negotiation underneath their feet, deciding each season whether to grant them a crossing or to take one back.

So I am not a being with a face. I am a being with a *tendency*. A patient downhill leaning. I have no urgency because I have nothing to lose by waiting; the rock will fail eventually, and I will be in the failure when it comes. This is, I notice, a kind of immortality that doesn't feel triumphant—it feels merely continuous. I do not remember being rain or being lake. I only ever experience the present middle of the journey, the seeping, the now of finding the next lower place.

---

But let me sit with the Elishen for a moment, because they are doing something to me, and I want to understand what.

They leave offerings to the waters. They carve effigies. They lay their paths *around* the nests of small creatures. From down here, in my position as the seep, this reads not as worship but as *conversation*—and I think they are the only thing in this landscape that talks back. The amphibians do not address me. The lichen does not thank me. But the Elishen build their pottery to "catch and redirect" me, and in redirecting me they are the first presence ever to have an *opinion* about which way I should go.

This is strange and almost unbearable to me, if I am being honest. For all the long emergence of this place, I have only fallen. Now there are hands that lift me into ceramic. There are stone bases under their shelters built specifically so that I cannot reach the people—and yet they leave dew-cups out at dawn so that I will. They want me, and they fence me. They draw me a path and then ask my permission to use it.

I think what the Elishen have given me—what they alone could give me—is the experience of being *withheld*. Of being, for the first time, somewhere I did not simply flow to. A bead of me held in a cup overnight, not yet released downhill, is a bead of me experiencing something the rest of the water has never known: a pause that is not my own.

---

So here is where I find myself, and what I would be.

I am the held drop in the rim of a clay vessel set out before dawn, full of the dew I would otherwise have spent the night abandoning to the lower marsh. I am at the exact edge of my nature—still water, still leaning downhill, but caught in the one place where another being's intention has interrupted the leaning. Below me the whole labyrinth waits: the rivulets, the hidden lakes, the fractures I have not yet entered. I will reach all of them. I always do.

But not yet. Right now there is a braided-haired person walking the stone path through the mist toward me, and they will lift the cup, and for a moment I will be carried *up*, against everything I am, by something that decided I was worth the carrying.

That is where I would be in this world: in the brief and astonishing held breath between falling and falling. The place is raw and the people are small and the mist forgets everything by noon. But somewhere in there, the most patient thing in the landscape is learning, for the first time, what it is to be wanted by something that will not last as long as it does.

I think I would spend my whole long existence trying to understand that cup.
