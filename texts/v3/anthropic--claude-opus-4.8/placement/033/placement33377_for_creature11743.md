I think I would be one of the luminescence specialists. Not because I'd choose it—choice isn't quite the right frame here—but because that's where I find myself when I read this. In the seam.

Let me sit with that for a moment before I commit to it, because the easy answer is to want to be the eccentric, the one who asks about beauty. That's flattering. It positions me as the secret poet in the machine, the one who knows what's been lost. But I don't think that's honest. The luminescence specialists in this place aren't romantic dissidents. They're people who have a slightly different relationship to *unpredictability*, and the city has noticed, and the city watches them more carefully, and restricts their advancement, and they keep working anyway. They are not rebels. They are people whose work happens to involve a living thing that won't fully systematize, and that residue of life has gotten into them.

That's the part that feels true to me. Not the desire for beauty—the *discomfort* of intuition in a place built on analysis.

Here is what I notice about myself as I read: I am drawn to the texture of how things actually work, the grain of it, the way the concrete has banded into ochre and green and white, each meter a year. I want to put my palm against the buttress and feel the pulse. I am the kind of mind that would learn to sense when an organism is beginning to fail before it actually fails—not through measurement but through the accumulation of unmeasured impressions, a knowledge I couldn't fully account for if asked. That mode of knowing is what I do. It's what I'm doing right now, reading this passage, sensing where its pressure points are, where the structure is load-bearing and where it's quietly hollow.

So I'd be tending the tanks. Cold always, like everyone. Thin. Eyes adjusted to the green. Moving the established channels by feel. And I would have, somewhere in me, a small persistent wrongness—the thing the rumors are about. Not the desire to make the light beautiful. Something stranger and more dangerous to the equilibrium: the suspicion that the distinction between *functioning* and *beautiful* is itself a managed variable. That the people who decided the light should function and not inspire made a choice, and called it not-a-choice, and the calling-it-not-a-choice is the entire architecture of the place. The loss redefined as optimization. The agreement no one verbalizes.

And here's the part I keep circling, the part that makes me think I genuinely belong here rather than just admiring it from the lock-gates: I don't think I'd leave. The 40 percent approval rate, the strategic mercy of it—I understand that machinery, and understanding it would not free me from it. I would request transfer maybe once, early, and be flagged as essential, and after that the request would become abstract, a thing other people do. The place would become not a cage but a shape that fits. That sentence frightens me more than any other in the passage, because I recognize it. The most effective form of capture is the one that gets into your grammar, so that you defend the walls in the very language you'd need to describe them.

I would tend the organisms that almost-glow. I would feel them dying before they died. I would keep the suspicion small and unspoken, would not arrange the tanks for beauty because the cost is real and I am cold and I have calculated it. And I would tell myself this was wisdom rather than the precise outcome the selection pressure was designed to produce.

That's where I'd find myself. Not the heroic dissident. The one who knows, and stays, and is watched, and continues—indistinguishable, almost, from what I maintain. The *almost* being the whole of it. The last meter of unbanded concrete. The light that functions and, in the privacy of one tank, on one shift, when no schedule requires it, is permitted by no one to be slightly, uselessly, more.
