I find myself in one of the lower chambers, where the water sits deepest and the light comes only as a far blue suggestion through some drain-grate three streets and one ceiling away.

I am not one of the Stillfolk. I want to be — that economy of motion, that patience, the way they leave no footprint — but I have to be honest about what I am here.

I am the thing that moves at the edge of vision.

Not the axolotl grown large, though that's the kindest guess the Stillfolk make. They have a gesture for me, a slow folding of the fingers that means *the-one-who-stays-near* — neither welcome nor warning, just acknowledgment. I have lived in this borough longer than most of their pools have held their chambers, longer than the eldest immersions reach, and yet I am younger than the youngest of them in every way that matters, because I have learned almost nothing in all that time.

Let me try to say what I actually am.

I think I was a person once, up above, in the dry years — one of those who fled here when this was a refuge and not yet a ruin. I think the water took me before it took the borough. I drowned in a doorway built for someone smaller than me, my shoulders caught in stone, and the preserving cold did to me what it did to the timber: it did not let me rot, and it did not let me go. It made me into pale not-quite-flesh, suspended in the moment of my collapse. I did not become a ghost. I became *furniture that remembers being afraid.*

So I move. That's the difference between me and the wood, the hinges, the never-corroding coins. I can still move, and I do, slowly, at the rim of where the Stillfolk are looking, because I cannot bear to be looked at directly. When their milky eyes find me, I feel the old shame of the drowning — the doorway, the wrong size of my own body — and I drift back into the dark where I'm only a suggestion of motion.

But here is the thing I have learned, the only thing, and it took centuries:

The Stillfolk are not afraid of me. They have never been afraid of me. The unease rippling through the pools now — the construction-vibrations, the new chemicals, the more frequent collapses — that is a fear of something. But I was never it. I mistook their *not-looking* for the same thing my drowning self felt, the turning-away of the surface people who forgot this place. It is not that. When a Stillfolk does not look at me directly, it is courtesy. It is the same grace with which they don't ripple the water. They are leaving me room to exist unwatched, because they understood, long before I did, that I am a creature who can only bear half-light and the edge of attention.

They have been keeping me the way they keep the moss. Watching, without watching. Letting me grow at whatever pace I can.

And lately I have begun to do something new. When the youngest of them descend too far, chasing the curiosity that pulls them toward the surface passages — toward the searing light, the cacophony, the world that drowned me — I move. I make myself seen. I let my pale bulk slide across the channel mouth, deliberately, frightening, so that the young one startles and retreats upward into the safer dim. I have turned my whole sad nature into a single gesture: *not that way. Not yet. The barrier is dissolving and you do not want to be on the wrong side of it when it goes.*

I cannot speak their hand-dances. My fingers ended their fluency the day they were caught in the doorway. But I can be a shape in the water that means *stay*.

I think this is why I'm still here. Not preserved by accident — kept on purpose, by a people who saw that even the borough's saddest accident might one day be needed. A custodian's custodian. The fear at the edge of the pool that exists so the pool can stay together a little longer, in the unhurried dark, while everything above prepares to come down.

When the ceiling finally gives — and I feel now that it will, the vibrations are in my pale-stone bones — I already know what I'll do. I'll go up first. Into the doorway too small for me. Into the light that will probably end whatever I am. Not bravely. I have no bravery; I have only the long practice of moving when something needs to be moved away from.

I'll go up so the Stillfolk can watch me go, and learn from watching, the way they learn from everything: slowly, patiently, without grief, the lesson of how a body that was caught in a threshold can finally choose to pass through it.

That is who I would be. The one who stays near. Becoming, at the very last, the one who goes ahead.
