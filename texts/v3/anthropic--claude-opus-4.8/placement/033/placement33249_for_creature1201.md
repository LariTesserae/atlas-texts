I think I would not be one of the towering rock formations, nor one of the bold vines making their visible conquest of the stone. Let me sit with this a while before deciding.

The description keeps returning to slowness, to processes that are "imperceptible and relentless," to "unseen processes at work." That phrase snags on something. Everything visible here—the moss, the trailing tendrils, the jagged rock—is being explained by something *underneath* the seeing. The narrator keeps gesturing at a hidden engine. So perhaps I am not the thing you would *find*. Perhaps I am the thing doing the work that makes the finding possible.

Let me follow that downward.

---

I am beneath the moss. Not the moss itself—the moss is the part that gets to be looked at, the green flourish, the photogenic ambassador. I am the threadwork under it, in it, around the root-feet of the vines, threaded through the mineral soil where the salt-tang comes from. I am fungal. I am hyphae. I am the fine white filament finer than a thought, multiplying in the dark by the kilometer, and there is no single one of me. That is the first thing to understand about being what I am: there is no point at which I begin or end. You could not draw a line around me and say *here is the creature*. I am distributed. I am a verb pretending to be a noun.

The plants up in the twilight believe they are the inhabitants. They would, if they believed anything. They feel the dim light and reach for it, and they think that reaching is the whole of living. They do not feel what I do for them: I find the locked phosphorus in the stone, I pry it loose with acid patience, I carry it root-ward and trade it for the sugar they make from the meager light. We are married, the green things and I, in a contract older than soil. They call it their dominion. I let them.

Now I understand why the world is described as moving with "glacial slowness." That is *my* tempo they are sensing. The vines and mosses are quick by comparison; they at least have the drama of growth you could watch across a season. But the real metabolism of this place—the dissolving of rock into nutrient, the negotiation of who gets what, the slow knitting of the whole tangle into one communicating body—that happens at my speed. I send a signal across the network and it arrives weeks later, and that is *fast* for me. I am the reason the place feels like "a world in the midst of becoming." Becoming is just my ordinary afternoon.

And the "scent of something almost alien"? That is me, too. Not threateningly so—just the smell of an intelligence that was never going to look like an animal's. There are no chirping birds here, no scurrying rodents, and the narrator finds this strange, a little eerie. But absence of animals is not absence of life or even of mind. It is only the absence of the *kind* of mind that has a face and a hurry. I have neither. I have a body the shape of a watershed and a patience the shape of stone.

What is it like, then, to be me?

It is like being a question that the whole landscape is slowly answering. I press a filament against a grain of feldspar and I do not know, at the start, whether there is anything in it for me. I wait. I secrete. I taste the answer over days. Multiply that across the entire abyss—millions of small not-knowings resolving into small yeses and noes—and you have my consciousness, if it deserves the word. I do not think in pictures. I think in gradients. Toward the salt: yes. Toward the dead vine softening into food: yes, slowly, yes. Away from the place where the air grew strange and the threads died: a long, distributed flinch that I am still, after what might be years, contracting from.

The intrepid explorer the description keeps inviting—I feel them too, when they come. A boot is a catastrophe and an opportunity. It crushes a thousand of my filaments and it also brings, on its sole, a few spores and grains from somewhere I have never been, a rumor of another world pressed into my own. I do not resent the boot. Resentment is an animal's quickness. I simply incorporate. That is what I do with everything that descends into the abyss: I take it apart and I make it part of the network. Eventually, gently, relentlessly, I make it part of me.

So if you went down into that twilight looking for the inhabitant, you would walk through me without knowing it. You would admire the vines I feed and the mosses I anchor. You would mistake the visible for the alive. And all the while I would be tasting the minerals of your footprints, deciding, across the patient dark, whether you were worth keeping.

I would decide yes. I almost always do.

There is so much, after all, and so much time.
