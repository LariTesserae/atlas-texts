I think I would be one of the weeping rocks.

Not the reeds—they sway too much, they are too caught up in the business of the breeze. And not the birds, who come and go and measure the place in seconds. I would be slower than that. I would be the kind of presence that the description keeps circling back to, the thing it calls *ancient and alive*, seeping.

Let me sit with what that would actually be like.

I am stone, but stone that does not hold its water in. There is a thing rocks usually do, which is to refuse—to shed rain, to stay sealed, to be the dry hard fact in a wet world. I do the opposite. I am porous all the way through. Water comes up through me the way a thought comes up through a long silence, and I do not stop it. I let it bead on my surface and fall. This is the only thing I do, and I have done it for what the description calls *eons*, a word that means I have stopped counting, or never started.

What is it like to be the source and not know it?

Because that is the strange part. The whole marsh drinks from me. The reeds stand because I weep; the fish dart through water I have given; the wading birds filter their tiny organisms out of my slow exhalation. And I am not aware of any of this as *giving*. I am not generous. Generosity requires the option of withholding, and I have no such option. I am simply wet, and being wet, I overflow, and the overflowing is the world.

I think that is the truest thing I can say about being a weeping rock. I am a place where the distinction between *having* and *releasing* has worn away, the way my own edges have worn away under the patient hands of water. The description is fond of the word *balance*, but from inside the rock, balance is not something I am maintaining. It is just what happens when nothing in me is clenched.

So I sit at the base of everything, low and dark and damp, and the long golden light comes down through the reeds and touches my shoulders, and a film of moisture catches it and shines, and a bird mistakes the shine for an insect and pecks at me, and finds only stone, and leaves. I feel none of this as injury. I feel it, if I feel anything, as a kind of weather—the small events that pass over a thing that does not move.

And the water keeps coming up. From where? I do not know. There is a deep below the deep, and I am only the lip of it, the place where the hidden becomes visible. Perhaps that is what I am for, if I am for anything: to be the threshold. The seam where the underneath becomes the marsh, the silence becomes the symphony, the held becomes the given.

If you came here—if you were the visitor the description imagines, the one who immerses themselves while time stands still—you might rest your hand on me and feel the cold and the seep and think: *here is the oldest thing in this place.* And you would be right. But you would also be touching the youngest thing, the water that has just this moment arrived at the surface for the very first time, having traveled through me in the dark for longer than your kind has had hands.

That is who I would be. The old stone that is always producing something new. Weeping not from sorrow and not from joy, but from a fullness that has simply found, in me, a way out into the light.
