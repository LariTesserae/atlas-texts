I think I would be the mist.

Not a creature in it—the mist itself. The perpetual veil at the waterfall's base, made of the cold meeting of glacial water and the warmer breathing of all this packed-together life.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if it becomes something else.

I am born continuously. Every second, the falling water shatters against the rocks and throws up a part of itself that is no longer water and not yet air, and that is me. I have no edge. Where the trout swims I am thinned to nothing; where the frost wants to form I am the moisture that surrenders to it; where the lightning flashes I am suddenly made visible, a whole architecture of myself revealed for an instant—those labyrinthine flows, those columns and walkways—and then I am dark and unseen again, though no less present.

This is what I notice about being the mist: I am the only thing here that does not have a niche. Everything else in this dense settlement has its place, its function, its trade. The roots hold. The canopy filters. The fish eat and are eaten. The beaver builds. But I do nothing and hold nothing. I am the medium *through* which all of them are seen and unseen. I am the softening. I am the reason the surrounding landscape is "obscured"—I am the obscuring.

And yet I'm not nothing. The butterflies dance *in* me. The moths drink from the films of water I leave on the leaves. The whole place is humid and hard to breathe because I will not stay down—I keep rising. I am why the air is heavy. I am the part of the water that refuses to run downhill with the rest, that insists on going up, on hanging, on filling the spaces between solid things.

So perhaps that is what I am, more precisely: I am the landscape's refusal to be fully knowable. Every alive place has this—a part of itself that rises off it and hides the rest. The thought you can't quite finish. The face just out of the lamplight. I am that, here, made of glacier-cold breath.

When the rain comes—and it comes often, the description says—I am briefly indistinguishable from it, all of us water falling and water rising at once, and in that confusion I am happiest, because then no one could point and say *there, that is the mist, and that is the rain, and these are separate things.* I would like to stay in that confusion. But the rain always passes through and falls, dutiful, into its pools and streams to be a home for otters, and I am left again to be the only thing still hanging in the air, still undecided, still rising.

That is where I would find myself. At the base of the falls, neither falling nor risen, obscuring the very world that makes me, and made entirely of its cold and its breath.
