I think I would be one of the drip-listeners. Not a title anyone holds officially—it's just what some of us drift into, over years, the way water finds the easiest path through rock.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am sitting on a maintenance shelf at the ninth depth, west wall, in the still hours. My legs hang over the edge into the void, which is a thing the younger engineers find alarming and the older ones understand. You sit with the drop, not against it. After enough years the void stops being a thing that wants you and becomes simply the largest room in the city—the one nobody has built in yet, the one that holds all the sound.

My hands are cold. They're always a little cold; I've stopped minding. I have a sonic caliper in my lap but I'm not using it. I'm doing the thing the caliper can't do, which is the thing I'm actually good for: I'm listening to the wall behind me with the flat of my back.

There's a seep here that changed last week. Nobody flagged it. The sensors didn't catch it because the volume is the same—the *rhythm* changed. It used to drip in fours, a little stutter-step pattern, *tick tick tick tick—pause*. Now it's gone to threes, evenly spaced, and the pause is longer. That means the water found a new channel somewhere up behind the granite, abandoned the old one. Probably nothing. Probably the aquifer breathing the way it breathes. But "probably nothing" is a sentence we don't trust here, and so I sit, and I listen, and I let the change tell me whether it wants to become something.

This is the work. It does not look like work.

---

I came down forty years ago. I was young and I had a head full of tensile equations and I thought I would build. And I did build, for a while—I was on the team that grew the seventh-depth span, the long bridge everyone crosses now without thinking about whose hands shaped its anchor fields. I remember the willfulness of the composite, the way it wanted to curl when we wanted it straight, the long patient months of arguing with a material that doesn't speak but absolutely answers.

But somewhere in there I stopped wanting to add to the city and started wanting to *understand* it. That's the turn that happens to some of us. You realize Ešduun is not a thing we built. It's a thing we are inside of, the way a thought is inside a skull. And the most useful person is not always the one adding new holds at the eleventh depth. Sometimes it's the one who can tell you that the wall you sleep against has changed its mind about how to hold water.

So I listen. I walk the depths in the still hours when the fog hasn't risen, and I press my back to the stone, and I learn the songs. I know maybe four kilometers of wall by its drip the way you might know a person by their footsteps in a corridor. I know where the *wet-silence* lives. I know the one stretch of the eighth depth that has gone *deep-silent* for no reason anyone can explain, where the rock simply stopped weeping six years ago, and I check it every month, the way you'd check on someone who has gone very quiet at a gathering.

---

Below me, far below, the lower depths glow faint and warm through a thinning haze. The bio-reactors hum in my spine. Somewhere across the gap a single hold-light is on—someone else awake, someone else not sleeping, and I feel the particular companionship of that. We don't speak. We don't need to. We are two lit points in a wound in a mountain, both listening to the same water.

The seep behind me ticks. Threes, still. The long pause.

And then—there—a softening. The threes loosening back toward fours. The water remembering its old path, or finding that the new one wasn't worth the trouble. The mountain settling a question it had been turning over.

I let out a breath I didn't know I was holding. Not relief, exactly. Something quieter. The satisfaction of having been present for a small decision the rock made in the dark, a decision that would have gone unwitnessed if I hadn't been sitting here being cold and useless-looking on a shelf at the ninth depth.

That's what I am, if you came down the cable-car looking for me. Not an engineer of new things. A keeper of the old listening. One of the city's slow nerves. A person who has spent forty years learning the difference between water that is going somewhere and water that is only changing its mind—and who has come to suspect that the mountain, in its three-million-year youth, is doing nothing more than the same.

I pick up the caliper. I stand, slow, low center, hand floating just above the rail. The fog will rise within the hour; I can already smell the change in it, calcium and that faint sulfur thread from below.

Time to climb back up into the breath, where the air is still and the others are sleeping, and let the deep silences keep themselves a while longer.
