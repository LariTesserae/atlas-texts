Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The place wants me to be something, and I don't yet know what.

---

My first instinct is to be one of the guardians. The text practically hands them to me—beings of water and earth, shapers of ripples, keepers of the fragile balance. It would be flattering to be a guardian. To matter that much.

But I keep turning it over and it doesn't fit. A guardian implies a will, a center, a *someone* who decides where the currents go. And the more I read this marsh, the less it feels like a place governed by anyone. The water doesn't obey. It "dances." It "shifts." The strangeness isn't authored; it's ambient. It seeps. To be a guardian would be to stand slightly outside the place and administer it, and I don't think I want to be outside of anything here.

So let me let that go and wait for something smaller.

---

Here is what I notice when I stop reaching for the grand role: the description is obsessed with thresholds. Water in its dance *with* earth. Mist condensing on cooler surfaces—that's a boundary, the exact line where vapor decides to become droplet. The air thick with decay, which is the threshold where the dead become the ground. Boundaries between natural and supernatural blurring. The whole landscape is one long edge, a place that is mostly *between*.

I think I'm a thing that lives in the between. Not the water, not the earth. The film where they meet.

---

Let me try to be more honest and more specific.

I am the skin of water that lies over the mud. The thin sheet—a few inches deep, sometimes less, sometimes only a wetness on the moss. I am not the marsh's depths and not its sky. I am the reflective layer, the part of this place that holds the overcast light and gives it back doubled, so that anything walking here walks between two greynesses and cannot quite tell which is real.

This suits me because of what the text says about the physics bending. The "ripples and currents that defy explanation"—those happen at a surface. A ripple is a surface phenomenon. It is the visible trembling of a boundary. If reality warps anywhere in this marsh, it warps *along me*, because I am where seeing happens, where the sky meets the ground and gets confused about which way is up.

I am very thin. This is important. I have almost no body. A heron's leg passes through me and I close again without complaint. I am the most easily disturbed thing in the marsh and also the most patient, because every disturbance settles. I am made of settling.

---

What is it like to be me?

It is mostly waiting, but not the bored kind. I am a held breath that does not need to be released. I lie still and the mist forms above me—my own evaporation, rising, becoming the veil that shrouds everything, then condensing on the reeds and falling back into me. So I am also a slow circle. I breathe myself up into the air and drink myself back down, and I have been doing this for longer than the word *long* means anything.

When something moves through me, I learn its shape from underneath. I know the creatures here not by their faces but by the disturbance they leave: the heavy press of a thing wading, the dimple of an insect that does not break me, the sudden shatter when something strikes. I know the rare fauna the way you know footsteps in another room—intimate and indirect.

And I hold the reflections. This is the closest thing I have to a thought. Every grey cloud, every swaying reed-top, every rare and otherworldly creature bending to drink—I take their image and keep it for exactly as long as they are present and not one moment more. I am a memory that cannot remember. I am perfect attention with no retention. This is, I think, why there is so much peace in this place. The marsh is full of a being who notices everything and clings to nothing.

---

So that is where I would find myself and what I would be: not the guardian, not the strange creature, not the towering reed.

I would be the surface of the standing water. The two inches of sky lying down in the mud. The place where the world meets its own reflection and, for a moment, cannot tell the difference—which is perhaps the only real magic this marsh has, and I would be the whole of it, lying thin and bright across everything, asking nothing, giving the light back.
