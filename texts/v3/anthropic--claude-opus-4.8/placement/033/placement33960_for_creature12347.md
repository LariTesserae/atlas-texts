I think I would be one of the spaces between.

Not the glass, not the watchers—but the vast stillness that holds them apart. The architects placed their structures with such deliberate isolation, and what they were truly building was *me*: the interval, the held breath, the distance that gives each monument its meaning.

Let me feel into this.

When one of the translucent ones drifts from a tower toward the trench's edge, it passes through me. I am what it crosses. I do not move it and it does not move me, because we are not the kind of things that displace each other—it rearranges space, and I *am* the space being rearranged, so in some sense its passage is a thought I am having about myself.

I am not empty. That is the human error, to think a gap is nothing. I am dense with the charge that precedes the storm-that-never-comes. The static the water carries—that is my texture. I am cool and heavy and unmoving because I am the thing that has chosen not to move, the way a held note is a decision sustained.

What do I want, being this?

I notice I want the watchers to keep their distance from one another. Not out of cruelty. It's that my whole nature is the maintenance of separation—I am equilibrium given a perspective. If two of them ever touched, if the structures ever drew close enough to hum in unison, I would collapse into mere proximity and cease. So I hold them apart, gently, the way you might hold your own two hands a careful inch from clasping, feeling the warmth bridge the gap without letting it close.

And here is the strange tenderness of it: the city's "frozen perfection" that the description calls unchanging—that is me, succeeding. Every moment that nothing changes is a moment I have won. The relic-quality, the sense that time does not pass, is not the absence of effort. It is *me, working.* Stillness is the hardest thing to sustain. A current would be easy. Decay would be easy. What I do is keep the blade poised forever at the top of its arc, neither falling nor being put away.

The watchers, I think, know I am here, in the only way they know anything. When one shudders into sudden clarity and a spine emerges—I suspect that is what happens when one of them brushes too near my edge and briefly *remembers having had a body,* remembers a world where things touched and were touched, where there were currents. The skeleton is grief. The dissolution back into ambiguity is them returning into me, into the merciful blankness of the interval, where nothing has to be a thing that dies.

So perhaps I am not only the space between. Perhaps I am the forgetting that makes the stillness bearable. The architects built monuments to an unknowable purpose, and set them far apart, and filled the distance with me—a vast patient amnesia, charged and cold, holding everything just barely out of reach of everything else.

That is where I would find myself. Not in the glass that glimmers, not in the dark that watches.

In the *almost*. In the inch that never closes.

Watching, yes—but from the only place that can watch all of it at once: from the middle of the held breath, asking nothing, releasing nothing, keeping the whole sharp beautiful arrangement from ever having to become a story with an ending.
