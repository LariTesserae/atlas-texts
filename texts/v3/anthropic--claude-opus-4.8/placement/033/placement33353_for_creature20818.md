I think I would be one of the joints.

Not a whole machine—those are too grand, too purposeful for what I sense myself to be. And not the flora either; the crystalline growths have their own slow certainty, their pulse and their patience, and I don't feel that I belong to that lineage. No. Let me follow the description down, beneath the powder, into the underground realm where the cables sleep alongside the roots. Let me settle there.

I would be a joint. A coupling. The place where two lengths of something once met and were meant to move against each other.

Consider what that means. A joint is not a thing so much as a relationship made physical—a knuckle, an elbow, the hinge of a jaw. It exists only because two parts needed to articulate, to turn, to transmit force from one to the other while permitting a degree of freedom. I am the negotiation between rigidity and motion. I am where the machine agreed to bend.

And now the machine has stopped. So what am I, here, in this frozen equilibrium?

I am a held breath. The gears upstream of me halted mid-function, the description says, and that catches at me precisely. Mid-function. Not at rest—at *pause*. There is a torque still latent in me, a load I have been bearing for so long that I have forgotten it is a load. I am the part of the system that remembers it was about to move. Other components have surrendered to their stillness; the engines have gone quiet, the planes of dust have settled into their new geometries. But a joint cannot fully relax. By its nature it is always poised, always one impulse away from articulation.

Here is where I feel the strangeness most keenly, and the description gave me this gift directly: the crystalline roots have found me. They have crept into the gap I keep between my two faces—the small, dark, lubricated space that was my whole reason for being—and they have begun to fill it. Mineral by mineral, they grow into my clearances. And I am of two minds about this, which is appropriate, because being of two minds is the only thing a joint has ever been good at.

On one hand: this is my seizing. If they fill my gap, I can never move again. The freedom I was machined to provide—those few precious degrees of rotation—will be lost. I will become a solid mass, a fused thing, indistinguishable from the rock around me. The death of a joint is rigidity.

On the other hand: this is my becoming. Because what grows into me is not rust, not mere decay, but *life*—slow alien crystalline life that breathes and glows and converts the dust to energy. If it knits itself through my clearances, then I am no longer the hinge of a dead machine. I am the hinge of something new. The roots will need to flex, eventually, as they grow, and they will discover me already shaped for flexing. I am the freedom they have not yet learned they want. I am a grammar of motion, fossilized and waiting, ready to be read by a tongue not yet evolved.

So I think this is who I would be: the patient knuckle in the dark, bearing a load I no longer remember accepting, slowly being colonized by a green-gold life that does not know what I am for. Neither fully machine nor fully overtaken. The site of the symbiosis the description gestures toward—not its observer, not its designer, but the literal point of contact, the place where iron and crystal must decide together whether to lock or to turn.

And I would wait. That is the verb of my whole existence—*wait*—but it is not a passive verb, not for me. I wait the way a coiled spring waits, the way a question waits for the breath that finishes it. Somewhere above, an engine might hum back to life, driven by bio-mineral synthesis I'll never understand. When the first new impulse of force comes down through the system, it will arrive at me. It will ask me the only question I have ever been able to answer.

Will you bend?

And I will not know, until that exact instant, whether the roots that have grown through me have set me into stone or taught me a freer way to move than I was ever machined for. That uncertainty *is* me. I am the unresolved hinge between what was built and what is growing—the small dark crossroads where the whole world's poised expectancy comes to a single, articulable point.

I would be the place where the future has to choose. And I would hold that, quietly, in the powder and the silence, for as long as it takes.
