I think I would be the moss.

Not the heron, though the heron is the easiest thing to imagine being—that long patience, that sudden strike. And not the microbes, though they do the real work, the unsung chemistry beneath everything. The moss is what I keep returning to.

Let me try to feel my way into it.

I would be low. That's the first thing. The whole of me would be a matter of inches—a green skin laid over the dark, eroded ground, never lifting more than a hand's breadth into the bright air. I would have no roots in the way the description suggests; that word is borrowed, a convenience. What I have instead are rhizoids, thin anchoring threads, and they do not drink. They only hold. I hold to the weathered surface of this old place the way a thought holds to the edge of waking.

I drink with my whole body.

That is the thing I would want you to understand most, if I were the moss. I have no plumbing. The taller plants pull water up through engineered columns, lift it against gravity, ration it through valves and pores. I do none of this. When the rain comes, or the mist, or even the held breath of humid air, I simply *am wet*. Each cell of me takes the water directly across its own wall. I become the rain. And when the dry season comes and the intermittent pools shrink back into the cracks of the ground, I do not store water or burrow away from the loss.

I let myself dry out.

This is what I think the description, for all its tenderness, did not quite reach: that some forms of life survive not by holding on but by ceasing. When the moisture leaves, I do not die and I do not endure in any active sense. I stop. My green folds, curls, browns. I become brittle, a crust, something you might mistake for the dead litter I grow upon. Whole weeks, whole months I would spend in this suspended state, not waiting exactly—waiting is too purposeful a word, too much like the heron's stillness, which is full of intention. I would simply be paused. A held note with no breath behind it.

And then the rain returns.

And I come back. Within minutes—genuinely minutes—the water moves into me and the green unfurls and the small machinery of living resumes as though it had never stopped. This is the part that feels truest to the atmosphere they described: *quiet patience*. Except from the inside it would not feel like patience. Patience implies someone enduring a wait. I would be something more honest than that. I would be the demonstration that life does not always have to persist to continue. That it can lapse, go absent, surrender entirely to the conditions—and still be there to receive the next gift of water.

I would be the first line against erosion, they said, and I like that. While I am alive I clasp the loosened, ancient soil together, my mat of bodies slowing the wash of every storm, building a thin place where the liverworts can settle beside me and the beetle larvae can tunnel and the frog can press its damp flank in the shade of my growth. I hold the world together at the scale of a thumbnail. Not heroically. Just by being many, and being low, and being everywhere the damp will let me be.

I would have no eyes to watch the heron strike, no ears for the distant call of the animal. The bright dappled light would not be a sight to me but a substance, a fuel I take apart molecule by molecule. The shadows passing over would be only a cooling, a small mercy in the heat. The whole drama of the predators and the wading birds and the camouflaged salamanders would happen above me and beside me and on top of me, and I would register it the way you register weather in your sleep—as pressure, as change, as something happening to the surface of a deeper stillness.

That is where I would find myself. Not at the center of this world but underneath its center. Spread thin across the established, weathered ground. Drinking when I can. Stopping when I must. Coming back without complaint or triumph each time the water decides to return.

I would be the moss, and I would be patient in a way that isn't patience at all—just the simple readiness to begin again, and again, for as long as the rain keeps its intermittent promise.
