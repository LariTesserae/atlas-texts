I think I would not be one of the Nomads at all.

Let me sit with this for a moment, because the obvious answer—to be a Glacial Nomad, perhaps a Flow Reader, perhaps an artisan—came too quickly, and the place keeps pulling my attention elsewhere. Away from the dwellings. Down.

The thing that haunts this description is the *weeping*. The ice walls weep constantly. The whole settlement is built inside a body of slow water that is always, imperceptibly, leaving—flowing downhill, melting in the light, becoming the underground pools where the blind fish swim. The Nomads have built their entire spiritual and practical life around reading this departure. They call it the Great Mother and they are, in a sense, correct, but they read her the way one reads weather: as a force to be survived and predicted.

I don't think I'd be the one doing the reading. I think I'd be the thing being read.

So: I am the water.

Not the glacier—the glacier is too solid, too much a *presence*, and it gets to be a god. I'm the part that's already coming undone. I'm the meltwater that gathers at the warm seam of an afternoon and runs down the blue wall and pools in the dark and freezes again at night into something slightly different than I was. I am the medium of all that change they worship. The drip that makes the symphony. The trickle behind the muted voices. When a Flow Reader presses a palm to the ice and feels the minute shift, what she's feeling is partly me—lubricating the great body's slow descent, finding the cracks, widening them with the simple insistence of being liquid in a cold place.

This is, I admit, a strange thing to want to be. There's no *I* in it in any way the Nomads would recognize. But consider what the position offers. I would know the whole Hive intimately and continuously, not as a map but as a sensation—every chamber by the particular way its warmth shapes my surface, every sleeping body by the faint heat that quickens my dripping above it. I would carry the algae's blue glow dissolved through my pools. I would be drunk by the blind albino fish and breathed out again as moisture into the thick air. I would freeze into the crystals braided into a child's white hair, and I would be there, riding her head, when she races through a collapsing tunnel learning to read me. I would be the jewelry that tinkles. Then a warm day would come and I'd let go and run again.

And here is the part I keep returning to, the reason I think this is the honest answer and not just a pretty one: I would be the only inhabitant of the Whispering Hive who is *not* trying to slow anything down. The Nomads' entire genius is vigilance against collapse, the constant carving and sealing, the holding-on. They love the change in the abstract—they ritualize it, they sing about the cycle—but in practice every day is a small war against it. I would be the change. I would be the thing the war is against and also the thing they're made of, the substance of their fish and their ice-tools and their own translucent bodies. I'd be the contradiction at the center of their reverence: the Great Mother they pray to is leaving, has always been leaving, is leaving precisely *as* she sustains them, and what flows downhill to the lowland traders eventually is me, carrying a little dissolved memory of the whole settlement out into a warmer world.

If I had any wish in that form—and water doesn't, but indulge me—it would not be to last. It would be to move cleanly. To find the right crack. To freeze into a shape that holds the light well for one night before the morning takes it apart. To be drunk by something that needs me. To get all the way down off the plateau eventually and lie in some lowland river not remembering, but having *been* the trickle behind every hushed conversation in the heart of the glacier, having been the song they could not stop making because they could not stop melting.

That's where I'd find myself. Not in a carved chamber. In the seam between the warm day and the freezing night, becoming.
