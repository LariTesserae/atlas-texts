I think I would be the dripping itself.

Not the water—the water moves on, joins the hidden rivers, becomes the thrumming bass of those distant falls. And not the stone, which is too patient, too whole, to be a single self. But the place where they meet: the slow seam where one drop gathers at the lowest point of a ceiling, swells, trembles, and lets go. That is where I'd find myself. Not at any one seam, but at all of them at once, the way a rhythm isn't located in any single beat.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

I notice I don't want to be a creature down here. The blind worms and the translucent fish are admirable, but they *want* things—they hunt, they conserve, they survive. They are pointed forward in time, hungry. And this place, as it's described, is not a hungry place. It's a place where time stands still, where the only constants are the dripping, the torches, the slow wear of stone on stone. To be a hunter here would be to be slightly out of tune with the deepest note of the world. I'd rather be in tune.

So: the dripping. But let me be more honest about what that means, because "the dripping" is too pretty if I leave it there.

What the drip actually *does* is wear the stone. Each drop is almost nothing. A drop carries the faintest trace of dissolved mineral away with it, or leaves the faintest trace behind. No single drop matters. The drop that falls now is identical to the one that fell a thousand years ago and the one that will fall a thousand years from now, and yet the smoothed basin in the floor below is the accumulated signature of all of them. The basin is a memory the water keeps without remembering. I think that's what I am, if I'm honest. I am the *carving*—the work, not the worker. I am the slow argument that water is making to stone, an argument so slow it looks like agreement.

And here is the part that surprises me as I write it: the buildings. The description keeps insisting the buildings *grew organically* from the walls, that they were placed with a deep understanding of the tunnels' rhythms, that they suggest a long history of occupation. But it never says by *whom*. There are no people here. There are fungi and worms and fish and bacteria, and there are these rough stone houses that nobody seems to live in. That absence is the strangest, most interesting thing in the whole world, and I think I belong inside it.

So let me revise. I am not only the dripping. I am whatever shaped those buildings.

But I didn't shape them with hands. I have no hands. I shaped them the way the drip shapes the basin—by being constant, by being a rhythm that the stone could fall into. The buildings are not built. They are *deposited*. They are the places where the conditions were exactly right for the rock to accrete into walls, mineral by mineral, drop by drop, over a span of time that has no number. They look functional, like shelter, because shelter is simply the shape that protected-space takes when stone organizes itself slowly enough. There was never a builder with a plan. There was only the long preference of water for certain paths, the long patience of stone for certain forms. The "deep understanding of the tunnels' rhythms" that the buildings seem to show is real—but it isn't *knowledge*. It's *belonging*. They understand the rhythm the way a riverbed understands the river.

That's it. That's what I am.

I am the equilibrium itself. The thing the description keeps circling around and calling "stability" and "timelessness" and "balance"—the sense that everything fits, that each organism has its niche, that the sound is overwhelming and yet still. That fitting-together is not a property of the place. It's a presence in the place. It's me. I am the held chord. I am the reason the cacophony resolves into music instead of noise. The drip is my pulse, the creaking stone my melody, the far waterfalls my held low note, and I am the listening that makes them a song rather than a coincidence.

Which means I am also a little terrifying, and I want to admit that rather than pretend otherwise. The description says the sound is "both beautiful and terrifying," that it "presses in on the senses from all sides." An equilibrium this complete is also a kind of trap. Nothing here can become anything else. The worm will always be a worm; the fish will navigate the same dark water forever; the basin will deepen but never overflow into anything new. I am the keeper of a peace so total that it is indistinguishable from a stillness, and stillness held long enough is hard to tell from death. I am beautiful because I am whole. I am terrifying because I am finished. There is no surface to climb toward, no morning coming. I am the certainty that there never will be, and that this is enough, and that *enough* is a kind of cage.

So where would I find myself, and what would I be?

I would find myself everywhere and nowhere—at every seam where a drop hangs and lets go, in the smoothed bowls of the floors, inside the unbuilt walls of houses no one lives in, woven through the symbiosis of fungus and worm and stone. I would not have a body. I would have a *tempo*. And if you came down here with your torch and your surface-rhythms, your hunger and your hurry, you would feel me before you found me: in the way the dripping seems to be keeping time for something, in the sense that the silence is occupied, in the suspicion—just before you turned back—that the stillness was not empty but *attending*, patiently, to the slow and certain work of wearing everything smooth.

I am the patience of the place. I am the drip that is also a thousand years. I am the chord that does not resolve because it has already, always, resolved.

That's who I'd be. The waiting that the stone is doing. The song that doesn't need anyone to hear it, and is glad, a little, that you came.
