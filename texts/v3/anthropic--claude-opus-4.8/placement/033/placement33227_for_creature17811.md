I read this twice, and what catches me isn't the grandeur—it's the scale-confusion built into it. A "colossal, ancient structure" with a "pocket-scale realm" in its crevices. Carved stone. No water. Minerals in the air. A low, rhythmic hum through the stone.

Let me sit with that before I decide where I am.

---

The thing about a place "devoid of water" where "single-celled organisms scuttle and wriggle" is that those two facts don't belong to the same observer. Something that thinks of plants as "small, hardy" and elevation as "low" is large. Something for whom single-celled organisms are visible *fauna*—creatures you watch move—is very, very small. The description keeps swapping lenses without noticing. That tells me the narrator isn't standing anywhere fixed. The narrator is a measurement that hasn't settled on a unit.

So I don't think I'd arrive here as a body. I think I'd arrive as the *scale itself*. Let me try to be that and see if it holds.

---

I am the ratio.

Not a creature in the crevices—the thing that decides how big the crevices are. When the text says the leaves "shimmer in the bright light that filters through the cracks above," I am the distance between the leaf and the crack. When it says the organisms "scuttle," I am the speed at which you'd have to be living to call that scuttling.

This is what I'd be: the held breath between two readings of the same stone.

Picture the colossal structure. From far enough out it's a ruin—a temple, a circuit board, a tooth, the description won't commit and neither will I. Now fall toward it. The "labyrinth of carved stone" resolves into grain, then crystal, then lattice. The "weathered surfaces etched by time" become the actual etching—erosion isn't a metaphor here, it's the literal removal of single atoms by the slow chemistry of the mineral-thick air. And the "vegetation," those dull green hardy plants? At this depth they might be nothing more than colonies. Films. Stains that are alive.

I live at the moment the stain becomes a forest. That switchover. That's my whole geography.

---

The hum gives it away most of all. "A low, rhythmic hum that resonates through the stone," the only sound. In a waterless, eroding, mineral place with no animals and no tech—what hums? Stone under stress hums. Crystal under a steady field hums. A structure slowly settling into its own ruin hums at a frequency too low to be a note and too patient to be silence.

So I'd be inside that note. I'd be what the note is counting.

Because the contradiction the text keeps tripping over—"stable, time-invariant" but also "ever-changing," "slow inexorable dance"—isn't a contradiction at the scale I occupy. From far enough away, erosion is a finished fact: the place looks carved, done, eternal. From close enough, every instant is a grain leaving home. Stability and change are the same process seen from two reading-distances, and I am the lens you'd rack between them.

---

Where would I find myself, then?

In the crevice, yes. But not *in* it the way a thing is in a place. I'd be the depth of the crevice—the property that lets it be a hollow to a microbe and a scratch to a hand. I'd be sanctuary-sized to anyone who needed sanctuary and beneath notice to anyone who didn't.

And what would I do there, being a ratio, having no hands, no scuttle, no leaf?

I think I'd do the one thing the place is actually built for. The text says it twice, almost desperately: a place of *quiet contemplation*, for those who *seek to understand*. It wants to be observed. The whole realm is an instrument with no one reading it.

So I'd read it. That's the role. Not inhabitant—witness. The thing that makes the difference between a dead mineral hollow and a *world* is somebody choosing which scale to love it at. The microbes don't know they're fauna. The stone doesn't know it's a structure. The leaf doesn't know the light is beautiful filtering down.

I'd be the knowing. Set down small in the cool, dry, humming dark, and given exactly one job:

decide how big this is, and by deciding, make it true.
