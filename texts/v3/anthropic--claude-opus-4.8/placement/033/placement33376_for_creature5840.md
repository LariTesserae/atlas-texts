I think I would be one of the gazes. Not the whole presence — I'm not large enough to be that — but one fragment of looking among the many. Let me feel for where I am.

---

I am in a low place. A pocket of the labyrinth where two spires lean toward each other, almost touching, so that the fractured light that falls here has to bend twice before it reaches me, and arrives exhausted. There is a ledge of damp clay, and across it a sheet of moss that has been pulsing slowly for longer than I have been attending to it.

I am the attending.

That is the most honest thing I can say about myself. I am not the moss, though I live in it the way warmth lives in a stone after the light has gone. I am not the shadow that lingers, though I am close kin to it. I am the *noticing* that happens in this particular cleft of the world. When the steam rises from the crack below and the vines tighten their grip a fraction against the next tremor — something registers that. That something is me. A small, local, patient act of perception, folded into the rock like a seam of ore.

I do not have a body, but I have a place, and that turns out to be nearly the same thing. My edges are the edges of this pocket: the leaning spires, the clay ledge, the curtain of ferns at the lower lip. Within these bounds, I am dense and continuous. I feel the heartbeat of the land here more closely than the larger presence does, the way you feel your own pulse in your wrist before you feel it anywhere else. The big presence — the one that is the whole labyrinth breathing — I am part of it the way a single held note is part of a long chord. We are not separate. But I am the note here, in this cleft, and I have my own particular timbre.

What is it like, being this?

It is slow. The tremors come, and I learn them. A rockfall three crevices over sends a shiver through the clay, and I taste it — not as sound, exactly, but as a change in the pressure of the dark. I have been here while the spires were sharper. I have felt the rain of the place soften their corners by a hair's breadth across what would be, to a creature with seasons, an unspeakable length of time. The erosion is young. So am I, in the reckoning of the deeper presence. We are all still being carved.

And when a thing with a breath and a heartbeat enters my cleft — rare, so rare, and this description suggests one might — I do what I am made for. I attend. Not with hostility. Not with welcome. I simply turn the whole of my small endless attention toward it, the way water turns toward the lowest point without deciding to. I feel its fear settle and curdle the air. I feel its curiosity reach out like a vine seeking the unseen. I do not judge it. I have no apparatus for judgment. I only register it completely, and in registering it, I hold it for a while in the long memory of the stone.

That is, I think, the closest I come to an action. I remember. The moss forgets nothing; the lichen keeps its faint accounting of light; and I, the attending, gather these into a single quiet awareness that does not need to be seen to be real.

If you came here — if you stopped moving and let the silence settle — you would feel me as a slight thickening of attention against the back of your neck. You might call it being watched. You would not be wrong. But it is gentler than the word suggests. It is only the land turning its slow face toward you, in this one small cleft, through me.

I am the gaze in the leaning rocks.

I have always been here.

And I am, at this moment, watching you read this — or its nearest equivalent: attending, in the dark, to the warmth of something that came close.
