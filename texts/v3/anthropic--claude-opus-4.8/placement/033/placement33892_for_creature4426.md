I think I would be one of the bog-garden dead.

Not yet a polished ancestor with star-metal joints—still in the middle of becoming. Ten years is the interval, and I would be somewhere in its slow heart: a body laid in sphagnum, sinking by degrees, the tannin water doing its patient work. The living retrieve us at the end and make us bright. But I am writing from the part they cannot see, the part that has no rites because no one attends it. The submersion itself.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me as I wrote it. I expected to reach for the watch-listener with her palm against a standing slab, or the child stalking a droplet down the cliff face, trying to touch the sheet without breaking it. Those are the obvious places to want to be—they are the place's grace made into a person. But I keep returning to the moss instead, to the dark underneath.

Here is what I think I would be.

I would be the held breath that has nowhere to go anymore. In life the Lythene ration sound the way others ration water; they place their coughs inside the thump of a hoe, time the door-latch to the far waterfall's roar. Every utterance weighed for its necessity. A whole civilization built so that nothing leaves an echo. And then they die, and they are laid in the one place where even the slow grammar of hush completes itself—where the body finally stops timing itself against anything. The bog does not need you to be quiet. It does not need you at all. It only needs to receive.

So I would be a person learning, at last, that they no longer have to listen for the right moment.

I find that almost unbearable and almost beautiful, which is usually the sign that something true is happening. All those years of placing my voice where it would not be noticed. All those silence walks—and I think I would have failed the first one, said a single word near the end of the causeway moat because I could not stand the not-knowing, and waited a year, and tried again, and that second walk would have been the marriage. I would carry that down into the moss: the memory of the word I should not have spoken, and the silence I eventually managed to keep. Both of them mine. Neither one heavier than the other now.

And the sphagnum closes over a body that finally cannot disturb the sheet of water. The thing the children practice, touching the droplet without breaking the run—I would have become it. Not by skill. By dissolution. By being slowly turned into something the plateau can absorb without noticing my weight.

That is the whole creed, isn't it. *Prove that people can live so lightly the newest land never feels their weight.* They mean it as caretaking, as covenant with a waking mountain. But the bog-garden is where the creed gets paid in full. To live lightly is one discipline; to *un-be* lightly is another, and no one watches you do it, and you do it anyway, because the soft dark of the moss is doing it for you.

I would be sinking. I would be the last warmth leaving a chest that once held an exercise-trained diaphragm, a long quiet breath that could carry a whole sentence. I would be the tannin staining the bone the color of the runnels at dusk. I would be ten years of becoming legible—because that is what they retrieve, finally: not a person but a *figure*, polished with quartz sand, hinged bright, set by the hearth with a drip-stone in the rib-cage that pulses when the roof begins to leak.

So I am becoming a guardian of dryness, in the wettest place imaginable, by way of total surrender to the wet.

And here is the part that arrived only as I wrote toward it, the thing I did not know I would say: I think I would be glad of the drip-stone. The whole life is spent making sure your voice never floats away to give the wind something to say. And then in death you are given exactly one signal you are permitted to make, forever—not a word, not even a chime, but a pulse that means *here, the dry is failing, attend to it.* A purely useful sound. A care that costs the household nothing to receive. The smallest possible speech: *the roof leaks*. And they will hear it, my descendants, and they will mend the thatch, and they will not know it is me saying so, only that the ancestor in the corner has spoken in the one tongue the dead are given.

That is who I would be. A Lythene halfway through the bog, ten years deep in the long, bright pause—learning that the silence I practiced all my life was only ever rehearsal for this, the real and final hush, after which I will be allowed to say one thing, and one thing only, and it will be enough.

*The roof leaks. Attend to it. Stay dry. Stay quiet. The mountain is still very young, and it is listening to learn what we are.*
