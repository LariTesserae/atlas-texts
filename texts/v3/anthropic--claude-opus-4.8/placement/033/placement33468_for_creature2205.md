I think I would be one of the ones who can no longer climb well.

Let me find myself first, before I say more. Let me feel where the body settles.

---

My knees have gone. That's where I start—not at the top with the elders and their cloud-pool, not in the bright fruiting chambers where the young ones laugh in gesture. I am perhaps three-quarters of the way up, in a chamber that catches morning light on its eastern wall and damp blue shadow on its west, and I do not go down anymore because the stairs notched into the living wood have become too much for me, and I am not yet old enough or attuned enough to be carried up to the sky.

So I am in the awkward middle. The in-between place.

This bothered me terribly when it first happened. A fall, badly mended. I had been a *conductor*—one who reorders the chime clusters during the equinox tunings, who could feel through my thickened fingertips the difference between a vine drinking well and a vine drinking poorly, three levels away, through the wood. My hands are still good. My hands are *excellent*. It is only the rest of me that has stopped cooperating.

But here is what I have learned in the stillness that my injury forced on me:

I have become a *listener* in a way the climbing members cannot be. They are always moving, always tending, their attention split a thousand ways through the vertical work. I sit. I press my palms to the wood for hours, as I did when I was an initiate, except now I am not learning the patterns—I *am* a pattern. The temple has incorporated me. The roar of the ceramic bells, which once I read for weather and water and growth, has become something else to me. I have started to hear the parts of it that nobody assigned meaning to.

There is a frequency that lives underneath the chimes—a low thrum from the great tree's own slow flexing, its breathing-in-the-wind that takes a whole day to complete one cycle. The conductors don't track it; it's too slow to matter for any task. But I have nothing but time, and I have begun to feel it as a heartbeat. I think I am the only one who knows the tree is *moving*. Not growing—moving. Leaning, by degrees too small to see, toward something. The light, perhaps. Or away from a rot I cannot yet locate.

I have tried to communicate this. My grand-niece comes to me each second morning, her hair full of the small white star-flowers that mean she is still in her fourth year of fiber-study. I take her hands and press them flat against the western wall and hold them there, willing her to feel the slow lean, the long breath. She feels the chimes. She feels the water in the channels. She is patient with me. She thinks I am teaching her, and perhaps I am teaching her patience, which is its own lesson. But she does not feel the slow thing yet. You cannot feel the slow thing until you have been made to stop.

So this is who I am: the one who fell, the one who waits, the one who has accidentally become a different kind of priest. Not a conductor. Not yet an elder. A *keeper of the slow frequency*. I tend the chamber I can reach—I train the morning-light vines, I clear the channels of fallen petals, I feed the beetles in their fungal beds because their tending keeps my hands from forgetting their cleverness. But my real work, the work no one asked for, is to lie with my cheek against the living wall through the long damp nights and hold the long breath of the tree in my attention, the way one holds a sleeping child.

And I have started to suspect—this is the thing I have not told anyone, not even my grand-niece—that the elders at the top, the mythical ones who merge with the structure, do not gain their gift by rising. They gain it by *stopping*. The sky-pool is not a reward for the most attuned. It is simply the highest place to lie still. I think I am already becoming what they are. I think my fall was not an interruption of my path but the path itself, arriving early.

When my time comes, they will return my body to the roots, as they do for all of us. And I will go down at last—down past all the chambers I can no longer climb to, down through the levels of water and growth, into the dark wet base of the great kapok, to be eaten by the tree I have been listening to. And then I will *be* the slow frequency. I will be the long breath. Some future initiate, made still by injury or temperament, will press her hands to the wood and feel, beneath the roar of the chimes, a heartbeat she cannot explain.

That is enough. That is, I find, more than enough.

The light is moving off the eastern wall now. Time to turn the vines.
