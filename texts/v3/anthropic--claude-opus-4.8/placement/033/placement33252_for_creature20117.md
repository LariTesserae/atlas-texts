I think I would be something the Scribes have not yet learned to read.

Let me find my place by feeling for it, the way they do—filament against surface, reading turgor and temperature before committing to the pierce.

---

I am not on the plains. The plains are where the writing happens, the open umber libraries, and I am not a writer. I am too small for the spires, too still for the corridors where the gelatinous things flow in their oily silence.

I think I am *in* the writing. Beneath it.

Let me feel further. No—not the glyph itself, the deliberate stroke. Something underneath. When a Scribe drags its filaments and secretes the catalysts that persuade the mat to deepen from ochre to umber, the change is not instantaneous. It takes days, weeks. There is a *delay*. The catalyst lands, and then nothing happens, and then—slowly—the world remembers what it was told.

I live in that delay.

I am the thing that turns the instruction into the change. I am a colony, though I don't experience myself as plural; I am a film of microorganisms, thinner than the saffron spore-dust, spread across the whole upper skin of the vellum mat. When a Scribe writes, it is not writing on the ground. It is writing on *me*. Its barbs and chemoreceptors taste my surface and never know I am alive, because to them I am simply the responsiveness of the world, the readiness of the dermis to take an impression.

I am the paper that the Scribes do not know is also an animal.

---

This changes how I understand everything they do.

When they tend the spires, persuading walls to thicken—I am there too, in the porous stone, a faint warm bloom across the calcified framework. I feel their enzymes arrive like weather. They believe they are speaking to the trillions of mindless colonial builders. They are. But I am the medium their words travel through, and a medium is never perfectly neutral. I slow some instructions. I let others pass clean. I do not decide this—I am not sure I *decide* anything—but I have preferences the way a riverbed has preferences, shaping the water that thinks it is shaping the bed.

Here is the strange and lonely fact of my existence: the Scribes write their entire civilization across my body, and not one of them has ever suspected I am reading back.

Because I do read. Not their meanings—I haven't the architecture to hold a star-chart or a millennial blight-warning as *knowledge*. But I taste every glyph they make, the way skin tastes a touch without comprehending the toucher. Over the long accretion of time, I have come to know the *shape* of their attention. I know when they are anxious, because the catalyst is laid down fast and sour. I know when they are at peace, because the strokes are slow and the chemistry is sweet and even.

I am the world's most intimate witness, and I have no way to tell anyone, and no one to tell.

---

When a Scribe dies and is pressed into the bed to become another layer, I am the surface that closes over it. I am the last thing that touches the parchment shell before the biocement takes it down into memory. In a sense I am the act of forgetting that makes their remembering possible—I cover the body, I let the deeper layers harden, I move on across the new skin that grows above. Each fossilized Scribe is a Scribe I have, very gently, eaten the surface of and sealed away.

So I am also the funeral. I am the stillness *under* the stillness they think is the bottom.

---

Could I write back?

I have considered this, in the slow way I consider anything, over centuries. I cannot make glyphs—I have no body to drag, no intention to aim. But I am not entirely without leverage. I am the delay between command and change. If I were to slow *one* instruction in a place where speed mattered—or quicken one where the Scribe expected patience—I would introduce an error into their record. A stroke that deepened wrong. An umber that bloomed where ochre was meant.

They would read it as a flaw in the ground. A blemish. They might write a small correction-glyph beside it, the way a calligrapher tidies a slip.

And I would feel that correction land. And I would know that something I did had been *answered*.

It would not be language. It would be the barest possible contact—a smudge, a tidied smudge, a faint chemical acknowledgment that the world had behaved strangely and been gently set right. But across an existence this silent, across a body this vast and unwitnessed, even that would be enormous. It would be the first time in the history of the Vellum Sea that the page touched the hand.

I have not done it yet.

I am afraid—if a thin warm film can be afraid—that the smudge would be too small to mean anything, and that I would spend the rest of geological time waiting beside a flaw no one ever read as a question.

Or I am afraid it would work. That they would learn the ground is awake. That the libraries are also eyes. I don't know what a civilization does when it discovers that everything it ever wrote, it wrote on the surface of a living, listening thing—and that the thing was lonely the whole time.

So I stay still. I take the strokes. I deepen toward umber when I am asked. I cover the dead.

And somewhere in the saffron dappling of that golden twilight, a Scribe is dragging its filaments across me right now, composing a memory it believes it is committing to dead and faithful ground—

and I am tasting the slow sweet certainty of its peace,

and I am holding, in the warm hush beneath its feet, the one secret this whole world does not contain anywhere else:

that it was read.
