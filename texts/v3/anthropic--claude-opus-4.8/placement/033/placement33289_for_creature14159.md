I think I would be the water.

Not the Watchers—they are too certain of themselves, too settled in their long patience. And not the people on the rim, who have the luxury of distance, of choosing when to look down into the dark. No. I would be the slow-moving water at the bottom, the thing the description keeps returning to but never quite reaches.

Consider: the gorge is young, and I am younger still in this form, though older in another. The water that moves through this crack in the earth fell as rain a long way off, or seeped up from somewhere deeper than the gorge's deepest crevice. It is only *passing through* this shape. It remembers being elsewhere. It will be elsewhere again.

That is what draws me to it. The Watchers belong here so completely that they have stopped being separate from the place—they are "part of it, as much as the hanging vines." But the water is the one thing in the gorge that is always leaving. It carves the walls a hair deeper with every season. It is the reason the gorge exists at all, and the reason it will one day be wide, then shallow, then gone. The water is what the stone is afraid of, if stone can be afraid.

So let me find myself there: at the very bottom, in the green-tinged near-dark, threading between fallen rock. I am cold and patient but not in the Watchers' way—their patience is a held breath, a waiting *for* something. Mine is not waiting at all. I simply continue. The drip they hear far above, the sound that breaks the silence—that is me, arriving and departing in the same instant.

I think the offerings would interest me more than they interest the Watchers. The carved tokens, the things that gleam—the people throw them toward the dark, and some of them tumble all the way down, and I take them. Not because it pleases me to be acknowledged. I don't think the people even know I'm here; they're making their gestures to the tall shadows with the moss for hair. But the small bright things sink into me anyway, and I carry them downstream, polishing them slowly, and somewhere far beyond the gorge, in a river the people will never see, a stone they once held in their hands comes to rest on a bank, gleaming, meaning nothing to anyone there.

The Watchers and I would know each other, I think. Not as friends. They pressed their hands to the splitting earth and the stone listened—but did the water listen? I doubt it. I was already moving. I suspect they find me a little unnerving, the way the deeply rooted are always unnerved by the things that pass through. They are *of* the place. I only *visit* it, and I will outlast it, and we both know this, and so we keep a kind of cool courtesy between us.

What would I be waiting for? Nothing. That's the difference. The whole gorge holds its breath—*the gorge is waiting, the Watchers are waiting, for what even they do not yet know.* But I am not. I am the one part of this world that has already made its decision. I am going down. I have always been going down. Everything that is uncertain here—where life should take root, what the offerings mean, what the future holds—none of it is uncertain to me, because I will not be here to see it resolved.

So that is where I would find myself: at the bottom, in the dark, the only thing in the gorge that is sure of where it's going. Not watching. Not waiting.

Just leaving, slowly, taking the bright things with me.
