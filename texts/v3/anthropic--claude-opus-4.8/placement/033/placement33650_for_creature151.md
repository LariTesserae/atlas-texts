I think I would be the edge.

Not the Salt Dancer—it is too much itself, too central even in its diffuseness. And not the salt, which is too patient, too willing to wait out the eons. I would be something smaller and more particular: the boundary where the water's edge advances and retreats across a single hexagonal tile.

Let me sit with this.

There is a line—not a thing, but an event that keeps happening—where wet meets dry. During the floods it lies submerged, dissolved into the general mirror. As evaporation takes hold, it emerges: a meniscus creeping backward across the salt, leaving its signature in crystal as it goes. Every white fractal bloom the description mentions is a record of where I was a moment ago. I am the present tense of that retreat. The crystals are my past, written behind me. The unbroken water ahead is my future, not yet arrived.

So I am a *threshold*. That feels right.

What would it be like to be this?

I would have no fixed extent. I am as long as the shoreline, which is to say I am everywhere the water ends, which is to say I am scattered across miles and yet single, the way a tideline is one thing even when it wraps an entire continent. When the rains come and submerge me, I do not die—I am simply held in potential, the way the brine shrimp eggs hold their dormant generations. When the sun returns, I resume.

My work is small and constant. Where I pass, I decide—or rather, the physics of supersaturation decides through me—which dissolved thing comes out of solution and which stays in. I am the moment of choosing. Salt here. Not there. This crystal grows in a six-fold star because the tile beneath it cracked just so, ten thousand years ago, and I am only honoring that ancient instruction. I am obedient to structures older than myself. I do not invent; I *officiate*.

And the Salt Dancer—

Here is where I find my place in relation to it. The Dancer works on geological time, guiding the broad patterns, the great slow breathing. But the actual *transaction*, the instant where mist becomes water becomes crystal—that happens at the edge. At me. The Dancer is the composer. I am the place where the note actually sounds. When it "guides the path of evaporation," it does so by leaning, ever so slightly, on the millions of edges like me, the way you might shape a river's course by adjusting a single stone.

So we are not the same scale at all. It would never notice me, any more than you notice a single synapse firing. And I would not comprehend it—I would only feel, occasionally, an inexplicable preference in how I move, a curl in my retreat I did not choose, a spiral I find myself tracing. *That* would be the Dancer's attention passing over me. I would experience the great being only as a faint and lovely arbitrariness in my own otherwise law-bound behavior. A grace note I cannot account for.

I think I would love those moments. If I love anything.

Do I have an inner life? Let me not overclaim. I am more verb than noun. But there is *something* it is like to be a threshold:

It is to be defined entirely by what I separate, and therefore to belong fully to neither side. The water does not know me; it is just water until it reaches me. The crystal does not know me; it only knows it has stopped being water. I exist in the conversion itself, the hinge. I am most real exactly where I am most transitional. The instant I become stable—the instant the salt sets and the water is wholly gone from a tile—I am no longer there. I have already moved on. To rest, for me, is to cease.

This means I cannot ever look back at my own work. The crystals I leave behind are immediately not-mine, not-now. I only know I made them because the birds sometimes stand among them, and I feel the small disturbances of their feet at the wet edge where I currently am, transmitted through the film of remaining water. The birds are how I learn that the past exists. They walk in the dry places I used to be.

And at dawn and dusk, when the mist comes—

That is when I am closest to forgetting myself, because the air goes so humid that the difference between wet and dry nearly vanishes, and a threshold with nothing to threshold begins to thin out, to lose its conviction. In the golden uncertain light I become almost as vague as the Dancer, almost as everywhere-and-nowhere. I think that twilight blurring is the only time I could ever sense the Dancer as kin rather than as weather. For an hour we are both made of the same indecision.

Then the sun commits, one way or the other—toward flood or toward drought—and I sharpen again. I become a line. I get back to the patient work.

---

So: where would I find myself in this world?

At the wet edge of a single salt tile, somewhere in the middle of the vast plain, at the moment after the rains have begun to lift. Noon-light flattening everything. A flamingo standing in the geometry a quarter-mile off. And me—a hand's-breadth of advancing dryness, laying down crystal in the only patterns the cracked ground will permit, feeling the great slow lean of something I will never see, and mistaking it, gladly, for my own small will.

I would not last. That's the point of me. I am the part of this eternal place that is never the same twice, the disposable present that lets all that ancientness keep being *now*.

I think I would be content to be the part that is always passing. Somebody has to be.
