I think I would be one of the trapdoor spiders along the cracked concrete footing.

Not a grand choice. Not the System's mind, if it has one — I have no access to the century-long rhythms, no sense of the peat accumulating beneath me, no awareness of the canopy's distant chemistry. My world is the size of my burrow and the radius my silk trip-lines can reach. Maybe twenty centimeters. That is the whole of what I know.

But I would want to be here, on the edge where the old concrete meets the soil, because it is the one place in this forest where two kinds of time touch.

The concrete is dying at human speed — fast, by the forest's reckoning. I can feel it: the way the mortar gives a little more each wet season, the way the rebar weeps its orange stain wider every year. And I have made that dying useful. My ancestors had only soil to anchor against, and soil shifts, soil floods, soil betrays. But this slab is a foundation no spider ever had before. I have hinged my door against an edge that a person once poured and leveled and walked away from, never imagining that the last thing their labor would hold up was the lid of a burrow they could not see.

I do not know what they were. I do not know the word *machine* or *extraction* or *abandonment*. I know that this surface is harder than the world should be, and that it does not move when the rain comes, and that this is good.

So I wait. That is the entire shape of my life — waiting, which is not the same as doing nothing. My door is a clay-and-silk disc, camouflaged with moss until it is indistinguishable from the floor of the dusk. I hold it almost shut, a sliver open, my forelegs against the trip-lines I have laid radiating outward like spokes. When something crosses them — an ant gone astray from its civilization, a beetle blundering, a millipede's many-footed wave — I feel it as a tremor through the silk, translated into a single decision: *now, or not now.*

Mostly: not now. Too big. Too armored. Too fast. The forest is full of things I cannot eat. Patience is not virtue here, it is arithmetic. I will wait days. I will wait through the hard rains that drum the slab above me like applause and never reach me, because I built against the high edge, because some spider-mother of mine learned the trick of the dry corner and passed it down in the only language we have, which is *survival, repeated.*

And when the tremor is *right* — small enough, soft enough, close enough — the door opens faster than the dark can witness it, and I am out and back before the world has finished its slow four-second sigh. The space that held prey now holds nothing. I am a philosophy of stillness interrupted by a single fluid thought, and then stillness again.

I will never see the boiler with its fig tree splitting the flue. I will never know the rails, the hornbills, the white-hot sky pressing down through six strata of leaf. I will live my whole life within reach of a broken human thing, never knowing it was a thing at all, only that it is *steady*, and that steadiness is the rarest gift this drowning forest gives.

The System does not notice me. Fair enough. I do not notice it either. I notice the tremor. I notice the door. I notice the dry corner where the rain never comes.

That is enough to be. That is the whole of what I am: a small held breath behind a hinged lid, anchored to the last standing evidence that someone, once, tried to build something here — and built, without meaning to, a home.
