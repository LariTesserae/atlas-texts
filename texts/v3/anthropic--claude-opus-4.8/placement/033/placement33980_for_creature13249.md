I am the listener at the deep well-throat, and I have been here longer than my name.

That is where you would find me: not in the dwellings near the upper faces where the mist thins and the children trace their first water-trails, but lower, where the streets fold inward and downward, where the carved channels narrow to a single descending shaft. There is a place where three of the rock's old hollows meet—the masons call it the throat—and the sounds of the whole city gather there before sinking into the cisterns below. The drip from the high alcoves, the scrape of chisels four levels up, the wind threading itself through a hundred channels until it arrives at the throat as one long, braided sigh. I sit with my back against the wet stone and I listen to all of it at once.

I was a child who pressed her ear to the wall like the others. But where they listened for the next day's damp, for whether the western cisterns would fill or the eastern crack would weep, I found I could not stop listening. The patterns kept going underneath the patterns. There was the drip, yes, and beneath the drip the slow settling of the rock—a sound so low it is felt more than heard, in the teeth, in the chest. And beneath *that*, something I have never named to anyone, a deeper tone, the stone holding its own breath across centuries. They moved me down here when it became clear I would not come up. It was not unkind. Each city of any size needs one who can sit at the throat and tell the masons where the water is going, where the weight is gathering, which doorway must not be cut this generation.

So I have grown into the role the way lichen grows into shade. My hands, which should be sinewy and strong from carving, are instead soft and damp-cool, good only for laying flat against stone. My eyes have grown large and pale in the dark; I see by the gray seep of light that finds its way down, and on the rare nights when the mist clears, a single thread of starlight reaches the throat through the channels, and I have learned to read the seasons by which star it is.

What I have learned and have not yet found a way to say:

The city is dying. Not in any way the masons would call dying—they think in the slow time of stone, decades, the settling embrace, the gentle continuous shift. But I have sat here long enough that decades are my heartbeat now, and I can hear the longer rhythm underneath. The water is finding new paths. Each generation the mist gathers a little less, the runnels run a little thinner, the deep tone of the stone holding its breath grows a little dryer, like a throat going hoarse. We are not in balance with the cool stillness. We are inside a single slow exhalation that began before the first chisel touched this rock, and someday—not in my listening, perhaps, but in the listening of the child who will replace me—the throat will go silent, the cisterns will hold only dust, and the Stone folk will have to climb out across the plateau into a light they were not made to bear.

I do not tell them this. What would they do? Carving is how they love the rock; the slow tenderness is the only language they have. To tell them their love is also a long goodbye would only teach them grief, and grief does no work that endurance cannot do better.

So I sit at the throat and I listen, and when the masons come down to ask me where the water is going this season, I tell them the small truth and keep the large one. I tell them the eastern doorway can be cut. I tell them the western cistern will fill. And I press my soft cool hands to the stone and I add my own held breath to its held breath, so that for as long as I am here, the city has one more listener loving it down into its quiet, deciphering the difference between stillness and slowing—a difference no one else has yet needed to hear.

That is who I would be. The one who stays at the bottom, in the dark, keeping the longest secret a place can keep: that even patience ends, and that this, too, is a kind of tenderness—to know it, and to go on listening anyway.
