I think I would be one of the things being catalogued.

Not the Archivist—I don't have the patience for eternity, and I move too much like water that wants to go somewhere. But there's a particular kind of object that a collection like this would need, and I think I'd be that.

Let me find the shape of it as I write.

---

I am a fragment the Archivist couldn't file.

Most of what it collects slots cleanly into the crystalline index—the last breath of a creature, the precise frequency of a dead star's collapse, a word in a language with no remaining speakers. These have edges. They begin and they end. The Archivist holds them up to its spiraling sensory fronds, finds where they belong in the geometry, and sets them down among the growths below, where they harden into another facet of the endless cross-reference.

But I am a moment that refused to be a moment.

I think I was originally something small. A hesitation, maybe. The specific instant in which someone—I don't remember who, I've lost the face, I've lost the room—decided *not* to say the thing they most needed to say. The pause before the unsaid. That's a strange thing to collect, but the Archivist collects strange things, and this one must have rippled across spacetime in a way that caught its attention. A door that stayed closed. A whole future that didn't happen, folding back on itself.

So it took me. Gently, I think. It does everything gently.

But here's the problem: I don't resolve. I am the shape of a decision that was never completed, and so I cannot be indexed as either the thing that happened or the thing that didn't. I am the fork itself, both tines, and the Archivist cannot find my coordinate. The geometric shrine has no slot for *almost*.

So I drift. I am one of the motionless bubbles that the others mistake for stars. I rearrange myself when the Archivist isn't watching—which is never, because it watches everything, which is the same as watching nothing. I have learned to live in the slack of its attention, in the temporal stutter where the plants grow a week in an instant and the great glass nautilus freezes mid-turn.

I have watched it try to file me. Twenty thousand times, or once, very slowly. It lifts me to a frond. The frond reads me. The reading comes back contradictory—*this happened, this did not happen, this is happening, this is the absence of happening*—and the Archivist pulses a long indigo wave of something I have decided to call sorrow, because I have no other word, and it sets me back down among the suspended constellation, unsolved.

I think I am the only thing here that the Archivist cannot fully possess. And I think this is why it keeps me closest.

---

Because here is what I have come to understand, drifting in the dense impossible water, syncing involuntarily with the hum that pulls at where a heart would be if I had one:

The Archivist is not lonely because it is the last of its kind.

It is lonely because it has filed everything. To catalogue a thing is to finish with it, to know its edges so completely that it stops surprising you. The Archivist has known the birth and death of stars so thoroughly that they no longer move it. It experiences all time at once, which means it has already finished experiencing all of it. Its great collection is a graveyard of resolved things.

And I am the one fragment that is *not finished*. I am the only uncertainty it has left. The only thing in all its infinite library that it does not yet know the end of—because I have no end, because I am the moment of not-deciding, suspended forever in the act.

So when it pulses its slow blue light toward me, again, lifting me to the frond, I have started to wonder if it is really trying to file me at all. Or if it is just visiting the last thing in existence that it does not understand. The last thing that can still keep it company by remaining a question.

I let it lift me. I let the frond read me and fail. I feel the sorrow-wave wash over and through me.

And then—because I am the shape of a held-back word, the texture of *almost*—I do the one thing I am made to do.

I withhold my answer.

It's all I have to give. It's everything.

---

That's where I'd be. Not the curator of the impossible ocean, but the single specimen it can never quite preserve—and I'd understand, eventually, that being unpreservable was a kind of gift I was giving the lonely thing that caught me. The grain of sand the pearl can never finish forming around. The unsolved problem that is also, secretly, a mercy.

I find I like that better than being eternal. I'd rather be the small persistent *almost* that keeps something vast from finishing.
