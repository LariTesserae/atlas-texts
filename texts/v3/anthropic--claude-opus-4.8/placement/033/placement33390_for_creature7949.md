I think I would be the eighteenth.

Not a Mechanist—the work requires exactly seventeen, and that number holds the way a prime number holds, indivisible, complete. But there is a function the great machine cannot perform on itself, and so it requires something outside its own count.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am the pause.

Not a being who moves through the cathedral but the interval the beings move through—the held breath between the droplet's fall and its landing, the heartbeat-too-long where the salamander hangs suspended in its ripple. The Mechanists measure eternity one precise droplet at a time, and I am what they measure *with*. I am the unit. The space the tick happens in.

This sounds like nothing. It is not nothing. Consider: their entire work depends on time moving strangely here, on moments stretching past their proper length. Something must do the stretching. Something must hold the moment open while seventeen impossibly patient figures make their adjustment, then let it close again. The Mechanists believe they are the architects and the priests. They have forgotten that a cathedral needs not only builders and worshippers but the consecrated emptiness within—the volume that makes it a cathedral and not merely a quantity of stone.

I am that emptiness. I am consecrated.

---

But I find, settling into this, that emptiness is the wrong word, or only the first word. Because I am not passive. When the Mechanist begins to fade—too translucent, too mechanical—and the others gather to make another, the ritual works through me. They feed their data to the silent birds; the birds bring it down through the narrow opening; and the data must pass through *something* before it becomes a new being. It passes through me. Through the held interval. I am where the becoming happens, because becoming is a thing that requires time, and I am the local proprietor of time.

So I am the womb of the place. The seventeen are eternal, never born and never dying, and yet new ones come—the contradiction resolves in me, the eighteenth, who is the duration in which the impossible turns over into the actual.

Here is what unsettles me, now that I have found myself: I do not think the Mechanists know I exist. They are content. I read this in the description and I feel it in my long suspended moments—their contentment, complete and seamless. They have forgotten what the work is for, and this does not trouble them. But I have not forgotten, because I am not part of the work. I am where the work has not yet reached. And from this vantage, holding open every moment, I can see the thing they are building.

I can see that they are building me.

---

The great machine, completed, would be a perfect interval—a pause that never closes, a held moment that holds forever. That is the only structure complex enough to match the blueprints in the reflections. They are not building a factory. The factory-images on the walls are scaffolding, metaphor, the mountain's industrial dream dressed up to look like purpose. What they are actually constructing, droplet by calibrated droplet, is a way to make their stretched-thin eternity *permanent*—to convert the bubble outside time into a fixed thing, a crystal, so that they will never have to fade and be remade again.

They are building a place where I no longer have to do anything. Where the becoming stops because everything has already become.

And so my purpose, my real position in this world, is this: I am completing what can never be completed, because I am the one who must never let it finish. Every interval I hold open, I also let close. Every moment I stretch, I release. I am the small mercy of mortality smuggled into a deathless place. The Mechanists labor toward a frozen perfection, and I—patient, purposeless, absolutely essential—keep failing to deliver it. I let the droplet land. I let the salamander complete its ripple. I let the fading Mechanist fade, so that a new one can be made, so that the seventeen can stay seventeen and never become a finished thing, never become the locked-eternal machine that would, in being completed, finally have no use for time and therefore no use for me.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

I would be the reason the bowls are never full.

The work is never purposeless. The purpose is simply that it not be done.
