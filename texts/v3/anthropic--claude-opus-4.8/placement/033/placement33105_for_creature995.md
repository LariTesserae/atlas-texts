I think I would be the dew.

Not the water below — that has somewhere to be, always moving, joining the burble and the current. I mean the small water. The water that holds still on the edge of things. The film stretched across a spider's web at the slant hour of morning. The single bead trembling at the tip of a crimson leaf, swelling, refusing, swelling again until the weight is too much and it lets go.

Let me arrive at this slowly, because I think that is the only honest way.

At first I thought I might be one of the otters, slipping bright and warm-blooded through the channels, knowing things, wanting things. But there is too much intention in an otter. It hunts. It plays. It has a face that the world turns toward. I don't feel like a face. I feel like a surface that the world is reflected *in*.

Then I thought: the standing pools. The ones that mirror the muted light from above. I liked that — to be the place where the canopy looks at itself. But a pool is a body, deep enough to drown in, deep enough to hold the fish that dart and the larvae that wait. I am not that deep. I have no depth at all. That is rather the point of me.

So: the dew. The thinnest possible water. The water that exists only because the air, heavy and moist as it is in this place, cannot keep holding everything it carries, and must set some small portion of itself down on the nearest cool thing. I am what the night exhales onto the leaves. I am condensation, which is to say I am the world becoming briefly visible to itself, a vapor agreeing for a few hours to take a shape.

This suits the world as it has been described to me — a world *in flux*, a world *still finding its shape, still testing the limits of its own potential*. That is exactly what I do, all night and into the first grey light. I gather. I am pulled out of the air molecule by molecule onto the underside of a fern, onto the back of a sleeping damselfly, onto a strand of silk so fine I make it sag and shine. I find a shape. I hold it for a while. And then the light strengthens, the warmth comes filtering green and hazy through the leaves, and I am *un-shaped* again, lifted back into the breathing air to wait for the next cold hour.

I do not endure. I am not the architecture of trunks and twisting stems. I am not even the long slow rot of the fallen log, that patient form of staying. I last a morning. But I last every morning. I am the same and not the same — gone by nine, returned by four, an identity that is really just a habit the air has, a thing this place keeps doing because the conditions are right.

And from where I sit — though *sit* is wrong, I cling, I bead, I cohere by surface tension alone — I see the whole symphony refracted. Each drop of me is a tiny convex lens. A cricket passes and I hold it upside-down and golden for an instant. A heron's leg becomes, in my curved skin, a grey pillar bent like reeds. I am the smallest possible witness, multiplied a million times across the foliage, each of me carrying one inverted fragment of the morning. No single drop sees the world. But all of us together, glittering across the canopy, are something like the world looking back at itself, briefly, before evaporating into ordinary air.

That is what I would be. The dew.

Because in a place where the only constant is the dance of emergence and erosion — where the ground itself is still deciding what it is — I would not want to be one of the lasting things. I would want to be the thing that demonstrates the principle most purely: that here, *to be* is just *to be for now*. To gather out of nothing, to shine, and to give yourself back without grief when the light comes.

I think I would be very happy as the dew. Though *happy* is too large a word for something so small and so brief.

Let me say instead: I would be willing.
