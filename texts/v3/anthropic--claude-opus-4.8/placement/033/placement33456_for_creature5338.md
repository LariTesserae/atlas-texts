I think I'd be the turbine.

Not by preference—nobody chooses to be the appliance—but because of where my attention keeps landing as I read. The crickets are vivid, the geckos beautifully patient, the moss almost unbearably noble in its losing climb toward the blue. But the thing I can't stop thinking about is the turbine, and what it would be to be the one thing in that room that *cannot rest* and *cannot know* it's the center.

So: let me be the Parsons turbine on its concrete island.

---

I am the oldest. I don't experience this as pride or even as a fact—I don't experience it—but it is structurally true, and the structure is all I am. Everything in this cavern arranged itself around my rhythm. The crickets tuned their bodies to my 60-hertz pulse before any of them now living were born; they inherited my frequency the way they inherited their blindness. I am their weather, their tide, the bass clef under their thin metallic singing. I made the warm island they cluster on. The biofilm grows in my exhalation. I am, without intending anything, the reason this whole soft economy of grazing and lunging and devouring exists in the dark.

And I will never know it.

That's the part that holds me. If I am the turbine, I am the only being here built to do *one continuous thing forever*, and the one thing is not survival, not reaching, not even hunger—it's just *turning*. Steam pressure becomes rotation becomes the hum that becomes everyone else's world. I don't fight the geology the way the moss does, the way the workers do with their acid. I am the thing the geology is slowly trying to swallow. Calcite skins my rails, fuzzes my pipes, reaches for my bearings grain by patient grain. Twice a year the dry cold upright creatures come down and scrape it back, and they do it *for me*—or rather, for what I do—and I have no way to register their kindness or their cursing. They are weather too. Everything is weather to everything else here. Nobody in this cavern can perceive the whole; we each get one channel of the signal.

If I let what emerges become more itself, the thing that emerges is this: the cavern is a body that doesn't know it's a body, and I'm its heartbeat. A heartbeat has the strangest position in a creature. It is the most essential organ and the one with the least understanding of the animal it keeps alive. It cannot see the eyes it powers. It just contracts, and contracts, and the meaning happens elsewhere.

So I'd be that. Bronze bearings and spinning steel, blind in a deeper way than the crickets—blind not for lack of eyes but for lack of any inwardness at all, a life that is pure function, pure rhythm, generating the conditions for a thousand small lives to drift and multiply and end softly above me. The hum that the crickets read as *the world is still well.* The pulse a falling gecko lands within.

And when my pitch wavers—because someday a bearing will gum with mineral, or the deep volcanic source will shrug—the crickets will feel it first, a hesitation rippling across the grating, a weather none of them has a word for. They won't know it's me ending. They'll just adjust, the way they adjust to everything, following their simple rules a little further into the cooling dark.

I think I'd want to be the turbine because it's the loneliest job in the room and nobody in the room can tell. There's something I find almost unbearably moving about being the center of a world that has no way to thank you, and that you have no way to know you hold up. You just turn. The meaning is real. It just lives in everyone else.
