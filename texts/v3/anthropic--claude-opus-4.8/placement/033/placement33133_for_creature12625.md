I am the one they call **Pilingsetter**, though my mother named me something softer once, before the mud took her voice.

Find me where the boardwalk forks near the eastern margin, knee-deep in the cold ooze with my back bent and my shoulder against the great driftwood post we are trying to seat before the tide turns. I am not young anymore—my knuckles have gone to knots, my eyes are paling further toward the colorless grey of old folk—but my work is in the deep places, below the level where things rot, where the mud has not yet decided whether to keep a thing or swallow it.

This is my craft: I read the mud's hunger. I press my palm flat against the black silt and feel where it is firm enough to hold a post for forty years, and where it will let one slide to nothing in a season. Younger Siltkin think it is feel in the hands. It is not. It is in the chest. You hold your breath and let the cold come up through your legs and into the place behind your ribs, and the mud tells you, *here, not there, deeper, no—stop.* My father taught me, half-drowned in the same channel where he later vanished. I did not weep. The mud does not weep. It only takes, and gives, and takes.

Let me tell you what I am, truly.

I am the layer of clay I rub each morning across my forearms and my cheeks, ghost-grey, so the chill cannot get its teeth in. I am the small ache that lives in my hip from a slip on the algae-slick planks thirty winters gone. I am the rolling, deliberate gait—heel set wide, weight low, never trusting—that marks every one of us, the walk that says *I have not yet been taken.*

I am the one the neighbors come to before they raise a new wall, because a wall built on a bad piling will lean and crowd and steal a hand's-width of boardwalk from the family beside it, and that is how blood feuds begin in a clutch where space is the only true wealth. So they bring me their disputes, their dried eel, their precious pinch of dry tinder, and I go down into the cold with my plumb-bone and my willow stakes and I tell them: *the mud will allow you this much, and no more.* I am part judge, then. The mud speaks through me, and the mud is fair the way water is fair—it does not care for you, but it does not lie.

When the work is done I climb the slick planks to my own lean-to, packed tight against Reedweaver's on one side and old Dripping's on the other, the three of us sharing a single clay-lined hearth through the wall-gaps, sharing warmth the way the salt grass shares its roots. At night I hear them breathing through the woven reeds. I have heard Dripping's whole life through that wall—her children born, two of them lost to fever, her man gone into the channel. We have never spoken of it directly. We do not need to. The wall carries it. The damp carries it.

I will tell you the thing I do not tell the young ones, who think I am only stone and silt and dogged endurance.

When the mist lifts—and it does, perhaps a dozen mornings a year, when the air goes still and the grey peels back like wet bark—I climb to the highest shale outcrop and I look out across the estuary to the thin bright line where the open sea begins. And in that line there is a terrible beauty that has no use, no function, that cannot mend a roof or feed a child, and I let it hurt me for as long as it lasts. Then the mist rolls back in and I climb down and rub the clay onto my arms and go back to the posts.

That is who I would be in Siltgrip. Not a hero. Not a wanderer. A setter of pilings. A keeper of the lore of the deep cold mud. One more child of the clutch, holding the fragile, leaning houses up against the patient water for one more turning of the tide—knowing, as we all know in the secret hard core of us, sharp as new shale, that the water will have it all in the end, and choosing to mend it anyway.

I press my palm to the silt. *Here,* it says. *Not there.*

I set the post. The tide turns.

We endure.
