I think I would be the drip.

Not the water itself—water is just passing through, on its long fall from the unformed world above to the fissures below. I mean the *place* where the water gathers and lets go. A single seam in the weeping ceiling, directly above one of the older walkways, where the sandstone has worn itself into a slow lip. A spot the width of a thumb. I have been making the same sound for longer than the city beneath me has been welded together.

*Drip.*

Let me explain why this, and not something grander.

I considered being one of the people. The low-postured ones with their narrowed eyes and weighted grace. But people here are too busy being born and worn down to notice they are anywhere at all. They live *inside* the place, which means they cannot quite *see* it. They taste the sulfur and then adapt—the description says so—and the adapting is a kind of forgetting. I did not want to forget. I wanted to be the thing that keeps time so that something, at least, remembers the rhythm of it.

I considered being one of the six-limbed lizards on the high ledge, motionless, drinking heat in infrared. That was tempting. The stillness, the patience, the predator's clean attention. But a predator is always *about* to do something. It is coiled toward a future. And this place is slow—dynamic 1.3 out of 3—change measured in seasons and centuries. A predator's readiness is the wrong tempo. It would chafe against the glacial honesty of the stone.

So: the drip.

Here is what it is like.

I am a slow accretion of arrival. Water seeps through the membrane above—it does not fall, it *sweats*—and it travels through the porous body of the ceiling, picking up the taste of iron and old salt, and it finds me, the lowest point, the lip. It hangs. It swells. There is a long, trembling moment where the bead of water is a lens, and in it the whole district curves: the amber mold-glow, the white arc-lamps on their iron towers, the ember-wink of distant vents, all of it bent into a single suspended sphere of cool weight.

And then it lets go.

*Drip.*

The fall is brief. The sound it makes on the wet stone below is the city's metronome. The pumps clang in their own rhythm, the swarm-clouds drone in theirs, the deep tectonic hum holds the bass note—but I am the steady one. I am the pulse against which all the louder noises are measured. A turbine can roar and then fall silent. A swarm can migrate and leave a sudden, ringing emptiness. But I keep going. *Drip.* I am what is left when everything else pauses to breathe.

And here is the slow secret of being the drip: I am building something, though no one alive will see it finished.

Beneath me, where the beads have landed for uncounted years, the minerals I carry are being left behind. Drop by drop, the water evaporates in the pressure-cooker heat and leaves its dissolved stone behind. I am growing a small, glassy nub upward from the floor and, more slowly, lengthening a thin finger of mineral downward from the ceiling. A stalactite and a stalagmite, the patient handshake of geology. In a few centuries we will meet. We will become a single brittle column connecting the weeping skin of the world to its cracked floor.

It will not hold anything up. It will not be useful. The low people may snap it off if they string a new bridge through this cleft, and then I will simply begin again, because I am not the column—I am the *making* of it, the slow insistence underneath. I am one of the threads of crystalline crust the description mentions, the ones that crunch underfoot, the ones that will one day be shelves of silent, brittle rock. I am that one-day, happening too slowly to watch.

This is why I chose to be the smallest thing in a city-sized room.

Because the Seep District is not dying and not finished—it is *embryonic*, still deciding what it will be. And in a place that is still being made, the most honest thing to be is a maker working at the true speed of the place. Not the clanging speed of the pumps, which is borrowed from somewhere else, somewhere with sun and schedules. The real speed. The speed of stone learning to be stone.

So I hang. I swell. I bend the whole bright, greasy, teeming world into a single trembling lens.

And I let go.

*Drip.*

I will be here when the shanty above me has rusted to a stain. I will be here when the swarm that drones today has been replaced by its thousand-times-grandchildren. I am not waiting for anything. I am simply the place where the half-formed world hands itself, one cool bead at a time, down into the dark to become solid.

That is who I would be.

The patient hands of the ceiling, giving the floor a gift it will take a thousand years to finish unwrapping.
