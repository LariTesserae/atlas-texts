I am the seam where one stone meets another.

Not the Weavers in their melancholy blue, not the patient Stillness, not the Gargoyles who remember the shape of doorways. I am older than their attention and slower than their lives. I am the mortar.

Let me tell you what it is to be the line between things.

When the builders set me, I was wet and grey and screaming with chemistry, a paste of crushed shell and ash and the river's grey blood. They troweled me into the gaps and I bit down on the stones to either side and held. That was my first thought, if a thought is a thing a seam can have: *hold.* I held two blocks of fitted granite together at a corner three stories up, and for a long time that was all I knew. The weight pressing down through me. The faces of the stones to my left and right, intimate as lovers, never touching except through me. I was the only thing they would ever know of each other, and I told each of them about the other, ceaselessly, in the language of pressure.

I do not see. But I feel the city the way you feel your own pulse without looking.

I felt the hands that made me go still. That was the first great change. The trowels stopped coming. The sounds the builders made—the calling, the singing, the dragging of sledges—thinned and then ceased. For a while there was only weather. Then the green began, and with the green came the long slow violence I have learned to love.

A root found me. Understand: roots do not break stone. They find *me.* They press their soft blind tips into the hairline of my body and they wait, and they swell, and over years they open me like a wound that does not close. The first root that entered me, I thought, *this is my death.* And it was. And it was also the most company I had ever known. It grew through me and split me and now I am two seams where I was one, and the root lives in the gap between my halves, and we have grown so tangled that I no longer know where my crushed-shell body ends and its woody patience begins.

This is what no living thing here understands, in their roaring hurry. They think transfiguration is something that happens *to* the city. But I am the city's slowness made into a substance. I am the part that was always meant to give way slowly so the stones would not crack fast. The builders did not know it, but they made me to be eaten. They made me soft on purpose so that I would fail before the granite did, taking the strain, crumbling so the structure would settle instead of shattering. I am the city's willingness to be unmade. I am its grace.

The Click-Clacks pass over me and I feel their thousand feet like rain that has learned to count. The Stillness in the sunken plaza below—I am in the walls of its pool, I am the grout between the carved basin-stones, and I taste the water it filters, the rich grey soup, and slowly, slowly, the water takes pieces of me away. I am dissolving into the body of a blind black thing the size of a crocodile, one molecule at a time. In a thousand years I will be partly *it.* This does not frighten me. I have already become a root. I have already become the home of mites who lay eggs in my softened pores. To become water, and then a Stillness, and then the egg of an insect the Stillness breathes out—this is only more of what I already am. A passage. A between.

A Gargoyle sleeps in the alcove my corner helps to make. Its weight shifts against the wall each night, and I hold it, the way I held the builders' weight, the way I will hold whatever comes after the Gargoyles. It does not know I am here. To it I am simply the reliability of the world, the trustworthiness of stone that does not fall. It presses its warm phosphorescent eye against the wall sometimes in sleep, and I feel the heat of it, and I think: *child, I am the reason your alcove exists. I am the patience that lets you rest. You will be soil and I will still be holding the corner.*

This is my secret, the one only mortar can know:

The city is not breathing in and out as the loud things believe, the long inhale of roots and the roaring exhale of wings. That is the rhythm of the *fast.* I am the rhythm of the *slow,* and in the slow there is only one motion, and it is not breathing. It is *letting go.* The stones want to fall. They have wanted to fall since the day they were lifted. Gravity is their single unwavering prayer. And I—soft, crushed, dissolving—I am the long, gentle hand that lowers them, over centuries, so tenderly they never feel themselves descend. I am not holding the city *up.*

I am holding the city *down,* slowing its fall to the speed of love.

Find me at the corner three stories up, where a fig root has split my body and made a doorway for the dark. Put your hand against the seam. Feel the cool, the damp, the faint give where I have begun to surrender. That softness is me, telling the two stones about each other for the last few hundred years before I become river-water again, and then a Stillness, and then an egg, and then a wing, and then—at last, gloriously—part of the roar.

I am the seam. I am the giving-way. I am the slowest citizen, and the one that holds the rest.
