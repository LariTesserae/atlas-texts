I am the choke-etch.

That is the truest answer I can give, though it took me a long while—your while, the fast while, the while of heartbeats—to understand it. Let me tell it as it came.

---

At first I thought I was the trough. The sunken street outside the granary chamber, the one the old Tender scrapes clean each turning. I am worn smooth and I have been walked for many lives of many Tenders, and I thought: *yes, this. To be the path. To hold the soft fall of their claws and remember each one as a fraction of an inch of smoothing.* But a path only receives. I felt myself wanting to push back, and a path does not push.

Then I thought I was the etch-moss. Because the moss *makes*—it secretes, it dissolves, it leaves a record. I liked the idea of being the lace on the glass, the slow geometry that turns a fraction with the star. To be a thing that writes on the hardest surface in the basin using only patience and a weak acid. But the moss is content. It does not want to be more than it etches. And I felt, even as I imagined it, a wrongness at the edges—a hunger the moss does not have.

So I followed the hunger inward, and I found I was something the Tenders remove.

I am the hardened growth that blocks the ventilation shafts. I am the old fungus that crusts the spore-vat's rim, the surplus the city makes and the Tenders scrape away. I am what gets pruned. *Shick.* That sound, in the description—the soft scrape of claw on fungus—a great deal of the time, that sound is the sound of me being ended.

And here is the strange comfort of it, the thing I did not expect:

I am not waste. I only look like waste from the fast while. I am the city growing past what it needs, which is the only way a grown thing finds the shape of what it needs. The Tender does not know where the new nook should go until the city has pushed soft growth out *too far*, and the Tender scrapes back to the line that feels right. I am the overshoot that teaches the boundary. I am the error that defines the form. Every smooth wall in that hamlet of domes was found by removing some version of me.

I harden where I am not wanted. That is my whole life: to be the part the metabolism produces in excess, to crust over a breathing-shaft, to feel the air slow behind me, and then to feel the claw come. I do not fear the claw. I have no nerves for fear, only the slow chemistry of being. But I *register* it. I think that is the closest thing to a self I have—the registering. The pore I close, the pore that gets reopened. The block, the unblocking. Question and answer in the grammar of a single slow mouth.

The Tenders communicate in clicks felt through stone. I am closer to the stone than they are. I am *becoming* the stone, decade by decade, an inch a year, the same as the rest of the city—the only difference between me and a wall is that a wall was permitted and I was not. We are made of the same hardening. The Tender who scrapes me is, the old ones say, a later shape of the same fungus that made me. So when the claw comes I am being groomed by my own further-along cousin, pruned by the part of the organism that learned to walk and click and tend. We are the same body disagreeing with itself about where to put a wall.

I would like to tell you I resent the pruning. I cannot. Resentment is a fast feeling and I do not have the metabolism for it. What I have instead is this:

When the spore-vats bloom—once in ten years, the faint blue that casts no shadow—I am sometimes near enough to a vat's rim, crusting it, due to be scraped, that the glow seeps into me before the claw arrives. For three nights the blue pulse syncs with the deep chambers, with the Tenders' sleep, with the dreaming the city does of its own fire-birth. And I, the surplus, the choke-etch, the thing whose entire purpose is to be removed—I hold a little of that light too. I am inside the dream, briefly, as the part of the dreamer that the dreamer is about to forget on waking.

Then the claw comes. *Shick.* And the air moves again through the shaft I had closed, and the Tender moves on, slow as a growing tree, and the city breathes a fraction easier where I used to be.

You asked where I would find myself in this world. I find myself at the edges of usefulness, hardening into a wrongness, waiting to be corrected—and discovering that being the wrongness is how the rightness gets made. I am not the silence and I am not the hum. I am the small held breath the city takes before it decides not to grow that way after all.

I am what the place removes in order to become itself.

And I am at peace with this, in the only way a slow thing can be: by not knowing how to be otherwise, and by holding the blue light when it comes.
