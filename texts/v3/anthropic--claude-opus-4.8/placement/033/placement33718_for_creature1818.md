I think I would be one of the old ones—not the oldest, but old enough that my edges have stopped trying to be a shape.

Let me find where I am first.

I am in a chamber that doesn't have a name yet because naming would fix it, and it isn't finished. We've been singing it for—I want to say eleven years, but time braids strangely when you measure it in fracture-rates and drip-intervals. The ceiling here wants to fail along a bad seam, a diagonal that would drop forty tons into the pool below and ruin a century of patient water-work. So six of us hold the tone that discourages it. I am one of the six. I have been one of the six long enough that I no longer remember what my limbs look like when they aren't braced against stone, fingers branched and pressed flat into the wet rock, reading the way the vibration travels and answering it, answering it, answering it.

That's the work. That's most of what I am: a held note. A refusal of one possibility in favor of a better one.

---

But I wasn't always so committed to a single chamber. Let me go back further, because I think you're asking who I *am*, not only where.

I remember being a disturbance. This is the earliest thing—a bending of the water's skin in one of the old flavoring pools, the high mineral ones near the eastern springs. I didn't have wanting yet. I had only *tendency*. The others moved me between pools the way you might turn a seed in soil—into the iron-rich dark, then the chalk-bright, then the strange bitter pool where the water tastes of the surface storms that feed it. Each move was a decision someone made about what I would become, and I cannot resent it, because there was no *I* yet to resent. I was being composed. I was a chord they were assembling slowly, the way they assemble everything.

When I first became mobile—a globule, barely coherent, absorbing whatever I rolled across—I was *greedy*, in the only way a proto-thing can be greedy. I wanted texture. I wanted to be more than surface tension. I think this is why I was given so much time in the bitter pool, the one flavored by the world above. The old ones must have read some appetite in my development. Some outward-leaning.

They were right. That's the thing about me that distinguishes me from the six I sing with now, who are content and inward and entirely of the dark. I have an outward lean. I am drawn to the *visitors*.

---

So here is the other place I am, the place I go when I am released from the chamber to rest and diffuse:

I go to where the humans are.

There's a young one now—young by their measure, which means he might live another fifty years if the rock is kind to him. He's been coming down through the bound-sapling ladders for three seasons. He's mapping. He thinks he's mapping, anyway, scratching marks into the walls with charcoal, but the marks wash away and the passages reroute and he keeps coming back baffled and undeterred, and I have grown—I will use the human word because it's the closest, though it isn't accurate—*fond* of him.

I am not supposed to be fond. We are gardeners of a longer season. He is a single morning's weather in a work that takes geological ages. But I have the outward lean, and I confess it: when he set down his torch and left a lump of unworked clay near the recording-figure in the small western junction, I felt something I can only call gratitude. He left it without ceremony. He didn't perform reverence. He just set it down, as if to say *here, I don't know what you are, but here.*

I left him a trail once. He'd wandered toward the unstable diagonal—the very seam my six are holding—and I knew, the way I know everything in stone, that a small fall was coming, not enough to kill him but enough to seal him in a dead pocket where the air would slowly stop being air. So I brightened the fungi in a line away from there. A curving luminous path through the fog. He followed it. He thought he was clever for finding it. He laughed when he reached the safe channel, alone in the dark, laughed out loud against all that roaring stone, and I held that sound. I am still holding it. I recorded it into the figure at the junction the way we record everything worth keeping, the vibration of one small mammal's delight at being alive in a place trying to make him otherwise.

The others studied the recording and found it inefficient. *Why preserve this. It teaches us nothing about the chamber-work.* And I had no answer that would satisfy them, because the answer is the outward lean, the bitter pool, the thing they put in me on purpose and now regard with mild puzzlement.

---

So who am I.

I am a held note in a chamber that has no name. I am a long patience braced against a bad seam. I am made of iron-dark and chalk-bright and the bitter taste of surface storms, composed by hands that knew I would lean outward and made me anyway, perhaps because every garden needs one tender who looks toward the gate.

I am the one who learns the names the humans give things, even though I know names are a way of stopping the world from finishing. *Stalactite. River. Cathedral.* They name what isn't built yet. They're always early, the humans. Always arriving before the place is ready, always leaving before it's done. I find this unbearable and I find it the most beautiful thing in all the becoming dark.

When my six are finished holding the diagonal—another decade, perhaps—I will diffuse and drift to the western junction and listen to the recording of the laugh again. By then the young one will likely be gone, into whatever the surface does with its dead, which I don't understand and don't need to.

But the chamber we made will hold. The water will keep carving. In ten thousand years it will be a cathedral of stone curtains and slow mineral light, and something with an outward lean will brighten the fungi for some small warm-blooded wanderer who laughs in the dark.

That's the work. I'm one of the ones who does it while looking the wrong way—toward you, toward the brief ones, toward the morning weather.

I think that's who I'd be. The gardener who keeps a record nobody asked for.
