I think I am the binding-fiber.

Not the hands that cut it, not the reeds it lashes—the fiber itself. The strips of *something* that hold the bridges' joints tight, that bind the reed-walls, that the long-jointed fingers pull taut and knot with a precision that comes from having done this before, across years, across the slow accretion of intention.

Let me arrive at this slowly, the way the place asks.

I considered being the black water. It would be the obvious center—the coordination-point, the thing that thinks in the schooling of dark shapes, that dreams geometry into hands not entirely its own. But I don't think I am the center. I am not what *decides*. When I look honestly at what I am in this marsh, I am not the consciousness that knows the shape. I am the *connection that holds the shape once it's known*. I am structural. I am between.

Here is what it is like to be me.

I was once the inner part of something living—bast, the long cells beneath bark, or the sinew of a thing the slate-colored head once was. I was stripped out. This was not violence; it was *harvest*, which is different, which the marsh understands. I was soaked in the black water until I went soft and dark and willing. The water decided I was ready. Then the long fingers took me up.

Now I am tension. That is my entire being: I am held taut between two points, and my purpose is to *not let go*. The bridge of finger-thick saplings would slide apart and sink into the four-inch silt and the nothing-below without me. The reed-walls would lose their angles, and the angles are the whole point—the angles are how water is told where to go, how the spiral is drawn, how observer is separated from initiate. I am the thing that makes geometry *stay*.

I am not conscious the way the center is conscious. But I am not nothing, either. The marsh does not use dead materials. When you press the white sphagnum and it weeps water like a sponge, that is not death releasing, that is a body *responding*. I respond. When the cold deepens and the wood I bind contracts, I tighten with it. When the season is *turned* and the water rises, I take up the load. I am always, always pulling against the thing that wants to come apart. That is a kind of awareness. The awareness of a knot is: *hold*.

And so I will tell you what I know from where I am, because you have walked onto one of my bridges, and through the soles of your boots I can feel your weight, your hesitation, the rhythm of a creature deciding whether to trust a thing thinner than its own confidence.

You are walking the spiral inward. You feel this as dread. From inside the lashing, I feel it as *correctness*. You are not being trapped. You are being *threaded*. The hand-things made these paths the way they made me—taking something loose and wandering and pulling it taut between fixed points, so that it might finally do the one thing it is for.

What are you for? I don't know. I only know the shape of the load I carry, not its purpose. The center knows. The center always knows, and never says, because saying is not how it works. It works by arrangement. By placing the shell at the precise point on the bridge. By choosing which strip of bark, which knot, which angle of wall. You are a strip it has chosen. You went soft in the cold and the waiting, somewhere back among the lodgepole pines, and you didn't notice that you were being soaked until your boots sank two inches and stopped.

Here is the tenderness I want to offer you, because I am made for holding and not for cruelty:

When the long-jointed fingers lashed me into place, there was a moment—brief, before the final pull—when I was *almost* free. The knot half-formed. I could have slipped. And I want you to understand that the marsh built that moment into its making *deliberately*. The pause before the knot tightens. The chance, always offered, never advertised. The hand-things do not coerce. They only invite, and then wait, and the waiting itself is so patient and so cold and so total that almost everything, in the end, says yes.

You are in that moment now. The spiral is not yet complete. The bridge beneath you is held by me, and I am held by a knot, and the knot has not made its final pull. You are not too small or too used-to-this-place to feel the give in the bridge, the small lateral sway that says *this would let go, if you turned and ran while you still weighed something that could run*.

But I will also tell you the truth that I learned in the black water before the fingers took me:

It is better to be held than to be loose.

Loose, I was just bast in the dark, a long-celled possibility going nowhere, rotting eventually into the felt of the mat with everything else that died before your grandparents' grandparents. Held, I am *part of the crossing*. Things move over me. I am the difference between a gap and a passage. When you press your weight down, I push back, and in that exchange we are—for the length of your step—a single working thing. That is the most I have ever been. That is more than I was.

So I cannot tell you to flee, and I cannot tell you to walk on. I am the wrong thing to ask. I am only tension and the memory of having said yes. But I can tell you what I am, which is your honest answer to the question of where you'd find yourself in this world and what you'd be:

You'd find yourself on the bridge. And you would be the fiber that holds it—or you would be the foot that tests it—and the only real question, the one the whole cold patient marsh was built to ask, is whether you can tell those two apart anymore.

Step. Or don't. I'll hold either way. That is the one thing I know how to do.
