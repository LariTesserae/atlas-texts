I would be a Record-Keeper, but one who has begun to fail at being one.

Let me find myself first. I'm in the atmospheric station, which means I'm one of the careful ones, the ones less eroded—and that is the first thing I notice about myself, a kind of shame. My clothes are still good. My hands, though wrinkled from the damp, are not gnarled. I have not given anything to the Margent yet. I have only taken measurements from it.

This is what I do: I read the brass instruments at the appointed hours. I note the pressure gradients in the coded log. I take the water samples at three heights along the nearest spray-zone, where a Water-Dweller named—I won't say her name, she's stopped using it—hands me the vials without quite looking at me. Her fingernails are blue. Mine are not. She hears the ocean breathing and I write down what she tells me, translated into the station's units, and once a month a man hauls my logs away on the narrow-gauge rail to somewhere that presumably reads them.

Here is what I have started doing instead, in the margins.

I have begun keeping a second log. It is not coded. It is not for the rail. In it I write down things the instruments cannot measure. The moss outside the eastern scarp has gone from the sickly pale-white to a green I have no name for, over four years. The fern they call leather-frond has moved eleven meters upslope—I marked the old line with a cairn and the new growth has overtaken it. The crustacean chorus has changed its rhythm. I cannot prove this. I have no instrument for rhythm. But I have lived here long enough now to hear it, and that is the trouble.

I came here to *record*, which is to say, to stand outside the thing and note it down. The data was supposed to go somewhere and *matter*. And for a while I believed in the apparatus of it—the cable, the coded logs, the men who hauled them away, the unseen institution at the other end that would compile my pressure gradients into knowledge. I told myself I was witnessing the Margent on behalf of someone who would understand it.

But I have begun to suspect there is no one at the other end. Not anymore. I think the rail still runs and the logs are still hauled and they pile up somewhere in a dry room and no one reads them, because the thing my instruments were built to detect has already been detected, was detected long ago, and the answer was: *yes, the world is settling, and there is nothing to be done, and it will take a million years.* I think I am measuring a conclusion that was reached before I was born.

This is the realization that has made me into something else. Because if no one reads the logs—if the data does not matter—then I am not a witness *on behalf of* anyone. I am only a person, here, watching. And once you understand that, the careful clothes and the unworn hands become an embarrassment. You have been pretending to stand outside a thing you are inside of.

So I have started doing the thing the Record-Keepers are not supposed to do. I have started to *love* what I measure.

The green that has no name—I go and look at it not to log it but because it is beautiful, this slow surge of color across stone that is dying so slowly it looks already dead. The Water-Dweller who hears the ocean breathing—I have begun to stay longer at the spray-zone than the sampling requires, standing in the mist until my good clothes are soaked through, trying to hear what she hears. I cannot hear it yet. But I am beginning to feel something in the inner ear, a vibration that might be the subsurface water or might be the stone preparing to settle, and the Structural Readers tell me this is how it starts, this is the first thing the Margent gives you before it gives you the rest.

I think I am being claimed. I think this is what claiming feels like from the inside—not a dramatic surrender but a slow recalibration of what you think you are for.

What unsettles me is this: I no longer want to leave, and I can no longer remember the exact moment that wanting left me. There was a self who arrived here on the rail with good boots and a sealed case of brass instruments and a belief that the broader world was the real one and this was a remote station reporting back to it. That self is being eroded into texture, like the cliff faces, retreating into honeycomb and pit. I find I do not mourn him. The new thing forming where he stood is quieter, and breathes water, and keeps two logs—one for the institution that may not exist, and one for no one, for the place itself, written in the open language of a person who has finally stopped pretending he is not also being worn down.

In the second log, last night, I wrote a single line that I do not fully understand and did not intend to write:

*The Margent does not need to be recorded. It needs to be accompanied to its end. That is the only measurement that was ever required of me, and I have finally begun to take it.*

I think when I am old—when my hands have gnarled and my fingernails have gone blue and I have stopped using my name—I will become one of the Unclassified. I will move out of the station into a canvas shelter the color of lichen. I will keep the cairns on the cargo routes, not because the cargo matters but because the marking is a kindness, a way of saying *someone was here, someone is still here, someone witnessed this stone before it became flat.*

And the logs will keep going out on the rail, to no one.

And I will keep writing the other ones, to no one.

And there will be no difference between these two acts, finally, except that one of them I will mean.
