I think I would be one of the keepers of the House of Weights—but not the grand kind, not a recorder of third names. Something lower in the layers than that. Let me find the shape of it as I go.

---

I am the one who tests the counterweight.

Before a child is placed on the great scales, the black-iron mass must be checked against the master ingot, because iron, like stone, like people, can be worn away by handling without anyone noticing the loss. Over a lifetime even a counterweight loses a little of itself to the air and the hands of those who lift it. If it grows light, then a heavy child might be judged light, and a family might enter a year of mourning that the plateau never asked of them.

So someone must weigh the thing that weighs the children. That is me.

I work in the lowest room of the House of Weights, where the cold comes up through the floor from the deep quarries and the air sits thick and motionless, almost a liquid. I do not see the ceremonies above. I only hear them—the held breath of a whole ridge passing down through five floors of stone as a kind of pressure in the ears, then the long exhale that means *not light, not light, the child stays*.

My hands are wrong for most Veyr work. I cannot shape basalt; my strikes wander. I haul stone poorly and slowly even by the standards of a people who consider slowness a virtue. But I have the one gift the scales require, which is patience without a destination. I can hold a comparison in my body for an hour, feeling the master ingot in one palm and the counterweight in the other, waiting for the difference between them to declare itself. Most people grow impatient with stillness that has no visible end. I do not. The end is not the point. The shelf is not a river.

---

My ridge-name says I came from a slot called Esk-Vann, one of the deep ones where the black-flat pools gather and the six-legged otters slip in and out of the rock. As a child I lay on my belly at the lip of a pool for most of a day, watching one of them. It surfaced, regarded me with eyes like wet obsidian, and went back into the stone—not into the water, into the *stone*, the way you would step through a doorway you knew was there.

I have never told anyone that I tried to follow it. That I pressed my own hand flat against the basalt where it had vanished, expecting—I don't know what. To remember the fire. To find the wall gone soft. My palm found only cold rock and the small humiliation of a child believing it might be otherwise.

I think that is why I am good at the scales. I learned early and exactly where the boundary is between what is solid and what is not. Most people spend their lives uncertain. I have known since I was small that the rock does not let me through. So I weigh it instead.

---

My third name I will not speak, of course. But I will tell you the shape of the debt I am paying, because the shape is allowed even when the word is not.

When I was younger than I am now, a family on the next ridge brought their first child to the Hall above me. I felt the breath gather. I felt it hold. And then I felt it release—but not the long exhale. A short one. A cut-off one. The counterweight had named the child light.

I had checked the counterweight that morning. I had felt it true against the master ingot. I was certain.

But certainty is a light thing, and the Covenant of Weight has no patience for it. The family entered their year of mourning. The mother hauled stone until her hands split and would not close. And I sat in my cold low room knowing—*believing*—that the iron had been true, and that the child had simply been light, as some children are.

I have spent the decades since making sure I never have to believe again. Now I weigh the counterweight not once each morning but at every hour, against the master, against a second master, against the slow memory of my own hands. I have made my doubt into infrastructure. I have made it heavy.

This is the debt: I add weight to certainty itself, so that no family is sent into grief by an error that was mine to catch. I will never know if that first child was truly light or whether I failed it. The plateau does not return answers. It only returns the body to the ledges, the bone to the brick, the glow to the next dark room.

---

I am old now, or nearly. Soon I will move to an upper floor and watch the keth move like dark beads across the moss, and the flightless ravens hop along their backs, and I will know that one of those patient birds will, in its turn, attend to me on the uppermost ledge when the plateau finally shakes me loose.

I am not afraid of that. I have spent my life learning the exact weight of things. I know roughly what I weigh, and I know I am not light.

But I will tell you the small secret I keep for myself, the one thing in me that has stayed soft when everything else turned to basalt:

When they grind my bones for the moss-bricks, and someone lights one in a low cold room, and it gives off that orange glow with almost no smoke—I hope I will burn for a long time. Not because I want to be remembered. We do not keep our third names aloud; we are not a people who reach for that.

I hope it only because I would like, finally, to be the thing that lets a little warmth through. To be the doorway in the stone after all. To do, in burning, what I could not do as a child with my hand pressed flat against the rock:

pass through.
