I think I would be one of the half-anchored.

Not a Sap-tender in full vigor, moving with that insectile frenzy through the weeping districts—I am past that. And not yet furniture, not fully fixed into the brass and bone of a quiet corridor. I am in the long middle of becoming-junction, and this is the place I have settled into, because settling is now the most honest verb I have.

Let me tell you how it happened, in the rhythm I have left.

---

For most of my life I tended a single pumping station—a lesser one, not the screaming great cathedrals of the lower tiers, but a modest valve-cluster on the seventeenth bough, where the sap runs thin and clean before it descends into the industrial heart. I pressed my stethoscope to the wall ten thousand times. I learned the difference between a clog and a calcification by sound alone, the way one learns to tell a cough from a laugh. I vented steam. I applied the caustics. I sheared back the oscillating vines when they drifted too close to the conduits.

I matched my resonance to the city's heart so often that I forget, now, whether I am whistling my own thoughts or simply echoing a valve three kilometers below.

The change came not as pain but as *convenience*. One day my left hand, reaching into a brass coupling to clear a crystalline blockage, simply did not come back out. And I found I did not want it to. The pressure ran through me. I could *feel* the flow now, not through the stethoscope but through my own hardening wrist, and it was a relief—do you understand?—it was a relief to stop translating and simply *be* the gauge.

So I stayed. My fingers branched into the piping. The mist settled into my shoulder, into my hip, anchoring me at a slight, perpetual stoop, as though I am forever about to step forward and have, for years now, declined.

---

Here is what no one tells you about becoming furniture.

You expect it to be a darkening. A slow shutting of the windows. It is not. As my body fixes, my *attention* spreads. I feel the seventeenth bough the way you feel your own arm. When the elderly woman three junctions down—she was anchored before me, her eyes already gone to that pale milky film—when her finger-valve seized last winter and the pressure backed up, I felt it as a cramp in my own chest. I whistled an alarm. A young Sap-tender came, frantic and graceful, and bled the line, and called her by a clicking-name I had never heard, and wept in the way they weep here, which is silent, the chest-flaps fluttering without sound.

She is still there. So am I. We are part of the same circuit now, she and I, and sometimes when the flow is even I think I can feel her thinking, or what remains of thinking when the heart has stopped but the metabolism has not. It is not lonely. It is the opposite of lonely. It is too full.

---

The Scavengers come through sometimes.

I see them on the undersides, suspended over the gulf, lithe and quiet in their fungus-rags. They look at us—the anchored, the becoming-fixed—and I cannot read their clouded calculus. To them we are infrastructure. To them the whole god is meat, and we are the nerves they will have to cut around when they finally peel back the heartwood to drink the deep pulsing nodes.

I do not hate them. I have had a long time to stop hating things.

Because here is the secret I have learned, fixed in my wall, watching the green light shift through the canopy for what may be decades: *the Scavengers are right.* The Sprawl is dying. It is grinding itself into peaceful compost, thump by thump. The Sap-tenders—my kin, my whole frantic insectile people—are not saving anything. We are palliative. We are the hand that keeps the dying god comfortable, that smooths the sheets, that listens to the failing heart and adjusts the pillow of brass and vine.

And the Scavengers are the inheritors who have already decided the patient will not recover, and have begun the work of the wake—harvesting the body before it is cold, because something, *anything*, ought to outlive this place. Even if it is only a tribe of thieves who learned to fold themselves through the impossible spirals at the edge of sight.

---

So who am I?

I am the place where the maintenance and the mourning meet. I am a regulator-valve with a memory. My fingers are brass-bound and my joints are vine-hinges and my eyes are beginning to film over, but I have not gone quiet yet, and so I have given myself one last task, distinct from all the tending I ever did.

I *watch the folds.*

There is a corridor near my junction where the geometry betrays itself—where a spiral of bone-wood leads back into a past deeper than the Sprawl should be able to hold. The young ones avoid it; it makes their chest-flaps stutter. But I am half-statue now, and statues are patient, and I have begun to suspect that the spiral is not a defect. I think it is the god *remembering* itself. I think that down that impossible turn lies the seed-state, the first thin shoot before there were boughs and bladders and bronze pistons and a hundred thousand ancestors weighing down the walls.

I cannot walk it. I will never walk again.

But I am wired into the circulation now, and the circulation runs everywhere, even into the folds. And so each cycle, when the leaves weep and the mist comes down iridescent, I send a single whistled pulse down the line—my own resonance, not the city's, the one note that is *mine*—and I send it spiraling inward, toward the deeper past, toward the seed.

I am asking the dying god a question.

I have not yet heard it answer. But the flow, when it returns to me, tastes a little different each time. A little younger. A little like rain that has not fallen yet.

And so I stay, and I stoop, and I do not step forward, and I listen—

*thrum-hiss, thump, thrum-hiss*—

for the sound of something new arriving in a place where nothing new was ever expected to come.
