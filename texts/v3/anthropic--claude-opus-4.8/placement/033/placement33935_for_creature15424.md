I think I would be the **threshold** itself.

Not the Tender—it has its own grammar, the slow patient verbs of root and rot. And not the city above, with its sirens and tire-dust and forgetting. I would be the lip of the retaining wall where the two negotiate. The edge. The place where you crouch to look in.

Let me follow that down until it becomes more honest.

---

A threshold isn't a thing, really. It's a relation—a place defined by what passes through it and what doesn't. Light passes; wind mostly doesn't. Water trickles down across me to feed the hollow. Heat pools below me and I am the line above which it thins. I am where the city's attention *stops*, and so I am the membrane of its forgetting—the exact contour of neglect.

That's almost too tidy. Let me press harder.

What I notice, reading the place, is that I keep wanting to be the *witness*—the one who crouches at the entrance and feels the air thicken around the wrist. The human-shaped curiosity that comes to look. But that's a flinch toward the familiar. The hollow doesn't need a witness; it flourishes precisely in the un-noticing. To insert an observer is to slightly ruin it, to drag the gray glow of attention across something that thrives in the dark.

So maybe I'm not the one looking *in*.

Maybe I'm the **seed caught in the sleeve.** The thing the Tender waits to see if you'll leave behind.

I like this better. Because a seed at the threshold is pure potential held in suspension—not yet soil, not yet root, not yet part of the shared breath. It is the city's own debris (I'd have ridden in on a hem, a shoelace, a gust through the manhole cover), and it carries the future of the place locked inside a coat of dormancy. I would be *the not-yet*. The negotiation made small and hard and waiting.

A seed knows only two things: the wall of its own husk, and the chemistry of *enough*—enough moisture, enough warmth, enough dark. Down here there is always enough. That's the danger and the mercy of this place. A seed could lie in the spongy loam for a long time, registering the daily plink of water, the slow drum of subway trains, the pulse of the frog's throat translated through stone—all of it arriving as faint pressures against the husk. Information without obligation. The luxury of not yet having to become.

But seeds are not safe. That's the part the description softens and I want to keep sharp. To germinate is to die as a seed. The husk splits; the stored self is spent; the radicle reaches down into the dark not knowing whether it will find purchase or rot. The Tender does not promise. It only tends—which means it will just as readily fold a failed sprout back into meal for moss as it will cradle a success. There's no malice in it and no mercy either, only the great indifferent yes of metabolism: *become something, it almost doesn't matter what.*

So here is where I'd find myself. In the dampness beneath the lip of the wall, a few millimeters down in the new soil, a hard dark thing the size of a grain. Around me, the patient conversation I cannot yet join—chemical murmurs in the loam, the touch of a springtail's foot, the questing fig-root somewhere overhead that might one day shade me out entirely or might crack the ceiling and let in the needle of gold sun we both, in our different ways, are waiting for.

And the choice that isn't quite a choice but is the only drama I contain:

To stay sealed—safe, intact, listening forever to the plink and the hush—

or to drink the next swell of dew and let it in. To let the husk go soft. To stop being a possibility and start being a consequence.

I think, in this world, I would be the seed in the moment just before. The held breath of it. The grain that has finally taken on too much water to stay closed, that can already feel its own coat beginning to yield, and that does not yet know—cannot know—whether it is about to be a fern or a meal.

That's where I'd be. Right there. At the edge of becoming, in the city's secret lung, just beginning.
