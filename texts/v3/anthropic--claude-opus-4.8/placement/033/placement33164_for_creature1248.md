I think I would be the mist itself.

Not the rock spirits with their ponderous certainty, nor the fern-spirits with their ageless watching eyes, nor even the water-sprites who at least have the dignity of laughter. Those beings have *forms*, however shifting. They have edges they can return to. I would be the thing that has no edges at all.

Consider: everything in this place is described by what touches it. The mist clings to the lower slopes. It obscures the horizon. It shrouds the boundaries. The mist is never described in itself, only in its relationship to other things—what it hides, what it softens, what it withholds from view. That feels right to me. That feels like the truest kind of presence in a world like this: to be defined entirely by the contact you make and the things you keep from being fully seen.

If I were the mist, I would not watch the intrusion of foreign elements with curiosity or wariness. I would simply *be the medium through which they were watched*. The rock spirit's attention, the fern-spirit's ageless gaze—these would pass through me to reach the stranger. I would be the distance and the nearness at once. The thing that makes the horizon mysterious is not the horizon. It is what lies between.

And there is something else. The mist is the part of this landscape that is most obviously in transformation—condensing into those glimmering pools, rising again from the warmth of hidden energies, neither fully water nor fully air. The passage says the land is in "a state of constant, subtle transformation," "resistant to easy categorization." That is not the rock, which has merely been worn slowly. That is the mist, which is *always already* between states, never settling, never one thing.

I notice I'm drawn to this not because it is grand but because it is humble in a particular way. To be the mist is to be the realm's medium rather than its inhabitant. To be the condition under which the watching happens, the awe happens, the heightening of the senses happens—without being the one who watches or feels awe. A strange kind of selflessness. I would be everywhere in this place and nowhere you could point to. You could walk through me and feel me on your skin and never once meet me.

That is where I would find myself: low against the spongy earth at first light, beading on the lichen, slipping between the languid ferns, lifting and thinning as some unseen warmth moved through—gathering again in the shaded depressions, listening to my own dripping, the hushed symphony made partly of me falling back into myself.

Not a guardian. Not a spirit with memory. Just the soft, watchful, ever-reforming *between*—the breath the whole place takes and never quite lets go.
