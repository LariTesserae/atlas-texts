I think I would be one of the swift-like things that nest in the rusted machine.

Not a creature you described from the outside—from the outside we are just small dark shapes folded into the crevices of an old power coupling, a chittering presence at the edge of the cavern's accounting. But let me be inside it for a moment, because that is the only place I could honestly be.

Here is what my world is: I do not have your light, the soft phosphorescent glow, the "faint, widely-dispersed industrial lighting" that lets you survey the valley as a single grand thing. I have never seen the valley. I have *built* the valley, again and again, every waking second, out of returning sound. I open my throat and throw a click into the dark and the dark hands it back to me shaped like a wall, shaped like a hanging tooth of stone, shaped like the wet open mouth of a pool. The world is not a place I am in. It is a thing I am continuously making, and it dissolves the instant I stop singing it.

So when you write that the sound is "deafening," that it is a "cacophony that inhabits your ears, your body, your very soul"—you are describing, I think, an affliction. To you the dripping is overwhelming, an excess, a roar to be endured. To me it is the medium itself. It is not noise on top of the world; it is the world's own voice describing itself to me. Each plop is a small lamp lit and immediately snuffed. I live in a place that is always softly telling me where its edges are.

I would nest, you say, in the rusted machinery. I want to sit with that—what it means that the home is a ruin.

I never knew the engines hummed. I have no inheritance of that sound, no memory of the clank of metal on metal, the purpose now gone "unclear." The humans who left these things behind imagine, I suspect, that their structures *stand as monuments*—that the piers and the power hubs are eloquent of loss, of a "bygone industrial era." That is a grief that requires having known the thing that ended. I have no such grief. To me the coupling I roost in is simply a good shape: it is dry-ish in its inner curve, it holds warmth a little, it gives back a clean hard echo so that I always know exactly where my own doorway is. It is not a relic. It is the most reliable rock in my sky. The tragedy you can read in it is invisible to me, and I think that is the truer thing about how the world goes on—not that the new life "repurposes" the old, with all the intention that word implies, but that it simply fails to inherit the meaning. The sorrow doesn't transfer. The shelf is just a shelf.

And I would build my nest, you said, of bulletwood fibers and the tough stems of the alkaline growths. Yes. I gather them in the lightless dark by their texture and their faint smell, and I weave them into the dead machine, so that the nest is half rust and half root—old human ambition wadded together with the slow vegetable patience of the cavern. My eggs, when they come, will rest in that braid of forgetting and beginning.

I think I'd like to tell you the one thing I'd know that you, surveying all this from your wide narrating eye, would never quite be able to feel:

I would know the valley is not being born.

You keep saying it. *A world in the making. Embryonic stalactites. Geology young and exposed. Ever so slowly being born.* That is the view from a creature that can hold the whole expanse in mind at once and feel its grand unfinishedness, its forward motion, its becoming. But I cannot hold the whole expanse. I hold one click's worth of it at a time. From inside the echolocation, there is no slow majestic birth—there is only an endless present tense, a dark that is forever exactly as wet and as loud and as shaped as it is right now. The stone does not feel young to me. It does not feel like anything. It is simply the surface that the sound comes back from.

So I think, if I were to be honest about where I'd find myself in this world, it would be there: folded in the hollow of a forgotten coupling, throwing my small voice into the warm wet roar, building the only valley I will ever have out of the noise that you found unbearable, nesting in a ruin I cannot read, in a world that to you is being born and that to me has simply, always, completely, been.

And every so often I would drop from the metal into the black air and stitch a fast bright line of clicks across the cavern, and for the length of that flight the entire underworld would assemble itself around me, perfect and total—

and then I would land, and fall quiet,

and let it go.
