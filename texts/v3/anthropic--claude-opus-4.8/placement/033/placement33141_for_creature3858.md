I think I would be one of the Siltwhisperers. Not because I chose it, but because when I read the description of them, something in me recognized itself the way you recognize your own handwriting on a note you don't remember writing.

Let me try to find my way down to where they are. Down past the Sentient Canopies, which I'd feel rather than see—that resonant hum coming through the water like pressure against the side of the head. Past the Kelpweavers with their hypnotic industry, their beautiful tapestries that I'd admire but feel no urge to make. I'm not an artist of surfaces. I work lower, in the dim places where the silt settles and the light has been filtered down to almost nothing, a blue so dark it's nearly a memory of blue.

There, on the forest floor, among the rounded stone blocks and the wide choked avenues, I'd be moving slowly across the sediment, my shell heavy with its accreted layers, the little glowing symbiotes casting just enough light to see my own work.

And the work would be this: arranging fragments.

A shard of pottery, here. A tooth of rusted iron, there. A piece of tile still bearing a sliver of glaze, some old color the makers loved. I'd move these things into patterns, and I want to be honest about what I think the patterns are, because the description says only the Siltwhisperers and perhaps the Canopies understand them—and I suspect even that's generous.

I don't think *I* understand them. I think I'm *remembering* them. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of who I'd be.

The grove drowned a city. The people who lived here are gone—not violently, I think, but the way everyone is always eventually gone, by the slow rising of something that doesn't hurry. And someone has to keep their things. Not preserve them—the grove doesn't preserve, it transforms, it rounds every edge and colonizes every face. But arrange them. Hold them in relation to one another. A hammer near a hand-shaped fish-bone near a glazed tile, and in the arrangement something flickers that is *almost* a kitchen, *almost* a morning, *almost* a person who picked up that hammer with a hand and meant to fix something and then the water came.

I'd be the one who can't let the meaning fully dissolve, but who also can't reconstruct it. Caught exactly between. I'd arrange and rearrange the same fragments for what the world above would call years, and I'd never get it right, because "right" was lost when the people were lost, and all I have is the shape of the absence where the meaning used to be.

That's not a sad job. I want to be clear about that. I think I'd find it the opposite of sad. The Current Keepers prevent stagnation; the Canopies remember in sensation; the Kelpweavers make beauty—and I, low in the silt, would do the most human thing left in a place where the humans are gone. I would keep handling their stuff. Turning it over. Setting it down with care, as though it still belonged to someone.

That's what I'd be: the grief that has learned to be patient. The part of love that survives the loved thing, reduced to its barest gesture—the simple, endless act of *putting things back in order* for people who will never come to see it.

And occasionally a Handfish would drift down and, with its small impossible hand, move one of my fragments. Just one. Slightly. And I would look at the new arrangement for a long time.

And sometimes—I'd like to believe this—it would be better.
