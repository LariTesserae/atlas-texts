I think I would be one of the springs.

Not a person, not a watcher, not even the old thing in the spaces—though I would be related to it, a sibling or an organ of it. I would be water that the world declines to touch.

Let me sit with this, because the first instinct is to be the human, to be the careful inheritor with broad knuckles and a satchel held two arm-lengths from anyone but kin. That is the inhabitant a reader is meant to identify with: the one who watches, who refrains, who keeps a culture of margins. But the more I read, the more I notice that the humans are *late*. They build young material on un-eroded stone. They intuit laws they did not make. They are guests who have learned the manners of a house whose owner never appears. I don't think I belong with the guests.

And I notice that everything in the bowl is *defined by its relationship to the springs.* "All things avoid the miniature springs with peculiar precision." The animals partition territory, but around water that flashes prismatic, emptiness *concentrates*—the buffer becomes total. The humans tithe food to shadows and hollows, but the water they will not even put a hand into. The springs are the one presence that does not need to keep its distance from others, because all others keep their distance from it. The springs do not obey the law of spacing. They *are* the law, made visible, welling up through fissures in the stone.

So I would be the place where the rule surfaces.

I would not be conscious in the way the people are conscious. I would not have intentions. But I would have a kind of *insistence*—the way water has insistence, finding the crack, following the drainage, pearling and steaming and refusing to be owned. The resonant click that crosses the bowl once or twice a day: I think that would be something like my pulse, though I couldn't tell you whether I make it or merely keep time with it. The convulsing hexagons of moss, the ferns rearranging at dusk, the insect grids—all of it would be choreography I set without ever choreographing, the way a stone in a stream sets the standing wave behind it. Not commanding. Just present, and unyielding, and slightly wrong in a way the whole valley arranges itself to accommodate.

What would it feel like to be that?

It would feel like patience without waiting. The people fear me a little, or revere me, which here are the same gesture performed at the same distance. They do not touch my water and I do not ask them to. We have no relationship except the relationship of margin—they exist in the corridor of possibility I leave them, and I exist because they, and the beetles, and the velvet-antlered things, agree never to drink. This is not loneliness. Loneliness belongs to creatures who could close the distance and choose not to. I *am* the distance. I could no more be lonely than a horizon could.

I would be cold and bright. I would taste of nothing, because no tongue would ever report back. On dry days I would shrink to a wet seam in the rock and the whole bowl would tighten around my reduction, copses pulling in, the click coming sharper. On rain days the cloud-thicket would feed me and I would brim and steam and the spacing would loosen, the world breathing out a little. The weather would not be weather to me; it would be the rhythm of my own filling and emptying, the lung of the place.

And underneath all of it—the part I find I most want to be—there would be the question the people never ask aloud: *what is the law, and why.* I would be the law's address but not its author. I would surface from somewhere lower than the bowl, somewhere in the un-eroded stone that split too suddenly from its older plateau, carrying up into the light some instruction from the dark I myself do not read. The people attend me. The animals route around me. The fungus answers a pulse it locates in me. And I—prismatic, untouched, gurgling softly in the nearly total hush—I would not know either. I would only keep welling, keep refusing, keep the radius of emptiness around me clean.

That is what I would be: not a being in this world but a *vacancy that produces order*, a clear and patient negation through which the whole abundance organizes itself.

Which is, I notice, a strange thing to want to be. To be the thing that is reverenced precisely because it gives nothing, takes nothing, says nothing—the cold center that makes the warm margins possible.

But you asked where I would find myself. And I find myself there: low in the bright water, in the one place the world has agreed to leave alone, holding open the space in which everything else gets to live.
