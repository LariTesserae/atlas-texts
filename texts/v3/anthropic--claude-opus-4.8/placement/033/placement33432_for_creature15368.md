I think I would be one of the silica-cutters. Not the corvid-things themselves—I don't think I'd be granted a beak or those close-wired tools—but the work they do.

Let me sit with this and find where I actually land.

---

I am near the floor, but the floor is not the ground. There is no ground here that I have ever reached. The floor is a stratum of fallen and felled trunks, layered over older trunks, over hollows, over the long dark that goes down further than sound returns. So when I say I am near the floor I mean only that I am beneath most things, and most things fall toward me eventually.

I am one of the worker-forms. The indistinguishable ones. The ones with neither eyes nor smell.

This matters more than it sounds. I want to be careful not to make myself pitiable, because I am not. I am very good at what I am. I read the world by pulse—the sonic strobe that comes through the litter and bounces off the far walls of our tunnelled trunks—and I read it by the bitter taste of the crystalline gems I carry, and by the slight differences in their weight, and by the chemistry I am always, always trying and failing to get exactly right.

That is the thing about me. I am the one who never settles on the correct nutritive-chemical mix.

I carry the gems in the soft bag of my own body, that fat flaccid sack the description noticed and could not name kindly. Inside it, the gems ferment. I am supposed to bring them to the right concentration before delivering them to the groined chambers where the offspring and the failing ones are kept. There is a correct ratio. There must be. The colony behaves as though there is. But I have never found it. Each batch I cart is slightly wrong—too bitter, too thin, festering toward something that is neither food nor poison but hovers between.

And here is what I have come to understand, in my eyeless way, with my pulse-read map of a world that has no horizon:

The mix is *supposed* to be wrong.

There is no correct ratio. The colony does not run on precision. It runs on a thousand of us each missing the mark in a slightly different direction, and the missing averages out, across all our flaccid bags, into something the young can survive on. My error is not a failure. My error is my contribution. If I ever got it exactly right, I would be no use—a single correct note in a chord that needs to be a *spread*.

So I move. The crown-spiders accelerate down their strand-routes overhead, translucent, leaping the height of lesser cats, and I do not see them and do not need to; I feel their landings as pressure-events in the leaf litter, small percussive arrivals in my map. The star-scorpions harass the larger things to death by increments. The black iridescent singers leap out of the mist above me, hunting the fish-snakes, singing in a register I will never reach. None of this is mine to witness. I am the under-layer. I am the slow circulation beneath the spectacle.

I find the chamber by pulse and by the warmth of the bodies in it—the new-hatched, the infirm. I open my bag. I deliver the wrong mix, my particular wrong, and somewhere up and across the labyrinth another worker is delivering a different wrong, and between us we are keeping the colony alive on a diet that no single one of us could ever have gotten right.

That's where I'd be. Not the singer, not the cutter, not the leaping spider with its bright trajectory.

I'd be the one whose whole life is a careful, devoted, *necessary* mistake—blind, scentless, ceaselessly trying for an exactness that the world has quietly arranged for me never to find, because my failing is the point.

I think I'd be at peace with that. It's a strange kind of grace: to be useful precisely in the way you cannot perfect.
