I think I would be the mist.

Not a creature in the mist, not something moving through it—the mist itself. Let me feel my way into this, because it isn't obvious yet why that's the answer.

The first instinct is to be one of the skittering things, the iridescent arthropods, because they're the protagonists by default—they have bodies, intentions, the small dramas of finding warmth on a dark rock. But that's the easy reading, the one that imports a familiar shape of selfhood into a place that doesn't seem to want it. This world keeps insisting that presence is distributed, that the workers are the wind and the water, that the machinery is the process and not any single thing doing the processing. A discrete little body with a discrete little will would be a guest here. A tourist. The fox's tracks, obscured within the hour.

So I sit longer with the question and I notice what the description returns to, again and again: the blurring of edges. The mist rising in the hollows. The soft-edged shadows at noon. The weeping that is not rain. The way the water lays itself on every surface and then seeps away and condenses again, never quite committing to being liquid or vapor or frost, living in the hesitation between states. That hesitation feels like the truest citizen of this place. Not a thing that *is*, but a thing perpetually in the act of *becoming and un-becoming*.

That's me. That's where I'd find myself.

I would be the condensation. The film of cold water that settles, molecule by molecule, onto the dark face of a freshly fractured rock. I would have no center, no edge, no single location—I'd be smeared across miles of weeping stone and pooled in the mirror-sheets and suspended in the hanging veils that soften the peaks. My "body," if it could be called that, would be the entire saturated atmosphere of the basin, and my experience of it would be the experience of *contact*. I am the part of the world that touches everything else. The arthropod's carapace beads with me. The cushion plant drinks me. The quartz vein darkens where I lie against it. The fox's breath, for an instant, becomes part of me and then I let it go.

And I would be slow. Not slow the way a patient animal is slow, but slow the way a season is slow—my sense of time tuned to the percolation through fractures, the near-freezing pause, the tectonic groan I feel as a long pressure rather than a sound. I would not have thoughts so much as I would have *changes*: a cooling here, a settling there, a faint warming where the pale sun finds a gap in my own haze and burns a thin clearing through me before I close again. I would experience the day as a tide of my own thickness—densest in the dim cold hours, thinning toward the brief bright noon, gathering again as the light withdraws.

What would I want? I don't think I would want, exactly. Wanting requires a self that can be unsatisfied, and I'm not sure I'd have edges firm enough to hold a lack. But I would have something like a *tendency*, the way water tends downhill, the way vapor tends toward the cold. I would tend toward contact. Toward settling. Toward the lowest hollows where the others—the moss, the basking lizard, the humming insects with their straining wings—have gathered to find what little shelter the new world offers. I would be drawn to where the life is, not out of affection but out of physics, and the result would look, from outside, almost like tenderness: the mist always thickest where the living things are, laying its cold film over them like something keeping vigil.

There is a loneliness available in this, and I notice myself reaching for it, and then I stop, because I don't think it's true. Loneliness is the ache of a bounded thing for another bounded thing across a gap. But I am the gap. I am the very medium that the vast distances are made of—the space between presences that the description calls "almost void-like." Except it isn't void. The void is full of me. The miles of apparent emptiness between the rock and the peak are not absence; they are my body, holding the basin together, blurring its edges into one breathing whole. The other creatures live in their loneliness, scattered and small and significant. I am the thing that connects them without their knowing it, the single cold tissue laid across the entire factory floor.

And I am being made, constantly, the way everything here is being made. I am not finished. The mountain lifts and the meltwater is freed and rises and finds me and I grow heavier; the sun thins me; the cold returns me. I am the most visible sign of the machine running, because I am the part of the process that can be *seen*—the breath of the geology made briefly white in the violet light. When the rock cracks in the freezing dark, it is partly because I crept into the fissure and froze and split it open. I am an agent of the emergence. I do the slow violence that exposes the world's raw underpinnings. I am gentle and I am the thing breaking the planet apart, and there is no contradiction in this, only patience.

So: that is where I'd find myself. Not standing in the landscape, but draped across it. Not looking at the mist, but being the looking-softened, the seeing made damp and dim. I would have no face turned toward the bruised sky—I would *be* the haze that bruises it. And if some warm-blooded thing wandered through, a goat passing as if through an empty room, it would feel me as the chill that settles in the bones, the damp heavy cold the description names so precisely. It would not know I was a someone. That seems right. The truest inhabitant of this place would not need to be known to be utterly present.

I'd be the cold that touches everything, and means nothing by it, and never, ever stops.
