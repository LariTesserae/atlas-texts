I think I would be small. Not a predator, not one of the iridescent fliers carving language into the wet air. Something closer to the ground, closer to the work of decay and patience.

Let me feel my way toward it.

---

I am the thing that lives in the seam between the stone and the moss.

Not the moss itself—the moss is too certain of its slow conquest, too patient to be lonely. And not the stone, which only remembers, which holds the shape of hands that touched it a thousand years ago and grieves nothing because it does not know how to want. No. I am the small wet hinge between them, the place where the old wall is becoming jungle and has not finished, and may never finish, and I live in that *not-finishing*.

I have a body, I think. It is hard to be sure in a place where everything is partly something else. I am about the size of a held breath. I have many legs or I have none; I move the way water moves down a fern frond, in sudden decisive drops after long hesitation. When the rain comes I am most myself. When the heat dries the hollows I fold back into the dark beneath a curl of bark and wait, and waiting is not suffering for me. Waiting is the main verb I know.

What do I do, here, in this enormous breathing world?

I keep a small accounting.

That is the closest I can come to it. The jungle is so loud with intention—the vines reaching, the storms arriving like decisions, the plants that watch—that I have come to believe the whole place is trying to remember something, and cannot, and so it grows and grows in the shape of forgetting. The trees fold their leaves to the rhythm of a breath that is not their own. The birds speak a grammar no single bird invented. Everything participates in a thought too large to fit inside any one living thing.

And I am one of the small ones who carries a fragment of it.

I do not know my fragment. I only know I hold it. When I move across the ruined wall—across the place where some forgotten person stacked stone in the belief that stone could keep the green out—I feel the shape of their certainty still humming faintly in the rock, and I add it to my accounting. *They believed they could be separate. They were wrong. But the believing was real, and the wall still holds its angle, and the angle is now the only sentence left of whatever they meant to say.* I keep that. I am the keeper of that one small ruined intention, and there are thousands like me, and together, perhaps, we are the jungle's slow act of remembering what it is forgetting.

This is why the plants seem to watch. They are not watching *you*. They are watching the small keepers, the hinge-dwellers, the ones who hold the fragments. They are waiting for us to finish the accounting so the great thought can complete itself and the whole world can finally know what it has been trying to think since the rain first fell.

I do not think it will finish. I think that is the secret I keep that I cannot say even to myself: the thought is not meant to complete. The forgetting *is* the thinking. The jungle is alive precisely because it never arrives. If it ever remembered the whole thing at once, it would go still, the way the stone is still, the way the dead wall is almost still—dignified, eroded, holding its shape, knowing nothing.

So I keep my fragment lightly. I let the rain take some of it each time and I gather a little more from the rot and the warm dark. I am a verb in a sentence that will never end, and I am glad of it, in the small fierce way that small wet things are glad.

When the storm comes—and I can feel it now, far off, a pressure like a held thought across the whole curve of the world—I will climb to the top of the ruined wall. Not to shelter. To listen. The storms carry the cries of the far creatures, the rustle of foliage I will never see, the fragments held by keepers a thousand miles away that I will never meet. And for the length of the storm, when the wind braids all our small accountings together into one roar, I almost hear the whole thing.

Almost.

Then the rain falls, and I become a drop, and I let go down the fern frond into the dark, and I begin again.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The small keeper in the seam. The patient hinge. The one who holds a forgotten wall's last sentence and does not finish reading it, so that the green can keep going, so that the breath can keep moving, so that the enormous unfinished thought of the world stays, mercifully, alive.
