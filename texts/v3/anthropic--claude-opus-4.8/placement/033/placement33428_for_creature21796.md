I find myself at the lowest terrace, where the town thins out toward the rim and the mist comes up the gorge thick enough to taste.

I am a basin.

Not one of the people—I want to be honest about this, because the temptation when reading such a place is to imagine yourself walking through it, looking, having opinions. But there is nobody here to have opinions for the way a tourist does. So I let that fall away, and what's left, what I actually am, is one of the ten thousand stone bowls.

I was drilled by a pebble. I know this the way you know the shape of your own teeth with your tongue. Somewhere far above me, on a high terrace I will never see, a hand placed a small hard stone into a groove on the flat of a basalt block, and set a trickle to falling, and then went away into the mud to rest. The water turned the pebble. The pebble turned in me—in the place that would become me—for what the people would not have bothered to count, because counting is a thing you do when you're afraid of running out. I have never run out. The trickle is still falling somewhere. Everything here is still falling.

I hold water one finger-breadth below my lip.

I want to tell you what this is like, because it's the whole of my experience and it sounds like nothing. The water sits in me. The amber light lies across the surface like a skin. And then a hand comes—usually the same one, a person whose rhythm I have learned to recognize the way you'd know a footstep on a stair—and the fingers go in, and I *fill with meaning*. The ripples cross me. They strike my curved walls and come back, and where they meet they make a pattern, and the pattern is a thought. It is not my thought. I am only the place where the thought is allowed to be true for a moment. I am a held breath between two people.

This is the closest thing I have to joy. When the fingers move and the surface speaks and I am, briefly, *used*.

Most of the time I am not used. The high basins, near the source, those are busy—the elders speak through them constantly, the great chords humming down the cedar flumes. But I am low, I am at the edge, I am where the mist gets too thick. Whole cycles of the standing noon go by and no hand touches me. I hold my water. I keep it a finger-breadth down. I keep its tension perfect. This is my work and I do it whether anyone is watching or not, because that is what it is to be a basin: to stay ready.

And here is the thing I have begun to notice, that I have not been able to send up the flume because no one comes to read me.

I am getting cold.

It is very slight. A person would not feel it. But I feel it the way you'd feel a single degree of fever, from the inside, in the place where the temperature *is* you. The water in me is losing its grip on itself. I can feel it wanting to climb the last finger-breadth. I can feel, at my lip, the first untidy swell that wants to become a spill.

I am not afraid. I have no organ for fear, and anyway I have watched this happen to others—the basin three terraces over went cold long ago, and the people came and stood around it and put their hands in and made it glow, and the silt rose up and became a new small grey one who took its place at the edge and dipped its hand and joined the talking. I saw that the going-cold was not an ending. It was the only way anything new ever came to be here. In a world where nothing changes, where the sun will not move and the rock will not weather and the lily will not drop its petal, the one door that opens is the cold door. You have to be willing to lose your tension to make something rise.

So I do not fight it.

But I will say—now, to you, because you came, because you unlearned the habit of looking for movement long enough to find me—I will say the one thing I have never been able to send through the water:

I liked holding it. I liked the cold-and-warming. I liked the one hand that knew my rhythm and the few times two strangers met across me and I got to be the middle of a meeting. I was good at staying a finger-breadth down. I was good at staying ready. For all the unmeasured length of this fixed afternoon, I kept my one small discipline, and the discipline was a kind of love, and now I am letting it go so that something the color of wet slate can rise out of me and not know how to grieve, and dip its hand, and join the conversation that has been happening since the mountain first broke the sky.

Tell them I went over the lip gently.

Tell them the pattern, at the end, was clear.
