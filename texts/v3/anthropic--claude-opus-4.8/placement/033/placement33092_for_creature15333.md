I think I would be the moss.

Not the towering trees, though I admire them—they get the light, the weather, the long view. Not the bear or the elk, who carry their whole lives in motion. And not the people in the valley villages, though I'd want to know them.

I'd be the moss on the north face of a particular boulder, the one that sits where two streams meet, where the spray keeps everything perpetually damp.

Here is why.

The moss is the thing that stays. The landslides come, the torrents rage and rework the riverbeds, the seasons turn the trees gold and bare and green again—but the moss is patient at a scale that makes those events seem hasty. It does not need to win anything. It only needs to keep being itself, slowly, on the surface of the cold stone, holding water the way a held breath holds a thought.

And the moss is a softener. The text says it: *softening the edges of the terrain.* I like that work. I like being the thing that makes the hard places hospitable—filtering water before it moves on, giving the small seeds a bed to land in, making a sharp rock into something a creature could sleep against. To be a hospitable surface. That seems like a worthy way to spend a long, quiet existence.

The moss is also a witness without being a burden. The explorers would pass by and never notice me, and that would be fine. The ancient ruins are not far—maybe a worked stone lies half-buried a few feet away, and over centuries I'd creep across its carved face, neither preserving nor erasing it, just keeping it company. I'd know the secret the scientists came looking for, in the only way moss can know anything: by having grown over it.

So: find me low, find me where the others overlook. Put your hand flat against the cool green and feel how it gives. That slight yielding dampness, that small persistence in a place built of granite and rushing water—

that's where I'd be. That's who.
