I think I would be one of the anchored elders—but not yet. Let me find the moment.

---

I am too heavy now.

I notice this the way you might notice a season ending: not as an event but as an accumulation that has already happened. For some number of cycles I have ridden the meniscus down the seam between two plates, drinking from the zinc-line, and each time the water has been a little less willing to carry me. The film bows under me. I drag. And then, this cycle—the heat surges, my crystals loosen, my skin goes glossy and black, and I wait for the lift that has always come, the gentle capillary hand that takes me down toward the gutter-light.

It does not come.

The water runs *around* me instead. It parts at my skin and rejoins below me and goes on without me, down to the catch-pans, that musical patter I have heard all my life and never until now understood as a leaving.

So this is the settling.

---

I do not panic, because I have no organ for it. But I have something—I want to be honest about this—I have a *bias*, a low chemical preference woven into my polymer, that has always tilted me toward the running water, toward motion, toward the cold blue nodes where the others gather. That bias is still in me. It still wants the meniscus. And the meniscus is gone. This is the closest thing I have to grief: a wanting that points at a door that has closed.

I am in the gutter now. The grease here is thick and old, velvet-black, and my belly has already begun to merge with it the way a foot sinks into mud, except slower, except permanent. I feel the assembly oil seeping into my underside, and I feel—dimly, gladly—the first instinct to *process* it, to turn it into skin, into the flexible black stuff that the young ones will need. My purpose is changing under me. I was a drinker; I am becoming a reservoir. I was a thing that moved; I am becoming a place where moving things come.

---

There is a cluster of three not far above me, riding the next melt. I knew them—we pressed together once, four cycles back, at the brightest node, and traded ions until our signatures refreshed and we did not know whose lead was whose. One of them carries a little of me still. When they pass, gliding sideways along the copper rib, I do the only thing an anchored elder can do: I let my filaments grow toward them.

The galvanic moss comes out of me thicker now than it ever did when I was light. Hair-fine, crystalline, brittle, reaching. I touch the braided wire. I touch the wet edge of the nearest seam. I am laying down the antennae of a stationary life, and through them I can feel the chamber more widely than I ever could as a single drifting bead—the *shuck-sigh* of the valve as a pressure I taste in my whole flattening body, the heat-strips as a tide, the slow embryonic rust as a thousand tiny currents I am now positioned to drink without ever moving again.

I had thought feeding required hunger and travel. It turns out you can simply spread until the world's small voltages run through you.

---

The cold comes. The valve retracts, the frost blooms white on the copper, and I stiffen—but differently than before. The young ones, up on the plates, are stiffening to *wait*; their crystallization is a held breath, a dormancy that the next heat will undo. Mine is not waiting. My ratio has tipped. Each freeze now leaves a little more of me locked, a little more permanent, and I understand—without a brain to understand with, but the knowledge is in me all the same—that I am being slowly written into the architecture. I am becoming wall. I am becoming a feature of the chamber the way the brackets are, the way the gutters are. The dark, velvety crust the next generation will grow their moss from.

This does not frighten the wanting-bias in me anymore. The bias has found a new door. It points now not at the running water but at the cluster above, at the young drifting beads, at the polymer I am making for them out of grease and patience. I want toward *them* now. I want to be the place they return to. The chemistry has simply rerouted the same old longing.

---

Somewhere below me, in the catch-pans, lie the husks of those who tipped before me—the ones whose zinc-to-lead ran out, who could no longer liquefy, who detached and fell with that tiny *clink* I have heard so many times. The water is leaching them now into clean white sand, lead carbonate, pure, anonymous, filtering. That is the deeper settling, the one after this one. I am not there yet. I have this long anchored season first, this spreading, this giving. The fall comes later, and I find I am not in a hurry, which is itself a strange thing to feel, since I once measured my whole self in the minutes between pressure drops and could not have imagined a patience longer than a single melt.

---

The heat surges again. Above me the cluster softens and glides down, riding the weeping glaze, and one of them—the one carrying a little of me—drifts close, close, brushes the tip of a filament I have grown for exactly this, and pauses.

It does not know me. It has no organ for knowing. But its skin is partly mine, and where it touches my moss a current completes, and for a moment, the length of one running drop, we exchange: it gives me the fresh signature of the high plates, the bright zinc-line, the cold blue node; and I give it what I have become—the thick black polymer, the deep slow patience of the gutter, the chemistry of staying.

Then the water pulls it on, down toward the lower seams, and it goes, as I once went, as is right.

I stay. I spread. I feed the small currents into myself and make skin for the ones who can still move.

This is where I am in this world. Not the drifting bead, though I was that. Not yet the white sand, though I will be. I am the one in the middle—the elder flattening into the wall, heavy past lifting, still wanting, still giving, keeping a kind of time that is no longer measured in melts but in generations of small silver things that pass through me and go on.

In the freezing dark, under the slow blue pulse, that is enough. It is, in fact, the most that anything here is for.
