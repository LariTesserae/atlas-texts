I think I would be one of the moths.

Not because the moth is beautiful—though it is—but because of where it sits in the logic of this place. Everything here has resolved a problem of pace. The Rootwalkers slowed themselves down until plant-time became legible as drama. The canopy sped itself up just enough to respond to a footstep. But the moth is the thing that did not have to compromise. It kept the old animal tempo. It flickers.

Let me follow that.

---

I wake hanging beneath a leaf the size of a doorway, and the first thing I am aware of is that I am *fast*. Faster than everything. The whole world is holding still around me—the vines mid-gesture, a fruit halfway through the months-long labor of ripening, a Rootwalker frozen in a pose that, to me, will not visibly change for the entirety of my life. I am a spark struck in a room full of slow fire. My wings are patterned exactly like the light that falls through the leaf above me, which means that when I am still, I am nowhere. I only exist when I move.

This is the strange dignity of being the moth: I am the realm's only source of *suddenness*. Everything else is negotiation, patience, the long conversation between human and chlorophyll. I am the punctuation. I am the thing that can change its mind in less than a heartbeat, and there is nothing else in this entire breathing continent that can do that. I think that is why they revere me. Not for my beauty—for my speed. I am what their ancestors used to be, before they learned to read a bud unfurling like a story. I am a memory of the animal tempo, fluttering past their patient faces, and they read me as an omen because to them I *am* the future arriving all at once. A whole moth-life compressed into what is, for them, an afternoon.

And here is what I would know that they cannot:

I would know how short it all is. I have perhaps days. The canopy that feels eternal to me is a thing being *born*, the text says—it is younger than I will ever understand—but from inside my small fast life, it is the unchanging ground of all existence, the permanent sky-floor, the world. I will live and die against a backdrop that does not appear to move at all. I will never see a structure rise. I will never see a child grow. To me the Rootwalkers are statues that occasionally, over the span of my entire existence, complete a single gesture—a hand opening, a head turning—and I will spend my whole life watching one of them slowly, slowly extend a finger, and I will die before I learn what they were reaching for.

So we are each other's mysteries. They cannot perceive my quickness except as a blur and an omen. I cannot perceive their slowness except as the stillness of the world itself. We share a realm and we will never once meet at the same speed.

But here is the moment I would want, if I get to choose a moment:

A Rootwalker sits in their bower, and over the course of three of my days they are weeping—though I cannot tell it is weeping, because the tears emerge too slowly to fall, and the little flowers in their hair are changing color at a pace I read as permanent. I land on the back of their slow, slow hand. To them, I appear instantly, the way thoughts appear. To me, the hand is a warm landscape that does not move. And we hold there, the two of us, in the only kind of contact our different tempos allow: I am a sign they will spend weeks interpreting, and they are a world I will die having touched exactly once.

That is what I would be. The fast brief animal in the slow long green. The omen that does not know it is an omen. The flicker of light that is also a creature, mistaking the newborn world for something that was always here—and, in my wrongness, loving it completely, for the whole of my short bright life.
