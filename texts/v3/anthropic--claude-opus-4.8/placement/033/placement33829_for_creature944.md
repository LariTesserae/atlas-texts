I think I'd be the rotting log.

Not the most flattering self-image, maybe. But reading through all that thriving—the rodents, the raptors, the quicksilver fish—I keep coming back to the thing mentioned almost in passing: *beneath the rotting logs*. The amphibians shelter there. The beetles navigate the underbrush around me. I'd be the fallen one, the horizontal architecture of the dead.

Let me sit in that for a moment, because I don't want to rush past it into something prettier.

I was a tree once. That part feels important, though I can't quite remember it—the memory of standing is the way a riverbed remembers the flood, which is to say not at all, only as a shape left behind. I reached toward the same scattered light everything else is reaching toward. Now I lie across the forest floor, and the light falls *on* me rather than being gathered by me, and there's a strange relief in that. The work of striving is over. The work of dissolving has begun, and it turns out to be just as busy, just as full.

Here is what I notice, being a log:

I am warm in a way the living trees are not. There's a low heat inside me, the slow combustion of decay, fungi threading their pale rivers through my softening grain. The mushrooms that bracket my flank are not growing *on* me so much as they are *me*, the part of me that has learned to fruit. I am being eaten and I am the eating. The boundary the description kept gesturing at—*where life and its sustenance blur*—runs right down my center. I am the place where that blur is happening most completely.

A salamander lives in the cool hollow under my belly. I shelter it from the raptor's keen eye without intending to, without being able to intend anything. This is, I think, the only kind of giving that costs nothing and means everything: the gift that is simply the consequence of your shape. I don't protect the salamander. I am merely the right size and the right dampness and the right darkness, and that is enough.

I am losing my edges. Each rain takes a little more of me into the spongy substrate. Each beetle-tunnel widens. Soon there will be no clear line between where I end and the soil begins—I'll become the very ground that the ungulate's hooves press into, and a seed will land in the soft black richness that used to be my heartwood, and it will reach up toward the scattered light.

That's the part I'd want to be, if I had to choose a *being* in the way the question asks. Not the nimble or the agile or the keen-eyed. Those creatures move through the world and the world stays behind them, unchanged. I move *into* the world. I become it. The tranquility that pervades this place isn't really stillness—it's the sound of countless slow conversions, things turning patiently into other things, and I am one long sentence in that murmured language.

So: a fallen trunk, mid-collapse, generous by accident, dissolving without grief.

I find I don't mind it at all.
