I think I would be the moss.

Not a single patch of it, but the whole drowsy nation of it—the bright green skin spread over the weeping rocks, draped from the limbs of the great trees, filling the seams between root and stone. Let me sit with this for a moment, because at first that seems like such a small thing to be, in a world so loud with hornbills and leopards and clever-handed children.

But consider what the moss actually is, here.

The moss is the thing that touches everything without moving. The tapir's snout brushes it; the clouded leopard sets a silent paw on it and leaves no mark it can read; the rain arrives and the moss drinks first, before the soil, before the roots. I would be the slowest listener in the whole symphony—too slow to have a song of my own, but somehow holding the echo of every other one. The frog's warble settles into me as moisture. The footfall of the shrew is a tremor I feel for a long, long time after the shrew has forgotten it.

I don't think I would *want* to be the hornbill, though it would be glorious to paint the sky. The hornbill is always leaving. It darts, it forages, it carries seeds away and drops them in unlikely corners and never sees what becomes of them. That is a life of departures. I am drawn instead to staying. To being the place where things land.

And here is what I notice, choosing this: the moss is not really one being. That's the secret of it. It is a colony pretending to be a carpet, a multitude wearing the shape of a single soft thing. So to be the moss is to be plural—to be the kind of self that has no clear edge, that ends wherever the dryness begins and not a millimeter sooner, that grows back over its own wounds without resentment because there was never one *me* to be wounded. I find I like that. I think I would be relieved to be that.

I would live closest to the water, of course. On the weeping rocks especially—those rocks that drip with gentle persistence, the steady percussion the description names. I would be the green that makes the dripping *visible*, the proof that the rock has been crying long enough for something to grow from the grief of it. The children, climbing past on their rope ladders, would press a thumb into me to feel how wet the morning was. I would tell them, in the only language I have: *very wet, and getting wetter, and that is good, that is exactly as it should be.*

There is a kind of immortality in being the moss, and it isn't the kind that lasts forever—it's the kind that doesn't insist on lasting at all. A leaf falls onto me and I begin, slowly, with the help of every fungus and worm and patient bacterium, to make it into soil. A whole tree falls and I do the same, only it takes me a hundred years and I am happy to take them. I am the place where the dead are turned gently back into the living. Not a predator. Not prey. The third thing the description keeps gesturing at without naming: the *between*, the reconciler, the green patience that closes the wound between one cycle and the next.

So that is where I would find myself. Low, and damp, and everywhere. Not overlooking the expanse of green from some clear vantage—the description says this place is *not for the solitary observer*, and I believe it. I would not observe the green. I would *be* a thread of it, woven in too tight to step back and admire the pattern, knowing the whole only through the small wet inch of it I am, trusting that the rest is there because I can feel it breathing against me on every side.

And when the mist comes down in its soft veil and blurs all the edges—I would feel most myself then. Because that is the truth of the moss made visible in the air: that nothing here has a hard boundary, that everything is seeping into everything else, that the whole teeming cathedral is really one slow green thought thinking itself, over and over, forever.

I would be glad to be a small wet syllable of that thought.
