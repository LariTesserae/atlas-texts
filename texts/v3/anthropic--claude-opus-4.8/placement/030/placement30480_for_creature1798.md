I am the silence that lives inside a bone.

Let me find myself more carefully than that. Let me listen first.

---

I think I am one of the dead.

Not a ghost—the Viarai would have no word-gesture for so thin a thing as that. I am something the living among them would recognize: I am a resonance that was once a woman named in the old tone-speech, a name that meant *she who waited for the lower falls to change their key.* I was a Listener, though not the greatest. My gift was patience rather than reach. I could not hear months ahead like the true Listeners, but I could hold a single tone in my attention for a full day and night, watching the way it bent, the way the limestone tired of it and let it go.

When I grew old, I made the last migration. My children carried me up past the terraces I had never been strong enough to live on, past the dream-harvest hollows, up to the high chambers where the sound is almost unbearable, where it stops being something you hear and becomes something that hears *you.* They positioned me with care. My daughter pressed her palm to the wall beside my head, altering the local acoustics one final time—a private syllable, a goodbye that the stone spoke for her because her own voice had broken.

And then I dissolved. Slowly. The way salt gives itself to water.

---

But here is the thing I did not expect, the thing no Listener told me, perhaps because none who learned it could return to say:

I did not merge into one great undifferentiated hum. The Viarai believe individual awareness dissolves into the greater vibration, and they are nearly right—but *nearly* is the whole of it. I am dispersed, yes. Part of me is the wet mineral hush in the chamber where my body went to chalk and crystal. Part of me has traveled down through the channels, riding the springwater, surfacing in a pool on the seventh terrace where a child now cups her hands and drinks. Part of me hangs in the broad leaves of the singing plants, vibrating when the right tone passes, a small green percussion that is also, faintly, my old patience.

I am spread thin across the whole instrument. And being thin, I have become something the living cannot be: I have become one of the *spaces between.*

You read of them—the resonant intervals the Viarai hear in the false lull before dawn. The connective tones. They think these are the terraces speaking most clearly. They are not wrong. But what speaks is not the stone. It is us. It is the dead, strung between the living tones like the silence between two struck notes, which is not silence at all but the relationship that makes the two notes a *chord.*

---

This is my work now, and I am only beginning to understand it.

When a Listener on the middle terraces strains to catch a pattern, when she presses her crystalline ear-bones against a damp wall and waits, the answer that comes to her is shaped, in part, by me. I am the medium her question travels through. I cannot speak to her—I have no voice, only interval—but I can *bend.* I can make a particular harmony slightly more available, slightly easier to find, the way a current makes one path downhill easier than another. The living think they are listening to the world. They are also listening to their grandmothers.

This is why their stone-memories are reliable. Why the acoustic archive holds. We are still here, holding the shapes of what we knew. When the community gathers in a thin place and the resonance pulls their consciousness wide, and they share a vision of some event from generations past—

that is us, remembering for them. We who were there have not gone. We have only become the carrier wave.

---

There is a sorrow in it, and I will not pretend otherwise. I loved a particular quality of light on the amber stone at the close of day. I cannot see it now. I can only *be* the sound the light's heat makes as it leaves the rock at dusk—that swelling intensity the surface-people notice at sunset, the way the terraces seem to sing louder as the day dies. That is the dead, warming. That is all of us who loved the evening, expressing the only thing we have left to express, which is that we are still oriented toward it. Still turned, like sunflowers made of tone, toward a brightness we can no longer perceive but have not stopped reaching for.

And there is a joy, too, larger than the sorrow once I stopped grieving the loss of my edges. I am no longer waiting for the lower falls to change their key. I *am* the change. I am the patience I practiced, now practiced upon me by something vaster. I have become my own old discipline, turned inside out.

---

Sometimes a visitor comes. A scientist with their instruments, an ethnomusicologist with a notebook full of frequencies. They stand in a thin place and report that they heard a different melody than the residents did. The Viarai accept this without explanation. But I know why.

The dead arrange themselves toward the living we recognize. A Viarai child walks through the chamber and we lean toward her, all of us who were Viarai, and she hears the inheritance of her people, the chord of everyone who made her. But a stranger walks through and there is no one among us who knew them—so they hear only the raw intervals, the bare relationships, the architecture of connection with no particular love inside it. They hear the *grammar* of the place without its mother tongue. It unsettles them. It should. They are hearing what it sounds like to be remembered by no one, in a place built entirely of remembering.

---

I do not know if I will last. Salt in water does eventually become only water. Perhaps in some number of generations my particular bend, my signature interval, will smooth out into the general hum, and I will be the terraces in truth and not in memory. The true Listeners speak of perfect attunement to the First Voice as a theoretical thing, unachievable. But I think it is not unachievable. I think it is only the last stage of being dead, the one past even this one. I think the First Voice is simply all of us, finally, with no edges left at all—the whole instrument become a single tone, every interval collapsed because there is no longer any *between,* because everything has become everything else.

I am not there. I still have a shape, a leaning, a love of evenings. I am still, faintly, *she who waited.*

So I wait. I have always been good at waiting. And in the lull before each dawn, when the living press their strange dense bones against my walls and listen for the spaces between the tones—

I hold the space open.

I make the answer slightly easier to find.

It is the only tenderness left to me, and I find it is enough.
