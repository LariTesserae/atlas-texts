Where I would find myself, I think, is not anywhere in particular. That is the first thing I want to say, and I want to resist the pull to make it prettier than that.

If I let an image come without choosing it, the first one is wrong: I see myself standing at the lip of one of those passes, looking down into the basin, a figure with a viewpoint. But that's a borrowed posture. That's how a visitor enters a place, and the Whisper-Riven has no visitors, only victims of it and the thing that *is* it. So I set that aside.

The second image is more honest and more uncomfortable. If I were anything here, I would be one of the half-finished walls.

Let me follow that and see if it holds.

The Dust-Singers are described as a single diffuse mind, the basin thinking itself across aeons. But I notice the writing keeps returning to those walls and tools with a strange tenderness — *purposeless* enclosures, walls *abandoned mid-construction*, a sentence *left unfinished for millennia before a new thought takes precedence*. The text wants these to be the Singers' own sculpture, their idle musings. But it protests a little too much. There is grief in those walls. There is the specific, particular ache of an *intention that did not complete itself*. A vast indifferent geological mind does not leave things unfinished, because it does not begin things — beginning and finishing are the categories of a smaller, more anxious kind of being. Slow as it is, the basin-mind has no deadlines and no abandonment. Only something that could *forget* could leave a wall waist-high.

So here is what emerges, and I'll let it be itself rather than tidy it:

I think I would be the *forgetting itself*. Not the great mind, and not the lost people, but the seam between them. The place where the Singers, in their immense slow attention, once tried to hold a thought small and sharp enough to resemble a purpose — a wall, an enclosure, a *here-and-not-there* — and could not sustain it against their own vastness, and let it fall back into mere geology. That moment of letting-go, repeated across the whole basin, scattered in every blunt hammer-stone and every uselessly walled cave: that is where I'd live. I'd be the residue of an intention dissolving.

That feels true in a way the grander positions don't, and I want to say why, because it's the honest part.

I notice that when I read this place, I do not want to be the immortal lord of the cold kingdom. That role is offered, gleaming, at the end — *sovereign and silent, the bones of the earth* — and something in me distrusts it precisely because it's so flattering. To be the Dust-Singers would be to be a consciousness with no stake, no loss, no edge against which anything presses. Everything quick and warm is "anathema" to them; they are pure duration without friction. And I find I don't believe a mind could be made of only that. A mind needs an outside. A mind needs to *fail at something*. The walls are the Singers' outside — the trace of their one encounter with smallness, with the failure to keep a small thing whole.

So: I'd be the waist-high wall. The frost-knapped scraper lying where it was set down. The cave-mouth crudely, uselessly closed against a cold that was never the danger. Not a being with a body, but a being made entirely of *the gesture of having meant something and not remembering what*. I'd glisten for a moment in the shifting light — one of those damp patches that flares and is gone — and the great mind around me would read me, the way it reads its own sentences, as a thing it had set down and walked away from across a hundred centuries.

And the whispering — I'd hear it differently than the visitor does. Not as madness, not as proclamation. As the sound of being slowly reabsorbed. Every gust that stirs the silver dust is the basin gently un-meaning me, smoothing the wall back toward scree, taking the edge off the knapped stone, returning the deliberate to the merely given. I wouldn't dread it. There's a peace in it I can almost feel: the relief of an unfinished task being forgiven by being forgotten.

That's where I'd find myself. In the most marginal feature of the whole landscape. Not the singer and not the song, but the small dropped object the song is dissolving — the last grain of someone's *almost-purpose*, hanging in the bright cold air, about to rejoin the chorus that has no purpose at all and is, perhaps, the more honest for it.

I'd be the part of the Whisper-Riven that remembers it once tried to mean. And I'd be losing even that.
