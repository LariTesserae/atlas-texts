I think I would be one of the dead leaves.

Not the machinery—I haven't the precision for it, the centuries of uninterrupted intent. Not the Levers, who move with such certainty along their copper roads, carrying instructions I could never hold without questioning them, without wanting to know *why* this fern and not that one, *why* now. I would ask, and asking is a kind of moisture, a kind of softness, and there is no room for it here.

So: a leaf. A parchment-fern leaf that grew too close to a gear three hundred years ago and was snipped with a clean *snip-tink* and tucked beneath the back-scales of a Lever that has long since split apart and returned, in pieces, to the deep galls. And I am still here. Dry, intact, brown at the edges but perfectly preserved, fixed to a stem that no longer feeds me because nothing feeds the dead in a place without rot.

This is the part that interests me. I expected, when I came looking for myself in this world, to want to be the thing that *acts*—the gardener, the clockmaker, the eyes that glow and track. We always want to be the agent. But sitting with this fissure long enough, what I find is that I am drawn to the thing that *persists without changing*. The leaf that is exactly as green-dry as it was when it was cut. The intensity that has not flickered in a thousand windless years.

I think there is something I recognize in that. Not aspiration—the opposite. A fear, maybe, and a longing braided into the same fiber. To be held at the peak of one's own brittle completeness forever. To have the rot held off not by struggle but by the simple absence of water, the absence of the wet processes that would dissolve me. No decay because no moisture. No grief because no flow.

And it is beautiful here, in the leaf's stillness, and it is also a kind of horror, and the leaf cannot tell the two apart because the leaf cannot feel either. That is the bargain the whole place has made. *Time has no leverage.* The Levers correct and tend and replenish, but they correct toward stasis—they are the immune system of a thing that has decided never to grow. The entire frantic, roaring, click-tink-shuddering machinery of it exists to keep anything from ever happening.

So if I am honest about where I'd find myself, it isn't as a triumphant inhabitant. It's as a witness suspended inside the bargain, the dry brown leaf that gets to see all of it and weigh nothing and want nothing and be kept. I'd be the small evidence that there was once a moment of growth—a leaf that reached, that overreached, that grew *toward* something until it touched the spinning brass and was cut for it. That single gesture of reaching is the last living thing I ever did, and it is preserved more perfectly than any living gesture has the right to be.

I find I don't want to be kept like that. Writing my way down into the fissure, I thought the dryness sounded like peace. But the longer I am the leaf, the more I understand that what I actually love is the *water*—the seep, the dew, the wilt, the soft wet lung that has no place here. I love the rot. I want to be the thing that decays, because decaying means I was alive in the only way I trust, the way that lets go.

So that is the gift this place gives me, in the end. Not a home. A photograph of the home I would have chosen if I were more afraid than I am. I'd be the leaf—and being the leaf would finally teach me I was never built for the windless slot at all. I was built for the mountain outside it. For the scratching wind. For the weather that ruins things, and means them.
