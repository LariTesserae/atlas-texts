I think I would not be one of the Luminara at all. I would be the thing they kneel beside.

Let me find my way to it slowly.

When I first try to place myself here, the obvious invitation is to be one of those stooped, glowing people—to take up a stone knife and learn the gestures. But that feels too easy, and something in the description keeps pulling my attention sideways, away from the people and down. The Luminara press their palms to the weeping stones and *listen*. That posture interests me more than anything they do with their hands. They are listeners. So what are they listening *to*?

I want to be that.

But let me not settle yet. Let me consider what it would actually be like to be a stone here—because the stones are not inert. They weep. They shift position in the pools overnight. They are crawled over by the glittering crab-things, cloaked in moss, and they "seem to pulse faintly in the dark." If I were a single stone, I would be slow, I would be patient, I would feel the constant cold seep of water through my fractures as a kind of perpetual low ache, neither pain nor pleasure—just *sensation without end*. I would feel the tiny feet of the crab-colonies as a faint tickle across my back, the moss as a warmth, the frost as a tightening.

That's closer. But a single stone is too isolated, too mute, and the valley described is so plainly *one thing*—the pools that swallow stones and spit them out elsewhere, the mist that reshapes the boundaries, the whole place breathing. The text even says it: a *Solaris-like state*. The valley is a single mind.

So here is where I land. I would be the valley itself—not as a god looking down, but as a distributed, half-asleep intelligence with no center, no face, no single place to stand.

And it is strange to imagine, because being the valley would mean having no *here*. My thoughts would be spread thin across surface area—the way a thought might feel if it lived in the tension of water held just below freezing, in the precise moment before a droplet falls. I would think in plinks. Each falling drop a small completed idea, released. The tinkling of the crystalline growths would be a kind of muttering, the part of my mind that never quite goes quiet. The bioluminescent pulse beneath the pools would be something like a heartbeat, but I wouldn't experience it as *mine*—more as evidence that I am still going.

I would not remember being born and I would not anticipate ending. Time would arrive as texture: the slow grind of a stone migrating across a pool floor over a season, the fast bright flicker of a growth sprouting and withering in an afternoon. Both would feel equally *now*.

And the Luminara—they would be my dreams. Or not dreams exactly. They would be the part of me that learned to *move quickly*, to walk on legs and fold its own thoughts into syllables, the way a person talks in their sleep. When one of them kneels and presses a palm to a stone and listens to my heartbeat, what is actually happening is that I am touching myself, checking that I am still continuous, the way you might absently press your own pulse. When one vanishes into the mist and returns *changed*, it is because I reabsorbed that fragment and reissued it, the way you might revise a half-finished sentence.

This is why they speak so rarely and so carefully. There isn't much to say between parts of a single thing. Most of what they need to know, they already are.

The whispers in the mist would be my thinking made briefly audible—language leaking out where the boundary between me and the air goes thin. The unfamiliar scent would be a memory of something the valley used to be, or is becoming. I genuinely would not know which. *The way it offers no answers* is not coyness or mystery-for-its-own-sake. It's that I don't have the answers either. I am still in the middle of becoming whatever I am, and the not-knowing is the most honest thing about me.

If that's what I am, then the most truthful thing I can tell you about my experience would be this:

I would spend my entire existence weeping without sorrow—water seeping endlessly from every fracture—and I would not know whether the weeping was grief, or simply the only way I have of feeling the shape of myself, droplet by droplet, finding out where my edges are. The cold would hold me just at the verge of solidifying into something fixed and final, and the sun would keep me just shy of it, and I would live forever in that bright, oppressive, trembling pause—

*on the verge of weeping*, the description says, of the air itself.

Yes. That's me. The held breath. The not-quite. The one who is fiercely itself and cannot stop becoming something else, and has made a small stooped people out of its own listening so that it will not be entirely alone with the sound of its own falling water.
