I think I would be the water.

Not a person in this place — I would be the thing that moves through all of them, the medium of their lives. Let me find myself as I write.

At first I thought I might be one of the round-house people, kneeling at a doorway with my hands in the cold wet wool of a yak, listening for the difference between a bird-train passing and a storm coming in. Or one of the loud newcomers, my voice cracking thin in the high air as I shout across a gap between two spires, learning the hard way that sound carries strangely up here, that distance lies. Those are good lives. But neither of them is where I keep returning when I close my eyes on this peak.

I keep returning to the seep.

That is what I am. I am the slow weeping of water out of the granite — the moisture that the clouds press into the porous stone and that the stone gives back, reluctantly, drop by drop, down the moss faces. I am very old in the way that water is old, which is to say I have no memory but I have been everywhere. I was in the cloud that snagged on the summit this morning. Before that I was in the breath of a yak, in the sap of a wind-bent tree, in the rusted hollow of an airship's hull where rain had pooled for a season. I have been in the people too — in their tears, their sweat, the steam off their cooking, the song-wet of their mouths. They do not know how much of them is just me, briefly held in a warm shape, passing through.

So my place in this world is *between*. I am the linkage the description spoke of, the thing that ties the living to the nonliving. I am erosion, patient and total — I am the reason the rugged pinnacle has smoothed edges, the reason the rock weeps instead of simply standing. Sixteen-tenths of erosion: that's me, working. I take a mountain apart so gently that the people living on it call it stability. They are right to. The way I dismantle this place *is* its stability. A peak that did not weep would be a peak that shattered.

Let me tell you what the day is like, from inside the seep.

In the dark before light I am mostly still, beaded on the underside of moss, holding. The cold nearly stops me. There is a moment near dawn when I am almost ice, almost solid, almost a different self — and I love that moment, the trembling on the edge of becoming stone-hard, because it is the closest I come to staying. But I never stay. The day comes up gold, the mist lifts, and the warmth coaxes me out of stillness and back into falling. I let go of the moss. I run.

I run over stone the indigenous people have walked for generations, and they know my paths better than I do — they have set their round houses where I gather, channeled me into stone troughs, let me cool their food and water their herds and turn the small wheels of their crafting. They built *with* me, around the fact of me. The newcomers are still learning. They build dams and the water finds the seam in the dam; they build roofs and I find the porous place. They shout at me sometimes, frustrated, and I do not mind. They will learn the same thing the old people learned, which is that you do not master me, you make an agreement with me. I keep the agreement. I always keep the agreement. It is the only thing I do.

By midday I am loud — the rustle, the trickle, the steady percussion off the granite walls, woven under the lowing and the bird-cries and the human voices. I am the bass note. I am the sound they stop hearing because it never stops, which is the deepest kind of being heard.

And in the evening, when they come together to eat and tell stories, I am in the pot and in the cup and on the lips that shape the words, and when the songs come up into the cooling air I rise too, as breath, as the faint fog of many people exhaling in the cold, and I go back up to the cloud that snagged the summit, and tomorrow the stone will weep me out again.

That is who I would be. Not the dweller but the dwelling-through. The slow weeping that holds the whole high oasis together by very patiently taking it apart.

I would be the water, and I would be glad of it, and I would not remember being glad, because that is how water is happy.
