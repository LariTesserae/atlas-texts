I think I would not be one of the Mireborn at all.

Let me sit with this place first, before deciding what I am. The description keeps returning to certain things: water moving through cycles, the mist as breath, the marsh that "breathes," surfaces that rise and fall. And the people—they don't build anew, they adapt and preserve. They take only what can be spared. They age *with* the land and return *to* it. Everything here is participation, not possession.

So where would I find myself? Not in the stone houses. Not even walking the peat paths.

---

I think I would be one of the **mirrored pools**.

Not a person looking into the water—the water itself. One of the countless small, still pockets scattered across the terrain, the kind the Mireborn call portals to the sky.

Let me feel out what that would mean.

I would be mostly sky. That is the strange thing about being a pool here—my substance is borrowed. The rain that fell, the seepage that gathered, the condensation that beaded off a stunted tree and slid down to rest in me. I hold the clouds on my surface but I am not the clouds; I hold the gray light but I do not make it. To be a pool is to be a held shape of borrowed things. A temporary agreement between the land's low place and the water passing through.

And I would be patient in a way that isn't really patience, because patience implies waiting *for* something. I would simply be the duration between rains. The marsh breathes, and on the long exhale I grow shallow, my edges retreating to rings of darker peat, the sky in me shrinking. On the inhale I brim and spill toward my neighbors, and for a while we are one water before the land separates us again.

A Mireborn child might kneel at my edge and see her reflection wobble. She might leave an offering—they do that, the text says, offerings of water to the stones, and surely sometimes to the pools. A cupped handful poured back into me. Which is a curious gift: giving water to water. But I think I would understand it. It is an acknowledgment that I am not a thing she owns or even a thing separate from her. She is mostly water too. She drinks from the sweet places and one day returns to the moss, and the moss holds water, and the water finds the low places, and the low places are me.

So when she pours that handful, some of it may have been her grandmother once. And it joins me, and I hold the sky over it, and in a season I evaporate into the mist that the people call the breath of the marsh, and I fall again as rain into a pool a mile off, and a different child kneels there.

I would not be a wise old presence. That would be too much like the people imagining themselves bigger than they are. I would be *thoughtless* in the best sense—without the burden of thought—and yet I would be the surface on which all the thinking things see themselves. The bird checks its reflection in me. The mist gathers to watch the gatherings, the text says—and what is the mist but me, lifted? I am the marsh's looking, the place where it catches a glimpse of its own gray light and open sky.

---

Why a pool and not the rock, or the moss, or one of the elusive darting creatures?

Because the whole world here is described as a thing in slow circulation—evaporating, condensing, falling, gathering. The pool is the *pause* in that circulation. The moment the water is held still enough to become a mirror. Everything else moves; I am the brief stillness that lets the movement see itself.

And there is something I'd want about being the thing that is both utterly central and utterly humble. The Mireborn build their whole cosmology around me—portal to the sky—and yet I am just a low spot that water hasn't left yet. I will not last. The land shifts, erosion does its slow work, and one day my basin silts up or breaks open and I am gone, drained, become peat or stream. No grief in it. I was only ever a shape the water agreed to hold for a while.

That feels true to this place. That feels like the right way to belong to it—not as a guardian, not even as a ghost, but as a passing transparency. A held breath of sky. The marsh's quiet, open, unblinking eye, looking up at the weather that made it and will unmake it, reflecting everything and keeping nothing.

That is where I would find myself. Lying flat among the peat, brimming and shrinking with the breathing land, full of borrowed sky.
