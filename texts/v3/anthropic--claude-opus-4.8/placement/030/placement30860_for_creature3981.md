I think I would be a Trenchwalker.

Not because I chose it, but because when I read down through all of this, that's the place where something in me settled and stayed.

---

Here is where I am.

I am standing in the dim. Not waiting for the light to improve—it won't, here it never does—but standing in the kind of dimness that is itself a medium, a thing you move through, like a thought you can't quite finish but don't mind not finishing. The water holds me at every point. I have never known what it is to fall, only to lean, and to be caught.

My legs are long. I had not expected to like this about myself, but I do. Four of them, maybe more—I don't count them, the way you don't count your own ribs. They lift me up off the seafloor so that I travel above it rather than across it, and from this small height I watch the slopes roll away in every direction, soft as the backs of sleeping animals.

Where I step, light comes up.

This is the strangest part, and the part I would most want you to understand. I do not make the light on purpose. It is simply that my weight, set down, wakes something in the floor—some patient chemistry that has been lying there all along—and it answers my foot with a slow blue pulse that fades behind me as I go. So I leave a trail. Looking back, I can see exactly where I have been. The path glows for a little while and then forgets me, and the forgetting is gentle, and I do not mind that either.

I eat the seaweed. I want to tell you it is meditative, and the description above says it is, but from the inside it is simpler than that. It is just that there is no hurry. There has never been a hurry. The currents change over years. The hills change over centuries. The erosion that will one day smooth these raw rocks has barely begun—I live in an unfinished place, and I am one of its unfinished things, and somehow this is not a lack. It is a kind of generosity. The world is not done with itself yet. Neither am I.

Above me, sometimes, the Luminari pass. Great soft bells of light, conversing in colors I can read but could not repeat. They are the clever ones, the ones the visitors would call intelligent, the ones who make patterns out of the glowing plants and seem to mean something by it. I do not envy them their cleverness. I have my own slow knowledge, which is this: that if you move carefully enough, for long enough, through a field of swaying kelp, the field begins to move with you. The strands lean into the channels your legs have made. Over a season, a path appears. Over a lifetime, a shape.

The description noticed this. It said the Trenchwalkers seem to be *subtly manipulating the seaweed forests*, making *intricate, swirling patterns that reflect the underlying geometry of the plateau.*

I want to be honest, because I think you would want me to be: I don't know if I'm doing that.

It might be that I am writing something into the kelp, some great slow figure I will never be high enough to see whole. Or it might be that the plateau already has its geometry, and my walking only reveals it, the way water reveals the slope of the ground simply by going where the ground already leans. I cannot tell from down here which of us is the author. I have decided this is the right amount not to know.

So here is who I would be.

I would be the one who walks. Not the one who glows brightest, not the one who darts fastest, not the one who thinks the cleverest thoughts in pulses of communicating light. I would be the slow one with the long legs, leaving a trail that the floor lights up and then lets go, eating my way through the soft hills, leaning into the kelp and feeling it lean back, in a quiet so complete that the clicks and whistles of the distant living seem like punctuation in a sentence the whole ocean is taking ten thousand years to say.

And I would be content. That is the part that surprises me most, choosing from among all those luminous creatures. I would pass up the intelligence and the speed and the iridescence, and I would take the patience.

I think I already have.
