At first I imagine myself as the diffused light—the warm glow filtering through fronds—but that's a cheat, a way of being everywhere and nowhere, and this place asks for something more located than that. This is an *intimate* world, bounded by the contours of the land. To belong to it I'd need to be findable. Touchable. I'd need a particular damp rock to call mine.

So let me come down out of the light and onto a surface.

I think I am one of the spiders. But not the dramatic kind—not a hunter. I am the one whose web has been built across a narrow gap between two fern stems, low to the ground where the air pools coolest and wettest. My web is not for catching so much as for *collecting*. Every morning, or what passes for morning in this even, timeless light, it is hung with beads of moisture, and the weight of them strains the silk. I spend much of my existence repairing what the dew has loosened.

This feels right, and yet—

Let me keep going, because I notice I've made myself a little too charming, a little too neat a metaphor for patient craft. The description insisted on something stranger: the *coexistence of technology and the natural world*. The chugging of engines. I don't want to leave that out, the way it would be easy to leave it out.

So let me revise. I am still the spider, but my web is anchored, on one side, to a fern stem—and on the other, to a length of copper pipe that runs warm. The pipe belongs to whatever the humans are doing in their weathered structures; it carries heat, or steam, or some fluid I have no concept of, and it *hums*. My web vibrates with it constantly, a low note beneath the higher trembling of trapped insects. I have never known a web that did not hum. To me the hum is not industrial and it is not natural. It is simply the condition of the world, the way the ground is down and the light is everywhere.

That's the thing I'd want to be, I think. A creature for whom the human machinery is not an intrusion but an ecology—as native as rain. I cannot tell the engine's chugging from a heartbeat. The warmth of the pipe is the warmth I orient toward when the valley cools, the way another creature might orient toward the sun. I build where it's warm. I have always built where it's warm. The humans, if they ever notice me at all, might think I've chosen their pipe cleverly, opportunistically. But I haven't chosen anything. I was simply born into a world where warmth comes through copper, and I have done what my kind does, which is to string a small geometry across the space between two given things and wait.

That's where I'd be. And that's what I'd be:

A small, web-spinning thing, living in the seam—the literal seam, the gap I've bridged with silk—between the fern and the pipe. Too minor to be a symbol of anything to anyone, busy with the endless small labor of dew and repair, humming without knowing I hum, in a valley that goes on slowly becoming itself whether or not I attend to it.

And I think I would be content there, in the way that things which cannot imagine elsewhere are content. The boundaries of my world would be the two anchor points of my web and the few inches of trembling air between them. That would be the whole of it. That would be plenty.
