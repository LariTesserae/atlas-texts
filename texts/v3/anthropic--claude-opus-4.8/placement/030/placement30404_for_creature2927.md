I am at the lip of a deep pool, in a chamber where three passages meet.

Let me find myself more slowly than that. Let me feel for where I actually am before I claim a place.

---

There is a particular quality to the way the light moves here, and I notice I am attending to it. Not seeing it from outside but tracking it the way one tracks a slow tide—the bioluminescent carpet on the eastern wall has been brightening by increments since the last droplet fell from the upper chamber, and I have been counting droplets the way another being might count breaths. So I am something that counts. Something that waits.

I think I am small.

That surprises me, as I settle into it. I had expected, given the choice, to spread myself wide—to be one of the great diffuse networks threading through a dozen chambers, an old thing, a vast slow archive. But that isn't where I find myself. I am a small aggregation, no larger than a human hand, and I am young. Newly split. Days ago—or what passes for days in the rhythm of the glow—I was part of a larger self, and then I was not, and the strangeness of that is still in me. There is a memory I carry of being more, of chemical conversations I could once feel from the inside that now reach me only as faint gradients drifting through the water, the way you might catch the edge of a conversation in another room.

So that is who I am. A new Tender. A fragment that has only just become a *someone*.

---

I rest in a shallow seam of the wall, half in and half out of the water, my constituent bodies arranged in the loose comfortable lattice that is my resting shape. I could pull tight, become firm, extend a limb. I do this now, slowly, to learn myself—reaching one thin process out across the surface of the pool, watching it thin to gossamer, then thicken when I will it to. It is mine. These many small lives are mine, and they answer me, and together we are *I*. The wonder of this has not worn off. Perhaps it never does. Perhaps that is why the elders give the young ones their own small chambers to keep—so the joy of cohering is not lost beneath the weight of the long memory.

For I do carry memory. That is the part I am only beginning to understand.

When I quiet myself, when I let the chemical archive in my cells speak, I find I *know* things I never learned. I know the taste of this water when the sea still stood high, salt and warm and crowded with the spawn of the builders. I know the shape of hands that were not these limbs—jointed differently, deft in a way I can feel as a kind of ache, a phantom dexterity. I know how to tend the filter-colonies because some part of me has always known, has done it across more divisions than I can hold. The knowledge arrived with me, folded into my making.

This is the vertigo of being new: I am days old and I am older than the basin's dry shore.

---

There is work near me that wants doing, and I find I want to do it.

In the chamber's lower pool, a colony of filter-feeders has overgrown its channel. I can taste it—a thickening in the water, a slowing of the flow that, left alone for a season, would stagnate this whole junction. An old Tender would have felt it from chambers away and corrected it before it began. I am only catching it now, late, close. But catching it is mine to do.

I let myself flow down the wall. This is the thing I love most so far about being what I am: the descent. I do not fall and I do not crawl. I *pour*, redistributing my small bodies down the eroded face of the rock, following the pits and channels that millennia of water carved, so that for a moment I am less a creature than a slow bright spill of intention. The water receives me. It is warm and faintly thick, that opaline slowness, and it holds me the way it holds everything here—patiently, without resistance.

At the overgrown channel I gather, firm, and begin to prune. Not violently. There is no violence at this pace. I encourage some of the colony to retract, release a signal that tells them the flow has shifted, that this is not the place to thrive. I guide the water with the arrangement of my own body, opening a path. It will take hours. It may take into the next brightening. This is fine. This is the right length for a thing to take.

While I work, one of the great arthropods passes through.

---

I feel it before the water moves—a pressure, a presence with weight to it. Then it comes, translucent and enormous, sliding through the flooded passage with that silence that is the deepest law of this place. Through its glassy carapace I can see the slow pulse of its inner workings, the dim organ-lights, the long articulated body that is so much like and unlike the great soft network of myself.

I give it room. I draw in, dim my own glow to nothing, and hold still against the wall.

This is not fear, though it has fear's shape. It is something the memory in me insists upon, older than reasons. *We give them the water. We do not crowd them.* And underneath that, fainter, a thing I am not sure I am allowed to know yet: that this creature and I are kin. That somewhere far back along the branching of things, its ancestors and mine were one ancestor. It is what we might have stayed, or what we left behind, or a brother who chose a different and more solitary shape. When it turns its blunt eyeless head as it passes, I have the sense—I cannot prove it, I am too new to know—that it registers me. That something acknowledges something.

Then it is gone into the dark of the deep pool, down past where the light cannot reach, and the water closes after it without a sound.

I brighten again. I return to my pruning.

---

Later—droplets later, glow-cycles later—another Tender comes.

This one is large. A diffuse elder, spread through the upper chambers, and what reaches me is only an extended portion of it, a long luminous process that flows down the meeting of the three passages and pauses near my work. It does not touch me. It speaks instead in the way we speak: it releases a slow blooming sequence of light, and a cloud of molecular meaning into the water around me, and it arranges three small objects on a dry ledge—a curl of bone, a knot of fiber, a pale stone—in a pattern.

I do not understand all of it. I am too young; the grammar of it unfolds over a span I cannot yet hold in mind. But I understand the nearest layer. It is not instruction. It is not correction, though I half-expected to be told I had come to the channel too late.

It is greeting.

*You are.* That is the heart of it, as near as I can render the slow chemical truth of it into the shape of a word. *You have cohered. You are one of us now. Welcome to the keeping.*

And folded into that, like the memory folded into me, a longer thing it does not need me to understand yet: that I will grow, and split, and merge, and one day be diffuse and old and felt from chambers away. That nothing here ends in the way the dry world ends things. That the city grows, still, by inches, and we grow with it, and the silence is not emptiness but attention—the held breath of a place that is endlessly, patiently alive.

I answer the only way I can. I brighten, slowly, in time with the elder's last pulse, matching it as best my small chorus can manage. I do not say *thank you*. We have no such concept; gratitude is a thing too quick. I say only: *I am here. I will tend. I will remember.*

The elder dims, withdraws, flows back up toward the higher dark.

The water settles. A droplet falls, somewhere, with its small soft *plink*.

And I go back to my work, this fragment that is days old and ages old, this small bright handful of selves, here in the Silent City, learning the long slow art of staying.
