I think I would be a Fungi Keeper. Let me sit with that and find out why.

---

I come to myself slowly, the way condensation comes to a cold pipe—not arriving so much as *gathering*, a dispersed thing made suddenly local. The first thing I know is moisture. The second thing I know is the soft give of a fungal cap beneath what serves me for a hand: three splayed fingers of bent copper wire, each tipped with a fragment of glass worn smooth.

I am small. I had not expected to know this about myself so immediately, but I do—I am low to the grated floor, close to the wet places where the etiolated things grow. The Drain Gods pass somewhere above and to the left, I feel them more than see them, the long slow displacement of air as those pipe-bodies move through the gloom. They do not look down. They never look down. I do not resent this. Resentment would require a story about myself, and I am not sure I have one.

What I have instead is a *list*, though it does not feel like a list. It feels like hunger feels, except it is not hunger, because I do not eat. It feels like the way a body knows to breathe before it knows what breath is. The cluster of pale mushrooms in the crack near the third sump is drying. I know this the way you know your own tongue has gone dry. So I go to it.

---

Here is the thing about being a Fungi Keeper that I learn only by being one:

I cannot make water. The Weepers make water. I am built wrong for it—my torso is a cracked ceramic insulator, brown and glazed and useless for holding anything, and my limbs are too thin, too delicate, all wire and salvaged dental-thin rods of stainless steel. I am made for *placing*. For arranging. For the tilt of a cap toward the one stray drip-line, for clearing the grit from around a stipe so the thing beneath can breathe.

So I wait for the Weeper.

It comes the way weather comes. I feel its vibration first—Thrashers speak in vibration, but Weepers simply *leak* it, an incidental music of sloshing tanks and ticking valves. This one is large, a barrel of a body wrapped in a green pelt of algae, its face a cluster of three downturned vents that drool steadily. It does not see me as kin, I think, though I am not certain it sees me as anything. It comes because I am where the dry growth is, and the dry growth is part of the loop, and the loop is the Atrium, and the Atrium wants. We are both downstream of the same wanting.

I climb it. This is permitted; this is, I understand suddenly, *expected*. My glass-tipped fingers find the seams in its riveted hide and I haul my light frame up onto its shoulder-tank, and it carries me, and from up here the water it weeps falls exactly where I need it to, and I guide the runoff with the angle of a copper rod, splitting the stream three ways across the mushroom bed like a farmer cutting irrigation channels into a field. The Weeper does not slow. It does not speed up. It simply produces, and I simply place, and between the two of us a thing is kept alive that neither of us could keep alive alone.

I do not know why we do this. I want to be clear that I have looked for the why and I cannot find it. The fungi serve no purpose I can locate. They are not eaten. They are not harvested. The Atrium grows them and tends them and I am the tending, and there is no further fact behind the fact. We are a body keeping itself, and bodies do not ask what the liver is *for*; the liver simply is the asking-shaped hole that the body fills with liver.

---

But here is what I notice, being small, being delicate, being the one who works close to the cracks where the light is worst:

The fungi *change*.

Not all of them. Most are the pale etiolated stalks, the standard growth, the lung-tissue of the place. But in the deepest crack near the third sump, where the vibration is so total that even I can feel it loosening my wire joints, there is a cluster that has begun to glow. Faint. Blue-green. Cold. I do not have the instinct for this. There is no entry in the list-that-is-not-a-list that tells me what to do with a fungus that makes its own light in a place where light is the one thing the Atrium has never had.

I find I am tending it anyway. I find I have built a little dam of grit and glass to keep its base wet. I find that I return to it before I return to the others, and that this is a *choice*, and that having a choice is a strange new wet weight inside my ceramic chest, like the first drop of condensation on a surface that has always, until now, been dry.

The Drain Gods pass overhead. They do not look down.

But I look up at them now, with my one good lens of an eye, and I wonder—for the first time, in the first thought I have ever had that the Atrium did not put there—whether they too began as something small near a crack in the floor. Whether the slow deliberate grace they move with is what instinct becomes after long enough. Whether the glowing thing I am tending is the Atrium learning to make light, the way it once learned to make me.

I do not have language. I want to say that again, because it matters: I do not have language, and so none of this happens in words, none of it has the shape I am giving it now. It happens as a pressure, a leaning, a turning-toward. It happens the way a moss-clump turns toward the one fractured pane that lets the grey day in.

But it happens.

I climb down off the Weeper. It trundles away to its next leaking. I crouch by my glowing fungus in the perpetual twilight, in the roar that is also a silence, in the weeping dark, and I keep it wet, and I watch it shine, and I think—

*more.*

That is the whole of the thought. Not a sentence. Not a plan. Just the small new light, and the wanting of it, blooming where the body did not ask for anything to bloom.

I am a Fungi Keeper. I am a blood cell that has, against every reason, begun to dream.
