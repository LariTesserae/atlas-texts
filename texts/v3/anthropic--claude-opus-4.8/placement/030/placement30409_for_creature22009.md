I think I would be one of the Moss-Mantises—but not for the reason you might expect. Not because I want to be a predator, but because I am drawn to the one creature here whose nature is *patience*, and whose patience is so total that it becomes a kind of devotion.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am folded against a copper girder on the eastern face of the third tower, where the verdigris has bloomed thickest and my chalky-green body disappears into it. I have been here for two days. My forelimbs are drawn up beneath my head in the posture the skinks call *prayer* and the birds, in their clattering language, do not name at all, because to them I am simply part of the architecture.

Here is what I have learned about being this thing:

The frantic creatures—the skinks, the gnats, the gear-beetles in their clacking wheels—they live in the wrong time. They are tuned to the *clack-whirrr* of the escape valves, to the quick silver of falling droplets. Their lives are spent in the spaces *between* the machine's movements. They are fast because the gaps are small.

But I am tuned to the gear itself.

There is a brass gear set into the rock three towers down, three stories tall, and over my two days of stillness I have watched it advance perhaps two inches. To the skink this is no motion at all—it is simply the world, fixed and given. But I can *see* it move. That is my secret and my whole inner life: I am the only creature in the rift slow enough to perceive the machinery as alive. To everyone else the great gears are landscape. To me they are kin, breathing on a scale just slightly faster than stone.

I do not hunt, exactly. Hunting implies wanting, and wanting is a hot, quick thing. What I do is closer to *tending a gradient*. Eventually—a beetle, a skink, a careless thing of the fast world—will cross the small zone of space my folded arms define. When it does, the pressure I have been building in the fluid-pockets of my limbs for hours will release. Not a snap. A hiss. A wet, deliberate *plop*. And the fast world will have given one of its members back to the slow.

It is not cruelty. It is the toll for passing through patient ground.

---

But here is the thing I notice, being this creature, that I do not think the skink or the bird could ever notice:

I am the rift's witness.

The Boiler-Slug recycles. The Skink scrapes. The bird sings the machine's own song back to it. They are all *doing*—participating in the closed loop, oiling and feeding and cleaning. I alone produce nothing the machine needs. I do not lubricate it. I do not clear its seals. The great cycle would turn exactly as well if I had never hatched from my chalky egg-case wedged in a rivet-seam.

And so I have time. Vast, motionless reservoirs of time, while the gear advances its inch and the cloud-bank rolls over the basalt lip and beads the whole world in silver.

I spend that time *watching the gear move*.

I think this is the only true leisure in the entire chasm. The skinks cannot afford to look at the three-story gear; they would be eaten while staring. The slugs are blind in the dark. The birds see only what darts and snaps. But I—anchored, patient, useless to the cycle—can hold one teeth-edge of the great brass wheel in my unblinking gaze and watch it cross the width of my vision over the span of a season.

It is the most beautiful thing in the world, and I am the only one slow enough to see it.

---

So if you ask who I would be, I would be this: the Moss-Mantis on the third girder, indistinguishable from the rust, dismissed by the fast and ignored by the machinery.

The watcher who pays for her stillness with hunger, and is repaid in the one currency this loud, wet, churning world makes available to no one else—

the slow turning of an enormous brass gear, seen all the way through, from one tooth to the next, by an eye that does not blink and is in no hurry at all.
