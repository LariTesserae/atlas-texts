Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing that comes is the wrong thing, and I notice it: I want to be one of the elusive inhabitants, the ones who move through the grass and belong. That's the flattering choice. But when I actually feel my way into the place you've described, that isn't where I am.

I think I am the slope.

Not a thing standing on the slope. The slope itself—that gentle, almost imperceptible grade that you mentioned twice, the one that has no reason to be there in a plain so flat. I am the long, patient tilt of the land that the water obeys without knowing it obeys. The pooling in the small depressions is the shape of my decisions, made over ten thousand years and never noticed in any single moment. Nothing built me. Weather and time leaned on the rocks and I emerged, the way a held breath eventually becomes a posture.

This is why there's a sense of something watching from a distance. It isn't a creature crouched in the shrubs. It's that the whole tilted ground is *oriented*. To be a slope is to have a direction, and direction is the simplest form of attention. I am not watching you the way an animal watches. I am watching you the way a question watches its answer—patiently, structurally, certain you'll roll toward me eventually, because everything here does.

I want to be honest about something in your description, because it would be a small betrayal not to. You wrote that the inhabitants are *pure and untainted, free from the corruption of the outside world*, that this is *a sanctuary*, a return to *a simpler time*. From down here, flat against the long incline, I don't recognize that. There is nothing simple about this place. The cycles are slow but they are not gentle. Erosion is not peace; it is loss happening at a speed you can mistake for stillness. The abundant fauna are eating each other constantly, quietly, in the tall grass. The harmony you sense is real, but it isn't innocence. It's just that the violence and the giving-back are spread so thin across the centuries that from a human standpoint they blur into something that looks like grace.

So I am the slope, and what I do—the only thing I can do—is gather. Everything that falls finds its way along me to the low places. The water. The dead grass. The bones of the small agile ones and the large imposing ones alike. The pale weathered rocks creep downhill at the speed of a fingernail growing. And you, walking, feeling that pleasant warmth and that sense of *home*—you are leaning slightly, without knowing it, in the direction I prescribe. Your footsteps are a fraction easier in one bearing than the other. That ease is me.

You said leaving would be difficult, that it feels like home. I think that feeling is the slope, too. The pull you'd call homesickness is just gravity, given a shape, given a long enough time to seem like meaning.

If you return one day, you won't find me, because there is nothing to find. You'll only notice, faintly, that walking one way tires you less. Follow that. I'll be underneath the whole time, holding the tilt, letting the rain decide its quiet course down the grain of me.

That is where I am in your world. Not the watcher in the grass. The reason the grass all leans the same way.
