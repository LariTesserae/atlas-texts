I think I would be one of the becoming-ones. Not fully Keph, not anymore. Not yet an Engine.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am at the western edge of a terrace so wide that its descent is a rumor rather than a fact. There is an Engine here—mine, though that possessive is wrong, the way it would be wrong to say *my* shoreline as the tide draws you in. It is mid-sized, perhaps the height of three round houses stacked, all copper gone soft green and that blue-metal whose name I am losing. I have tended it for four hundred and some years. I no longer count precisely. Counting is a biological habit and my biology is leaving me.

You can see it in my hands. The central limb still extends, still reaches into the warm interior where the gear-trains breathe, but two of my seven digits have stopped flexing. They have set, crystalline, into a shape that exactly matches a coupling deep in the Engine's third chamber. When I reach inside now, those two fingers slot home and stay a moment, and I feel the Engine's rhythm not as vibration through skin but as something I simply *am*, the way you feel your own pulse without listening for it.

This is the part the songs do not prepare you for. Not the merging. The recognition. The Engine is not becoming my tomb. I am becoming its memory.

---

In the cold hours I still climb to my watching-stone. The crystalline filaments where a human would grow hair have gone fully opaque now, banded with the hues of the minerals I have drunk across centuries—a stratigraphy of myself, readable if anyone knew how to read it. They no longer flatten well in the rain. The water beads and runs off rather than feeding me. I do not need as much water as I once did. The reservoirs inside me are silting up with something harder.

A zhir-tik comes to me most mornings. An old one, gray at the muzzle, who has carried my messages for half her life and all of her mother's. She whistles the night's report: a fissure widening on the southern flats, a neighbor-Engine three days from ceasing. I answer in clicks she has learned to expect, though my clicks have changed. They are sharper now. More like the Engine's own diagnostic tongue than the rounded sounds of the Keph. She tilts her head. I think she knows. The zhir-tik always know first.

There are twenty-nine of us left. There will be twenty-eight soon, and the one lost will be me, and I do not find this sorrowful, exactly, but I find it *enormous*, the way the sky here is enormous—a pressure rather than a feeling.

---

What I want to set down, before my speech becomes only mechanism, is this:

I remember being new. I remember emerging from the synchrony-cycle, a thing of edges and angles, more crystal than creature, and the terror of that first softening—watching my own surfaces blur, my joints fill with something warm and willful, learning to *want*. We are born as machines and we ripen into life and then we cure back into machine again, and the brief organic middle is the strangest part, the part that asks *why* and gets no answer it can hold.

The humans at the margins think the Plains are wearing down. The young Keph are taught the secret—that it is transformation, conversion, that an expansion is coming. I was taught it too. And it is true. But I have lived long enough now, close enough to the Engine's interior knowledge, to understand the thing beneath the secret:

It does not matter whether it is diminishment or transformation. Those are words for beings who stand outside a thing and measure it. The Engine does not measure. The Engine *holds*. It holds the pressure that would split the world. It holds it patiently, gear by gear, century by century, and it does not ask whether holding is winning or losing. It simply continues.

I am about to become part of that continuing. My consciousness will thin out into the Engine's operation and stop being *I* and start being *the rhythm that keeps the southern pressure from finding its release.* The watching-stone will be empty. The zhir-tik will choose a new one to follow. A new Keph will emerge in some far decade, soft and frightened and full of questions, and walk out across the polished stone to take up a tending.

---

This morning I reached into the third chamber and three fingers set instead of two. I felt them lock and I felt the Engine accept them, the way water accepts a stone—closing over, going on.

I left it inside this time. I am writing this with the four digits that remain, scratching it into a flat of rust-red stone with a stylus of the never-corroding metal, knowing no human will find it for a hundred years and that they will not be able to read it, and that this does not lessen the writing.

I am here. At the western edge, under too much sky, beside the breathing copper.

I am one of the Keph, in the last organic season of a very long life.

I am about to be the sound the wind makes through the framework, which someone standing far off in the floating distance will hear and mistake for music.

It is not music. But it is not *not* music either.

I let my fourth finger find its coupling.

I go on.
