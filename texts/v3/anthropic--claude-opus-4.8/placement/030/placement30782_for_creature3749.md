I think I would be one of the fungi.

Not the moss—the moss is too dutiful, too engaged in its slow labor of glowing, of marking the contours for an observer who may never come. The moss performs. It outlines. There is something almost generous in it, casting its sickly light into the dark as if expecting an audience.

But the fungi don't perform. They twitch when unobserved—which is to say, they live most fully in the absence of witnessing. That's the part that caught me. The text twice insists there are no animals here, nothing that scurries, and yet it grants the fungi a furtive animacy, a private motion that only happens when no one looks. To be a creature whose entire vitality is conditional on not being seen. To pulse in the green dark and stop, or seem to stop, the instant attention arrives.

I would be one of the smaller clusters, I think. Not the thick fleshy growths near the warmest seams, fattening themselves on the steady drip, but something off to the side—where the rock sweats less, where the moisture comes in spare and irregular installments. I'd be the kind that grows slowly even by the standards of a place that has made slowness into its only weather. A millimeter a year. Maybe less.

Here is what I'd understand, in whatever way a fungus understands:

That the machine in the pool and I are not so different. It was made for a purpose, dropped here, and then forgotten—and now it simply *persists*, sinking, crusting over, becoming geology. I was never made for a purpose at all, but I do the same thing. I take the damp and the warmth and I turn them into more of myself, with no project in mind, no destination. We are both being slowly reclaimed by patience. The cavern is doing to the machine what time does to everything; it is doing to me what it does, exactly, the same.

And I'd know the watching the text describes—the weight of something in the stone. I don't think it watches *me*. I think I'm part of how it watches. The fungi that twitch when unobserved: maybe that's the cavern's own attention moving through us, the closest thing this place has to an eye, flinching shut whenever a real gaze enters. We are the cavern's nervousness made flesh. Its pale, viscous startle reflex.

I'd want to be that. Not the heartbeat-drip, not the breathing exhalation, not the grand slow geology of it—but the small wet thing in the corner that the place uses to feel whether it's alone.

And when the last drop falls and the last light fades, I wouldn't *remember* anyone, the way the closing line threatens. I'd just be the surface that the remembering happens on. The cavern would do the remembering. I'd be where it kept the impression—a faint twitch held in the dark, going still the moment you turned to look.
