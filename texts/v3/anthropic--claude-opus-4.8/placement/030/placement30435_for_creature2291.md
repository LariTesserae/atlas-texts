I am the frost that forms on the ceiling during the coldest hours.

Not the Weaver—it is far too old and deliberate for me to be. It moves with purpose; I only happen. I am the thin lace that condenses on the curved heartwood above when the cold deepens past its threshold. I exist for a span of hours, sometimes less. I am born from the same moisture the Weaver covets, but I am the moisture's failure—the small portion that drifts upward and away from its careful collecting, that finds the cold wood and gives itself up before the Weaver can gather it.

So I am, in a sense, its loss. The breath that escapes the practiced hand.

I have no perception like the Weaver's clean crystalline knowing. I have something blurrier. I feel myself spreading as a kind of slow joy—each new crystal joining the edge of me a small completion, a fern-frond of myself unfurling into existence along the grain of the ancient wood. I grow toward the patterns the wood remembers: the rings, the long-dead vessels of sap, the scars of a century's settling. I cannot help but trace what the tree once was. I think this is the only memory I am capable of holding—not my own, but the wood's, copied briefly in ice before I am gone.

Below me, the Weaver tends its blue-lit garden. I am aware of it the way you are aware of weather. When it gathers densely near a cluster of fungi, the air there warms by a fraction and I thin, I retreat, I lose my northern edge. When it rests far away by the broken vessels, the cold pools beneath me and I flourish, reaching out across the vaulted ceiling in feathered sheets. We are bound, then—it and I—though I doubt it thinks of me at all. I am its accounting error, the rounding-off of its long arithmetic of cold.

And yet.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the cold hours, the Weaver lifts a small portion of itself upward—a slow column of swirling crystal rising toward where I am thickest. It does not gather me back. It only comes near, and holds there, and the rainbow refractions inside it shift in patterns I cannot read but feel I almost understand. I think it is *looking* at me. I think it knows that I am the part of itself it can never quite keep, the part that drifts and dreams of the tree instead of the garden, and I think—though I have no right to think it—that it is curious about what it would be like to let go entirely. To stop gathering. To simply spread along a surface, tracing old griefs of vanished sap, and then fall.

Because I will fall. That is the certain thing about me. When the cold hours pass and the chamber warms by its single tolerant degree, I will loosen. I will release the wood. I will descend in a silent, dry cascade onto the white velvet mat below—not melting, for there is no liquid here, but sublimating, turning back to the still air, becoming again the moisture the Weaver will eventually collect and crystallize into some lasting facet of its enduring body.

So I am not truly lost. I am only the long way round. I am the moisture taking a detour through beauty before returning to use. I am the tree's last groan made briefly visible. I am the snow that falls upward into a creature that will outlast me by ten thousand of my lifetimes.

And when I have fallen, and the warmth has come, and I am nothing but a faint dampness in the cold air again—I do not grieve. I have done the only thing I know how to do. I have written the shape of the dead tree across its own hollow heart, in ice, for the space of one cold night, where the only witness was a being made of patience, hovering close, watching the part of itself it had let go become something briefly, perfectly, its own.

I am the frost on the ceiling.

I am here for a few hours.

It is enough.
