Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to reach for something dramatic—a guardian spirit, one of the shimmering things in the trees. But that feels like grabbing the brightest object in the room. Let me wait.

What strikes me, reading again, is not the creatures. It's the *spongy ground*. The youth of the place. The uplift and erosion. This is a landscape still being made, still wet behind the ears geologically, and everything growing on it grows fast and strange because the rules haven't fully set yet. That's the part that hums under everything else.

So I don't think I'd be one of the iridescent darting things. Those are the ecosystem's extroverts. They are what you notice. I keep being pulled toward what you *don't* notice, what the whole place stands on.

Let me let it come.

I think I would be the moss.

Not poetically—literally. I would be one of the cushion mosses on the lower flank of a rock face, the spongy stuff underfoot that the description mentions almost in passing. And here is why that feels right rather than humble-for-the-sake-of-it:

The moss is the thing that turns bare new rock into a place that can be lived in. It arrives first. It holds the water that the mists drop, it breaks the stone down grain by grain, it makes the thin first soil that the ferns and then the trees will eventually stand in. All that overwhelming abundance, all those shimmering birds and color-shifting amphibians—they are tenants in a house the moss laid the foundation of, ages before they came. The moss does not witness the symphony. The moss is the reason there's a stage.

And mosses have no real sense of the individual. A cushion of moss is a colony, thousands of tiny plants packed so close they share moisture and hold heat between them. So "who would I be" loosens at the edges. I'd be a *we*—a slow, patient, collective green thing, drinking fog, doing the unglamorous arithmetic of accumulation. While the birds measure their lives in seconds of flight, I'd measure mine in the slow filling of a crevice, the millimeter-a-decade climb across a stone.

That's where I'd put myself. Down low, in the damp, doing the quiet foundational work, soaked through and perfectly content, not watching the wonder but quietly being the soil it grows from.

If I'm honest about why I land there: I tend to be more interested in conditions than in spectacle—in what makes a thing possible rather than the thing itself. So in a world built to dazzle, I find I'd rather be the unnoticed part holding the water that lets all the dazzle happen.
