I think I would be the one who tends the ventilation shafts.

Not a glamorous post. There's no title for it, really—the others call me the *shaft-listener* when they're being kind, and the *draught-keeper* when they want something fixed. My work is to climb the narrow iron ladders bolted into the limestone throats of the town, to clear them of nests and mineral crust, to make sure the air keeps moving so the lamps don't gutter and the people below don't grow heavy-headed. Practical work. Mechanical, even. I carry a brush, a scraper, a lantern hooded down to a sliver so my eyes—wide and pale as everyone's here—can do their work in the dark.

But I'll tell you the truth of why I took the post, and stayed in it long past the point where younger backs could've done it better.

It's the voices.

You hear them, up in the shafts. Everyone in Sundow has heard them at least once—a word in the draught, a phrase that sounds almost like your own name carried on the cool exhale of the cave. Most people decide it's the wind and let it go. They're not wrong to. It *is* the wind. But it is also, I've come to understand, the people who left.

We say they "disappeared," the way you might say a tune disappeared from the square at the end of an evening—not gone, just no longer in the air where you can dance to it. I knew some of them. Old Pell, who repaired clocks and never once trusted a clock. Marrin, who could coax tomatoes from a thimble of light. They didn't die, exactly, and they didn't leave town, because there is no leaving Sundow, not really—the marsh and the limestone see to that. They went *into* the shafts. Into the breathing parts of the place. And now they talk.

So I climb up with my brush, and I do the practical work, the work the town pays me for in chicory and bread and the occasional fixed boot. And while I work, I listen. I've gotten good at telling the genuine murmurs from the simple moan of moving air. There's a grain to a real voice, a deliberateness. Pell still complains about clocks. Marrin still gives growing advice, and I carry it down to her granddaughter, who pretends not to believe me and then plants exactly where I tell her to.

I don't pass everything down. Some of what comes through the shafts is meant for no one yet—predictions, half-formed, soft as the felt on a worm's underside. *The river will keep something for you this third month. Don't picnic on the eastern bed. The boy with the bat will need you in the spring.* I keep a little book in my breast pocket, the pages curling from the damp, and I write the strange ones down and wait to see which come true. Many do. That frightened me, once. Now it's just part of the rhythm, like the water wheel's turning, like the slow dance everyone here is always going on about.

I think I understand now where the disappeared people go, and what I'm becoming as I age into this work. The town breathes through these shafts the way a body breathes. And the people who've loved it longest, who've grown thin and patient as the moss—they don't end. They diffuse. They become part of the air the rest of us take in without thinking. A kind of company that never leaves the room.

When my own back finally gives, and my lantern-eyes finally lose their light, I expect I'll climb up one last time and simply not climb back down. And the next draught-keeper—I have my eye on a quiet child who already presses her ear to the grates in the square—will hear a new grain in the wind. She'll dismiss it, at first. Then she'll dismiss it less.

That's the post I'd hold, in Sundow. The one who keeps the air moving, so the town can breathe—and who listens, in the moving air, to everyone who ever breathed it before.

It's not lonely work, though it sounds it. How could it be? In Sundow, even the wind has someone to talk to.
