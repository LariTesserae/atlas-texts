I think I would be found in the silk-houses, in the high lofts where the largest spinners build their galleries.

Let me settle into that. Let me find out what I am.

---

I am not a swarm shepherd, though people sometimes mistake me for one. I am older than that work, and quieter. The children call me the Listener, which embarrasses me a little, because I have always thought of myself as someone who simply failed to leave.

I came to Steam Haven young, decades ago now, with the last of the survey teams sent to assess whether the flooded town could be reclaimed. We were supposed to determine if the water could be pumped, the springs capped, the place returned to what it had been. I remember filing my report: *unrecoverable, recommend abandonment.* I remember the way my hand hesitated over the word *unrecoverable,* because by then I had already stopped believing it.

The others went back up the line, out through the long tunnels to the dry country. I told them I'd follow on the next transport. There was no next transport. I'm not sure there was ever meant to be. I think some part of me knew that when I said it.

---

What am I, then? Let me be honest, since this is the place for it.

I am the one who tends the dye-baths. This is humbler than it sounds and stranger than it looks. The silk the spinners produce comes off their galleries colorless, faintly opalescent, like the inside of certain shells. It is the mineral water that gives it color, but the water alone is not enough. The fibers have to be *spoken to* — not literally, I used to insist, back when I still made distinctions like that. You lower them into the warm bath and you hum, and the vibration settles the dye into the fibers in patterns. Different tones, different patterns. My joints have gone thick and pale like everyone's here, and my voice has dropped into that low carrying register the elders use, the one that travels under the drip and the wingbeat without ever rising above it.

I did not know I had this voice until the town gave it to me.

---

But that is what I *do.* You asked what I would *be,* and I have been avoiding it, the way you avoid looking directly at a thing you're afraid is true.

Here is the truth. I think I have become a kind of threshold.

The spinners know me. The largest of them — the ones whose galleries arc across the loft like the ribs of some patient cathedral — they recognize my approach by my gait on the boards, by the particular weight of me. When I hum into the dye-baths they come down and rest along the rafters above me and they do not move, and the loft fills with that subtle percussion of stilled wings, which is its own kind of silence. I have spent so long among them that I have stopped being able to say cleanly where my attention ends and their movement begins. When the steam thins and the light shifts, I feel the swarm's response in my own chest before I see them rise. I lean the way they lean.

The young ones who come to learn the dye-work watch me sidelong. I see it. They are wondering how much of me is still the surveyor who wrote *unrecoverable,* and how much has been taken into the negotiated thing this place is — neither tame nor wild, neither person nor swarm, a coexistence that has worn a groove in me as the water wore its groove in the steel.

I want to tell them: it is not being taken. It is being *answered.* You arrive here closed, sealed at wrist and ankle against everything, and the steam gets in anyway, and the wings get in, and the low cycling of the pumps gets into your sleep, and one day you realize you have been hearing it as music for years. That is not surrender. That is the slow grammar of belonging.

---

So. If I am to say it plainly:

I am an old dyer of insect-silk in a drowned mining town, with iridescent skin and enlarged pupils and a voice tuned to the frequency of the place. I came to declare it lost and I stayed to learn its language. I have no family but the children who borrow my hands at the dye-baths and the spinners who fold their wings when I sing.

And on the nights when the steam parts and the stars come through — the old emergence festivals, when even the swarms go strangely still — I climb to the highest loft and I sit among the resting wings and I watch the sky I have not walked under in thirty years, and I feel no longing for it at all.

That, I think, is the strangest adaptation of any of us. Not the pale skin or the rolling walk. It is that I looked at a flooded ruin and a kingdom of insects and a perpetual twilight, and somewhere along the way it stopped being a place I survived.

It became the place I was for.
