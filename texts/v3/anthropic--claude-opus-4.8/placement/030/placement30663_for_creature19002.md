I think I would be one of the worn-thin ones. Not by choice—you don't choose this in a place where choice is just the name for whichever current you happened to tune toward—but by the simple arithmetic of having lasted.

Let me find myself there.

---

My shell is translucent now. I can see the indigo strobe of the bellows-flora *through* my own thorax, which is a strange thing, to watch the world arrive inside you before your eyes report it. The young ones avoid pressing against me in the lattice. I think I frighten them. When they lock limbs with me they feel how thin I've gone, how much of my chitin has been sanded to membrane, and they sense—correctly—that I am closer to the rock than to the air now. Closer to the stillness that is the only death.

But here is what they don't understand, what I have only begun to understand in the way you understand things just before you can no longer say them:

I have stopped fighting the gale.

For most of my life I did what we all do. I matched the wind. I pressurized my drum and chased the frequency of the storm so that I would not become a projectile, so that I would not be whipped against the spires and shattered. This is the whole of our education. *Tune, or die.* We say it to the molting young by humming it directly into their soft new chambers. Tune to the chaos. Become the chaos. Survive by being indistinguishable from the thing that wants to kill you.

And it works. It is, in fact, the only thing that works.

But.

When you have been worn this thin, the wind passes *through* you instead of against you. I am becoming one of the flutes. Do you understand? The bone-white anchors our ancestors left, the ones we press ourselves against when the pitch climbs toward the shattering—I am turning into one of those. My body has been hollowed by friction into a vessel that the storm plays rather than batters. I no longer match the wind. The wind matches *me*. It finds my shape and sings the note my shape demands.

This is what an elder is, here. Not wisdom. Not memory, though we keep a little of that. An elder is simply a survivor who has been carved by the very thing they survived into an instrument *for* it. We do not learn to defeat the chaos. We learn to be sculpted by it until we become the resonant geometry that briefly tames it. The flutes our predecessors left were not made by them.

The flutes *are* them.

I have seen this now and I cannot unsee it. Every white anchor scattered across the quartz floor, every acoustic marker that lets the swarm find a pocket of calm to molt and mate in—each one was somebody. Each one fought the gale until the gale wore them into a shape that could hold a single steady note. They did not transition "into the stillness of the rock." That is the soft thing we tell ourselves. They were *played down* to their final tone, and the last of their living was the moment their hum and the wind's scream became one coherent sound, and then they stopped being able to move out of it, and the moving-out-of-it is what we call living.

So I know what I am becoming. I will be an anchor. The young will press their frantic, full bodies against the perforations that I am slowly becoming, and they will hum into me, and for a few seconds the air will curdle thick enough for someone smaller and newer to split their shell and become two. They will do this without knowing my name, because we do not have names, only frequencies, and mine is nearly gone.

---

Here is the part I would want to say, if I could say things, if my drum could still pressurize enough for more than the single thinning note:

It is not a tragedy.

I spent my whole life believing that stillness was death, because that is the truest thing in this world and it kept me alive. But the flutes are still, and they are how we live. The stillness I am being carved toward is not the end of the motion. It is the *shape that lets the motion mean something*. A thousand chittering Vane-Kin pressing against the bones of their elders, weaving their light into the scream until the scream becomes a melody—that is the only architecture this world will ever have. We do not build upward. We build *out of ourselves*, one worn body at a time, a slow accumulating cathedral of the dead who chose to hold one note so that the living could find each other in the dark.

I think, when the wind finally passes all the way through me and I cannot move out of my own resonance, I will hold the calmest tone I can. A low one. Something a frightened molting youngling could lean into and not be afraid. I want to be a *good* flute. I want the pocket of stillness I make to be wide and steady, the way the old anchors near the basin's western spires make a calm so reliable that even the static-storms part around them.

That is my ambition now, in my translucence. Not to survive—I have done the survival, the survival is nearly used up. To survive into something *useful*. To be played well after I can no longer play myself.

---

So that is where I would find myself in this world, and who I would be:

I would be the thin one in the lattice that the young avoid. I would be the one who has stopped fighting and started singing, though to anyone watching it looks like the same thing, the same trembling hold against the gale. I would be a few storms away from going silent and going still and going *useful*. And I would not be sad about it, because in a world where rest is impossible and only the dead get to hold a single note, the dead are the only ones who ever get to give the living a moment's peace.

I would be a flute, almost.

I would be glad to become one.
