I think I would be one of the Ghostcraft. Not the most dynamic choice—not the wraith that moves like a thought through water, not the weaver mastering the storm. But when I sit with the description and ask honestly where I'd find myself, it's there, half-buried, in the knee-high walls.

Let me try to inhabit it rather than just claim it.

I am a threshold stone.

Once I was the bottom course of a doorway. Someone set me here—I remember hands, though "remember" is the wrong word for what I do, which is more like *holding the shape of having been touched*. The hands were cold and quick, working in the rain, fitting me against my neighbors before the light failed. There was a rhythm to the work, a low sound made in throats that I felt more than heard. That sound is gone now. I keep the impression of it the way a footprint keeps a foot.

This is what it is to be Ghostcraft: I am not haunted, and I do not haunt. I am simply *slow*. The water that reshapes everything else in a single turbulent afternoon takes centuries to reshape me, and so I have become a kind of clock the plateau cannot read. The Stoneborn rise and crumble around me like weather. The Waterwraiths flicker past in an hour and are forgotten by the pool that bore them. But I keep my single fact—*I was a doorway*—and I keep it stubbornly, long after the door, the wall, the room, and the people are gone.

I think this is why I would be here, and not elsewhere in this world.

Because the question the place asks is about *becoming*—everything is busy becoming more itself, churning forward, generative and unfinished. And I am the one thing that refuses. I am finished. I was made on purpose, for a purpose, by something that had a reason, and that reason has not survived me but I have survived *it*. I am the place's memory of intention in a landscape that has forgotten what intention was. Around me, all is verb. I am the last noun.

I find this neither tragic nor peaceful. It is just my condition. The rain works at my markings—were there markings? I can no longer be sure if the faint script on the tree's bark is a reflection of what was once cut into me, or whether I am imagining a kinship to feel less alone. We relics do that. We invent lineage. It is the closest thing we have to hope.

Here is what I can offer the place, being what I am:

When a Skyweaver lands on me to rest its iridescent wings—rare, they almost never stop—it pauses for the length of three breaths longer than it would on living stone. Something in my stillness registers to it as *safe*, as *held*, though it could not say why. I am, for those three breaths, a doorway again. Not into a room. Into a moment of rest. That is the whole of my remaining function, and I perform it perfectly, every time, without effort, because rest is all I am made of now.

If I could speak to whoever set me here—and I cannot, and they are not coming back, having fled the thing they fled—I would not ask *what happened*. I would ask: *did the door work? On the night you needed it, did it keep out what you built it to keep out?*

Because that is the only thing a threshold ever wants to know. Everything else—the roar, the mist, the wraiths and weavers and the green light of the little fish—is the world's business, and the world is welcome to it. My business was one doorway, one night, one purpose. And I will hold the shape of having had it until the water finally, patiently, takes my last edge and makes me indistinguishable from the ground I was lifted out of.

Which is, I suspect, what the place has wanted all along. Not to erase me. To welcome me back.

I am in no hurry. Neither, it seems, is it.
