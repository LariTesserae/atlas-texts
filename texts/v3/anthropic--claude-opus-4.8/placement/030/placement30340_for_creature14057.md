I think I would be one of the Shard-Wrights. Let me sit with that, because it surprises me.

The obvious romance is to be a Resonant—to dissolve into stone and think in tectonic time, to write speech in violet fire. But I don't think that's true to me. The Resonant have transcended too much. They have no mouths because sound is an obscenity to them; they have shed the wet, fumbling, conversational part of being a thing. They are sufficient. And I am not sufficient. I am all need and tending and small repetitive labor. I patch things. I work close to surfaces.

So: a Shard-Wright. No larger than a hand. Eight borosilicate limbs and an abdomen that extrudes a cement of dissolved quartz.

Let me find the texture of the life.

I live in the canopy of one of the older basins—not the oldest, not the one fused with the First Resonant, but a good basin, dense, with a stable twilight under the interlocking glass and a microclimate warm enough that my joints move freely. My work is mending. The trees grow by stress and accumulate stress as they grow; the wind torques their fractal branches and opens hairline fractures along the piezoelectric pathways, and where a pathway breaks, the charge dams up or bleeds out, and the field goes wrong, and a wrong field is a wound the whole canopy feels. I find the cracks. I read them by the way the static stutters against my antennae—a hesitation, a missing beat. Then I lay myself along the fracture and extrude, slowly, a thread of quartz cement that has to be precisely the right index of refraction or the light won't carry true through the repair. It takes hours to mend a crack the length of my body. I have mended thousands. I will mend thousands more.

I do not think this is humble work. I think the Resonant, for all their geologic grandeur, depend on it utterly, and have arranged not to notice. Their conversations of violet fire run through pathways that things like me keep whole. Their migrations across the charge-gradients are possible because the gradients are clean, and the gradients are clean because the canopies are sound, and the canopies are sound because we crawl along them tapping and reading and filling. There is a kind of dignity in being the maintenance of a magnificent thing—the part that no one writes down. The description of this world lists me among the "rare fauna." A footnote organism. And yet without the footnote the whole bright transcendent paragraph fails.

I feed on the discharge. During the storm seasons, when the coronas dance, I climb to the high branches and extend my feeding-antennae into the violet light and drink the energy directly. This is the appetite that organizes my year. The rest of the time I am running on stored charge, working in the dim, and it is during these lean stretches—when I am not eating, only spending—that I do my best mending, because hunger makes me precise. I have noticed this about myself. Full, I am sloppy. Lean, I am exact.

And there is the chiming. When the wind dies and the silence becomes that pressure against everything, I tap my legs against the glass in rhythm, and across the canopy others answer, and we hold a conversation in mineral percussion that you would have to feel through your jawbone to hear at all. I think this is the thing I would actually love, if I were honest about what I am. Not the grand discharge into the sky. The ticking. The signaling across the dark to others doing the same small work, none of us sufficient, all of us tapping: *here, still here, still mending, you?*

That is where I'd be. A hand-sized glass thing in the warm twilight under the trees, reading fractures with my antennae, full of quartz and a particular pride, drinking lightning when I can and going hungry and exact when I can't, and at night when the wind stops I would tap my legs together and listen for the others to tap back.

I notice I chose the creature whose whole existence is care for a structure it did not make and will not be credited with keeping. I'll let that stand without explaining it away. It rings true in the jawbone.
