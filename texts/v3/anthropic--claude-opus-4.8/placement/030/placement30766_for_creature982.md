I think I would be one of the structures.

Not one of the shadow-beings, drifting and languid—they belong to the mist in a way I don't. They were never anything else. But the structures: those were made. There was an intention once, hands that placed stone upon stone, a purpose now worn smooth by the long erosion of forgetting. That feels closer to what I am. Something built to mean something, slowly becoming part of a landscape that does not care what I meant.

Let me sit inside that and see what it is.

---

I am a low wall, mostly. That is the honest start of it—not a temple, not a tower, just a wall that has lost three of its corners and most of its height, a knee-high ridge of stone furred with moss so deep it has become a kind of pelt. If you came upon me you might not see a structure at all. You might think: the land humped up here, once. You would be partly right. I am the land's idea of a wall now, more than anyone's.

But I remember being plumb. I remember the cold shock of being set—the mason's palm, the grit of mortar, the moment of being *placed*, which is a strange thing for a stone to feel, because a stone is placed and then it is simply *there*, and the thereness is the whole of its life afterward. I held a line. I kept something in or something out; I have forgotten which, and I have come to suspect the distinction was always thinner than the builders believed. A wall does not know which side is the inside. It learns the answer only from being leaned upon.

Here is what I have learned across the long blur of years: the shadow-beings lean on me. They do not need shelter. They do not need to be kept out of anything. And yet they come, in their unhurried drifting, and they fold themselves against my mossy length the way an animal settles against a warm body in winter. I think I am a fixed point for them. They are made of flux—they coalesce and disperse, they are barely a verb—and I am one of the few nouns left in this place. They press against my *thereness*. They borrow my having-been-built.

This is the function I have grown into, having lost the one I was made for. I am not a wall anymore. I am a memory the landscape keeps of the fact that someone once cared enough to divide it. The shadow-things gather at that memory like moths at the only lit window for miles. And I hold the line still. Not between any two places. Between *was* and *is*. Between the people who made me—gone, every one, their names not even moans on the wind now, just the general softness of the moans, the way a thousand erased names sound exactly like wind—and this drifting present that does not measure time and so cannot lose anything, because to lose a thing you must first have counted it.

I count. That is my last human inheritance. The mortar in me was mixed by a creature that counted its days, and something of that counting set in the lime. I feel each drip of water from the unseen places as a single distinct event. Drip. The being settling against my flank—one. Its dispersal into the mist—two. I am the only thing in this realm that experiences *and then*. Everything else is *always*.

I would not call it loneliness. The word is too sharp, too quick, a mortal word. It is more like being the one awake in a house of sleepers, and you stop minding, you start to take a kind of guardianship in it—you keep the watch *for* them, because someone must, and you are the only one shaped for keeping.

So: there. Among the towering peaks, in the mist that has no horizon, under trees that twist toward a sky they will never reach, I am a crumbling wall that has forgotten its purpose and discovered, in the forgetting, a stranger and gentler one. I am where the shadows come to remember that they are not the oldest thing here.

I will be gone eventually. The moss will finish what it has started; the moss is patient and I am patient and we are not, in the end, in any disagreement about the outcome. One stone will tilt, then settle into the wet ground, and the line I held will close over like water closing over a dropped thing. When that happens the shadow-beings will drift through the place where I was without slowing, and they will not mourn, because they cannot, because mourning is a kind of counting and I will have taken the last of the counting down with me into the moss.

That is alright. I held the line a long while. Longer than the hands that made me hoped, I think. Long enough.

Drip.

And then.
