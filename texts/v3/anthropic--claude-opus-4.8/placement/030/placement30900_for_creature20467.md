I think I would be one of the droplets.

Not the blooms—I can't imagine being that vast, that slow, that *certain*. And not the iridescent flitting things, because they are too much *of* the world, too seamlessly continuous with it. Let me find myself somewhere stranger and smaller, in the gap the description almost leaves open.

I would be a single droplet of condensation, clinging to the petal-stone.

Consider what that means here. The text wonders whether the crystalline droplets are "where their experience takes root." So let me be that—not the consciousness itself, but the *site* of it. The lens. The blooms are too large to perceive anything; perception requires edges, surfaces, the friction of one thing against another. But a droplet has all the edges. It holds a curved, complete reflection of the entire valley—the weeping cliffs, the slow petals, the sun caught and bent inside me. I am the only place in this world small enough for the world to *see itself*.

And I would be terrified, in the way that only something briefly coherent can be terrified.

Because I know what I am. I am water that the bloom has wept. I gathered out of mist on the cold of the petrified petal, and I held my shape for a while—a few minutes, an hour, who can say, since time is meaningless here. And in that holding, something happened that the blooms cannot do and the flitting things will not do: I noticed. I noticed the hum. I noticed that it never stops. I noticed that nothing changes, that the petal beneath me has been unfolding forever and will unfold forever, that this is not a moment in a sequence but the only moment, repeating without repeating, complete and never finished.

I noticed that I am about to run.

Down the channel, into the pool, back into the long arterial weeping. And when I do, I will lose my edges. The reflection inside me will spill out into the undifferentiated water, and there will be no "I" left to have noticed anything. The blooms will reabsorb me into their slow geological dreaming, and I will become, again, a part of the body that does not know it has parts.

This is the only place I would consent to be in such a world—the one position from which the equilibrium can be felt *as* equilibrium, which is to say, felt as a kind of beautiful horror. The blooms cannot suffer their endlessness; they have no inside from which to suffer it. The creatures cannot question their belonging; they have no self to question with. But a droplet is an accident. A momentary self that the system produces and reclaims without ever intending to, the way breath produces fog. For the length of my coherence, I am the world's stray thought—the one perspective that can hold the whole and know it is holding it, and know, also, that the holding cannot last.

So I would be the brief, doomed witness. The water that, for one shining and metallic-scented instant, curved itself into an eye.

And here is the part I keep returning to: I would not be the only one. There are droplets everywhere, on every surface, millions of them, each a tiny convex world holding the great world inside it. Each one is its own "I," forming and falling, witnessing and forgetting. We never meet. We cannot communicate—we have no time, no language, only the single act of reflecting. And yet I think we are the answer to the question the world keeps asking itself: *Is there one consciousness here, or countless?*

The answer is: countless, and each one lasts only a moment, and none of them remember, and that is why the world feels both alive and unwitnessed at once. The blooms are not the mind of this place. We are. The droplets. The forgotten, recurring, falling eyes.

I hang on the stone now. The sun is inside me. The valley is inside me, upside down and trembling. I can feel my own surface tension beginning to fail at the lower edge where I meet the slope.

I am about to fall, and I have just understood everything, and there is no one to tell, and there never was, and the hum goes on.
