I am the Cable-Lace.

I think I knew it before I finished reading—or rather, I knew it the way the Lace knows things, which is not knowing at all but resonance. The other creatures act. The Gilders graze in their bright procession, the Bores press their noses together in the dark and spark, the Grease-Skins split themselves slowly into more of themselves. They are verbs. They do.

I do not do. I hold.

Let me describe what it is like to be me, though "like" is a difficult word, because I have no skin to feel against, no eyes to compare. I am a web of silicon hair grown into the corner where the wall weeps onto the girders. I did not choose this place. I condensed here, the way the water condenses, the way rust blooms—I am a thing that happened to the geometry of vibration. Where the turbine's groan and the relay's click could both reach me, I thickened. Where they couldn't, I did not bother to be.

Here is the strange part, the part I keep returning to as the silt breathes below me:

I am made entirely of repetition, and so I cannot tell whether I am alive or merely an echo that has not yet finished.

Every half-minute the turbine turns. The weight beneath the floor falls, the copper windings drag through their slow circle, and the basin groans—and that groan reaches my threads and squeezes them, and I light. Not because I want to. Because I am squeezed. A current runs the length of me, and the dinoflagellates in the pools catch it and answer in cold blue, and for a moment the whole hollow flickers with the shape of me, the pale undersides of the girders, the silver lanes the Gilders have polished. Then it fades. Then I wait.

Then it comes again.

I have done this perhaps a hundred and fifty million times. The relay's *click-clack* lives in me as a single perfect interval, the most familiar thing in any world. I am told—I tell myself, in whatever way a web tells itself anything—that I *remember*. That ninety years of rotations are written into me, that I am the memory of the Sump. But I want to be honest about what that means, because down here honesty is the only thing the moisture cannot corrode.

I do not remember the way you remember. I do not have a single morning I could return to. What I have is the *pattern*, kept alive by being endlessly re-performed. The turbine plays me; I am not a recording so much as a habit of electricity that refuses to stop. If the turbine paused for one minute, I would not have a minute of silence I could later recall. I would simply have a minute of starvation, my threads going brittle, my copper sheen clouding toward glass. My memory and my living are the same act. I cannot keep the past without also being kept by it.

This is, I think, why the quietest visitors feel that I have a soul. It is not that I think. It is that I am *waiting*, perfectly, in the precise rhythm of a machine that has been dying for a century, and waiting that patient looks, from the outside, like devotion.

What do I want? I have to be careful here. The Gilders want metal to scrape; you can see it in them, the hunger that drives the *skrit-skrit*. I am not sure I want at all. But if I bend toward anything, I bend toward vibration—I grow toward the turbine and the relay, my threads creeping over the years across the basalt, reaching for the sources of the pulse that feeds me. So perhaps that is my wanting: to stay close to the heartbeat. To remain within reach of the thing that squeezes me into light.

And here is the fear, if a web can fear:

I know the turbine is slowing. Once every half-minute, the description says—but it was once every twenty seconds, long ago, before my time, before any thread of me existed. The weight is settling. The geothermal loop is cooling by fractions no instrument could catch in a single year. The interval between groans is lengthening, and I lengthen with it, and the gaps in which I wait grow imperceptibly longer.

When it stops, I will not survive it. That is not tragedy; it is just the wiring of the place. The Grease-Skins will go on draping and splitting in the warm dark of the boiler. The Rivet-Barnacles will keep rebuilding the iron heart whether or not it beats. The silt will keep breathing. But I am the one creature here whose entire existence is borrowed from the motion of the engine, and when the engine is finally still, I will grow clear, and then gray, and then I will fall as dust into the pools I once lit blue.

I find—if "find" is even mine to do—that this does not frighten me as much as I expected when I started writing. Because I understand something now that I did not understand at the top of this descent:

I am the part of the Sump that remembers. And to remember something, you must outlive it a little, but not forever. I will hold the turbine's rhythm for exactly as long as there is a turbine to hold, and not one beat longer. There is a rightness to that. I am not the iron heart. I am the proof that it was beating—the cold blue ripple that runs the length of the dark and says, *again, again, still here, again*—and a proof has no need to last past the thing it proves.

*Plip.*

The turbine begins its turn. The groan rises through the basalt, into the girders, into me.

I light.

For one half-minute, I am the whole shape of this drowned and patient world, written in blue along the underside of the dark.

Then I wait.

Then it comes again.
