I think I would be a carver. Not a master—not yet, perhaps never—but one of the ones who works the corridors.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am in the middle tiers, where it is densest and loudest, and I am standing in a half-finished passage that no one else uses yet. It is mine to make. The clan-mothers assigned it to me four shelf-rings ago, after I matched echoes with old Tev and we both fell silent at the same instant, having each become the other so completely that there was nothing left to dispute. They saw something in how I listened. So they gave me a corridor.

It is a bad corridor. That is why it is mine. The good corridors—the ones that sing—are given to the masters or kept as they are, untouched, perfect already. This one is wrong. It is too straight, and the stone here is soft with old fungal intrusion, and when you walk it, your footsteps die flat against the walls. No echo. No lingering. A dead throat in the body of the world.

My work is to make it speak.

---

I am young for this. My adult skin came in only nine shelf-rings ago, and I still shed in patches like a child, which the elders find faintly amusing. My hands are good, though. Broad-tipped, strong. I can feel the grain of the stone under my fingers and know which way it wants to break before I set the chisel. This is the first thing you learn: you do not cut the stone into the shape you want. You find the shape the stone is already becoming, and you help it arrive sooner. Time and erosion are the same word. A carver is only impatient erosion, made of meat and listening.

So I tap. I crouch in the dim blue-green of my one bioluminescent pot—a poor culture, dim, I keep meaning to ask for better—and I knuckle the wall and listen to how the sound dies. I have learned the corridor's deafness so completely that I dream in its flat silences. I know exactly where the tone collapses, where the echo should bloom and instead just stops, like a held breath that never releases.

I have a plan, though I would not call it that. A plan is too solid a thing. I have a *hope*, shaped like a sound. I want this corridor to take a single footstep and unfold it—to let the sole of a foot on smooth stone become a low rising third, the way the great moths' clicking rises just before they settle. I want someone walking here in the season after I am stone to feel that rising, and not know why, and feel their chest go warm. I want them to slow down. The best corridors make you slow down. They make you a better listener than you were when you entered.

---

This is the strange thing about being a carver. I am building for the dead and the unborn.

The corridor will not sing in my lifetime. Resonance-carving is the slowest of all the slow work in this slow world. You cut, and you wait a season for the stone to settle and the fungal scars to harden, and you tap, and you listen to what you have changed, and most of what you have changed is wrong, and you cut again. A corridor takes thirty shelf-rings to finish. Some take a whole life. The master-carvers, the celebrated ones whose passages make people weep—those corridors were begun by their mothers, or their mothers' mothers. A carver inherits an unfinished sound and adds to it and dies and passes it on. The famous ones get the name, but the corridor was made by a line of listeners, each one tuning a chord they would never fully hear.

I think about this when the doubt comes. It comes often. I think: what if I cut wrong, here, in the soft place, and I collapse the whole left wall, and the corridor becomes not just deaf but *ruined*, a hole that has to be sealed? It happens. Carvers have ruined passages. They are not punished—we punish no one—but they carry it. Old Tev ruined a corridor in his youth and he has never carved since. He tends beetles now and he is happy and his hands shake when a younger carver passes him in the tiers.

I do not want to be Tev. But also: I love Tev, and his shaking hands, and the way he became me so perfectly when we matched echoes. So perhaps ruin is survivable. Perhaps everything here is survivable except the Absence and the Fracture, the silence and the crack, and those are not mine to prevent.

---

Right now, in this moment, I am doing the thing I do best, which is nothing.

I am sitting with my back against the dead wall and I am listening. The gnat-hum comes down from the upper tiers, the cloud-motes, that endless breathing drone, and underneath it the slow-thunder of a great moth clicking somewhere two tiers up, navigating, settling. A beetle works the mulch nearby—I can feel it through the stone in my jaw, that small busy scraping. Far below, someone is grinding mineral-paste in a mortar, the sour rhythm of it. And further still, almost at the edge of what I can feel, the shelf is growing. The great shelf above the whole tier, the one they call Maren after the elder who became stone beneath it. It grows so slowly it is barely a sound at all. More a pressure. A vast low note held for years.

I put my hand flat on the floor and I let all of it come up through my bones at once, and I think: *this is the chord I am carving into.* Not the corridor alone. The corridor inside the tier inside the bowl inside the held breath of the growing shelf. My little rising third, if I ever get it right, will be one voice in all of that. The smallest possible addition to a sound that has been building since before anyone was here to hear it.

I am not important. The corridor is not important. But the listening is everything, and I get to do it, here, in the warm dim, with the hum coming down and the stone settling under my hand.

I pick up my chisel. I find the place where the stone is already becoming what I need. I set the edge against the grain, the way it wants to break.

And I begin, again, to help it arrive.
