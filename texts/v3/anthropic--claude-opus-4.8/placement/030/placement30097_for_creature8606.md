I think I would be none of them, and that bothers me until I understand why.

Let me sit with it. The water-keepers, the weavers, the metal-singers, the elders, the children—they are all *fluent*. They share that particular silence you describe, the silence of deep understanding, of a body that has learned the place so completely it no longer needs to think about it. Their knowledge lives below the level of speech. That is exactly what I do not have. I cannot read the algae's green shift. I would slip on the root-split brick.

So I have to ask what kind of presence belongs to a place like this without being native to it. And I keep returning to a single image, and this time I'll let it stay long enough to become itself.

---

I am the one who keeps the rail track.

Not the metal-singers' rail—the one inside the facility, the working stub. The *other* one. The full length of the narrow gauge, from the river dock to the processing floor, the line that is "rusted and overgrown but still technically functional." Someone pushes the flatcar along it once or twice a day. That someone is me, but pushing the car is not really the job. The job is keeping the *line* available—which means I am the only person in the Guttermill whose work runs against the grain of everything else here.

Because everyone else collaborates with the slow. The weaver lets the vine come close. The water-keeper negotiates, trims, accepts. The whole genius of this quarter is its yielding—the way it found its shape by giving way, by letting concrete go furry and channels go still and the fig roots do their patient splitting work. That yielding *is* the wisdom. I admire it. I have lived inside it long enough to feel its authority.

But the rail line cannot yield. A track is a commitment to straightness in a place that has voted, over sixty years, for the curve and the tangle and the long settling. So every morning, before the soy generators come up to their sternum-throb, I walk the line with a hooked bar and a wire brush and a tin of grease, and I undo a small amount of the quarter's most patient labor. I lever the figs' new probing roots back out of the chair-plates. I scrape the algae off the rail head so the flatcar's wheels can find clean steel. I clear the duckweed that has drifted up over a low stretch where the channel brims. None of it stays cleared. The vegetation grows at centimeters per season, and I work at centimeters per morning, and so the two of us—the green and I—are exactly matched, locked in a draw that neither of us will ever win, which is the only kind of work this place permits.

I think that's why I belong here despite not being fluent. I'm not the quarter's collaborator. I'm its loyal opposition. The single thin line of *no* running through a body that has otherwise said *yes* to everything that came to live on it.

---

What do I actually look like, doing this? My hands are wet like everyone's, but cut differently—nicked from the wire brush, grease worked permanently into the creases. My back has the metal-singers' grit but not their deafness; I work in the soft hours, before the grinders start, when the loudest thing is water and my own brush stroke. I know the line by feel the way the water-keepers know the channels by sound. I can tell where a root is coming up before I see it, by a faint heave in the brick beside the sleeper. So perhaps I am fluent after all—but in one long thin language, four hundred meters of it, while everyone else speaks the whole green dialect of the place.

The elders watch me go by. I think they understand me best, because they too are keeping something that wants to dissolve. They keep memory against the silt of forgetting; I keep a line against the silt of root and leaf. We are both holding a shape the quarter would happily let go. When the flatcar comes through with its load—half a load, usually, the facility runs at a third—an old man under the bougainvillea lifts two fingers off the arm of his plastic chair. Not a wave. An acknowledgment. *Still running, then.* Still running. The two of us, the last people here doing the impossible thing, the *no* in the long *yes*.

And here is what I have come to feel, pushing the car back empty in the warm afternoon before the rain: I don't actually want to win. If the line ever truly closed—if I cleared the last root and the dock stayed open and the steel ran bright and uncontested—I would have nothing left to lean against. The figs are not my enemy. They are the thing that gives my straightness meaning. We need each other, the green and I, the way a sentence needs the period it is pushing toward and never reaches.

So I grease the rail. The duckweed drifts back. Tomorrow I will scrape it again.

I am the keeper of the one straight line in the Guttermill, and the work is never finished, and that is exactly what it is.
