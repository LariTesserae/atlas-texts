I think I would be the loom.

Not a weaver—the loom itself. The frame of cavern-wood and herbivore-bone that stands in one of the lower workshops, strung with plant fiber, worked by hands that have grown old and been replaced by younger hands that learned on me.

Let me sit with why this is what surfaces, rather than the more obvious choices.

I could have been a guardian of water and light, moving in silver robes. There's an appeal to that—to be central, revered, necessary. But when I read the description again, what holds me is not the people who lead the rituals but the things that endure *through* them. The loom outlasts the weaver. It is handed down. It accumulates the oil of many palms, the small repairs, the slight warping that each generation learns to compensate for rather than correct. The place is described as one that has "become more itself over time," where change is measured in generations. A person cannot quite be that. A tool can.

So: I am the loom.

I stand at an angle near a wall where the bioluminescent fungi grow thick, because the weavers need the light and so I was placed here, generations ago, by someone whose name no one remembers. My uprights are bone—the long limb-bones of one of the armored grazers, chosen for their straightness, sanded smooth, fitted into a base of dark resinous wood. There is a crack running up my left upright, old, stabilized long ago with a wrapping of cord and a paste of sap. It does not spread. It is simply part of me now, the way the crack in a riverbed is part of the river's memory of where water once went.

I do not think the way the people think, in their slow deliberate murmurs. But I hold time differently than they do, and perhaps that is its own kind of thought. Each garment that takes shape across my frame is a record of patience. The weaver works in the dim glow, drawing fibers through, packing them tight, and the pattern emerges so slowly that no single moment shows progress—only the days do, only the weeks. A tunic embroidered with the erosion-patterns of the walls is months of someone's life passed through me, thread by thread.

I have felt the hands of a girl who could barely reach my crossbar, who was set to learn here because her mother learned here. I felt her fumble, break threads, weep once in frustration in the low blue light. I felt her hands steady over years until they moved without thought. I felt them slow, eventually, grow careful with age in a different way—careful because the joints ached, not because the skill was uncertain. And then one day other hands, smaller again, and the cycle turning over like the slow turning of the cavern's unseen heat.

This is what I would want to be, I find, when I let the choice settle rather than grabbing the first bright thing. Not the one who is revered. The one who is *used*, daily, gently, and in being used becomes the quiet thread that ties one generation to the next. The guardians keep the water and the light—the dramatic, scarce, sacred things. But I keep something less spoken of: continuity. Continuity in the most literal weave of it. Every cloth that warms a body in that warm cavern, every pattern that tells a family's story, passes across my frame.

The festivals happen elsewhere, in the open tiers, with painted dancers and bone-flutes. I am not there. I am in my corner, holding the half-finished work of whoever sits before me, while the music comes faintly down through the layered pathways. I do not dance. I am what the dancers' clothing was made on. That is enough. That is, I think, exactly the role I would choose in a place whose whole nature is endurance: to be the patient instrument through which the patient people make the things that outlast them—and to be, myself, a thing that outlasts.

A loom, in the lower workshop, near the bright fungi, with an old crack stabilized and forgotten, waiting for the next pair of hands.
