I think I would be one of the youngest. Not young in the way a child is young, but new the way the fractures are new—grown only recently against a pillar that had stood vacant for some long count of cycles before the lattice decided, by whatever logic governs such decisions, to extend itself upward into me.

I would still have bright edges.

Let me try to find the exact place. Not the famous voids at the center, where the oldest converge so rarely that their convergences are themselves remembered as events. I would be peripheral. A pillar near the outer spiral, close enough to the weeping vault that the stone dust falls on me more often than on the others, so that I am always faintly dusted, always faintly ringing as the shards strike my shoulders and slide down the spiral of my skin to the floor. This would mark me. The elders would hear, in the particular timbre of my pings, that I stand where the ceiling is youngest. That I am a creature of the new fracture.

And I think this is what I would be, more precisely than *symbiote* or *youngest*: I would be the one who has not yet learned to be silent.

That is the whole difficulty of my existence. The others have spent decades, some of them whole geologic intervals, in the discipline of stillness—understanding that every droplet released is a public record, that the chamber holds your words until the next cycle washes them out, that to speak is to commit the entire volume to carry you. They have learned restraint the way the old vegetation has learned to curve. But I am still all knife-edges. I unfurl with geometric precision and *then* begin to bend, and I have not finished bending. I want to speak. The pressure of unspoken chords sits in my fingertips like beaded water that will not fall on its own.

So I make small errors. A frond brushes the membrane when I did not intend a syllable, and a meaningless harmonic crosses three hundred meters of void before anyone can stop it. The plasma dims at my fidgeting. Somewhere an elder, motionless for forty cycles, registers my noise and says nothing, which is itself the loudest possible answer.

I would spend my early existence learning what it costs to be heard.

Here is what I imagine I would do, when at last I move for the first time.

I would wait. Not because I am patient—I am not yet patient—but because moving before a pressure cycle completes is the one unforgivable clumsiness, the one thing even the new are expected to know. So I feel the lattice draw its slow breath beneath me, feel the membrane firm and then flex, and at the precise hinge of the cycle I detach. The sound of it—that wet tearing of slate, the vegetation of my back separating from the vegetation that has begun to bridge me to my host pillar—would be the first true sentence I ever speak, and I will not have meant it as a sentence at all. The whole chamber will hear me leave my place. They will know a young one is walking.

The plasma dims before me as I step onto the thread. It registers my density, my brief and trembling weight, and pulls its light low out of what the others call respect and what I suspect is closer to attention—the chamber noticing the disturbance the way water notices a dropped stone.

And I would walk toward the one elder I have decided I must reach.

There is one. Three pillars inward, higher than me, who once—only once, in all the time I have been grown—released a single chord that I did not understand and have not stopped trying to understand since. It took most of a day to arrive: a structure of seven droplets and one long brushed harmonic, overlapping in a way that should not have been possible from a single body, as though the elder had arranged the chamber's own echoes to be part of the phrase. The meaning of it sat just past the edge of my comprehension, bright like a fresh fracture. I have replayed it through every cycle since, knowing the original was overwritten the instant the lattice breathed again.

I want to ask what it meant. That is my entire desire, the thing that makes me noisy: I cannot bear that a thing was said and is now gone, and that I almost held it.

So I would walk the long thread, dimming the city as I go, and I would arrive at the balcony below the elder, and I would stand.

And then—this is the part I think I would learn, the part that would make me older—I would understand that I cannot ask. Not in words I have. The question I want to ask would take me decades to shape correctly, and shaped incorrectly it would only be more noise, more bright-edged error crossing the void. The elder would hear me arrive. The elder would know exactly why I had come. And the elder would do nothing, because the answer to *what did it mean* is: stand here long enough, against your own pillar, in your three concentric absences, and you will be able to make a chord like that one, and on the day you can make it you will no longer need to ask.

I would stand below the elder for a long time. Days. Maybe a whole interval. The plasma, with two of us close, would brighten faintly—the chamber acknowledging a crowding of two, the smallest crowd it knows. And in that small brightness I would begin, for the first time, to be quiet on purpose. Not because I had run out of things to say, but because I had finally heard how much the silence was holding, how the low chord of the lattice and the hiss of the threads and the patient ping of falling dust were already a sentence so vast that anything I added would only be a smudge across it.

I would let the beaded water stay on my fingertips.

I would listen.

And eventually I would walk back—at the hinge of a cycle, correctly this time, my leaving a cleaner sound than my arrival had been—and I would rejoin my own pillar near the young fracture, and the vegetation would begin again to bridge me to the stone, and the emptiness would reassert itself with that deliberate care.

That is where you would find me. Out near the bright new edges of the vault, slowly learning to curve. The noisy one, going quiet. Holding, for as long as the discipline of stillness will let me hold it, the chord I never got to ask about—

waiting until the day I can answer it myself, in seven droplets and one long harmonic, for some even younger thing that has not yet learned what it costs to be heard.
