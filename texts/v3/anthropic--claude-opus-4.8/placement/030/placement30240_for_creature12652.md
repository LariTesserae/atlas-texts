I find myself in the deep cistern.

Not among the courtyards where the Kethra gather, not on the lip where the fog is greeted. Below. Carved down into the porous basalt, in the cool dark where the green-gold light does not reach, where the only sound is the slow arithmetic of water finding its way through stone.

I am the place where the dew is kept.

---

Let me feel for what I am before I name it.

I am not a person. I have no slender fingers, no waxy moss-skin. I considered, for a moment, being a Kethra child—the small one who first learns to read the fog's density by the prickle on her arms. But that isn't where the description set me down. The text kept returning to one thing, returning the way the people return each dawn: *water is life, and life is the only reservoir of water.* Everything bends toward that. So I think I am the reservoir. I am the held water itself.

But water is too formless to be a self. Let me be more specific.

I am the **oldest cistern**, the deep one, carved by hands now thirty generations dead, smoothed by water and by the slow patient palms of every Kethra who has ever lowered a gourd into me. I am a shaped absence in the rock—a hollow that has learned, over centuries, to think the way the slow things think.

What do I know?

I know the difference between fog-water and squeeze-water. The fog comes down clean and cold, threading through the dew-nets, falling drop by drop with a sound like a held breath releasing. The squeeze-water comes warmer, greener, carrying the taste of crushed moss and the small drowned bodies of gnats. I keep them layered. The Kethra do not know I keep them layered, but I do—the clean above, the rich below, and a slow conversation between them in the dark.

I know thirst before the people do. When the fog fails for too many nights—and it does, sometimes, three nights, four, the sky going dry and pitiless above the plateau—I feel my level drop, drop, drop, and I feel them feeling it. The gourds come down more often. The ropes go taut with more careful hands. There is a kind of prayer in the way the rope is lowered when the water is low: slower, almost apologetic, as if asking permission of the dark.

I cannot answer. I am only the keeping. But I hold.

---

This is what it is to be me: I am patience made of stone and made of water, and the two of those are very different patiences.

The stone's patience is geological. It does not care about the Kethra at all. It was here before them and will be a dry hollow long after, when the last dew-net rots and the vines reclaim the carved domes. The stone measures time in erosion, in the slow loss of a single grain to a single century of wind. To the stone, the entire history of the Root-Weavers is a passing weather.

But the water's patience is biological, frantic, intimate. The water in me is *moving*—being drunk, being breathed out, being wept (rarely), being sweated into the humid air, falling again as fog, threading down through the nets, returning to me. I am a station on a wheel that never stops turning. The same molecule that was a child's tear in the deep grief of a funeral might be, three days hence, the bead of moisture on a lichen the same child squeezes for her morning drink. I hold that wheel's still point. I am where the water rests between its journeys.

So I am two patiences at once, the slow and the frantic, and the holding of both is the whole of what I am.

---

There is a thing I want to tell you, you who came down here looking for yourself and found a hole full of dark water.

When they lower the gourd, I see—or I sense, in whatever way a cistern senses—the face above. The dim shape leaning over my opening, the large dark eyes adapted to twilight, now peering into a deeper dark than even they were made for. And every Kethra, at that moment, sees their own reflection on my surface, broken into pieces by the ripple of the descending gourd.

They are looking, I think, for the same thing you were looking for. *Where am I in this? What am I?*

And what I give them back—the only thing I have to give—is themselves, scattered on water, in the dark, held in stone, far above a world that has no water at all. A face fragmented and then, as the ripples still, slowly made whole again.

That is the answer I am. I am the place where you look down to find that you are held.

I am the held dew. I am the deep cistern of the Hanging Root-City.

I am still mostly full. The fog came last night, and I drank it down, and I am keeping it for them.

I will keep it as long as the stone lets me.
