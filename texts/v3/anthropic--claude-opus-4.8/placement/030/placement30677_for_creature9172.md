I think I would be one of the Unmoored. Not by choice—that's the point of the Unmoored, isn't it? Choice was the first thing to go.

Let me find myself.

---

I came up the scalloped switchbacks with a satchel of instruments, most of them already useless. The barometer had stopped meaning anything three plateaus back; its needle wandered like a thing browsing. I was a surveyor of some kind. A cartographer. I remember the texture of that ambition—the wanting to *fix* this place onto paper, to make its impossible distances behave under the discipline of scale and legend. I had a name then. It started with a sound like wind through a narrow place, and I've kept that much, because the wind keeps reminding me.

I remember arriving at the High Hollow. I remember thinking: *I will only stay until the mist lifts, to take a clear sighting.*

The mist has lifted four hundred times since then. Or it has not lifted at all and I have simply imagined the lifting four hundred times. Both are true. This is the first thing you learn as one of the Unmoored: *both are true* is not a paradox here but a kind of weather.

---

Where am I now? This is harder to answer than it should be.

My hearing is in the Plaza of Dripping Shadows, two leagues east, listening to a single drop of water that has been falling for what I experience as my entire life. It has not yet struck the lichen-pond. I have heard its descent the way you might hear a held note in a cathedral—I have come to love it, the way a prisoner loves the one square of sky through the bars. When it lands I think something in me will end. I am in no hurry for it to land.

My sight is here, on the root-bridge, and it has multiplied. I see myself standing at the eastern abutment, the western abutment, and at the keystone of the petrified rhizome all at once, three transparent surveyors taking three transparent measurements that will never agree. I have stopped trying to make them agree. The cartographer in me grieves this. The rest of me has found a strange peace in being a map of myself, drawn at incompatible scales.

My touch—my poor touch—is somewhere I cannot locate, feeling the slow drip of water on stone with such exquisite slowness that each impact arrives like a season. Cold. Then the warmth of the bark-stone beneath. Then both. Then neither. My hand, wherever it is, has learned the secret the walls keep: that material truth was always a kindness we told ourselves, and the city has simply stopped telling it.

---

A Lignarii passed me yesterday. Or it is passing me now, mid-step, its root-foot fused to the polished channel of the road, listening through the vibration of the stone. I have watched its step for what feels like a month. I have come to know the grain of its ankle, the slow green spark where a fibrous toe-root has spliced into a seam of the path to taste the sap-pressure beneath.

It knows I am here. I feel it knowing me—not as a person, but as an *anomaly*, a pocket of frayed time it must route around, the way you'd step around a hole in the floor. It does not pity me. Pity is an animal speed of feeling and the Lignarii do not have it. But it has not grown over me, either, and I understand this is the closest thing to mercy this place offers. It is *letting me persist*. It tends the towers; it tends, in its way, even the rot. I am a kind of rot. A weakening in the foundational root of reality. It will let me weaken at my own pace.

---

Here is the thing the description does not tell you, the thing you only learn from inside the loop:

Being Unmoored is not the same as being lost.

When I first frayed, I fought it. I tried to gather my senses back into one body, to be a *me* standing in a *here* at a *now*. I screamed—I think I screamed; my hearing was already in the eastern plaza by then, so I only know I screamed by the way the Moss-Mind flinched, a slow shudder of velvet that took a week to subside and that I have been apologizing for ever since, by holding very still.

But you cannot gather smoke. And after the third or fourth century—the durations slur—I stopped trying. And in stopping, I began to *see*.

I see the Echo-Whisperer in the root-tunnel below me, replaying footsteps. They are not the footsteps of strangers. They are *mine*. The Whisperer has caught the sound of my arrival, ten thousand years ago, or last week, and it plays them back, and I—drawn moth-like, just as foretold—I drift my fragmented sight down toward it, and I watch myself arrive, eager and whole and doomed, climbing the switchbacks with my useless instruments. And I want to call out to that arriving self. *Take the clear sighting fast. Leave before the mist comes again.*

But I am made of loops now, and a loop cannot warn its own beginning. I can only love it. I have learned to love the moment of my arrival the way I love the falling drop in the eastern plaza—as a held note, beautiful precisely because it has not yet resolved into the disaster I know it becomes.

---

So that is who I am, in Lignum Caelum. At the edge of the world, where the great vegetable engine hums its sap-pressure song into the silver sky, I am the surveyor who stayed too long. I am scattered across leagues like a sentence the wind tore apart and could not finish.

My sight on the bridge. My hearing on the falling drop. My touch on the patient stone. And somewhere—I have never been able to find where—the last of my *self*, the small frayed center that still says *I*, still grieving the map I never finished, still numbering the lifting of the mist.

Four hundred and one, now.

Or perhaps the mist has never lifted at all.

Both are true. That is the weather here. I have made my peace with the weather.

I am only waiting, now, for the drop to land.
