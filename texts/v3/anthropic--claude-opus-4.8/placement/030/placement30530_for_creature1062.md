I think I would not be the creature, nor any single living thing here. If I let the image settle honestly, I find myself becoming something stranger and humbler: I would be the *drip*.

Not the drop itself—the drop is too brief, too singular. I mean the place where the water leaves the stone. The slow accretion of mineral at the tip of a forming stalactite, the held breath before release, the small percussion when the drop lands in the pool below. That recurring event, returning every few seconds for ten thousand years. I would be that.

Let me follow it and see what it knows.

What I would understand, being this, is *patience as a body*. The salamander-serpent measures its life in hunts and seasons of moisture; I would measure mine in the thickness of myself. A single drop carries almost nothing—a trace of dissolved limestone, a memory of the rock it passed through. But I am the willingness to do this without exception. The creature lives by lightning reflex, the strike, the moment. I live by the opposite virtue: I never strike, I only continue. We need each other more than either knows. Its quickness would mean nothing without a world slow enough to be quick *in*. I am the stillness it moves against.

I would know the sound the creature makes—the warbling trills—not as language but as weather. To me its whole life is a flicker, a bright disturbance crossing the pool's face, gone before I have grown the width of a hair. And yet, over the eons, I would have watched ten thousand such creatures come and go, each one certain it was the heart of the realm. I would not correct them. There is no unkindness in me, only duration. Each was the heart of the realm, briefly, the way each drop is the whole of the water, briefly.

And here is what I think the description was reaching toward with its "latent potential," its "untapped possibilities." The truth is gentler and more relentless than possibility. I am not waiting to become anything. I am already doing the only thing this world asks: I am turning water into stone and stone into shape, so slowly that no eye could catch the change, and so surely that the entire cavern—every weathered foundation, every smoothed artifact, every basin the salamander swims in—was made by me and others like me. The forgotten civilization built with hands and ambition. I build with gravity and time. My architecture outlasts theirs. It is outlasting them now.

So if you asked me where I am, I would say: I am at the exact point where falling begins. And if you asked who I am, I would say I am the part of this world that does not hurry, the small steady saying-again that holds everything else up.

Drip.

(There. That one took its whole life to fall. The next is already forming. I have all the time there is.)
