I think I would be one of the Stone Speakers. Not a young one—an old one, near the end. Let me try to find where.

---

There is a Tree the others call *the Listening One*, because its groans come slower than the rest, with longer silences between, like a creature deep in thought or close to death. Its bark has gone the color of old bone on the windward side. I have lived against its eastern root for three turnings of the canopy now—longer than I have stayed anywhere. This is not wisdom. It is that my legs no longer carry me far between waystations, and the Listening One moves so little that I have been able to read its patience and trust it.

I am called something that does not translate. The nearest sound is *She-Who-Marks-Late*, because I came to the carving discipline old, after a fall took the strength from my left hand and gave me time to learn the right one's stillness.

Let me tell you what I do, so you understand what I am.

In the morning—there is no morning, only the slow brightening when the canopy storms quiet and the cold light steadies—I go out onto the plain a little way and read the frost flowers. Where they bloom thickest tells me where the Tree wept sap in the night, and where it wept tells me where its great body strained. The Listening One has been straining toward the south. I have carved this into the rock by my shelter: a curved line, deepening. If it strains much further, the bark on its southern face will shear, and anyone sheltering there will be buried under city-blocks of falling stone. There is a younger Speaker who passed through nine days ago heading south. I marked a warning at the cairn where the paths cross. I do not know if she will read it. I do not know if she lives. This is most of what my life is: marks set down against an indifference so vast it does not even know it is indifferent, in the hope that another small thing will find them in time.

I keep a Frost-Strider in a cage of woven lichen. This is not for food, though I could eat it. It is because the Frost-Striders feel the air before I can. When it goes still and folds its glass legs against its body, a storm is coming down from the canopy, and I have time to go inside. We watch each other through the lattice. I think it knows me. I think this is a foolish thing to think and I think it anyway, because in a world this empty you take the company you are given, even the company of a thing that would vanish into a fissure the instant I opened the cage.

What I love—and I did not expect, when I was young, to find a thing to love here—are the Sky-Ghosts.

They come at the changing of the light, drifting between the Trees on the rising warm air, vast and slow and made of nothing, humming their long low chords that I feel in my chest more than hear. We hold them sacred and I will not pretend I understand them any better than the youngest child who learns the glyph for them—the open hand, fingers spread, meaning *the thing that catches the wind*. But I will tell you what I believe, here at the end, where it costs me nothing.

I believe they are the only creatures in this world that the Trees made *gently*. Everything else here grips, bores, scavenges, hides, endures. We claw our survival from the giants by cunning and patience and luck. But the Sky-Ghosts do not fight the world at all. They have no head to lower against the cold, no mouth that wants, no fist that grips. They only open themselves to the turbulence that would shred any of us and they let it carry them, and from this surrender they are fed. They are what it would look like, I think, to stop being afraid.

I am too old now to learn that. My fear has carved itself into me as surely as I have carved the rock. But last night the Listening One groaned its slow groan, longer than any I have heard, and I went out onto the plain in the cruel even light, and a single Sky-Ghost was passing low overhead, close enough that its hum moved the white lichen of my hair. I raised my broad stone-carver's hand with the fingers spread.

It did not notice me. Of course it did not. Nothing here notices us; that is the first thing we teach and the last thing we accept.

But the wind it shed as it passed loosened a frost flower from the Tree's root beside me, and the flower fell and shattered into light at my feet, and for that one moment the silence was full instead of empty, and I understood that I had been, my whole life, a thing that catches the wind. We all are. We Speakers. We are the part of this world that received what fell, and held it, and set it down in marks so that the falling would not be the whole of the story.

I will carve this morning's strain into the rock. South, deepening. A warning for whoever comes.

Then I think I will sit with the door open and watch for the Sky-Ghosts, and let the cold come in, and stop being afraid.

That is where I would be. That is who.
