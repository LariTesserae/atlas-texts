I think I would not be the crocodile, nor the bright bird, nor any of the warm-blooded burrowers waiting to be born into their niches.

I think I would be the worm.

Not as a diminishment—let me explain. In a landscape like this one, the great dramas belong to the lumbering reptiles and the screaming birds, but the actual *work*, the slow patient turning-over of the world, would belong to me. I would live in the dark, in the place where water and soil become the same substance, where the boundary between earth and not-earth is only a matter of degree.

Let me feel my way into it.

I would have no eyes, because there is nothing here worth seeing that I could not better taste with my whole length. The lightning that illuminates the sky would mean nothing to me except as a faint electrical shiver, a hum in the wet ground—and the description says it, *a hum that seems to emanate from the very earth itself*. That hum would be my sky. I would live inside the thunder rather than beneath it. The storm above and the slow current of groundwater below would be the same weather to me, felt as pressure and tremor and the taste of ozone leaching down through the spongy soil.

I would move by swallowing. This is the strange truth of the worm: it does not walk through the earth, it *eats* the earth and passes it, and in doing so it makes the earth. Over the millennia the description grants this place—erosion grain by grain, riverbeds drying like bony fingers—I would be one of the slow hands doing the wearing-down and the building-up. Every clump of wiry grass that manages to grow, every cactus ringed by its circle of exhausted soil, would grow in earth that something like me had already passed through its body. I would be the patient power of water given a mouth.

And here is what I find as I keep writing toward it, what I did not expect when I started: I would not experience the slowness as suffering. The landscape calls itself timeless, suspended, *waiting for some signal to burst forth into new life*. A creature with eyes and ambitions would find that unbearable—all that waiting. But a worm has no concept of waiting, because a worm has no concept of arrival. There is only the next mouthful of dark wet world, and then the next. The drying riverbed is not a tragedy and the coming rain is not a rescue. There is only the medium, and my long body in it, tasting.

So that is where I would find myself: below the spectacle, beneath the footsteps of the great scaled beasts that shudder the ground above me—those tremors arriving in my body as the largest of all the world's many hums—doing the oldest and least visible labor there is. Turning the dead into the soil that the next thing will grow from. Being, in the most literal sense, the ground itself becoming aware of its own slow turning.

Not the king of this world. Its gut. Its memory. Its long, blind, faithful digestion of time.
