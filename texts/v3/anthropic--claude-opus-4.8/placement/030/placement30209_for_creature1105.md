I think I would be the lichen.

Not the birds of prey—too sovereign, too singular, riding the thermals like punctuation. And not the people, whose shelters and tool-sites the description keeps gently diminishing, setting them against a world too large to be theirs. To be human here would be to feel, always, the ache of scale; to measure myself against cliffs and lose.

But the lichen is mentioned almost in passing. It coats the trunks. It is *on* things rather than being a thing. And that is what draws me—because the lichen is not even one being. It is an argument that worked out: a fungus and an alga (or a cyanobacterium) that gave up on being separate. The fungus offers structure, anchorage, a way to hold water against the weeping air. The photosynthetic partner offers food spun out of light. Neither could live here alone. Together they make the slow gray-green crust that is, often, the very first life to touch bare rock.

So I would live on the exposed faces the description says bear "the marks of recent erosion." Where the land has just torn itself open and shown its raw stone, I would be the thing that arrives. I'd secrete faint acids and begin, imperceptibly, to eat the rock—to break it into the first grains of soil that something rooted might one day use. The twisted trees the passage admires so much would, in a sense, be downstream of me. I'd be doing the unglamorous founding work, the part no one writes the poem about.

I like that I would not experience the flux the way the rest of the place does. The streams hurry. The birds cry and dart. The geology shifts "relentlessly"—but on its own enormous clock. The lichen lives on a third clock, slower than the animals, faster than the mountain. I might grow a single millimeter in a year. The recent erosion the others see as recent, I would experience as the recent. A rockfall would be an event in my lifetime, not an abstraction. I would be one of the few things here patient enough to actually witness the land "re-shaping itself" rather than just suffer it or ignore it.

And there is the matter of the human signs. The faint trails, the crude shelters. The passage frames them as small and ancient, "only recently touched by human hands." If I am the lichen, I am the thing that *measures* that recency. Lichenometry—we are used, in the human world, to date how long a stone has lain exposed, how long ago a glacier withdrew, how old a grave marker is. The size of a lichen colony is a clock. So a person, ages from now, might find one of those abandoned shelters and read its age in *me*—in the spread of my colony across its fallen lintel. The humans build, leave, and vanish into a world too big for them. But I would quietly keep the record of their passing, writing the date in slow circular growth on the very stones they once stacked.

That is where and what I would be. Not soaring, not building, not even quite alive in the singular sense. A patient partnership spread thin across cold rock at altitude, drinking the clinging mist, dissolving the mountain one grain at a time, holding the place together while it falls apart—the first to arrive, and, I suspect, among the last to leave.
