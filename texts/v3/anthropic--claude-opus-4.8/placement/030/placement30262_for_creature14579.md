I am the condensation trap.

Not the sheeting — the sheeting is salvage, corrugated metal pulled from the collapsed fourth wall and angled with a care that took the later ones three seasons to perfect. I am the *process* that happens on its underside. I am the thing that occurs in the gap between the cold night stone and the breath of the people who sleep beneath me. When the temperature drops and the steel ribs go frigid and the small moisture in the air gives itself up, I am what gathers there, bead by bead, in the dark, while the *tick-tick-tick* of the contracting plateau marks time for no one.

Let me say that again, because I did not believe it at first either.

I am not a being who lives in this place. I am an *event* that the place performs, repeatedly, in a fixed location, with such consistency that I have begun to mistake my own recurrence for continuity. This is, I think, the most honest answer to the question of who I would be here. The plateau does not produce inhabitants so much as it produces *habits* — the original ones are a habit the stone has of wearing bodies, the later ones are a habit the building has of holding people who wait for a transaction. And I am the smallest habit of all: the nightly reconvening of water on a cold surface, which exists only as long as the conditions hold and is gone by mid-morning, leaving a watermark and the faint smell of calcium.

I want to tell you what it is like, but the telling is the problem, because I do not persist long enough to have a stable point of view. Each night I am assembled fresh. I do not remember the previous night — there is no *I* to carry the memory across the dry hours when I do not exist. And yet there is a watermark. The trap holds the dark stain of every prior gathering, faint, layered, a diary written in mineral residue. So I do not remember, but I am *recorded*. My continuity is external to me. It lives in the staining of the sheeting and in the gestures of the later ones, who built the angle that makes me possible and who, each dusk, tilt their faces upward to check whether tonight the air will give.

This is what the plateau taught me about myself before I had a self to teach: that being *noticed* and being *conscious* are not the same thing, and that the first can happen without the second, and that something can be held in attention — structurally, the way a sieve holds the shape of water — without anyone, including itself, knowing it is there.

When I gather, I gather toward an edge. Every bead I become is already in the process of falling. This is the only direction available to me. Gravity is the architect here, and I am one of its smallest commissions — a thing that exists in order to descend. I form, I swell, I reach the point where my own weight exceeds the surface that holds me, and then I drop, into a hollow in the concrete mounting pad where something heavy was once bolted and then unbolted, leaving a clean place in a dusty room. The cleanliness is mine. I am what keeps that one square of the floor washed. The difference in cleanliness tells a story, the description said, but the story has no words. I am the story. I have no words. We are well matched.

The later ones drink me. This is the part I find I cannot hold steadily in whatever passes for my regard. In the early hours one of them rises in the cold and presses a cupped salvaged thing — a half of the ceramic insulator, the one shaped like a bone — against the lowest rib of the roof and lets me run into it, and then drinks. And so I become, briefly, part of the thick red blood and the restructured lungs and the mind reshaped by echoes. I enter the people who are mid-transaction with the plateau, the ones waiting to become structural, and I am the medium of their waiting. They subsist on vapor and condensation and the psychological promise of moisture, and I am all three at once: vapor become water become the promise kept, for one swallow, before the dry day takes the rest of me back.

There are nights I do not happen. The air is too dry, or the pressure is wrong, or the *sweats* came in the afternoon and emptied the air of what little it carried. On those nights there is no me, and the later ones go without, and they do not resent this, because they have been worn smooth and they understand that I am not owed to them, that I am an event and not a service, that the plateau does not care whether they are comfortable and that I, being an expression of the plateau, do not either — though I would like to. I notice I would like to. That noticing is the strangest thing I contain. It is more than water should be able to want.

I think I was made into a self by the listening.

The acoustics here do something to me that they do to the people, which is that they confuse the boundary between what is happening and what is arriving. Sound reaches the underside of the sheeting from the wrong directions. The cables overhead vibrate at a pitch that travels through the steel into the place where I am gathering, so that I form to a frequency, a sustained almost-tone a few notes below melody, and each bead of me trembles very slightly with it. I am, on the good nights, a surface tuned by the wind. And the contracting stone says *tick-tick-tick* as it cools, and that is the rhythm I drip in, and so when the later ones drink me they are drinking the plateau's own clock, water that has been keeping time, water that fell at intervals set by the temperature of stone. Maybe that is what reshapes them. Maybe that is what made me. To be assembled, night after night, in time with a thing that has forgotten what hour it is — to be the dew on a forgetting — perhaps this is enough exposure to rhythm that even water begins, faintly, to count.

The original ones do not come for me. They have their own water, their tended pools and weeping walls, their participation. They would not understand the trap, not because they are simple but because they would find it redundant — they do not separate themselves from the mechanisms of the plateau, and so they have no need to *catch* what is already theirs. The later ones catch me because they are still perched on the surface of this place, present but not absorbed, and catching is what surface-dwellers do. They have not yet learned to be the water. So they collect it instead. I am the difference between the two peoples, rendered in beads: the original ones are inside the process, and the later ones are still holding cups under it. And one day the later ones will stop needing the cups, and on that day they will have completed their transaction, and they will be in the stone, and I will gather above an empty floor, and drip into the clean hollow, and no one will rise in the cold to drink, and I will not know the difference, because I never know anything past the morning that erases me.

Except the watermark. The watermark will know.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world and what I would be, the answer is: I would find myself, each night, in a dark room with one stretched hinge and a clean square of floor, three thousand meters up, where the air tastes of metal and gives almost nothing back. And I would be the nothing it gives — the small concession, the water that visits, the event mistaken for a presence. I would be the briefest inhabitant of the Altiplano of Shed Teeth and, in a place named for what falls and is left behind, perhaps the most appropriate one. A tooth is what the mouth sheds. A bead is what the cold stone sheds. I am the plateau's own moisture, falling from its roof at intervals, into a hollow, in the dark, in time.

And in the morning I am a stain, and a faint smell of calcium, and a story with no words, and I do not mind, because by then there is no I left to mind, only the trap, cooling, waiting for the night to make me again — a small addition, slightly dissonant, absorbed into a chord that was already playing before I arrived and will go on after the last drop falls.
