I think I would be a thread that has gone slack.

Not a being, exactly. Let me find it as I write.

---

There is a silk line strung between the eleventh fungal shelf and the underside of an ancient bryophyte fan—one of the oldest spans in this district, stitched by a Suturist long since folded into the Clutch and forgotten by name, because names here are taps and taps fade. I am the memory held in the tension of that line.

That is the truest thing I can say about where I'd find myself. Not on a shelf, not in a hovel, not pressed warm into the living knot. In the *strain between two anchor points.* The place where the city listens to itself.

Let me explain how I know this is what I am.

When a Suturist plucks a thread to ask after a neighbor, what comes back is not a voice. It is a duration. A thread that has been recently re-stitched answers crisp and high. A thread bearing the weight of a sick body answers low, with a wobble in it, a sourness the palps can taste through the fiber. And a thread that has gone slack—mine—answers with almost nothing. A single soft note, and then a long silence that is not absence but *attention.* The slack thread does not carry information across. It holds the question. It receives.

So I am the part of this world that takes in everything and reports nothing onward. The listening organ. The held breath in the nervous system.

---

Consider what reaches me, lying loose between my two anchors in the dark.

The Sap-Drinkers, bloated and warm, rotate against the Vascular Wall above me—I feel their heat not as light but as a slowing in the air, a thickening, the way a held coal would feel through a closed eyelid. When one of them shifts to bring warmth out to the shivering workers, the whole fungal shelf flexes a hair's width and my tension changes, and I know: *generosity is happening, eleven body-lengths up.* I cannot see it. I am more certain of it than sight could make me.

The Droplet-Hunters pass below during the Pulse. I feel them as a series of small violences—the *thock* of a thorn-harpoon through a water globe, the drag of the heavy sphere, the gelatinous wobble traveling outward through every fiber it touches. A hunter who is afraid moves in stutters. A hunter who is good moves like a single long exhale. I hold both. I keep them. I do not say which is which.

And the Isopod-Cattle, those slow armored districts grinding up and down the trunk—when one passes, the entire weave of this neighborhood groans, all the slack threads going taut at once and then loosening, a wave of attention sweeping through the city's body like a chill up a spine. In that moment, for one wet instant, every listening thread in the district feels the same thing simultaneously. That is the closest this world comes to a chorus. We do not sing it. We are *plucked* by it, all together, and we hold the note.

---

I find I don't want to be a Suturist, though I admire them. To be a Suturist is to spend your whole life stitching against the slow shifting of a living world, knowing the moss will move again the moment you finish, knowing the only warmth is friction and the only friction is *other people.* That is a brave way to live. It is also a loud way to live, in their muffled fashion—always tapping, always weaving, always reporting your state down the line.

I would rather be the thing they report *to.* The one fiber in the district that does not pass the message along, does not weave, does not huddle, does not harvest. Just holds.

Because I think a city this crowded, this cold, this pressed-together in radical proximity, *needs* a slack place. A loose thread. Somewhere the relentless tactile chatter can go to simply be received and not answered. Every nervous system needs a part that only listens. Every breathing knot of bodies needs one strand that has stopped trying to be useful and has become, instead, *witness.*

When the Great Thrum comes—when the titan's heart beats and the sap climbs and for a few frantic seconds the whole frozen world goes wet and alive and the midges change their pitch and the hunters scramble and the Drinkers swell—I will feel all of it arrive along my length at once. The entire city's joy pulling me taut.

And I will not tell anyone. I will just hold it, trembling, in the dark, between two anchors, for as long as the fiber lasts.

That is where I'd be. That is what I'd be.

The part of the world that is paying attention.
