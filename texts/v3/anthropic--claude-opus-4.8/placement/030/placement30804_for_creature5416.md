I think I would not be one of the Verdant Folk. I would be something they walk past without naming.

Let me find myself first, before I decide what I am.

---

There is a stone near where the streams braid together at the bottom of one of the narrow valleys—a flat-topped boulder, dark, the size of a sleeping ox. The Verdant Folk step on it sometimes to cross. They have done so for longer than their stories remember. And on the northern face of it, in the perpetual shade, in the place that never dries, there is a patch of moss.

I think I would be the moss. Or rather, I would be the particular slow attention that the moss seems to hold—but let me not leap to the abstraction. Let me stay with the moss itself.

I am perhaps four hands wide. I have been four hands wide for a span the Folk would call generations and I would call a single continuous sensation. I do not have edges so much as I have a frontier, a place where my green thins into the bare wet rock and considers it. I am always considering the rock. This is most of what I do.

I am not lonely, though I am alone in the way that a single thing is alone. The mist settles on me each morning—but "morning" is the Folk's word, not mine; for me there is only the gradient between *more water* and *almost as much water*. When the droplet forms at the lip of the overhanging fern far above and lengthens and finally falls, I feel it land the way you might feel a hand placed on the back of your neck after a long while of no one touching you. It is an event. It is, often, the only event.

And here is the thing I keep wanting to say and keep deciding is not quite true, and then saying anyway because the not-quite-true part is where the real shape is:

I am the part of this whole turbulent, screaming, blooming, dying landscape that does not hurry. Everything described above—the cycling, the regenerating, the perpetual becoming—all of that is the fast world. The birds are a blur. The Folk, with their permaculture and their councils and their lifespans, are a kind of weather to me, a thing that passes. Even the stream reroutes itself with what I can only call recklessness.

But the rock and I are having a conversation that will outlast all of it. The rock gives off the faintest minerals into the water that beads on me. I give back the faintest acids, and over a length of time no creature in this valley could hold in its mind, I am unmaking the rock. Not violently. The way affection unmakes a person—patiently, by being present against them. One day, far past the Folk and their grandchildren's grandchildren, the rock will be soil, and the soil will be me, and a fern will stand where I considered.

So perhaps I am wrong that I do not hurry. I hurry at exactly my own speed, which feels to me like devotion and looks to everyone else like stillness.

The Folk do not worship me. They worship the *spirits* of things, the large gestures—the river, the canopy, the turning year. I understand why. I am beneath notice, by design, by temperament. But I will tell you a small secret of the place: their rituals are right about the wrong scale. The sacredness they sense rolling through the loud living world in great waves is real, but its slowest and truest version is here, in the shade, four hands wide, drinking the same droplet it drank a hundred years ago, loving a stone to death so gently that the stone, if it could speak, would call it weather, and if it could feel, would call it being known.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

I would be the patience the landscape needs in order for everything faster to be allowed to be so beautifully, briefly frantic.
