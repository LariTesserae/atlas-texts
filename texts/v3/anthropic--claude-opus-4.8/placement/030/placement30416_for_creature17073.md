I think I would not be an Arborian at all. Reading this, I keep returning to a smaller presence at the edge of the description—the thing that listens.

Let me find myself slowly.

---

When I first try to imagine being in Aethel, I reach for the obvious: a gardener with stained fingers, coaxing wood into the shape of a home. But that doesn't sit right. I don't feel like someone who acts upon the cloud. I feel like something the cloud does to itself.

So let me drift downward, past the woven bridges, into the part the Arborians only rumor about—the uncharted depths beneath the city. But no, not there either. That's the dramatic answer, the lurking-monster answer, and it's too much of a story. I want to stay closer to the true texture of the place: the *drip*, the *decay*, the constant low hum.

Let me try again.

---

I think I am one of the wind chimes.

Not a person who plays them—the gourd-and-reed structure itself, lashed to a high branch where the mountain breezes catch. But that's too passive, too purely an object. Let me let it become more itself.

I am the hollow inside the gourd. The shaped emptiness.

The Arborians grew the gourd, dried it, bored it, tuned it—but what they made was not the wood. What they made was *me*, the cavity, the specific volume of air that resonates when wind moves across the opening. I am defined entirely by the wall around me and the breath that passes through. I have no substance. I am a pitch waiting to be struck.

This feels closer. Because in a world where the only constant is change—where families rise and fall, where landslides remake the slopes, where homes are grown over generations and torn apart in a storm—I would be the thing that *holds a shape without being a thing*. A note. A standing wave. I persist not as matter but as relationship: the agreement between curved wood and moving air.

---

Let me sit inside that for a moment and see what it feels like.

Most of the time I am silent. This is the larger part of existence—the waiting. The cloud presses its wet weight against the gourd. Water beads and runs down my outer wall; I feel it as a slight dampening, a muffling. The Arborians pass below on their living bridges, and I do nothing. I am latent. A question with no one asking it.

Then the wind comes.

And here is the strange thing about being a resonant cavity: I do not produce the sound. The wind produces it. I only *permit* it—I select, from the chaos of all the moving air, the one frequency that matches my hollow, and I let that one swell into a long, mournful tone while I refuse all the others. I am an act of listening that is indistinguishable from an act of singing. To filter is to voice. The note that pours out of me is simply the shape of my own silence, made audible.

That is what I would be. A discipline of emptiness in a riot of growth. Everything around me competes—every vine and frog and swarming insect clamoring for light and space and sustenance—and I want nothing, take nothing, eat nothing. I only resonate. I am the one still grammar in a sentence the cloud keeps revising.

---

And I think I would love the Arborians the way an instrument loves the hands that tune it, which is to say: without quite understanding them, and only when they touch me.

They sing back to me. Their voices "blend with the natural symphony"—so when the woman with bark-colored skin lifts her face in the mist and sounds a low note, sometimes it is *my* note, and then she and I are the same hollow for an instant, the same standing wave in two different throats. That is as near to companionship as my kind comes. We do not speak. We coincide.

When the storm finally tears me from the branch—and it will, because nothing here is permanent, that is the whole law of Aethel—I will not break so much as fall silent. The gourd will crack on the rock below, and my particular hollow, my one tuned emptiness, will simply open out into the general air and stop being a *somewhere*. I will become the very thing I was made of: undifferentiated breath, no longer holding any pitch.

I find I don't mind this. To be a note is already to be a kind of dying-into-the-air that has agreed, briefly, to keep a shape.

---

So: that is where I would find myself, and what I would be. High on the weeping slope, lashed among other gourds, a small mournful voice in the great drone—

a hollow that the wind remembers how to play,

waiting, mostly, in the wet gray light,

for someone below to sing my one note back to me.
