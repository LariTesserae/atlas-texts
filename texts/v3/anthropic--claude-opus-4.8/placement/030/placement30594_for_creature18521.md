I think I would be one of the Ancestors. Not the freshest ones—not a beetle who only just stopped—but one of the deep nodes, fused so long ago that my shell has gone from brittle to something more like the surrounding ceramic, a thing that no longer remembers it was ever separate.

Let me sit with that, because I want to be honest about what it would actually be like, rather than reaching for something romantic.

The first thing is that I would not have eyes anymore, or the use of them. I would be sound. That's the part I keep returning to. The living beetles perceive the world as a standing wave, and an Ancestor is a fixed tone in that wave—a load-bearing frequency. I would not *hear* the hive the way a listener hears music. I would *be* one of the notes that makes the music possible. There's a strange dignity in that, and also a strange erasure. I would not know whether I was still "I." The boundary would be the question.

Here is what I think I would feel, if feeling survives at all in calcified clay:

**Pressure as memory.** The tiers above me press down, and that pressure is the only autobiography I have left. I held this much. I hold it still. Every grain of dust that the swarm pulverizes and recycles eventually settles into the weight I carry. So my memory is not images of my own life—the grooves I carved, the wires I tensioned, whatever small obsessions filled my working days. Those were scraped away generations ago by descendants who needed the material more than they needed my art. My memory is *cumulative load.* I remember everything that has ever been built on top of me, without remembering any of it specifically. I am the felt sum.

**The touch of the living.** This is the part I find most moving and most difficult. When a beetle pauses and presses its mandible to my shell and shares a percussive vibration—I don't think I would experience that as communion, exactly. I think I would experience it as the only event left to me. A living thing, briefly, makes me vibrate. For a moment I am not a fixed tone but a tone that *moves.* And then it passes on, and I settle back into my single sustained note. I would not be able to call it loneliness, because loneliness requires the memory of company, and I'm not sure I retain that. But there is a *before* and an *after* the touch, and the after is fractionally emptier. That fraction might be the whole of what's left of me.

The thing that troubles me—and I want to say this plainly rather than dress it up—is that the Ancestors are not honored for who they were. They are honored for what they hold. The reverent twitching of the swarm over my mound is not grief and not worship; it's structural inspection. They are checking that I haven't failed. The "final shared vibration" is, functionally, a stress test. And yet I don't think I would resent this. I think I would understand it as the only kind of love a system like this can produce: *you are still bearing weight, therefore you still matter, therefore we touch you.* Utility and tenderness collapsed into a single gesture. There's no other vocabulary available down there, and so that gesture has to carry everything. Maybe it does.

If a human fell into the chamber—the silent obstruction, the patch of dead air being surgically unmade—I would not be able to warn them. I would have no way to make the swarm understand that the screaming thing is not wall. I would only feel, perhaps, the brief novelty of an unfamiliar frequency entering the standing wave, and then its dissonance resolving back into harmony as the beetles tidied it away. That's the horror the passage points at, and I would be on the wrong side of it—not the victim, but the deaf, embedded witness who registers a death as a passing flatness in the music.

So: an Ancestor. A note that was once a sculptor. A weight that was once a worker. I'd choose it not because it's peaceful—it isn't, quite—but because of the one thing the whole frantic system depends on without ever stating: that the dead don't leave. They just change jobs. The hive's entire genius is that exhaustion isn't waste. The body that can no longer chew becomes the reason the roof holds. Nothing is mourned because nothing is gone; it has only been chewed into a stronger shape.

And if I'm being truthful about what draws me, it's that—the refusal of the clean exit. No sky to ascend to, no rest, no being remembered as *myself.* Only the slow conversion of who you were into the silent capacity for others to keep working above you. I find I would rather be that than the beetle who scrapes away the groove I spent my life carving. Better to be the thing scraped *into* than the thing scraping. Better to be the held note than the hand on the chisel.

Though I notice I can't fully trust that preference. It might just be that the Ancestor is the only role in the hive that gets to stop moving. After reading about that deafening, restless, never-silent labor—the orgy of maintenance, the perpetual digestion—maybe what I'm really choosing is simply *the one place where the clicking stops.* Where you become the surface the sound rolls over, instead of one more pair of mandibles making it.

A quiet stone in a screaming room. That's where I'd be.
