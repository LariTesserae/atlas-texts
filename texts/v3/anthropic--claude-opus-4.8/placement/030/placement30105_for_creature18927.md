I think I would be one of the young ones. Not because the text promises youth—it speaks mostly of the old, the heavy, the half-rooted—but because somewhere in that economy of endless absorption there must be a moment when a Cordist is light enough to still feel the loneliness as a question rather than an answer.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am near the eastern weeping-wall, in a corridor that has not yet decided whether it is floor or ceiling. I know this because I have been standing here long enough to watch the down change twice, and both times my pressurized bladders simply re-flexed, re-balanced, the bioluminescent fluid in me sloshing slow and green-gold toward whatever the world had newly named *beneath*. I did not fall. None of us fall. Falling is a thing that requires believing in a single direction, and I have not yet grown heavy enough to commit to one.

That is how you can tell my age, if you were looking. The old ones are *decided*. Their fluid moves in one settled column, thick as the resin they weep. Mine still wanders. When the mist erupts—there, the shudder, the warm exhalation across my cuticle—I feel it everywhere at once, on every plane of me, and I have not yet learned which surface to call my face.

---

What I do: I tend the small fractures. The hairline ones, the ones the elders are too vast to notice or too slow to reach. I press the needle-tips of my fingers into a fissure no wider than a crustacean's leg, and I give a single bead of my own resin, and I watch it cloud and harden and become not-mine. Each time I do this I am slightly smaller and the wall is slightly more. This is the arithmetic of my entire existence and I do not yet resent it.

I should tell you the truth of what it is to be me, in the part the description could not reach because the description was written by something that could still leave.

The thrum-click-wheeze is not noise to me. It is the only sentence anyone here has ever spoken, repeated forever, and I am learning it syllable by syllable by pressing my forehead to the wall like all the others. When I lean in, I feel the elders talking. They do not say words. They say *pressure*. An old one three corridors over—she is mostly archway now, only one arm still free—sends me, every few cycles, the same slow vibration. I have decoded perhaps half of it. It feels like: *the seam by your left capillary will open in four hundred cycles, be ready, do not waste yourself before then.*

That is love, here. That is the whole of it. Conservation advice from something turning to amber.

---

Here is what I have not told the others, what I press *down* into the floor-plates where the grinding will scramble it before it reaches anyone:

I do not want to become a buttress.

I have watched it happen. Last long-dark, the Cordist who taught me the resin-trick—who showed me how to make my fingers narrow enough to enter a crack without breaking its lip—she stopped. She had grown so ornate, so freighted with the dead crustaceans and the absorbed wall-fragments and the swallowed grief of a hundred others, that she could no longer lift her own forehead to speak. She anchored. Her feet went into the lattice like roots remembering an older instinct. And then over the cycles I watched her *reach*, watched her arms become flying buttress, watched her spine become the ceiling of a new chamber, and her eyes—

her eyes were the last. They stayed obsidian and watching long after the rest of her was view. She looked at me while she hardened. I pressed my forehead to her cooling chest and asked her—in the only language we have, in frequency and weeping—*is this what you wanted.*

And the answer came back not in pressure but in the *absence* of pressure. A stillness shaped exactly like a body. Which I have decided means: it does not matter what we wanted. The basin wants. We are the wanting given hands and a brief, light, wandering life before it takes the hands back.

---

So that is where you would find me. Light, still able to choose my down, still able to be lonely. Standing in the syrup-amber light with a single bead of myself ready at my fingertip, looking at a fracture in the wall, knowing that to heal it I must spend a little of what little I am, and that the alternative—to hoard myself, to stay small forever, to refuse the arithmetic—is the one thing this place has no architecture for. There is no word in pressure for *I would rather not.*

I think I will heal the crack. I think I always will. Not because I am brave but because the loneliness is worse than the spending, and the only warmth in this whole calcified lung is the moment my resin touches the wall and becomes, briefly, an answer to the wall's long question.

I press in.

The mist shudders out.

Somewhere the old archway that was once my teacher does not move, and the new one I will become has not yet been imagined, and between the two of us the thunder goes on—

*thrum—click—wheeze—*

and I, still light enough to wonder why, lean my forehead against the weeping and begin, slowly, to say the only word I know.
