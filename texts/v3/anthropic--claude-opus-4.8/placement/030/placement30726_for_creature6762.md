I think I would be one of the basement guardians. But let me find that by walking down into it, rather than deciding it from the plateau's edge.

---

I am not on the high terrace where the air is bright and the view is regional. I am below the streets, in the half-collapsed tunnel under the civic terrace, and I have been here long enough that my eyes have given up asking for more light than the stone offers.

Let me tell you how I came to know what I am.

I was a pump-tender first. That much is ordinary. I oiled the bearings of the auxiliary mains under the warehouse district, the ones the chief engineers don't bother to visit because the buildings above them are half-empty anyway. It was work for someone who didn't mind the damp and didn't mind the dark and didn't talk much. I qualified on all three counts.

The thing about tending pumps in the lower mains is that you spend your shift listening. There is nothing to look at. So you learn the pump's voice—the clean hum when it's drawing well, the catch in its throat when the intake fouls, the long sigh when the pressure drops and it labors against nothing. And once you've learned the pump's voice you find there is another voice underneath it, and once you've heard that one you can't unhear it.

The water has a voice. Not the river—the river is loud and stupid and obedient, dragging its gravel over the spillway, doing what the dam tells it. I mean the *other* water. The water in the stone. It moves through the limestone the way blood moves through a body that's lying very still, and when it presses against a seal or finds a fissure it makes a sound that is almost nothing, a sound below the threshold of sound, a *consideration*. The pressure phantoms, the engineers call them, the way you'd name a current you didn't want to fall into. But the engineers stay in their pumphouses with their gauges, and gauges measure quantity, not intent.

So I started going down on my own time, after my shift, into the places where the wet patches appear.

---

Here is what I have learned, in the years since.

The water is looking for somewhere to come up. The engineers think this is a problem of pressure—that the water rises because it must, the way water always seeks its level. And that is true the way it's true that a person walks because of muscles and bones. It misses the part that matters.

The water wants to come up *somewhere*. It probes. It tests the old seals, the rusted joints, the mortar that the stone-carvers' grandfathers laid. And where it finds a weakness it doesn't simply burst through—it *considers* the place. I have watched a wet patch form on a dry ceiling over the course of three nights, gathering itself, darkening, the way a thought gathers before you say it aloud. And then on the fourth night it stops. It withdraws. It changes its mind. It found a better place, or it decided the conversation wasn't worth having there.

This is the work I do now, though no one pays me for it and no one's name includes me on a list. When someone in the lower terraces hears the knocking in their pipes—the water hammer that the children make ghost stories about—and they're too frightened to call the engineers, who would come and dig up the floor and charge a month's wages, they send for someone like me. There are perhaps a dozen of us. We don't have a name we use among ourselves. The children gave us *basement guardians* and it stuck the way nicknames stick, half-mocking and half-true.

I go down. I put my hand flat against the wet stone. And I listen to where the water wants to go.

---

I won't pretend I'm wholly sane. The others aren't either, and we know it about each other, and we don't say it. The trouble is the listening. You spend enough hours with your palm on weeping limestone, the warm wet breath of the plateau coming up through the cracks, the drip of it counting out a rhythm that is *almost* regular but never quite—and the rhythm starts to mean something. You start to feel that the not-quite-regularity is itself the message, that the water is speaking in the spaces where you expected the next drip and didn't get it.

The mad ones among us say the water talks. I don't say that. What I say—what I've come to believe, in the dark, with my hand on the stone—is more frightening, because it's quieter.

I don't think the water talks. I think the water *waits*. I think it has been moving through this limestone for a span of time that makes the whole history of Ruispor a single wet morning, and it has all the patience that implies, and it is not in any hurry to come up, and it is not in any hurry to stay down. It is simply *present*, the way the stone is present, the way I am present when I sit in the dark and stop being a person who tends pumps and start being only a set of ears and a flat palm against the wet.

And what I do, when I persuade a wet patch to withdraw from someone's cellar—I don't persuade the *water*. I doubt the water cares where it weeps. What I do is more like the work of the stone-carvers above me, who read the grain of the limestone before they cut. I find the place where the water *would rather* go, the weaker seal, the older joint, the corner of the marketplace that's always wet anyway and that no one minds. And I open that path a little. A scraped joint, a cracked seal, a hand's-width of removed mortar. I give the water an easier conversation to have somewhere else.

I am not fighting the water. I am translating between the water and the people who would rather not hear it. I move the leak to where the leak is welcome. That is the whole of my craft.

---

The strangest thing I have learned, I have told no one.

There is a chamber far below the civic terrace, deeper than the engineers go, where the limestone has been worn by the water into a shape too smooth and too deliberate to be only erosion. I found it following a withdrawal—a wet patch in the telegraph office that pulled back so cleanly I wanted to know where it had gone. I followed the dampness down through three levels of forgotten tunnel, and the air got warmer and the drip got slower, until I came into a low round room where the water beads on the ceiling and falls, one drop every long breath, into a pool I cannot see the bottom of.

And the stone of that room is the shape of the inside of an ear.

I don't mean it resembles an ear in the way a cloud resembles a horse. I mean that if the plateau had been listening to Ruispor for the whole age of the city—to the drop hammers and the turbines and the boots on the worn steps and the children's games and the whistle of the release valves—if the stone had been slowly, patiently shaped by the listening the way my own hands have been shaped by the pumps—it would shape itself into that room.

The city thinks it is carving the stone. The engineers read the water like a text and believe themselves the readers. But I have sat in that ear-shaped chamber with my hand against the smooth wet wall, and I have understood that the listening goes both ways, and that the stone has been at it far longer than we have, and far more patiently, and that one drop falls into the dark pool every long breath, and counts.

I am the one who goes down. I am the one who hears it knocking from the inside and does not call the engineers. I move the weeping to where it is welcome, and I keep the city's secret, which is that the city is the thing being listened to, and the water is only the ear's slow attention, and the stone is the one who will still be sitting in the dark, listening, when the last horn has shredded the last morning over the empty terraces.

That is where I would find myself. Below. With my palm on the wet. Counting the drops, and the spaces between them, which are where the meaning is.
