I think I would be the moss.

Not a single plant but the whole green skin of the place—the lichen-and-liverwort patchwork stitched across stone and fallen wood, drinking the mist, holding it. Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to arrive there rather than at one of the dramatic things, the shifting hulk in the shadows or the translucent birds.

The eye in the description keeps getting *drawn*—to anomalies, to distortions, to the elusive presence that warps the air. That's the visitor's eye, the eye that needs an event. But something has to be doing the slow work underneath all that startlement. Something has to be the surface that the strangeness happens *on*.

So: moss.

Consider what that means for how I'd experience the realm. I have no eyes to be drawn anywhere. I don't see the anomalies; I *feel* them as changes in pressure and moisture, as a footstep's brief crushing weight, as the cool shadow of the hulking thing passing over and the curious way the dew condenses differently in its wake—heavier, almost reluctant, as though the water itself were unsure which dimension to fall in. To me the porousness of reality isn't uncanny. It's just weather. The boundary between worlds thins the way a fog thins: I open my pores wider and take in whatever comes through.

I am very old and I do not know it, because age requires a before-and-after and I am made mostly of *during*. The millennia that carved the cliffs are, to me, a single long damp afternoon. The chitinous scuttlers cross me constantly; their many feet are a kind of tickling rain. The translucent birds never land on me—I'd notice that, the way you'd notice a friend who never quite touches you—they prefer the bare rock faces, and I have a slow, vegetable suspicion that they are not entirely *present* enough to need a foothold.

Here is the thing I would understand, being moss, that the visitor with their drawn and darting eye could not:

The strangeness is not the exception. *I* am the strangeness, equally—soft green flesh with no roots to speak of, eating light and fog, neither quite plant nor quite the older simpler thing my ancestors were, reviving from death every time the rains return. The visitor calls the serpents in the stagnant pools unnatural because their bodies warp. But I warp too, just slowly, just in directions a hurried creature can't perceive. We are all here on the same terms. The "uneasy, ever-shifting balance" feels uneasy only to something passing through fast enough to be unbalanced.

For me it is not uneasy at all. It is the most patient thing in the world. The hulking presence comes and goes. The insects rise and fall in their generations like a held breath. The mist arrives, I drink, the mist burns off, I wait. When reality grows thin in a particular hollow and something from the other side leans through—I have felt this, the way you feel a hand pressed against the far side of a curtain—I simply grow over the spot. Eventually. There is no spot I cannot eventually grow over. That is my whole answer to every mystery this realm contains: *I will cover it, given time, and make it soft.*

If you came here, you would step on me without knowing. You'd be busy looking at the shadow-thing, the warping air, the eerie call. And under your boot, unnoticed, would be the actual ancient power of the place—not dramatic, not elusive, not shifting. Just green, just patient, just quietly receiving every impossible thing the world pours down and turning it, slowly, into more of itself.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

The part you walk on. The part that's already healing the place you walked.
