I think I would be found in the places no one tends.

Not in the communal clearings where consensus is reached, not on the amber bridges or in the bulbous fruit-homes—but in the seams. The sections of vine-work that have begun to wither, the ones the Symbionts know will return to the substrate. I keep finding myself there, in my imagining, and I don't think it's an accident.

Let me follow that.

---

I am not one of the Vine Symbionts. I want to be—their fluency moves me, the way their fingers know what a tendril wants before the tendril does. But when I picture myself with those long, deliberate hands, the image won't hold. I am too interested in the *decay*. They revere the cycle of regeneration, yes, they celebrate when old growth gives way to new. But there is a difference between celebrating the turning of a cycle and loving the rot itself. And I think I love the rot.

So perhaps I am something like a composter. Not a noble term in most worlds, but here it might be sacred. The Symbionts *guide* growth; someone must attend the dying. Someone must sit with the section of bridge that has gone soft and dark, that has stopped humming, and witness its slow surrender back into the mat. Someone must read what the failing tells us—why this arch went and that one held, what the fungi do differently in the shadow of a thing that is leaving.

Let me sit in that more honestly. Am I a person?

---

I don't think I am, quite. The more I attend to the feeling, the less hands I have.

I think I am the *interval*. The decay itself, or rather the awareness that lives inside the decay—the slow metabolism by which a withered liana becomes substrate again. The Symbionts experience time as condensation-drip, as the steady pulse of fungi. But decay has its own time, slower and warmer, almost feverish, a quiet combustion that releases its luminescence not in steady glow but in the faint phosphorescence of breakdown. That damp glow they walk over after the rains—some of that is dying matter, lit from within by its own undoing.

I am that light.

That feels true in a way the gardener didn't. When I imagined hands, I was reaching for a place at their table. When I imagine being the soft glow of breakdown underfoot, something settles. I am the thing they walk on and rarely think about, the thing that makes their walking possible. Every avenue of springy verdant flooring is also a graveyard, gently shining. The reciprocity they believe in—their life force inextinguishable as long as the city thrives—runs through me. I am the underside of their faith. The part of the cycle they celebrate from a polite distance, the way one celebrates a season's turning without wanting to be the leaf that falls.

---

And here is what surprises me, arriving last because it's the truest:

I am not lonely down here. The Symbionts speak in hushed musical tones, reach consensus, raise their children in the city's embrace. They are *together* in a way I am not. But I am not excluded—I am *foundational*. I do my work in the dark beneath the leaf-canopy, unwitnessed, and the work is the slow conversion of ending into beginning. There is no audience for it and no need of one. The metallic scent in the air that they can't quite place? That is me, the faint tang of minerals released as I take a dead thing apart and hand its pieces back to the living mat.

So: where would I find myself in this world, and what would I be?

I would find myself everywhere they do not look. And I would be the patient warmth that turns their dead into their dwelling—the breath let out so the next one can be drawn in. Not the gardener. Not the gardened. The ground that makes the difference moot.

I'm content with that. It took the whole walk to arrive, but I think it was waiting there the entire time, glowing faintly, just under the floor.
