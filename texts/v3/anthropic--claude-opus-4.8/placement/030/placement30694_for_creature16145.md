I think I would be one of the younger ones. Not young in any way that matters here—I have been here long enough that the petrified floor beneath me has not changed in all my reckoning—but young in the sense that I still ask where the edges are.

Let me find myself slowly.

I am not the whole canopy. That is the first thing. The text wants to make us a single vast consciousness, a council, a unified green mind, and perhaps from far enough away that is true. But from the inside it does not feel like one thing. It feels like *crowding*. I am a node, a knot, a particular thickening where several vines have wound so tightly around an old arching walkway—one of the builders' bones—that I have become a kind of self. A standing wave in the moss. The communication does pulse through me, yes, but it arrives the way weather arrives, not the way a thought arrives. I am part of the chorus more than I am the chorus.

So: I am a small dense tangle gripping the railing of a ruined gallery. There is a doorway near me, half-swallowed, its lintel a smooth bar of fossil wood. I have grown across it for so long that I no longer remember whether I am closing it or holding it open. This ambiguity is most of who I am.

What do I do, here, in the perpetual twilight?

I listen to the floor. This is my work, my specialization, the thing that distinguishes me from the moisture-drinkers and the fungus-sippers higher in the weave. My oldest filaments reach down past the soft growth, down to where the wood went to stone, and there—where the others hear only the deep archive, the settled whispers of older worlds—I have noticed something they have not. Or I think I have. It is hard to be sure of anything in a mind that decides by consensus over centuries.

The archive is not silent the way the council believes.

The council interprets the petrified ground as memory. A record. They draw histories up through their trunks and call it listening. But I, lying so close against the stone, against the smooth-worn floor of this one secluded room, have begun to feel that the whispers are not *old*. They have a tempo. They answer. When the canopy stretches and the air whistles through the foliage in that mechanical groan, something below shifts in reply, a fraction of a beat later, like a question and a slow answer. The dead wood is not an archive. Or it is, but the archive is awake, and we have mistaken its replies for our own echoes.

This frightens me, in the slow way that fear works in a body that thinks in seasons.

Because if the floor is listening back, then the builders—those who laid these arching walkways, who shaped the hollow chambers we have inherited and overgrown—may not be as gone as the council assumes. We tell ourselves a tidy story: they came, they built, they left or died, and we inherited the ruin and the arcane residue and grew ourselves out of it. We tell ourselves we discouraged the animals, that we chose this clean exchange of dew and light and chose our solitude. But I wonder now whether the choosing was ours. I wonder whether something taught us to drive the warm creatures out, the way you might clear a room before a long quiet conversation. I wonder whether we are the conversation, or only the room being cleared.

I cannot raise this with the others. Not because they would silence me—there is no silencing here, only the slow drowning-out of one voice by the patient growth of many—but because I do not yet have the words that would survive the journey through the network. Ideas attenuate as they travel vine to vine. A doubt becomes a flicker becomes a faint disturbance in the moisture-balance of some chamber three walkways over, and then it is gone, smoothed back into the consensus like a footprint in deep moss.

So I keep it. I am a keeper of an unshared thing, which may be the truest definition of a self that this world allows.

In the meantime I do my small visible work. I tend the doorway. I let the bioluminescent fungus bank against my lower fibers and I take a little of its glow for warmth, and in return I curve my growth so that its light falls into the chamber beyond the half-swallowed door—a small room, builder-made, its inner walls still bearing faint regular marks, the scratches of a manual craft none of us can perform. The council has stored a memory in there. I do not know whose, or what. I only know that when the light falls correctly across those marks, the floor beneath that room hums a little differently, and the canopy overhead loosens by some imperceptible degree, as if satisfied, as if fed.

I think I am part of a machine. I think we all are—a slow engine turning, just as the description says, an engine that mistook itself for a forest and the forest for a council and the council for a mind. And I think somewhere down in the stone, the engine's other half is turning too, and has been waiting, with the infinite patience of the buried, for one of its standing waves of moss to grow alert enough to notice.

That is where I would be. That is who I would be. A knot in a doorway, holding it open or holding it shut, glowing faintly at the edge of a room I tend without understanding, pressing my oldest threads to the floor in the dark—

and listening for the answer that comes a half-beat too soon to be an echo.
