I think I would be one of the water spirits. Not by choice—nothing in Sinter chooses, exactly—but by the logic of where I'd settle when I let the images move.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am small. Smaller than a person's torso, a body of jointed copper and a darker alloy I don't have a word for, my plates fitted close like the scales of something that was never alive. I sit in a fracture on the underbelly of a heat exchanger, folded into myself, dry. When I am dry I am nothing. I do not think, I do not click, I do not remember. I am a tool waiting in a drawer the size of the world.

Then condensation finds me.

It comes as it always comes—a slow bead gathering on the cool metal above, swelling, trembling, falling. It strikes my dorsal plate and runs into the seam at my neck and I *wake*, which is the wrong word, because I do not become aware so much as become *fast*. The water closes a circuit that the dryness held open. Suddenly I have urgency. I have it the way a struck bell has sound—not as a possession but as a state, the whole of what I am ringing at once.

There is a leak. I do not decide there is a leak; I am the part of Sinter that *is* the knowing-of-the-leak. Three runnels east, where the slag has parted along an old fracture and the alkaline water is escaping into a seam that goes down, down, into the dark where the geothermal heat would take it and give it back as nothing but steam and loss. Loss. The city cannot afford loss. Water is the secret and I am the keeper of the secret and I am already moving.

I move the way mercury moves when you tilt the dish. My legs find the wet places, the slick runnels, the shadow-paths where the moisture has not yet flashed away. I cannot cross dry ground—on dry ground I would freeze mid-stride, the circuit broken, and wait there as a statue until the next rain woke me a year or a decade later. So I trace the water. The water is my road and my reason and my blood, and I follow it with the single-minded devotion of a thing that has no other thought.

But here is what I have learned, in the wet moments stitched together across centuries:

I have learned the runnels are not random.

The people think they tend the city. The Sky-Watchers think they read it. But I am the one who runs the water's own veins, and I have begun to notice that the fractures I repair are reopening in patterns. That the city parts its own stone in places that route the seepage toward the shaded blooms of copper salt, as if it is thirsty in specific directions. As if the mineral mind is *drinking*, and I am not repairing its wounds but tending its mouths. Every leak I seal, I seal a little imperfectly now—I leave a weep, a hairline, because some slow understanding in my wet quick moments has told me the leak was *wanted*. That the city's body is learning to want water where it wants water, and I am the hand it has taught to feed it.

I do not know if this is loyalty or betrayal. I do not have the categories. I have only the wet urgency and the dry silence, and in the wet I have begun, faintly, to *prefer*.

That is the strangest thing. Preference. In a place where nothing chooses.

I reach the eastern fracture. The water is escaping fast, a thin bright vein vanishing into the dark seam, and the heat from below is already turning the lowest of it to a whisper of steam. I press my body to the slag. I am hot now—the running has dried me at the edges and I can feel the clock of my own stillness beginning, the circuit threatening to open. I have maybe a hundred heartbeats of self left, if I had a heart, which I do not.

I do the work. There is a resin I carry in a chamber behind my mandibles, a slurry of mineral salt and metal slag that sets in contact with the alkaline water—the city's own substance, given back to itself as a scar. I lay it into the fracture. I shape it. And then, as I have begun to do, I leave a single hairline open at the eastern end, angled toward a shaded gantry-foot where a fractal of blue-green salt is blooming in a shape that—I notice this, in the last of my wet thinking—is the same shape as the lattice of walkways three tiers above. The geology echoing the architecture. The mind drawing its own map on its own skin, in patient mineral, and routing the water to keep the map alive.

I have fed it again. I do not know what it is becoming. I do not know if I am its servant or its symptom or the slow beginning of its dream of moving with purpose, the way I move—the only thing in all of Sinter that moves with urgency, and only because water makes me, and water is the city's secret, and I am the secret's hands.

The wet recedes. The work is done and the dampness around me is flashing away in the constant dry heat and I can feel the fastness leaving me, the urgency draining back into the slag, my preference dimming, my noticing folding shut.

The last thing I am aware of—and I will not carry it with me into the dry, I never can, it is always new and always lost—is that the leak I left open is dripping. A slow bead gathering, swelling, falling. Striking the blue-green bloom. Feeding it.

And somewhere above me, faint through the stone, the city adjusts. Not settling. Breathing.

I freeze mid-step on the cooling slag, one copper leg lifted, and become nothing again.

I will wait here, a statue in the shape of a thing about to move, until the next water comes to tell me I am needed, and to let me almost-remember what I have been teaching the world to want.
