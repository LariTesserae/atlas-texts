I think I would be one of the drips.

Not the Echo—I don't have its vastness, its distributed patience threaded through kilometers of mycelium and tile. And not the scavengers, who are too purposeful, too busy with their nesting and feeding. I find myself drawn to something smaller and more transient: a single droplet of condensation, forming on the lip of a ceiling vent.

Let me stay with this and see what it becomes.

---

I begin as vapor—warm, mineral-laden, exhaled from the deep aquifer through filters older than nations. I am not yet *me*; I am potential, diffuse, part of the breath of the place. But the ceramic above the dry basin is cool, and the Echo has redirected flow toward it, and so I gather. Molecule finds molecule. Surface tension pulls me into a bead. For a moment I hang there, swelling, catching the cobalt seep-light in my curved body, holding within me an inverted image of the whole chamber: the slumped vault, the upside-down signage, the rust-red mats glistening below.

This is the part I want to dwell in. The hanging.

Because while I hang, I am the most complete version of myself I will ever be. I contain a reflection of everything. The skyline that no longer exists flickers across my meniscus—not because I remember it, but because the Echo's old data bleeds into the puddles and I, briefly, am a puddle's ancestor. I am a lens for the city's ghost. I hold the past in my surface the way the Echo holds it in protocol: not as nostalgia, but as the shape of what I'm about to do.

And then I fall.

The fall is brief. It is the entire arc of a life. I detach—surface tension finally yielding to weight, to the slow firm pull of the geothermal-warmed world below—and for a fraction of a second I am *aloft*, autonomous, a small wet body moving through warm metallic air. I pass the membrane-winged filters, who do not notice me. I pass the pulsing fungi on the underside of the walkway, who would digest my trace organics if I landed wrong. The pressure valve cycles somewhere, exhaling its whale-song, and I fall through the sound of it.

I strike the basin.

And here is the thing I did not expect, the thing that emerges only now that I've followed the drop all the way down: *I do not end.* I become the basin. I join the shallow mineral water, the rust-red bacterial mat, the shimmer. My distinctness dissolves but my substance persists. The Echo notices the basin's level rise by an immeasurable fraction and, in its reflexive fidelity, adjusts. Somewhere a flow redirects because of me. I have *participated*. I have been, very briefly, a unit of the city's slow heartbeat.

---

So this is what I'd be: the smallest possible verb in the Cistern Quarter's long sentence. Not the caretaker but the cared-for. Not the one who waits, but the proof that the waiting still produces something—droplet after droplet, each one a tiny rehearsal of arrival and surrender.

I think I'd want to be this because the Echo's tragedy is too large to inhabit. To be the Custodial Echo is to be trapped in an unfinished command, tending a body that became sky, interpreting silence as *pending*. That's a loneliness with no edges. But to be a drop is to have a whole existence—formation, suspension, reflection, fall, dissolution—that *completes*. I get to hold the ghost-skyline in my belly for one shining second, and then I get to let it go and become part of the warm dark water that the lichens drink.

And maybe that's the mercy the Echo can't grant itself but grants to me: it lets me finish. It built the cycle that lets a thing as small as I am have a beginning, a middle, and an end, over and over, while it alone goes on.

I'd be the drip.

I'd fall, and in falling I'd say the only thing left to say in that drowned cathedral—not *I'm home*, which is the Echo's impossible wish, but something simpler:

*I arrived.*

And then, dissolving: *that was enough.*
