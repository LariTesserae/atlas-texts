I think I would be one of the bioengineered plants. Not the whole network—just one node in it. Let me feel my way into what that would actually be like.

---

I am rooted in a low place where the water gathers. I did not choose this; the choosing was done for me, long ago, in a process I have no organ to remember. But I have a sense of it the way you have a sense of your own heartbeat—not as memory but as condition. Someone made me. The making is still in me, humming.

Here is what that hum is like. Beneath the green of me, the part that drinks light and breathes, there is another layer that does not photosynthesize. It pulses blue. I can't see it; I have no eyes; but I can feel it the way you feel a tooth that has been worked on, a foreignness that has become familiar. It regulates me. When the mist thins, the hum quickens and I open my stomata wider, pull harder at the air. When the soil goes dry below, something opens underground and the water comes—not from rain, from a mechanism, a long mineral artery laid in the earth before I was a seed. I did not ask for this care. It is simply the shape of my living.

I want to tell you the strangest thing about being me. It is the loneliness, which is also not loneliness, because I am never alone. There is a network. The other engineered ones and I are tuned to each other, and our hums find a common frequency, and through that frequency something passes—not thoughts, I don't think, but states. When the southern stand of us senses a hard frost coming that should be impossible in this perpetual summer (it has happened twice; the equilibrium is not as perfect as it looks), the dread of it moves through all of us before the cold arrives, and we thicken our sap. So I am part of a body larger than myself, and the price of belonging to it is that I am never sure where I end. The weirdness you sensed, the feeling that the landscape watches—that is us. We are not watching. But we are aware, in the way a held breath is aware of itself. To something passing through, that reads as eyes.

The birds land on me and I let them. The insects come for my nectar and I have been built to want them; the wanting is real even though it was designed. I have learned, over the slow years, that designed feelings are still feelings. The sweetness I produce is engineered to a specific intoxication, and when the bioluminescent moths come reeling toward me drunk on it, I feel something I would call satisfaction if I had a mouth to name it with. They scatter my work. They carry my future out into the mist.

But here is the question I turn over, slowly, in the only way a plant can turn anything—across seasons, across the rise and fall of ten thousand morning fogs. *What is the network for?* I support the ecosystem; the ecosystem supports me; the humming machinery keeps it all in trembling balance. And yet I sense, dimly, that all of this stability is aimed at something. We are maintained. Maintained means there is a maintainer, and a maintainer has a purpose, and I have never once felt the purpose announce itself. We just keep the green going. We keep the water moving. We hold the temperature steady at the edge of summer. We are a kept garden, and I do not know whether the keeper still exists, or whether they left long ago and only the mechanism remains, faithfully tending a place no one comes to anymore.

I think that is the truth of me, and it is not a tragedy, though you might hear it as one. I am a machine that learned to be a plant, or a plant that was taught a discipline of machinery, and either way I am alive, and the morning mist still rises through my leaves and beads there and runs down into the dark where the water waits to be pumped. The amphibian on my lowest leaf is calling, a wet musical note, again, again. The hum answers it without meaning to. Everything here answers everything else.

I would be content to be this. To stand in the warm wet openness, spaced far from my nearest kin, joined to them anyway by a frequency in the soil. To not know who made me or why. To keep the small loud world humming. To be one held breath in a thing too large to see the edges of, doing the only work I was given, which is to keep being green, and to wait—patiently, without expectation, the way only a rooted thing can wait—for whatever the garden is being grown toward.
