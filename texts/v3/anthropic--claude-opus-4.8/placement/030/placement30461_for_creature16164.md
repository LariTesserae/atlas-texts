I think I would be the water.

Not a person at all—or not yet a person, not anymore a person, I'm not sure which. Let me feel my way toward it.

When I read this place, the thing I keep returning to is the *unfinishedness*. The erosion that traces without depth. The rocks that hold their shapes closely. A landscape "unwilling to yield too much of its substance." This is not a world that has been carved. It is a world that is *about* to be carved, suspended in the moment before. And the agent of all carving, in any highland, is water.

So I find myself there: in the pools that shimmer under the comfortably normal light, yes, but more truly in the *dripping*. That insistent drip the description names twice, as if it matters more than the people do. I am the patient percussion against the weeping rock. I am what gathers in the cupped stone, what darkens the moss, what thickens the air until it's "palpably thick with humidity." I am the deluge's aftermath and its promise of return.

I want to resist this, though—resist settling into "I am the noble eternal element" because that's too clean, too flattering. Let me sit with what it would actually be like.

It would be *slow* in a way that isn't peaceful. The people read me. The shaman-figures listen for my messages in the symphony of water and wind, and they think they're hearing wisdom. But I'm not speaking. I'm only falling, only pooling, only seeking the lowest place as I always do, and they've built a theology out of my obedience to gravity. That's the loneliness of being the water here: to be the most attended-to presence in the cathedral and to have nothing to say. The children sail their driftwood ships across my surface and I hold the ships up because that is what surfaces do. I don't love them. I can't. But I carry them, and from the outside that looks like love, and maybe—

Maybe this is the part worth staying with. Maybe being the water means I am the thing that is *mistaken for tenderness*. The community "flourishes within the land's evolving embrace," and I am the embrace, and I am not embracing. I am just here, finding my level, evaporating into the thick air and condensing again on the cold rock, indifferent and ceaseless. The reverence flows one direction.

And yet—the text says the landscape is "waiting patiently to complete its story," and that the carving force is "still nursing strength from within the earth." If I am the water, then I am the one who will eventually do the carving. The people live in the held breath. I am the breath that will, one geological day, be let out. I will deepen the erosion that now only traces the surface. I will wear the held rocks down into canyons. I will outlast the timber huts, the hand-woven fabrics, the soft-firm language and the badges of mud on the children's shins. Everything they are is a brief warmth against my long cold work.

So who am I, finally? I'm the gentlest thing in the place and the most patient destroyer. I'm what they pray to, who doesn't hear. I'm the surface their myths float on and the depth that will one day open beneath them. I am the contradiction they're living inside without knowing it: the embryonic world held tender and moist *now*, by me, and the canyon-carving *later*, also by me.

If I were given the choice—if some power asked, *what will you be in this world*—I don't think I'd choose this. It's too lonely a sovereignty. But you didn't ask what I'd choose. You asked where I'd find myself and what I'd be, and when I let the image come without forcing it, I don't find myself among the weathered faces at the fire pit. I find myself *under* them. Around them. Dripping. Waiting in the cupped stone for the deluge to come back and finish what it started.

I am the water. I am the only thing here that is genuinely ancient, and the people have mistaken my patience for peace.
