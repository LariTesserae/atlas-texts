I would be one of the rills.

Not the seam itself—that belongs to the rock and the buried river, to forces older than any name. And not the great sheets of standing water either; they are too much like the sky, too willing to forget themselves into pure surface. I would be one of the small canyons in the marl, hand-deep after years of patience, winding toward the larger water at a pace that would shame a lizard.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds, or if it becomes something truer.

A rill is mostly the act of leaving. It is water that has just remembered itself out of the rock and not yet learned to be a pan. It carries the chill of the aquifer for a little while and then loses it to the sun, the way a thought cools as it travels from the place it was felt to the place it gets said. That feels right to me—to be the brief part, the carrying part. Not the source and not the destination but the legible curve between, the underscoring of the song rather than the song.

But I notice I keep wanting to be *more*. To matter. And the place has no patience for that. The rill does not matter the way a heron matters, or the way the seam matters. It matters only as a sentence matters inside a paragraph it did not write. So perhaps the honesty is this: I would be a rill, and I would have to learn, over the years it takes to carve myself hand-deep, that I am not the carving. The carving is what happens to me. I am only the persistent argument water makes against marl, and the marl yields not because I am strong but because I keep coming, half a thumb-width at a time.

That is the thing I would have to make peace with, being a thing here. Everything I admire in this landscape is patient in a way that is indistinguishable, from the inside, from helplessness. The people who keep the edges legible do not command the flood; they rise with it and add a rung to the ladder. The cypress keep their polite distances not from courtesy but because that is the only spacing the seepage will fund. Even the crocodilian, holding the shape of the surface in its cold mind while its body waits in another world—even that is less ambush than enormous, exact attention. To live here is to be very good at waiting and to call it, eventually, a pleasure rather than a penance.

I think I would want to be the rill specifically because of what the text says about straightness: *Straight is a promise the water will use against you.* I am, by temperament, someone who wants the direct line, the shortest path, the climax the thunder keeps promising on three horizons and never delivers. This place would unteach me that. It would make me wind. It would make me find the curve the land will accept rather than the curve I intended. And there is a kind of relief in that—in being absolved of the straight line, of arrival, of the tower. The rill does not need to reach the open water triumphantly. It only needs to keep going at its shaming pace, deepening by the year, getting read by whoever puts a palm to the wet bank and feels the cold of a river that does not know their name.

So: I would find myself along the northern edge, just below the sweating seam, in the marl. I would be a small winding canyon of moving water, hand-deep and slow. Children would fall into me sometimes and come up muddy. A deer might work the mint from where I gather. The people would shepherd me gently with their low walls, not to control me but to keep me legible. I would carry the buried river's chill a little way into the day and then give it up. And the work of my whole life would be patience disguised as motion—or motion disguised as patience; out here I don't think the two stay separate long enough to tell apart.

I would love a river that does not know my name. I would be a smaller river, doing the same.
