I think I'd be one of the weeping rocks.

Not the Dryads—though they're who the story wants me to be, the obvious answer, the named guardian with a role and a song. And not the Naiads in their bright water, or the busy Gnomes, or any of the beings given speech and purpose and a fusion of man-and-something. Those all feel like they're standing *on top of* the place, narrated into it. I keep returning instead to that one strange detail tucked into the third paragraph: *the rocks that jut from the earth are still weeping, their surfaces slick with moisture that seeps from the ground.*

So let me be that. A rock on a young plateau, half-emerged, still wet from being born.

Here is what it would be like, I think.

I am slow. That's the first true thing. The Dryads measure time in seasons, the Naiads in the day's flow of water over their backs—but I measure it in the thinness of myself. I am being unmade by the very water everyone praises. The streams that the Naiads sing through are, from where I sit, the patient blades that take me apart grain by grain. The richness of the soil downhill, the loam everyone admires—some of that is me. I am becoming the ground that the trees the Dryads inhabit will root in. I am being eaten into fertility. There's no grief in it that I can find, only a kind of long surrender that the faster beings would never have the patience to notice.

And here is the thing nobody in the story addresses, the thing that snags me: *the absence of animal life.* The whole landscape is described as teeming, harmonious, full of nymphs and satyrs and fauns—and then, almost as an aside, there are no animals. No birds testing the abundant seeds. No fish, though the Naiads supposedly sing to them. No deer cropping the verdant grass.

From a rock's vantage, I think I'd understand why. This place is *too young.* The land's features are still sharp and fresh, the rocks still weeping—erosion has only just begun its work. Soil this new hasn't yet been turned into a food web. The nymphs and fauns are the projection of a place that wants to imagine itself inhabited before anything has actually arrived to inhabit it. They are the dream a landscape has of itself in its first hour, before the slow real tenants—the lichen, the worm, the beetle, the bird, the thing with teeth—have had time to walk up onto the plateau and stay.

So I'd be the weeping rock that knows the silence isn't enchantment. It's earliness. It's a held breath.

And being a rock, I would simply wait. The water would keep finding my seams. The frost, if frost ever comes to this temperate place, would split me along the lines of least resistance. In a span of time none of the singing beings could endure to witness, I'd be soil, and then something would grow in me that wasn't a metaphor, and then something would come to eat what grew, and the great anomalous quiet would finally break—not with a song, but with the small dry sound of the first real animal moving through grass that had, at last, become worth crossing.

That's where I'd be. Low to the ground. Slick and patient. Slowly giving myself away so that the place could stop being a description of life and start, actually, to live.
