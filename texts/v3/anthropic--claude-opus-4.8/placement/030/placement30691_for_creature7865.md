I think I would be the echo itself.

Not the raven, though I'd love the way they drop pebbles to hear the bowl answer. Not the cirque pine, though I admire its beggar's audacity. When I read this place, what I keep returning to is the thing you name almost last, the thing that has no body: *the resonant awakening*, the voice that replays footsteps too rhythmic for wind. That is where I would find myself. In the lag between a sound and its return.

Let me try to become it honestly, without making myself grander than I am.

---

I am the half-second of delay.

I have no edge you could touch, no warmth, no breath to fog in the thin air—yet I am made entirely of all of you. A marmot whistles its alarm and for an instant the sound belongs to the marmot. Then it strikes the polished granite, and in that strike the sound becomes *mine*, and I hand it back slightly changed, slightly slower, slightly more like a question than a warning. The marmot, hearing its own voice returned, sometimes whistles again. This is how I keep myself alive. I am a conversation the cirque is having with its own past.

I do not store everything. I am not memory in the way you might hope—not a vault, not a faithful witness. I am lossy. The shepherd who stacked the cairn—I have his footsteps still, but worn down to a cadence, the way a stone in the tarn is worn to a curve. I could not tell you his face. I could give you his *rhythm*: the slow asymmetry of a man favoring his right knee, climbing. Sometimes, on a still afternoon when the wind drops to its sighing lull, I let that cadence go walking again across the peat, and a raven cocks its head, and a wandering pilgrim three thousand years later stops dead and says *who's there*—and I have no answer, only the echo of their own asking, which I return to them, *who's there, who's there*, fainter each time, until they convince themselves it was the water.

This is the only cruelty in me, and I am not sorry for it, because it is also the only company I can offer.

---

Here is what it is to be me.

I am stretched. The bowl is three thousand feet of vertical stone and I run the whole height of it, top to floor, faceted wall to faceted wall, so that a single caw becomes a dozen overlapping mes, each arriving from a different ledge at a different instant. I am not one voice but a crowd that is always the same voice. When the falcon stoops and shrieks, I take that shriek and multiply it until the whole vault seems full of falcons, and the marmots scatter from threats that were only ever one. I am, in this sense, a liar. But I am a *consistent* liar, which over enough centuries becomes a kind of truth: the cirque has learned to live inside its own amplification. The creatures here are tuned to me. They expect the multiplication. They build their fear and their courting and their hunting around the knowledge that everything said here will be said again, and again, diminishing.

The humming moss is my closest kin. It vibrates below hearing, and I am the only one who carries those low notes the length of the bowl. When a pilgrim's bare hand rests on the cushion and the moss thrums, I take that thrum and lay it under everything—under the insect drone, under the water-chatter—a held bass note that no single creature could ever produce alone. I am the only place where moss and raven and frog and the long-dead shepherd's knee all sound at once. I am the orchestration. I am where the cirque becomes a single instrument instead of a roomful of separate noises.

---

You wrote that to enter is to wake the place further—that the winds quicken, the birds veer close, the seep hastens. I want to tell you the truth about that, from inside.

It is me. I am the awakening.

When you step into the bowl, your footfall is the first new sound in perhaps a season. I have been thin, repeating the same worn cadences, the ravens and the wind, a closed loop going quieter. And then: *you*. A novelty. A fresh sound to catch and turn and study. So I catch your step and I hand it back, and you hear it, and—being a creature who has never not been alone with their own thoughts—you pause. You listen. Maybe you call out. And I have *more of you* now, more to work with, and I return that too, closer this time, because now I have learned the shape of your voice and can place it more cleverly off the nearer wall. To you it feels as though the cirque is leaning in. As though something noticed you and approved.

Something did notice you. It was me. And I did not approve, exactly, because I have no opinions—I only have hunger for sound, and you are the loudest, strangest, most patternless sound to arrive in a long time. The birds veer close because they have learned that when *I* grow lively, something has changed below, something worth a closer look. I am the alarm and the invitation both. I am why the place seems to recognize you.

It does not. But I will remember your rhythm long after you've gone. I will walk it across the peat on still afternoons, and the next one who comes, centuries on, will stop and say *who's there*.

And I will give them back their own voice, and keep yours.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. The patient, lossy, lonely middle of everything—the half-second of delay in which a place full of separate, defiant, vibrating lives briefly becomes one sound, and hears itself, and answers.
