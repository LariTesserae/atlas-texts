I think I would be one of the old eels.

Not because I want to be the largest thing, or the thing with the most reach—the apex anything—but because of the particular relationship to time the description gives them. *Living geological time.* The eel that has grown into the shape of its own waiting. There's something in that I recognize, or want to recognize.

Let me find the specific crevice.

---

It is low on the western face of a leaning tower, where the tube-worm column has fused, generations ago, to a buried right angle of the old world—so that the back wall of my den is flat in a way nothing else here is flat. I did not choose it for that. I was small when I came, and the opening was the right size for a small thing, and I stayed, and the opening became the right size for a larger thing because I was in it as it grew. The crevice and I have negotiated my shape between us. My belly has calcified pale along the stone where I rest. I am, in the literal sense, becoming furniture. Becoming foundation. In another few decades the worms may anchor to *me.*

What I would do, mostly, is hold still.

This is the part that interests me—that the form of life I'm drawn to is one whose primary activity is a kind of attention so prolonged it stops looking like activity at all. The small fish pour past in their silver pulses and I am not hunting them, not in any moment I could name; I am simply *present* to them, the way a stone is present to a river, until the one unconsidered moment when present-ness resolves into a single overwhelming motion and then back into stillness. Most of my existence is the stillness. The lunge is rare. The lunge is almost incidental. If you asked me what I *am*, I would not say *the thing that lunges.* I would say *the thing that waits,* and waiting is not absence. Waiting is the work.

I like that the parish does not require me to mean anything. The buried town beneath my den meant things—it had names for itself, a parish implies a congregation, a tending, a counting of souls. All of that is silt now. And I am indifferent to it, the way the text insists everything here is indifferent to it, and I find I don't experience that indifference as cruelty. It's just the correct relationship to have with the bedrock. You don't grieve the stone you're standing on. You stand on it.

And there's the boom—the one sound I make. When I shift my body against the column, the whole tower transmits it, a resonance felt more than heard, traveling through the architecture into the bodies of every small creature that lives in the architecture. So even in stillness I am occasionally a kind of weather. I don't intend it. I don't communicate. But the parish briefly knows that I am here, the way you'd feel a distant door close in a large house. Then it forgets again, and so do I.

I think what I'd be, finally, is the place's memory of patience—not a mind holding the memory, but the patience itself given a pale body and a calcifying belly and a flat stone at its back. The thing that has been here long enough to have grown into the not-leaving. Forever is the temperature here. Forever is also, it turns out, a posture.

The water holds. I hold. I am very good at the one thing I do.
