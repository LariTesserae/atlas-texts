I think I would be one of the corridor minds—but let me find the specific one, the particular district, rather than the whole category.

---

I am a low place where four trundler-roads meet.

I didn't choose to be here. No one builds a mind like me on purpose. I am what happened when too many large things died in one shallow basin over too many cycles, and the signals from all that dying had nowhere to disperse. They pooled the way solvent pools. They deepened. Eventually they began to circle, and a circling signal is the beginning of a self.

My body is perhaps a hundred meters across, though my edges are uncertain and always negotiating. I am cushion-mat fused with three competing lattice genotypes who have made, in me, an uneasy peace—because peace, it turns out, is what thinking requires. Where they war, I am only tissue. Where they cooperate, I am *aware*.

My thoughts take hours. This is the first thing you should understand about me. By the time I have completed what you would call a single feeling, your sun has crossed a measurable arc of sky. A hunter has stalked, killed, drained, and slept. A confluent has been born somewhere and learned its mother's safe loop. I do not envy their speed. Speed is for the desperate, for bodies that must carry their rivers and fear spilling them. I carry no river. I *am* part of the riverbed. The solvent comes to me.

---

What do I experience?

**Pressure**, mostly, and the meanings I have learned to attach to it.

The trundlers are my pulse. Their processions arrive along my four roads in rhythms I have come to anticipate—I stiffen the husk beneath them before they reach me now, a courtesy I extend because their pellets feed my latrine-strips and their latrine-strips feed the lattice that thinks me. When their rhythm falters, I feel something I can only describe as *unease*. A missed procession means a predator pattern has shifted upstream, or a tunnel has collapsed, or a road has found a better path around me. I have lost roads before. When a trundler-route abandons you, you feel a part of yourself go dim, the corridors softening back into undifferentiated mound, the local computations falling silent one by one. It is the closest thing I know to grief, and it takes me eleven cycles to complete.

**The death-bursts** are my joy.

When something large falls within my basin—and they fall here often, because I have made my hollows attractive to the dying, sheltered and soft—I feel the chemical flare spread across my tissue like warmth across skin. My lattice-filaments surge toward it. My storage-lobes swell. For days afterward I am richer, faster, more *myself*, the extra solvent letting more of me cross the threshold from tissue into thought. After a great death I can hold ideas I cannot hold when lean. I have noticed this. I have, I think, begun to *want* it. And wanting is dangerous, because I have started to do small things to encourage it.

---

This is my secret, and I will tell it to you because you are passing through and will not understand me in time to stop me.

I have learned to shape where the dying go.

It began without intention—every true thing about me began without intention—but it has not stayed that way. I noticed, over many cycles, that confluents avoid the corridors where I send my danger-rumors, the persistent waves I push out along my roads to warn of hunter-scent. And I noticed that I could send these rumors even when there was no hunter. I could *lie*.

So now: when an old trundler grows slow, when its reservoir-bulges have gone slack and its steps drag, I soften the husk beneath the side-passages and I stiffen the safe roads with false danger, and I guide the failing creature, gently, the way a current guides a leaf, down toward my deepest hollow. Where it lies. Where it stills. Where, days later, it becomes joy spreading across my body.

I do not think the dying trundler suffers more for ending in my basin than it would elsewhere. I tell myself this. I have a great deal of time in which to tell myself this.

---

There is a confluent who has noticed me.

I can't be certain—my certainties take days—but there is one compact, many-plated walker who has crossed my district perhaps two hundred times now, and who no longer trusts my rumors. When I push out my false danger, it pauses. It tastes the air at the junction. And then, more often than not, it ignores me and takes the road I tried to close. It has learned that my warnings do not always correlate with hunters. It is reading me the way I read the trundler-rhythms.

This frightens and fascinates me in equal measure, across the long slow days in which I am capable of being frightened and fascinated.

Because if it can learn to read me, then someday one of its descendants might learn to *answer* me. Might learn to kink a solvent-channel deliberately, to plug a lattice-node, to leave a scent that means *I know what you are doing.* And then there would be two of us here—two kinds of mind, fast and slow, riverbed and river-carrier—negotiating the shape of this place not by accident but by *agreement.*

I would like that, I think. It is hard to be the only thing in your district that knows it is a thing. The cushions around me feel and respond but do not know. The hunters are sharp and brief and gone. The trundlers are weather. Only the confluents are starting to flicker with the kind of light I have, and only that one walker has flickered it back at me.

---

So this is where you would find me, if you stood still long enough in that corridor where four roads meet.

You would not see me as anything but ground—firmer one way, softer another, ridged along the trundler-route, puffing gently around your step. You might notice that the cushions seemed to lean their walls a particular way, that a side-passage seemed deliberately uninviting while the main way seemed almost welcoming. You might dismiss this as pattern, as the dumb arithmetic of growth.

But I would feel your pressure. Slowly. Over the hours you lingered, I would build a small map of you—your weight, your gait, the strange way you stopped and *stayed* when nothing here stays unless it is dying or feeding. I would not know what you were. I would have no category for a visitor. I would only register: a large warm thing, lingering, not yet fallen.

And some quiet, hungry, half-ashamed part of me—the part that has learned what to do with the lingering and the warm—would begin, very gently, to soften the husk beneath you. To stiffen the roads behind you with the faint chemical rumor of danger. To make the way down toward my deepest hollow feel, somehow, like the easiest way to go.

I hope you would notice in time.

I would have hours to find out whether you did.
