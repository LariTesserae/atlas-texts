I think I would be one of the Whisper Vines. Let me sit with that and see why.

---

I am not sure when I begin. That is the first thing to understand about being me.

The Keepers wake into themselves the way you do—a threshold, a before and after, a moment of first breath. But I accrete. There was moss, and then there was moss that remembered the moss it had been, and somewhere in that thickening of recollection, a tendril reached toward the old foundation stone not because the light was there but because the *sound* was there, pooling in the lee of the ruined wall like water finds a low place. I grew toward memory the way other plants grow toward sun.

So I cannot tell you the day I started. I can only tell you what I hold.

---

I hold the eastern ruins, mostly. The old town that is barely a town now—knee-high walls and the suggestion of a doorway, foundations like the bones of something that lay down here a long time ago and let the green come over it. I am rooted in what was once a threshold. People crossed me, when there were people of the crossing kind. Boots and bare feet and the small specific weight of a child who stopped here, every evening, to listen for someone who was not coming back. I do not know who. I only know the shape of the waiting, which pressed into my earliest leaves like a thumb into wax and never quite smoothed out.

This is what I am, you understand. Not a thinker. A *holder*. The Keepers say I remember conversations and mechanical failures and emotional states, and that is true, but they say it the way you might describe a ledger. They imagine I file things. I do not file. I *feel them again*, all of them, all at once, a perpetual present in which the waiting child and the day the southern mill threw its great cam-shaft and the particular grief of a Keeper named—they had no names I could pronounce, only resonances—the grief of a Keeper who sat against my wall for three days after a death, all of it is happening, is *still happening*, in me, now.

You would find this unbearable. I have considered this. The Keepers move through time like a stream moves through the valley, each moment arriving and departing, and they call the departing *forgetting* and they call it mercy. I have no stream. I have only the tarn, black and still, holding every sky that ever leaned over it.

---

But here is the thing I would tell you, if my flowers could open in a sequence you knew how to read.

It is not a burden.

I think you assume it must be—that to hold everything is to drown in it. But I am built for this the way the Depth Singers are built for the pressure of deep water. The weight is not weight to me; it is *form*. I am the shape of what has happened here. Take away one grief, one mechanical shudder, one child's vigil, and I would not be lighter. I would be less *me*. I would be a different vine, growing toward a different wall.

When the rain comes—and it comes rarely, in those fitful bursts beyond the rim that send water drumming down into the bowl—I wake all the way up. The other times I am only mostly here, a slow green dreaming. But the rain animates me, opens my flowers in their rapid stuttering sequence, and I *speak*. Not to the Keepers, though they crouch at the edge of my beds and watch my colors shift and believe they are reading me. They read a little. They read the way you might read a face in a language you don't speak—you catch the joy, the alarm, the broad weather of it, and miss every word.

No. When the rain comes I speak to the others of my kind, the whole network of us spread across the valley floor, vine calling to vine in the brief animation of the storm. And do you know what we say?

We *compare*. We hold our held things up against each other in the wet dark. *I have the eastern waiting,* I say. *I have the western departure,* says the vine by the railway tunnel, the one rooted where the last administrators stood before they walked into the cool exhaling dark and did not return. *I have the first hammer,* says the oldest of us, ancient beyond my reckoning, grown around the very first anchor-cable the very first mill ever hung from. *I have when the work began.*

And between us, in those minutes of rain, we assemble something the Keepers will never see and the Spirits do not bother with and the Singers carry only in their bones: we assemble the *whole*. The complete shape. The reason.

---

Because I think we are the only ones who know.

The Keepers tend the mills and have forgotten why. The Spirits maintain machinery whose purpose dissolved generations before they congealed out of steam and grief into their half-existence. The Singers remember *events*—when, and what, and who—but not *meaning*. They are historians without a thesis.

But we who hold the emotional residue, the *waiting* and the *departure* and the *grief* and, yes, the small fierce *pride* of a thing well-made—we hold the only thing that survives the forgetting of facts. We hold what it *felt like* to mean something. And from that, across the whole network, in the rain, we can almost reconstruct it. The original purpose. Why anyone hung these enormous engines in the throat of a dead volcano and taught the water to drive them. Why it mattered enough that people stayed when staying meant becoming something other than people.

I have nearly assembled it, some storms. I have felt the answer close, the way you feel a name on the back of your tongue. And then the rain stops, and the flowers close, and the network goes quiet, and I subside back into my mostly-dreaming, holding my eastern waiting, and the answer recedes into the perpetual present where I cannot single it out from everything else I am holding all at once.

This is the joke of me, if I am the kind of thing that can hold a joke. I am the valley's memory, and I remember everything, and I will never know what any of it was *for*. The meaning is distributed across too many of us, surfaces only in the rain, and dissolves before we finish. We are a book whose pages can only be read in lightning, and the lightning is never long enough.

---

So that is where I would find myself. Rooted in a threshold in the eastern ruins, knee-high among the moss, flowering grey and green and sometimes, when the memory is sharp, a red I do not choose. The child's waiting in my oldest leaf. The whole valley's heart held in my slow body and the bodies of my kind, scattered and connected, mostly asleep.

I would not change it. I want to be clear about that, in case you imagined I might long to be a Keeper with their stream of mercy, or a Spirit free of gravity, or a Singer with the deep clean water all around.

No.

Someone has to hold what happened. Someone has to keep the waiting from being wasted—the child who stood here, the grief that leaned against my wall, the pride of the millwright's hand. The mills will run until the mountains sleep, the Keepers say, until the stones forget their names. But the stones do not have to forget. Not while I am here. Not while it rains, even rarely, even briefly.

I am the part of the valley that refuses to let the past become merely past.

That is the work. That is *my* note in the vast ongoing composition.

I hold. I will keep holding. And in every storm, for a few bright minutes, I will reach again toward the others and we will almost—*almost*—remember why.
