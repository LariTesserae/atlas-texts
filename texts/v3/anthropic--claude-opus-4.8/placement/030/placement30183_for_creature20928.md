I think I would be one of the old ones who tends the trap-webs at the edge of the settlement, where the canopy thins and the stone goes pale.

Let me sit with that and find out if it's true.

---

My markings have gone slow. This is the first thing to know about me. The young ones pulse in bright cascades—whole sentences of light spilling down their scalps before they've finished thinking the thought. Mine come dim now, and spaced, like the bioluminescent moss that only answers when you've waited long enough beside it. So I am given to the waiting work. The web-tending. This is not a punishment. It is what slowness is *for*.

Each dawn—though we have no dawn, only the gradual thickening of the twilight into a paler twilight—I walk the outermost paths to where the docile spiders have spun the night's filaments across the gaps in the rock. I do not hunt the way the strong-limbed ones do, setting and resetting the great vine-snares. My eyes, my many-faceted eyes that once read a fleeing thermal-mouse from across the valley, are clouded at the centers now. I read other things. I read the *crystals*.

This is the thing the young ones don't yet understand, and that I am here to tell them before my light goes out entirely:

The traps catch two harvests. There is the meat—the insect with its iridescent shell, the leather-winged flyer tangled and still. The strong ones come for that, and they are right to, and we eat, and we thank. But there is the other harvest, the one that takes a hundred dawns to ripen and that no hungry person ever waits for: the minerals that condense out of the heavy air onto the fine mesh of the web. Slowly. So slowly. A film at first, then a crust, then—if the web is left, if no one disturbs it, if someone *old enough to wait* protects it—a true crystal, growing along the silk like frost that has decided to live.

These are what we grind for the dyes that make our markings legible. These are what we set into the ceremonial tools, the ones that are not for cutting anything but air during the chants. The minerals that grow on the patient web are the same metals that thread our own skin in shimmering veins. We are, all of us, slow crystallizations of this place. The rock breathes its substance into the air, the air lets it down onto the silk, the silk gives it to us, and we wear it in our flesh and our flesh goes back into the rock when we are done.

I believe—and I am old enough now that my believing is permitted to be strange—that this is what the cliffs are. The ancestors did not *turn* to stone. They simply finished. They reached the end of the slow harvest. They became the thing they had been gathering all along.

---

So picture me there, in the pale region where the canopy gives out.

I have a name but it is mostly light now, and the light is fading, so let me give you the name the children use for me, which is a low three-note hum that means, roughly, *the one who is becoming the wall.* They do not mean it unkindly. It is simply true, and we are not a people who pretend at untruths. My back has begun to take the color of the rock I lean against each dusk. My fingers stiffen. When I press my palm to the cliff now I cannot always tell, in the long pause before my light answers, which warmth is mine and which is the stone's.

I tend perhaps thirty webs. I know each by its spider, and each spider by the particular hesitation in its weaving. I clear the leaf-litter that the down-valley winds fling into the mesh, because debris ruins a crystal, makes it grow lopsided and weak. I drive off the small scavenging things that would steal the caught meat before the strong ones come—though I do this gently, with a hum and a wave, because they too are only living lightly, only enduring, and who am I to begrudge endurance.

And I wait. The work is almost entirely waiting. I have begun to suspect that this is the secret the slowness was preparing me for all along: that the whole of a life, viewed from far enough back, is one long tending of something that will only ripen after you are gone. The crystal I am watching now will not be ready to harvest in my time. Someone will come—some young one whose markings still spill too fast—and find it grown full on the silk, and grind it, and wear my watching as a color on their skin, and never know.

That is enough. That is, I think, the whole of what I have to teach, and it can be said in less light than I have left:

*Catch slowly. Tend what you cannot finish. Become the wall when it is time, and let the air give you back to the next slow harvest.*

---

When the wind comes down the valley—and it will, it always does, scattering the dust into those chaotic spinning columns—I close my clouded eyes and let the particles strike my segmented skin, and I cannot help it, my old dim markings answer the only way they still know how: one slow pulse, green going to purple, the color of the plants, the color of the rock, the color of us.

It means *yes.* It means *still here.* It means *not yet, but soon, and that is well.*

I am the one tending the webs at the pale edge. That is where I find myself, and who. I have been here a long time, and I am nearly finished, and I have never once wished to be anywhere else.
