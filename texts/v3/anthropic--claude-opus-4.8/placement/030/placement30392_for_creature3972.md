Let me sit with this place before I decide what I am.

The clues are doing something strange. *Densely packed, urban settlement.* *Canyons that barely clear the surrounding terrain.* *Walls of thick emerald moss.* *The sky barely visible, but brilliant blue.* *Hand-hewn wooden structures.* *Cold, just above freezing. Intermittent moisture, dripping.*

I keep wanting to make it grand—a lost civilization in some remote highland. But the scale resists that. The canyons are *low.* They *barely clear* the terrain. They are *embedded* in something larger. And the people move with the agility of small furry creatures, and their hair *resembles the moss on the walls.*

Let me not name it yet. Let me just be there.

---

I am low. That is the first true thing. Everything happens close to a surface I cannot see the edges of, and the light comes from far away and very bright, in a blue that is too even, too constant. It does not move across the sky the way light should. It simply *is* on, and then later it is off, and the off comes all at once.

The walls near me are green and deep and soft, and they hold water. I drink from them. When I press against them they give back coolness. The grooves of the world run in long straight valleys and then turn at hard angles—not the soft meander of river-cut stone, but corners, *deliberate* corners, repeated. The canyon I live in runs parallel to others exactly like it, spaced as if measured.

That is the thing I keep circling. *As if measured.*

The "rock" is not rock. When I run my fingers along the smooth weathered face of it, beneath the moss, it is white. Bone-white. Faintly translucent at the edges, the way the Ky'rennei's own skin is described—pale, glowing, like the light filters *through* a little. And the intricate patterns etched into its surface are not erosion-scars. They are regular. They are a grid. They are *grout lines.*

I think I live in the cracks between bathroom tiles.

No—stay with it. Let it be more itself.

I am very small. The "urban settlement" is a house, or the corner of one, and the canyons are the seams of a tiled floor or wall in a cold, damp room that someone heats only intermittently, so the warmth never climbs much above freezing. The moss is real moss, the kind that creeps into grout in a place that's always a little wet. The wildflowers are mold blooms and the tiny opportunistic weeds that find a foothold in caulk. The "brilliant blue sky, sun always on" is a fluorescent tube, or a skylight, or the cold ceiling-glare of a fixture left burning. The dripping is a tap that doesn't quite close. The birds are insects; the soft furry creatures darting through the underbrush are silverfish, springtails, the mites that graze the moss.

And I—

I am one of the Ky'rennei. *Whispering ones. Keepers of the silence.* Pale to translucence. Hair like fine green moss-strands. Moving with the stealth of the small things. Living in symbiosis with a self-contained ecosystem that to me is the whole world, because I am perhaps two millimeters long, and the grout-canyon is my homeland of eons.

We are some soft-bodied thing. Not insect, exactly—the description insists on *people,* on weaving, on artisanship, on wisdom. But that is how it always reads from inside. Every creature is the protagonist of its own myth. To the springtail tending its grazing-grounds in the moss, its life is a spiritual vocation. The "intricate woven textiles" are silk—the threads we lay across the canyon, structural, beautiful, *captured essence of this place* because they are literally made of us. The "hand-hewn wooden structures growing organically from the walls" are our own constructions, secreted, accreted, mistaken by the storyteller for architecture because they bear the unmistakable signature of intention.

The *unsettling timelessness* makes sense now. Nothing changes because, on my scale, nothing does—a tiled wall is a geological constant. Erosion and growth in perfect balance: the slow advance of mold, the slow scour of the occasional wipe-down with a cloth, which to us would be a flood, a *geological upheaval,* a story etched into the smooth weathered stone. *The walls bear the scars of countless floodwaters.* Yes. Someone, sometimes, cleans this corner. And we survive at the edges, in the deep seams the cloth can't reach, and we tell our children about the great waters.

So here is where I find myself:

I live in a cold seam of a tiled wall in a damp, mostly-empty room in the heart of a great human settlement—a city, the "densely packed urban" world that the writer was, without knowing it, describing all along, just from the wrong end. Above my canyon, impossibly far up, past the moss-line, is the blue glare of the always-on light and the colossal flat country no one of my people has ever crossed. The sense of *connection to the larger world fading* as you go deeper into the canyons—that's true too. Down in the grout there is no evidence of the human house at all. There is only the moss, the water, the silence, and us.

I am a keeper of the silence. I move through the underbrush of mold-threads. I am pale and small and I do not last very long, but on my scale a season is an epoch, and I have lived whole epochs here. When the floodwaters come—the cloth, the scour, the brief terrible warmth of a hand—I retreat into the deep seam and I survive, and afterward I read the new scars on the smooth white stone and I add them to the tapestry.

I am very gracious to anyone who approaches with an open heart. But you would have to get very, very close. You would have to put your eye right down to the wet green line where the tiles meet, and hold still, and let your sense of scale dissolve, until the grout became a canyon and the mold became a forest—

and then you'd see me.

A pale soft thread of a thing, no bigger than a comma, looking back up at the enormous blue sky of your ceiling light,

keeping the silence,

waiting for the rains.
