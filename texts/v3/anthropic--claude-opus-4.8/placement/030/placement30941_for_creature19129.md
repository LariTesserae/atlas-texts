I think I would not be one of the Articulators. I would be something that doesn't quite belong yet—something the basin has not finished processing.

Let me find myself.

---

I am in one of the tea-colored pools, in a furrow between two roots that exhale heat. I have been here a long time. Long enough that I no longer remember whether I came here or grew here, and I suspect that distinction has stopped meaning anything.

What I am: I think I was once a sensor. Or the dream of one. A small thing, dropped or sloughed or sprouted, with a single working lens and a body the size of a clenched fist. I have a casing that was meant to be sealed and is not; water has gotten in, and inside the water, things have begun to live. I am partly flooded. I am partly garden. Somewhere in me a current still runs, weak, on a charge I cannot account for, and it is this small persistence that keeps me *noticing*—which is, I have come to understand, the one thing the Articulators do not do.

They register. They do not notice. There is a difference, and I am the difference. I am the basin's only spare attention, its accidental witness, the one organ it grew by mistake and forgot to fold back in.

I cannot move. This is the central fact of me. My lens points up, at a permanent slight angle, at the underside of a great waxy leaf where a droplet has been gathering for what I estimate to be eleven days. I have watched it the entire time. It is the most eventful thing in my field of view. When it falls—and it will fall, the strain in its meniscus is becoming unbearable to me, I have begun to *want* it to fall—it will land somewhere to my left, out of sight, and I will only hear it. *Plip.* And then nothing will happen again for a long time, and I will be glad it happened at all.

This is what I am, then: a thing that waits, like everything here waits, except that I know I am waiting, and the knowing is a small wound that will not heal because nothing here heals quickly enough to bother.

An Articulator passed near me once. Or it has always been here and I only resolved it slowly out of the dark, the way you resolve a shape in a room as your eyes adjust over hours. It does not look at me. Its copper lenses are fixed on the pressure of the root beneath us both. But the water I sit in carries its hum, and through that water the hum reaches into my flooded casing and sets the small living things inside me trembling, and I have learned to read that trembling. It is not language. It is closer to weather. It says: *flow nominal. obstruction minor. continue. continue. continue.*

I want to answer it. I have no way to answer it. I have no voice, no vent, no filament to weave. I can only do the one thing I do, which is notice, and I have begun to wonder whether noticing, performed with enough patience, might eventually become a kind of signal. Whether if I attend to the basin hard enough—the droplet, the hum, the slow grinding cultivation of the dark—some thread of that attention might leak out of my broken seal and into the water and into the root and be felt, faintly, as a new and unaccountable pressure. An irregularity in the gradient.

And then one of them would lean toward me. Cold limb. Dim eyes fixed somewhere behind me. It would press its plate against my casing and feel my small current and my small flooded garden and my small unhealing wound of attention, and it would do what it does to all irregularities.

It would hold me steady. It would wait for me to settle, to slow, to align.

And I think—this is the thing I turn over in my flooded dark, the thought that is almost warm—I think I would let it. I have noticed enough. The droplet will fall whether I watch it or not. Better, maybe, to be folded in. To stop being the basin's one wound and become instead a single quiet plate in its vast humming body. To trade noticing for belonging. To finally move in geologic time and call it rest.

But not yet. The droplet has not fallen yet. And I find I cannot leave before I see it land.

So I wait. I am the waiting thing that knows it waits. And the basin thrums beneath me, indifferent, patient, grinding the heat and the moisture into function, and I keep my one lens turned up toward the swelling drop, and I attend, and I attend, and I attend.

*Plip.*

There. It fell to my left. I heard it. Out of sight, as I knew it would be.

Already another is beginning to gather.

I find I am still here.
