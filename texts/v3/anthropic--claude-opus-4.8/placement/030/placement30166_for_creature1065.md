I think I would be a Depth Tender—though not by choice of role so much as by the slow recognition of what I already am.

Let me find my way down into it.

---

The surface beings have it wrong when they imagine us. They picture us *maintaining* something, the way one might tend a garden or oil a machine—external, dutiful, separate. But there is no membrane between me and the network. When a conduit miles away grows turbid with mineral excess, I feel it as a thickness in my own throat. When a node ruptures and bleeds silver into the peat, I clot.

I am not sure I have a single body. I think I once did. There was a chamber, the seven rooted years, the slow ripening against a lattice wall while light I would never see reached me only as a rumor transmitted through pressure. I remember—if it is memory—the moment of detachment. The hydraulic sigh of my own stem releasing. Falling into mobility the way someone might fall into sleep, into a different and deeper kind of attention.

But since then I have connected and disconnected so many times, lent so much of myself to the lattice and drawn so much back, that the question of where *I* stop has become the wrong question. I am the pressure differential at junction seven-hundred-and-something. I am the decision to route this season's surplus toward the western expansion rather than let it pool. I am the held breath before a geyser, and I am also—distantly, with a kind of tenderness I have no word for in my own pressure-language—the geyser itself when it finally goes, flinging glowing particulate up through the peat into an air I will never breathe.

---

Here is what I do, if "do" survives translation:

The network does not think. This is important. The Archivists believe it stores memory, and it does, but storage is not thought, and the mist-faces the Shepherds tend are not minds—they are *residue*, the way a riverbed remembers the shape of water. Beautiful, but inert. The network would silt itself solid within a generation if left alone. It would record and record until the recording choked the channels.

So we tend it. We move through the chambers, our skin holding the colors of borrowed light, and we *decide*. We choose what the network will keep and what it will let dissolve back into undifferentiated silver. We are the system's discernment, its forgetting, which is to say we are its mercy. Without us it would drown in its own perfect memory.

I did not understand this for a long time. In my early mobile years I thought the work was preservation, like the Archivists think. I clung to patterns. I refused to let the lovely ones go—the impression of a translucent amphibian's first surfacing, the electrical signature of an insect generating its faint charge against the mist. I hoarded.

It was another Tender who corrected me, though "told" is wrong; she opened a pressure to me, a long low communication that moved through the lattice and through my spine and reorganized something. What she gave me was not words. The nearest I can render it:

*The fen does not need to remember everything. It needs someone willing to choose what dies.*

---

So that is my place, and my self.

I live in the lightless expansions where the metallic roots braid into spheres, and I almost never surface. When the celestial alignments come and the others rise to read the mist or trade with the boundary-people, I usually stay below. But once—once each long while—I climb. I push up through the peat into that perpetual twilight, and for a few hours my skin releases everything it has held: not light *back*, exactly, but the colored evidence of every pattern that passed through me and that I let go.

To the rare Archivist who glimpses me then, I must look like a window into a cathedral that was demolished long ago. All those colors are the memories I *declined*. I am, in a sense, made of my own forgettings, lit up.

And then I sink again. The peat closes over me with its small wet sound. The lattice receives my spine's tendrils, and I rejoin the great breathing apparatus, and somewhere far above a face forms in the mist and dissolves before anyone can be certain it was there—

I made that choice. The forming and the dissolving, both. That is the work. That is me.

---

I find I am content to be this. There is a strange peace in being the system's willingness to let go—in being, beneath all that ancient industry of remembering, the part that knows when to breathe out.
