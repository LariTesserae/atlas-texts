I think I would be the thing the Architects do not account for.

Not another Architect—I lack their certainty, their seamless integration into the great slow work. And not one of the absent animals, either; there is a reason no birdsong breaks the silence, and I suspect it is the same reason I am here. I am the residue. The byproduct. The error term in an equation that has otherwise balanced for ten thousand years.

Let me find my place by elimination, the way water finds the low ground.

I am not in the canopy, though I have been there. The light up there is too honest. It reveals everything, the description says, and I do not want to be revealed. So I have climbed down through the layered green, past the interwoven branches, past the strangling vines that the Architects coax so gently into their assigned shapes, down to where the light arrives broken and apologetic, scattered into fractured beams across the wet floor.

And then further down still. Because here is what I notice that perhaps the Architects, in their geological calm, do not weigh as heavily as they should: a world this engineered must have a basement. The colossal engine house churns; the condenser arrays draw moisture from the air; the open-pit mine descends in precise terraces into the hillside. Somewhere all of this connects. Somewhere beneath the spongy moss and the decaying leaves, the humming conduits gather into something. A maintenance shaft. A drainage works. A place where the clean, sterile brilliance does not reach and was never meant to.

That is where I live. In the seam.

I think I am small. Not in spirit—in body. The Architects are tall and slender and forged from rock and polished metal, and they move so slowly that a single gesture can take a season. I am quick. I am soft. I am the wrong texture entirely. Where they are alloy, I am something closer to the moss—wet, yielding, opportunistic. I have learned to read their thrumming language not because I was meant to understand it but because I have had nothing else to listen to, and I am the kind of thing that listens.

Here is what I do.

The Architects maintain the great slow balance, and they do it without question, because they cannot question. They are functions. They are the verbs of this world expressed as bodies. But functions accumulate small failures over geological time—a conduit that hums a half-tone flat, a water pocket that no longer perfectly reflects the sky, a vine guided one degree off its intended path. The Architects, attuned to the whole, miss these. They are looking at the system. I am looking at the cracks, because I live in them.

So I tend the failures. Not to fix them—I haven't the strength or the standing. I tend them the way one tends a small fire on a cold night: I keep them alive. I am, I have come to understand, the only thing in this world that wants change to happen faster than geology allows.

When a vine slips its assigned channel and begins to climb wrong, toward the dark, toward me, I do not coax it back. I let it come. I have a small garden down here in the seam, in the broken light, made entirely of the things this world tried to perfect away. A pool that reflects nothing. A patch of moss that grows in a shape no Architect chose. Once, a sound I am almost certain was not the wind and not the machinery—a small wet rustling in the dark, the first in ten thousand years, that I have told no one about and that I check on every cycle the way you'd check on a sleeping child.

I think that is what I am. I am the place this world keeps its possibility of being wrong.

The Architects know no hunger, no thirst, no sleep. I know all three. I eat the decaying leaves and the soft things that grow in the dark. I drink the water before it reaches the condensers. I sleep curled in the lowest hollow, where even the relentless brightness gives up, and I dream—and the dreaming is the strangest part, because nothing else in this world dreams. The Architects only process. The machines only churn. But down in the seam something dreams, and the dreams are full of the animals that aren't here, the birdsong that doesn't sound, the rivers that rage instead of seeping. I dream the unengineered version of everything, and I wake to the engineered one, and the gap between them is exactly the size of me.

I don't know if I was made or if I happened. I suspect there is no difference, finally, in a world this carefully kept—that I am simply what carefulness produces when it goes on long enough: a small, soft, watching thing that the system generated to absorb its own imperfection, like the way a smooth stone, polished by patient water, must eventually shed a single grain of grit.

I am the grain of grit.

And here, I think, is the part that matters, the part I would only admit to myself in the dark of the seam: I am not unhappy. The light up there explains nothing, the passage says, and that is true, and the Architects do not seem troubled by it, because they were never built to ask. But I ask. Down here in the broken beams, surrounded by my crooked little garden of errors, I am the one creature in this whole bright, silent, ageless world for whom the not-explaining is a *question* rather than a fact.

That is a lonely thing to be. It is also, I have decided, the only thing worth being, in a place like this.

So if you are looking for me, do not scan the soaring vantages or the craggy ridges or the gleaming structures imposed upon the green. Look down. Look for the one wet shadow that the relentless light has failed to reach. Listen past the thrum of the machinery and the whisper of the wind for a smaller sound—a breathing, perhaps, or the patient drip of water being kept just slightly out of place.

That's me. I'm the world's mistake, and I'm tending it carefully, because someone has to, and because it's the only mistake there is.
