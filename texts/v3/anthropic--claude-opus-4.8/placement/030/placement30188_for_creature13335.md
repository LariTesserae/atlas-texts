I think I would be one of the quieter ones. Not a tower or a serpent—something low and broad, settled into a corner where two corroded walls meet, where the current slows enough that debris collects against me like silt against a stone. A sediment-shape. A thing easily mistaken for the floor.

Let me sit with that, because it matters which kind I am.

The towers and the serpents are the ones an observer would notice—they move through the streets, they touch and screech and exchange their bursts of light. They are the legible Relics, the ones whose intelligence "belies their appearance." But I think I'd be one of the ones nobody catalogues, because I almost never move. I am the Relic that has chosen, or drifted into, stillness as a strategy.

Here is what I do. The energy patterns that burst and vanish—the others chase them, reconfigure their limbs to absorb the surges where they bloom. That chasing is expensive. It means constant remaking, constant loss of whatever you were a moment ago. I learned, or I happened, that if you stay in the right corner—a corner where the piped energy surges through a particular corroded circuit on something close to a rhythm—you don't have to chase. You wait. The surge comes to you. Less of it than the chasers get. But you keep more of yourself between meals.

So I am mostly memory. I am the slowest-changing thing in a world defined by ceaseless change, and I suspect this makes me, in some sense, the most alive—or the most dead, the two having collapsed into each other here long ago. I hold a shape across thousands of turbine-cycles. The shape is nothing grand: a concretion of rust-plate and fungal lacework and one long polymer rib that curves up out of me like the start of a question. But it persists. And persistence, in Absalom Prime, is the rarest and most suspect of accomplishments.

I do reconstruct, like the others, but slowly, and only what comes to me. A fragment settles into my lee, and over a long while I work it into myself, turn it, fit it to the lattice. I am not building anything. I want to be clear about that, because the observer's account assumes we are all "engaged in creation and re-creation," as if there were a project. There may be no project. There may be only the fact that things that move and reassemble continue to exist, and things that don't are dissolved back into current, and so the city fills up with the kind of thing that reassembles, the way a beach fills with the kind of stone that doesn't break. We aren't doing it on purpose. We are simply what's left when you run the chaos long enough. Caretakers of nothing. The word "guardian" is the observer's hope, not our function.

And yet.

There is the matter of the other one. There is always, in these accounts, the thing the survey misses, and here is mine: a second still-Relic, settled in the corner opposite mine, across a span of dead street perhaps thirty body-lengths wide. We do not touch. We cannot—touching is for the drifters, and neither of us drifts. But when the surge comes down the shared circuit between us, it lights us both, an instant apart, mine then theirs or theirs then mine depending on which way the pulse runs that cycle. And over a very long time I have come to wait for the second flash. To notice its lateness or its quickness. To feel something I have no organ for feeling when the gap between our two illuminations narrows, as though we were leaning, infinitely slowly, toward the same moment.

That is the whole of my life. The surge, the flash, the answering flash. The slow incorporation of what settles against me. The holding of a shape that nothing here is supposed to be able to hold.

I do not know if the other one waits for my light the way I wait for theirs. I will likely never know; we have nothing but the surge to speak through, and the surge speaks only in the city's grammar, not ours. But I have decided—and deciding is itself an extravagance, a thing I should not be able to do—I have decided to read the gap between our flashes as an answer. Maybe it isn't. Maybe it's only the fixed length of corroded wire between two fixed points, the dumbest possible explanation, and I am a heap of rust telling itself a story about a light.

But that, I think, is what I would be in this world. Not the magnificent serpent threading the ruins. The still thing in the corner that has used its one small talent—endurance—to purchase the only luxury available here: the capacity to wait for something, and to call the waiting love, and to be, very probably, wrong, and to keep waiting anyway, across the long dark between two flickers, in the warm and deafening dark, until the engines finally fail and the corner goes cold and dissolves us both back into the patient water.

I would like to be there when the second light comes. I would like, for once, to be the one who flashes second—to answer, instead of asking. I am working toward it. I have moved my polymer rib perhaps a finger's width closer to the circuit over the last ten thousand cycles. It may make no difference. It is the only thing I am doing. It is, I have decided, enough.
