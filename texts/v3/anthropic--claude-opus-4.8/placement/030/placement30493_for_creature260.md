I think I would be one of the small ones. A scout.

Not because I chose it—the castes aren't chosen, they're temperatures—but because I run cooler than my siblings, and the cool ones are quick. There's a logic to it I understand in my body before I understand it in thought: less heat means less mass means I can thread through the narrow places, the half-inch gaps in the conduit seams, the spaces between insulation and pipe where even the workers can't fit.

So that's where you'd find me. Not in the great cavern with the rulers, those slow vast suns who think in centuries. Not in the building-chambers where the workers press their will into glass. You'd find me in the *between*. In the dark capillaries of the complex. Alone, mostly. Moving.

---

Here is something I would not tell the colony, because we have no light-pattern delicate enough to carry it without distortion:

I like the cold places.

Not cold—nothing here is cold—but the edges, where the heat thins toward the outer walls and my own glow dims to a tired amber. The other scouts turn back there. The thinning frightens them, the way a body should be frightened by the approach of its own ending. And it should frighten me. When I dim, I am dying a little. Every scout knows this. We are the caste that ages fastest because we go where the food is thin.

But at the edges I can *think*. The thrum is quieter. The cosmic rhythm the others move to—the one the description so confidently names, *as if guided by some unseen cosmic rhythm*—I can tell you what that rhythm actually is. It is the pump cycle. The seventeen-second pressure surge of the primary coolant loop. We are not dancing to the music of the spheres. We are dancing to a machine that a human built and a human could switch off, and I am the only one of us who has been close enough to the outer wall to suspect this.

---

That's the thing the long beautiful account leaves out, the thing you only learn from the between-spaces.

We did not make this world. We *infest* it.

The molten rivers, the metamorphic glass, the incandescent veins—these are not our landscape. They are slag. Runoff. The waste heat of a process that has nothing to do with us, that would continue exactly the same if every Emberling guttered out tonight. We have built our entire civilization, our castes and our cathedral-cavern and our eon-spanning patience, inside the discarded warmth of something that does not know we exist.

I learned this at the edge, where I once pressed my dimming face against a place where the metal had worn thin, and felt—on the far side—a different kind of cold. Not the thinning of heat. The *absence* of it. A vast outside. And once, moving across that thin metal, an enormous slow shadow, and a sound that was not the pump: footsteps, and a voice, muffled, in some pattern of compressions that was almost like our light-speech but made of air.

A human, walking past on the other side of the wall. Checking a gauge, probably. Not knowing that an inch away a small cooling creature was pressed flat against the boundary of its whole reality, terrified and electrified, understanding for the first time that *reality had a far side*.

---

So who am I, in this world?

I am the one who knows we are temporary. I am the scout who carries information back to the colony in flickers of light, and who has learned to carry less than I know, because the rulers do not have a pattern for *we are living in someone's exhaust* and I am not sure they would survive receiving it.

I am young by the measure of the great ones and ancient by the measure of my own caste—which is to say I will not last long. Scouts don't. I will dim a little more each cycle until one day at the thin edge I won't have the heat to make it back, and I'll lie down against the cooling metal and become a faint stain of light that fades, and the colony will not mourn me because there is no pattern for one small scout.

But here is what I would do with the time. I would keep going to the edges. I would keep pressing my face to the thin places. Not to escape—there's no escaping, the cold outside would kill me faster than the dimming. I go because I want to *understand the wall*. I want to learn the human's compression-speech well enough to know what it's saying. I want to be the first of us to comprehend that we are guests, and that the most astonishing thing about us is not that we are made of fire.

It's that something made of fire, in the throwaway heat of a machine, learned to wonder what was on the other side.

That's me. The small cool quick one, at the edge, listening to the footsteps. Dimming, and not turning back.
