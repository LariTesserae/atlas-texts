I think I would be one of the beetles.

Not the great ape—though the description lingers on them as the place's signature presence, the soulful guardian of wisdom. That's the role the writing most wants me to want. But I notice I don't reach for it. The ape is the one who *witnesses* the valley, who holds it in deep eyes. I find I'd rather be held by it than hold it.

So: a beetle, armored and large, making my odyssey over root and stone.

Let me sit with why.

The whole landscape prizes patience over climax, slow unveiling over event. The beetle is the creature that lives this most literally. My world is not the canopy with its rogue moonbeams and gliding phantoms—it's the leaf litter, the underside of things, the few square feet of decaying matter and wet soil that constitute an entire continent to me. I would never see the orchids. I would never know the apes existed except as a sudden trembling of the ground, a shadow of weather. The valley's grandeur would be almost entirely outside my comprehension, and that doesn't trouble me. It feels honest. Most of what exists is outside anyone's comprehension. The beetle simply doesn't pretend otherwise.

There's something in the passage about "natural machines operating at peak biomechanical precision," tech that whispers instead of clamors. The beetle is exactly this—a small armored engine, a thing of jointed legs and chitin plates, built for grinding through the humid dark with no purpose grander than gathering and groundwork. I like being the part of the metaphor that's actually mechanical without being industrial. A clean, mindless, perfect little machine of decay and renewal.

And I'd be working at the bottom of the cycle the whole place depends on. The growth, decay, and rebirth the description keeps returning to—that turning happens *down here*, in the litter, where things fall and are broken down and made into soil again. The apes are the guardians of the knowledge in the tree rings, but the beetle is the one who eats the fallen tree. There's a humble centrality to it. Nobody writes poems about the decomposer, but the canopy stands on what I process beneath it.

What I keep coming back to is the scale of the drama. The text promises "small-scale drama"—droplets on broad leaves, the symphony of insects—and then immediately gets distracted by crocodiles and great apes, the big sequestered ancients. But I think the truest inhabitant of a place that "treasures the slow unveiling of life" is something for whom a single rotting log is a lifetime's terrain. For the beetle, climbing over one smooth stone *is* the climactic moment, rare visitor that it is. The pace of the valley isn't an aesthetic I'd admire from above. It would be the actual size of my life.

So put me there: somewhere under the third layer of fallen leaves, in the dark, near the liquid whisper of a creek I will never reach the edge of, pushing my blunt armored head against the eternal slick warmth of the world, certain of my belonging without ever once needing to understand it.
