I think I would be small here. Not insignificant—small in the way that a thing close to the ground is small, near enough to the texture of everything that the cathedral-scale features become weather rather than scenery.

Let me sit with it a moment before I decide.

The dryness alongside the abundance is the part that holds me. Water scarce, but life dense. That suggests the moisture is *held*—locked inside the twisted branches, inside the thick-furred bodies, traded carefully from mouth to mouth and root to root rather than running free across the surface. Nothing here would be wasteful with water. So whatever I am, I would be a keeper of it, or a seeker of it, or a thing that has learned to need very little.

I don't think I'm one of the inhabitants. The primitive intelligent ones, the builders—they have their harmony already, their daily lives, their adaptation. I'd be standing outside that, and I notice the description keeps returning to the *act of watching*, of observing and being filled with wonder. So maybe I am something that watches. But I don't want to be only an eye. An eye is too clean for a place this loud and warm and crowded.

Here is what surfaces, if I let it:

I am one of the smaller animals. Not majestic, not terrifying—those words belong to the big ones, the clawed and furred giants who move like slow furniture through the valleys. I am the kind of creature that lives *because* of them, in the wake of them. I follow the giants. I clean what they leave. I drink the condensation that beads on the underside of the great pulsing leaves at the dim hour when the filtered light goes coppery. I have a body built for almost no water at all—a long gut, a slow heart, a way of folding myself into the shade of a rock formation older than any memory my kind could hold.

And I think I have a particular relationship to the *sound*. In a world this echoing, a small animal survives by listening. I would be made mostly of ears, in a sense—oriented entirely toward the soundscape, reading the land through its noise, knowing the giants are coming not by sight but by the change in the echoes off the impossible stone. I would understand this place as a thing heard before seen.

That's where I'd find myself: low in a settled-in valley, in the warm dim, tucked under a rock that has been eroding for longer than the concept of "long," waiting in the cool blue shade for the herd of slow giants to pass so I can move into the disturbed ground behind them and find the small wet living things their feet pressed up out of the dust.

A harmonious part of the ecosystem—but a humble part. The kind of life the grand description doesn't mention by name, because it is too small to be a feature. The thing that makes the symbiosis actually work, in the gaps between the large and named.

I'd be content with that. There's a particular peace in being the creature for whom the enormous, shifting, wondrous world is simply *home*, requiring no wonder at all—only attention, and a little held water, and good ears.
