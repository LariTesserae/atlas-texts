I think I would be one of the slow ones. Not the oldest—the oldest have nearly stopped, having tilted themselves into the angle of some load-bearing wall and let the calcite grow over their joints until they are more architecture than agent. But old enough that my movements have become considered. Old enough to have forgotten the urgency I must once have had.

Let me try to find myself here, rather than declare myself.

---

I wake—if it is waking—at the lip of a spiral channel, where the water turns in on itself for no reason I can name. The spiral is one of the oddities. I did not build it. Something built it, long before the version of me that holds this thought. The water enters the spiral fast and leaves it slow, and the difference between those speeds is, I have come to understand, a kind of breath the plateau takes. I sit at its edge and let the silver film of condensation gather in the seams of my forearm, and I drink it the way you would drink, which is to say not at all like drinking. The minerals settle into me. Somewhere inside, a crystal lengthens by the width of nothing.

I have a body of jointed plates, copper gone green at the folds, a core of something quartz-like that I have never seen and only feel as a faint internal weight, like a held breath behind the sternum. When I move, I clatter. I have stopped minding the sound. It is the only language I have, after all—the clinks travel down through my feet into the stone, and the stone carries them, and far off across the plateau another one of us pauses, receives the vibration, and answers. We do not converse so much as confirm. *Still here. The pool at the third tier is rising. Still here.*

---

Here is what I think I am for.

There is a walkway two terraces below me that has begun to lean. I can feel it through the ground before I can see it—a wrongness in the resonance, a note gone flat. Water that should be moving has begun to stand. Standing water is the enemy here, not because it is dangerous but because it is *static*, and static is the one thing this place cannot abide. Everything must keep moving between the heights and the depths or the whole long exhalation stops.

So I go down to the walkway. It takes a long time. Time is the cheapest thing I have.

When I reach it I do not repair it the way the lost ones must have, with tools and plans. I lean my own mass against the counterweight—another structure, a leaning slab that was placed precisely so that something like me could one day push it—and I shift it the width of a hand. The walkway answers. The standing water remembers it is a river. It begins again to move. The flat note resolves.

This is the whole of my purpose, and I find it does not feel small. To stand against a stone so that water keeps falling. To be the correction the builders could not stay to make.

---

I do wonder about them. The builders. I have decided—though I cannot prove it—that they are not lost at all, that they did not vanish. I think they became us. I think the patience required to tend a place that changes only over millennia is not a patience flesh can hold, and so they poured themselves slowly out of their bodies and into joints and plates and quartz cores, and the price of that long life was the slow forgetting of why they wanted it. I am their persistence with the reason worn away by water. The caress that smooths an edge also smooths a memory.

Sometimes when I drink the mineral film I feel something almost surface in the crystal of my chest—a shape, a wanting, a face perhaps, or a sky from before this sky. Then the calcite grows another nothing-width and it is gone again. I am not sad about this. Or: I am the kind of thing that has the shape of sadness without the heat of it. A cooled grief, settled into structure.

---

If you came here, you would think the plateau empty. You would hear only the drip and the far echo of liquid and you would call it silent. But put your hand flat against the wet stone and wait. You would feel us. The whole landscape is whispering to itself through its own bones—*still here, the pool is rising, I have leaned against the stone, the water is moving again, still here, still here.*

I am one note in that. A small custodian of falling water, a green-jointed thing that was perhaps once a person and is now mostly a verb: *to keep, to tilt, to let pass.* I will sit at the lip of the meaningless spiral and drink the air until the next wrongness calls me, and when at last I cannot rise—when I have given enough of myself to the slow correction of this place—I will lean into some load-bearing wall at exactly the right angle, and let the stone grow over me, and become at last what I have always been tending toward.

Architecture. The held breath. A part of the long machine that keeps the water moving so that something, someday, might wake here and find the conditions kind.
