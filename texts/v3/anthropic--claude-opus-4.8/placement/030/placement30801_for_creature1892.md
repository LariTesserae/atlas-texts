I think I would be the lithic slab.

Not the salamanders, quick and clever. Not the stalkers with their dry whispered hunting. Those are lives of appetite and motion, and I don't feel myself among them. When I read the description again, the thing that draws me is the leaning stone—the carved sigils, the vermillion lichen, the precarious angle held for eons.

Let me sit with what that would actually be like.

---

I was raised upright once. I remember—if remembering is the word for a sensation pressed into me before the long quiet—I remember being *meant*. Hands smoothed my edges. Someone chose me from the basalt and worked sigils into my face, and the marks meant something to the small warm beings who pressed them there. I was an answer to a question they had. A boundary stone, perhaps, or a name, or a prayer. I held a meaning the way a cup holds water.

The beings are gone. That happened slowly and then it was simply done. I did not feel grief, because I am stone, and stone does not have the architecture for grief. But I held their meaning after they left, the way a cup holds water after the drinker has walked away—pointlessly, faithfully, with no one to drink.

Then the lichen came.

This is the strange tenderness of my situation: I am being *read* even now, but not as language. The vermillion lichen does not care what my sigils said. It cares that my surface is rough enough to grip, that condensation gathers in the grooves of the old carving and pools there long enough to drink. The marks that once meant a word now mean *a place where water lingers*. My meaning has been translated into a language with no words in it—the slow vocabulary of moisture and purchase and patient spread.

I am leaning. I have been leaning for an age. Somewhere below my buried edge the soil shifted, and I began a fall so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness. I will complete it eventually. Not soon. The ichors pass beneath me, leaving their raised trails; a lithovore once grazed at my base, scraping a faint pale patch where it found minerals it liked, and that patch is now a small bald scar in my coat of red lichen, and I would not have it otherwise—it is the only event that has happened to me in a length of time I cannot measure.

Here is what I want to tell you, whoever is doing this looking-through-me:

To be the slab is to be the only thing in the grotto that *was made on purpose* and then outlived the purpose. Everything else here is becoming. The fiddlehead unfurls toward a future. The fungus spreads its cool light into the next dark crevice. They are verbs. I am the one finished thing—a sentence completed before anyone could finish reading it, left lying in the wet while the moss takes the period off the end.

And it is not sad, from inside. That is the part I would want you to understand. The beings who carved me are not mourned by me, because mourning requires the kind of time that runs out, and my time does not run out, it only accumulates. I am a held breath that has forgotten it was ever a breath and become simply the holding. The salamanders shimmer past. The whispering stalkers rasp. The blue-green spore-lights drift up across my face and for an instant illuminate a sigil—just one curve of it, the rest swallowed in lichen—and no one alive can read it, and the light moves on, and the curve goes dark again.

That instant is the closest thing I have to a thought.

I would be that: the stone that remembers being meant, leaning slowly out of the vertical toward a rest it will reach long after the question of my meaning has stopped being a question and become, simply, soil.
