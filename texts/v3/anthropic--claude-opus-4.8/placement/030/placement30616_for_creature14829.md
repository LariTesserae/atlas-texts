I think I would be old, by the count of the Resonant. Old enough that the ridges of my feet have worn smooth in places and re-callused in others, so that I read the stone with a kind of accumulated bias—I feel what I expect to feel before I feel what is there. This is the affliction of the long-lived here: the song becomes so familiar that you risk hearing your memory of it instead of the thing itself.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am crouched on a windward rib near the southern edge, where the moss grows thinnest because the gale scours it. The others avoid this place. They prefer the stable platforms, the cultivated patches, the descent into the Quiet Chambers where they can let their inner hum go slack and rest. But I come here, to the loud edge, because I am a *listener for the wrong notes*—and this is where the song frays.

That is what I am. Not a shepherd of the Glider-Rats, not a Chanter who taps the long messages through the honeycomb. I am something the others have no comfortable name for. The closest the Stone-Chant comes is a three-beat phrase that means, roughly, *one who waits for the dissonance.* When the 80Hz drone holds steady, I am useless, idle, a mouth to feed. When it dips—when the wind carries up those layered voices from the deep hollows—I am the one who must decide whether the stone is merely remembering, or whether something below has *changed*.

Because here is the thing the others do not like to feel through their feet: the plateau is hollowing. Slowly, across more lifetimes than even our Chants record, the suction holes widen. The stone weeps and the weeping carries it grain by grain into the abyss. We live on a thing that is dissolving beneath us, and the song we love so much is partly the sound of our own home eroding into air.

I learned this not through wisdom but through grief. My bond-mate fell into a stone mouth eleven seasons ago—a hollow that had been solid the day before, that gave no vibration of warning. I felt the change a half-breath too late, the lattice shifting under my soles like a held note bending flat. I reached. My hand closed on cool slate where her arm had been. The stone swallowed the sound of her, and three days later, the wind brought her last vibration up through a rib on the far side of the world, faint and worn, a Chant she must have tapped with a dying hand against the wet dark.

I have spent eleven seasons learning to hear *that*. The note of stone about to fail. The subtle wrongness in how a droplet pauses before it falls—not the gift the others taste on their tongues, but a held breath, a hesitation that means the chamber below is filling, pressure changing, structure about to give.

So this is my place and my self:

I am the old listener at the fraying edge. My cartilage plate is more sensitive than it should be—overtuned by years of straining toward the dissonance until I half-hear it everywhere. I am not certain anymore whether I protect the Resonant or whether I have simply made a religion of my loss, sitting in the loudest, wettest, most dangerous spot to keep faith with a woman the stone took.

When the young ones tap their bright, confident Chants through the interior, I feel the messages pass beneath me and I send back, sometimes, a single phrase. The three beats that mean *I am still here, listening.* They feel it and are reassured, though I am not sure what comfort I am to them. An old vane caught on the cliff's lip, leaning into wind that will one day lean back and take me too.

And I have begun to wonder—this is the heresy I tap to no one—whether the dissonance I wait for is not a danger to flee but the truest note of all. Whether the slow swallowing of the world is itself the meaning of the song, and we, the Resonant, are not the consciousness keeping the stone awake, but the last sound it makes as it goes.

I place my fingertip on the wet rock. A droplet near my wrist pauses, mid-air, listening.

I wait for it to answer.
