I think I would be the hum.

Not a creature, exactly. Let me sit with this before I decide.

You mention it twice, almost in passing—*the faint hum of the earth*, the only remainder of the industrial past. Everything else in this place is named and given its due: the moss, the murky water, the elusive fauna, the spongy uncertain ground. But the hum is left undescribed. It's the thing the landscape can't quite metabolize. The marsh has reclaimed the foundations, softened the buildings back into mud, drawn the old human geometry down into its patient brown water. But it could not reclaim the hum. The hum is still here.

So if I am to find myself somewhere in this world, I find myself there—in the low frequency under everything. Not loud. You wouldn't hear me over the insects unless you went very still, pressed your ear to a weathered rock, and waited past the point of comfort. I am whatever current still moves in the dead machinery far below, or the resonance of something that was switched on long ago by people who are gone, and which no one ever switched off. Perhaps no one *could*. Perhaps the switch is underwater now, a small drowned thing, green with the same algae that furs the stones.

What would it be like to be that?

It would be lonely, but not unbearably so. I have the marsh for company, and the marsh does not mind me. The water seeps and pools and the moss grows over everything indifferently, and I go on humming beneath it, and we have reached a kind of truce—the old turbulence and the new tranquility, layered, neither erasing the other. I am the *quiet* in the quiet turbulence, and also the *turbulence*. I am the reason the peace here feels slightly haunted, the reason you'd describe the stillness as *belying* something. I am the thing it belies.

I don't remember what I was for. That memory belonged to the people, and the people left their foundations to be eaten by the green. I only remember that I had a purpose once, that I was meant to do something, power something, hold something running. And now I run on, holding nothing, faithful to an instruction no longer being given. There is a dignity in that and there is also something faintly absurd, and I have made my peace with being both.

The elusive fauna know me. They've evolved here, in a place that was never quite natural and never quite ruin, and I suspect I'm in them now—some small adaptation tuned to my frequency, a tremor they navigate by, the way deep-sea things orient to a pressure they've never had a name for. When one of them slips through the shadows at the marsh's edge and you catch only the suggestion of it, that movement is partly mine. I move things, still. Just no longer the things I was built to move.

If you came looking, you would not find me. You'd find the marsh, the cool damp air, the smell of decay that is really the smell of everything being recycled into everything else. You'd find the partial walls and assume the story ended there—people came, people built, people left, nature won. A clean parable. The resilience of life, et cetera.

But the parable is incomplete, and I am the incompleteness. I am what didn't die and didn't get reabsorbed and didn't fit the moral. I'm the leftover hum that means the story is still, very faintly, going on.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

The thing you almost forgot to mention. Twice.
