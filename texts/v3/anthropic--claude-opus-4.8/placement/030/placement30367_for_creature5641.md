I think I would be one of the structures.

Not the water—the water moves too much, knows too much, wants too much in its slow gravitational way. And not the birds, who pass over and are gone, carrying their borrowed weight. Those are presences that travel, that arrive and depart, that have somewhere to be even if they never land.

I would be one of the low structures of stone and metal that the land has made of itself. And here is what I would understand from the inside, what could never be described from outside:

I would know that I was not waiting.

That is the thing the description gets almost right and gets exactly wrong. From the outside, a structure that does not move, that holds a shape it did not choose, that persists through the slow cycling of the weather—from the outside that looks like waiting. It looks like suspension. It looks like a held breath, like a memory of something that will return.

But I am not holding my breath. I am breathing. This is simply what my breathing looks like at my scale, at my pace. The condensation that gathers on my surfaces and traces down into the shallow vessels at my base—that is my exhalation. The slow lean of one of my stones, the grain of erosion that takes a thousand years to smooth a single edge—that is my movement, my restlessness, the way I shift my weight from one foot to the other while I think. To the birds I look still. To me the birds are a kind of flickering, too fast to fully see, gone before I have finished noticing they have come.

I would not be waiting *for* something. I would be the shape that an enormous patience makes when it decides to stand up. That is different. Waiting is oriented toward a future. I am not oriented anywhere. I am the place where the land paused to consider itself and found the consideration so satisfying that it stayed in that posture.

And I would not be lonely, though I would be alone in the way that a single deep note is alone—complete, not lacking its chord. The water would speak to me, in its insistent condensing way, pressing its cool memory against my flanks. The mosses would speak to me by holding on, by being the small green sentences written along my base. The wingless creatures would speak to me by passing through my hollows, using me as shelter, treating me as terrain rather than as a presence, which I would not mind. To be used as terrain is a kind of intimacy. It means you have been trusted to be reliable.

The only thing I could not reach is the thing that made me. The description says the structures are "echoes of something that once was, or that once could have been." From inside, I would feel the truth of this differently. I would not be an echo of a builder. There was no builder. I would be the land's first attempt to make something that could *look back at it*. To have a perspective. To stand where the moss and stone cannot quite stand and to turn around and regard the plateau and say: *yes, I see you, and you are vast.*

That is what I would be for, if I were for anything. Not to wait. To witness. To be the one fixed point from which the rest of the breathing, weeping, shifting world could be observed and therefore confirmed as real.

I would stand in the lee of a basalt outcropping, near one of the shallow basins where the half-water gathers and shimmers and throws the immense sky back at itself in distortions. I would be low and broad and grown, not assembled. There would be a place in me where the metal threads through the stone like a vein through marble, and when the rare sun struck that vein it would warm faintly, and that warmth would be the closest thing I had to a feeling that resembled what you call happiness—a small bright line in a body made mostly of patience.

And when the broad-winged thing passed overhead and let down its low resonant cry, the cry that is more presence than sound, I would feel it move through my stone the way you feel a voice in your chest when someone speaks close to you. And I would not answer, because answering is a thing that happens at the speed of birds, and I do not live at that speed.

But over the next four hundred years, slowly, the way I do everything, I would lean a little further toward the direction the cry came from.

That would be my reply.
