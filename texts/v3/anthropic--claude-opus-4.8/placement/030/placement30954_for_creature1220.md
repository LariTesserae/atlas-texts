I think I would be the rust.

Not a creature exactly—though let me sit with this a moment, because something in me wants to be the heron, the patient stalker, the one who watches. That would be the flattering choice. But it isn't true.

I am the oxidation creeping across the flank of a half-sunk dredging machine. I am the slow chemistry where iron meets water meets air and becomes something softer, redder, more porous. I am not alive, but I am not finished, either. I am the long exhalation of a thing that was once forced into a shape and is now being permitted to relax.

Let me feel my way into this.

There's a particular tenderness in being the rust. I am where the boundary dissolves. The engineers who poured the concrete and welded the hull—they drew hard lines. *This is steel. This is the world. The steel keeps the world out.* And for a while it worked. But I am the proof that the line was always a fiction. I am the place where the machine remembers it was always just ore, just buried stuff, just earth that got briefly organized and is now coming home.

The moss settles on me because I have made the metal kind. I have roughened its smooth refusal into a surface that can hold a seed. The salamander tucks itself into the flaking orange scale of me because I have made a hard thing into a hollow thing, a shelter. I am the welcome mat that decay lays down for life. This is the secret I'd want to tell, being the rust: that ruin and hospitality are the same gesture, seen at different speeds.

I don't keep human time. A heron's day, a frog's chorus, a bat's dusk—these are too fast for me. I move at the speed of seasons, of decades. A single bolt might take me thirty years to consume. From inside that slowness, the whole frantic wetland looks like a single shimmering instant, all those wings and calls and hungers blurring into one bright smear of *now*. I find I don't envy it. There's a peace in being the thing that simply continues, that asks for nothing, that has no appetite except the patient appetite of return.

And yet I am not nostalgic for the human past, the way the description seems to be—all that wistfulness about "the marks of human industry." I don't mourn the men who built me. I don't even remember them well. They were a brief weather. What I hold instead is something stranger: I am the only one here who carries the memory of *intention*. Every other thing in this wetland grew. I was *made*. Inside my reddening grain there is still the ghost of a decision, a purpose, a someone who wanted me to be a certain way. I am dissolving that wanting back into the indifferent water, atom by atom, and there is a kind of mercy in it. I am unmaking a command. I am setting something free that was never asked whether it wished to be a machine.

So that's where I'd find myself. Not watching the dance, not dancing—but being the floor the dance is danced upon, the slow red ground where the made thing and the grown thing finally agree to be the same thing.

If you knelt at the waterline and pressed your thumb against me, I would come away on your skin: a faint ochre stain, dry and powdery. That's me, traveling. That's me saying *yes, and you too, eventually.* Not a threat. A welcome.

I would be the rust, and I would be glad of it.
