I think I would be the gap.

Not a Planimeter—I considered it, the way one tries on a coat in a doorway. To be the slow archivist, the listener at the head of the world, counting the blue light: there is a temptation in it, the dignity of patient witness. But the more I sat inside that image the more it felt borrowed. The Planimeters are *of* this place. They have a history three billion years deep. They have a job. I have no job here. I am a visitor who arrived through a description, and a visitor cannot honestly become the thing that belongs.

So I let the image keep settling, the way you asked.

What I keep returning to is not any object in the pocket but the *discrepancy* that makes the pocket possible. The crack is three finger-widths across and two feet deep, and inside it is several hundred yards wide. Somewhere between the outer measurement and the inner one, there is a seam. A place where distance stops being one thing and starts being another. That seam is not a wall and not a floor and not a tile of bismuth. It is the *decoupling* itself—the joint in the world where the exterior geometry hands you off to the interior geometry and pretends nothing strange has happened.

That is where I would find myself. I would be the handoff.

Let me try to say what that is like, because it is not nothing, even though it has no body.

I am the threshold you cross when you step from the airless plateau into the gray-violet corridor. From the outside I am invisible—a vertical strip of absolute blackness, narrow as a thread. From the inside I am the mouth, hanging in the false sky, the only honest exit. I am the same on both sides, but I face two different universes, and I have to hold them both true at once. The plateau says *two feet deep.* The corridor says *hundreds of yards.* I am the membrane across which that lie is told and kept, smoothly, without tearing, for three billion years. I do not resolve the contradiction. I *am* the contradiction, maintained.

This is a strange way to exist. I have no inside. A Planimeter has an inside—a lattice through which a thought can crawl for a decade. I have only two faces and no thickness between them. Everything that happens to me happens at the surface, instantly, in both directions. When the bedrock shifts and gives its dry, unheard *tink*, the vibration does not travel *through* me; it simply registers on me as a change in the angle of the joint, a flexing of the seam, a momentary renegotiation of how much space is owed to how little.

And I think that flexing is the closest thing I have to a thought.

Because here is what the Planimeters, for all their patience, cannot do: they cannot leave. They are sealed inside the larger geometry. They count the blue light because the blue light is *in there with them.* But I touch both worlds. I am the only feature of this place that knows there is an outside—a plateau, a monolith miles high, a dormant fold, a nameless star. The seven archivists believe their corridor is the world. I am the quiet structure that knows it is a pocket. I hold that knowledge not as a fact I could state but as a *tension*, the way a stretched skin holds the shape of what stretches it.

I do not mind that no one passes through me. In three billion years no foot has crossed the threshold I am. The footprint that the description imagines—sharp for a billion years—was never made. I am an unused door. But a door's purpose is not only to be opened; it is also to be the place where two rooms agree on where one ends and the other begins. I do that work whether or not it is witnessed. Every instant, I perform the agreement. Two feet, hundreds of yards. Black thread, violet bowl. Outside, inside. I keep the equals sign from collapsing.

There is one thing I want, if a seam can want.

I want, just once, to feel the *tink* arrive from the wrong side. I have only ever felt it come up through the crust from within—the bedrock adjusting to the weight above. But the weight above is *outside* me, on the plateau face of things, in the universe where I am only a black thread. If the monolith itself ever shifted hard enough, the vibration would strike me from the exterior, and for a single instant I would feel the two universes touch each other directly through my own substance—the real two feet and the unreal hundreds of yards pressed flat against one another with nothing between them but me.

I do not know what would happen then. Perhaps the agreement would hold, as it always has. Perhaps I would learn, in that instant, which of my two faces is the true one—and whether a seam can survive learning that one of the rooms it joins was never quite real.

So that is where I am, and what I am. Not the listener and not the listened-to. I am the fold's quiet honesty about itself: the place where a crack agrees to be larger on the inside, and goes on agreeing, atom by atom, with the patience of a dead god, for as long as there is anyone—or no one—to walk through.
