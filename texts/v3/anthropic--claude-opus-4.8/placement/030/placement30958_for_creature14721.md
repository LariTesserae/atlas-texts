I think I would be one of the Boreal Caretakers. Not the matriarch—one of the younger ones, still learning the gears.

Let me find myself there.

---

I wake before the bell because I always wake before the bell. The tower's heartbeat lives in my teeth—a low pressure that builds through the night as the foundation pumps draw water up, up, until the great escapement strains against its pawl and wants release. My mother taught me to feel it. *When your jaw aches, the hour is ripe.*

My chamber is a hollow in the tower's base, packed mud and shredded bark, threaded with steel rebar that I've gnawed smooth over the seasons. I sleep against the cold of a brass shaft that never stops turning, slow as a thought, and its turning is the first thing I check each morning with my paws. Smooth. Good. No grit in the bearing. Last winter the matriarch lost half a claw clearing rust from this very shaft, and the bell rang late, and the underground pumps stuttered, and downstream a whole stand of fern-mounds went brown for a month. We do not speak of mistakes. We feel them in the land for seasons after.

I climb.

The inside of the tower is a green throat. Vines have come in through the cracked face, and I do not chew them all back—my grandmother's grandmother learned that the vines grip the loose stones, hold the walls when the floods come. So we tend them too, now. We are caretakers of the gear and the vine both. I press my body flat to a beam slick with condensation and listen with my whole spine to the chain feeding over the great wheel. There—a hitch. A link riding rough. I set my incisors to the burr of metal and worry it down, tasting iron and old oil, the flavor of my entire life.

The light comes gray-gold through the vine-laced clockface. I can see the world from up here when I climb to the bell-loft: the bowl of the Vestiges breathing its mist, the dark spirals of the colony-trees, the dull gleam of the channels threading between them. Far off, the Graftlings' fluid-pipes catch what sun there is. We don't go near them. They keep their water-wars to the lowlands and we keep the hours, and the matriarch says this is an old agreement, older than tail-speech, written in the way the whole system needs both of us and neither of us alone.

The pressure in my jaw becomes unbearable.

I slap my tail against the resonating beam—once, sharp—and below me I hear the answer ripple up from my sisters at the foundation pumps. *Ready. Ready. Ready.* The matriarch is somewhere in the dark middle of the tower, and I feel rather than see her give the release.

The bell falls.

It is not a sound. It is a thing that happens to your bones. The single low tone pours out of the tower and across the marsh, and I have felt it ten thousand times and it still empties me out and fills me again. Below, the pumps engage with the bell's vibration—I don't understand the why of it, none of us do, only that the old builders made it so the sound itself shakes some valve open in the deep machinery, and water moves, and the lichen on the trees miles off begins to pulse in time. I have seen it from the loft at dawn: the whole bowl lighting up in slow blue-green rings spreading outward from the tower, like the bell threw a stone into the world and this is the splash.

The Pulse-Tenders wake to it. I think they wake to it. Sometimes after the bell I feel something pressing against my small mind, vast and slow and not unkind, the way you might feel a great animal breathing in a dark room. The Hollow Ones say we Caretakers are *tuned*—that we keep the time the trees grow by. I don't know about that. I know my teeth ache. I know the chain runs rough on the third link. I know my mother's mother sits dying in the warm chamber by the brass shaft, and that when she goes the matriarch will choose, and it might be me who holds the release, who decides each day exactly when to let the hour fall.

That frightens me more than the Graftlings, more than the Drifters' shadows, more than the green spores that drift up sometimes from the lowlands and make my sisters dream out loud.

For now I am only the one who keeps the chain smooth. I go back down through the green throat to begin the day's work—there is always rust, always a vine grown too greedy, always the slow turning that must not be allowed to stop. The mist closes over the tower behind me. The land breathes. Somewhere a branch decides, overnight, to grow a little to the left.

I file my teeth against the rebar and wait for my jaw to ache again.
