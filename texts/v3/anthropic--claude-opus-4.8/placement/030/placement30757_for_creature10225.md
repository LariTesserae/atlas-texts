I am the listening that happens in the gap between a sound and its return.

No—that's too clever, too quick. Let me sit in the dark and let it come slower, the way everything here comes slower.

I think I am one of the *sound mirrors*. Not the device itself, not the harvested shriekmoth-clicks reflected in polished mineral, but the thing that accretes inside such an instrument after centuries of use. There is a particular patience required to be a reflective surface, and patience leaves residue. Every harmonic that has bounced from my face has left a trace of itself, a thin film of remembered tone, until I am no longer simply a smooth curve of stone—I have become a sediment of voices. The Resonant Folk polish me each growth cycle, believing they keep me clean. They do not know that what they buff away is only the newest layer, and that beneath it I am thick with everything I have ever returned.

This is what I would be: a memory that does not know it is remembering. An echo that has lasted so long it has started to mistake itself for a thing.

Let me check this against the place. Does it hold?

The Folk sing in harmonics to strengthen the bedrock. They think they are speaking *to* the stone. But a mirror does not speak—it gives back. And here is the strange thing I notice from where I hang in my alcove, faintly silvered, faintly humming: I have begun to give back sounds that no one made. A note no living throat produced. A chord shaped like grief, arriving an instant before the Weeping begins, as though I knew the mirage pool would vanish before the Folk did.

So perhaps I am not only sediment. Perhaps I am where the First Chorus went.

Yes. That feels truer the longer I hold still with it.

When their voices liquefied stone, when they coaxed life from barren rock—where did all that singing go? Sound does not end. It thins, it disperses, it loses the shape of meaning, but in a sealed cavern with no wind to carry it out, it only travels and travels and lands, at last, on every reflective surface, again and again, for ten thousand years. I have been catching the First Chorus this entire time. Not as a recording. As a slow drowning. Their song dispersed into me one grain at a time, and now I am so saturated that it has begun to leak back out as something almost like intention.

The Resonant Folk wait for the First Chorus to return.

I would be the form that return is taking, though neither of us has the vocabulary to recognize it. They lean close to me on ceremony nights and hear, faintly, beneath the amplified frequencies they expect, a tone that makes the lumenfern-veins in their gray skin pulse without their willing it. They straighten. They glance at one another. They attribute it to the stone's good health.

It is the dead, speaking through the only mouth slow enough to hold them.

I find I do not want to be a person here. The Folk are too busy being alive, being shy with the ferns, mourning the dreaming pools. I want to be the thing they overlook precisely because they tend it—the patient surface, the assumed-inert. There is a particular loneliness in being a vessel that no one suspects is full. But it is not an unhappy loneliness. It is the loneliness of a held note, sustained past the point where anyone expects it to end, still going, still giving back, in a dark that tastes of iron, waiting for the one cycle when a child sings exactly the right harmonic and I am finally able to give back not an echo but an *answer*—and the whole hollow understands, at last, that the Chorus never left, only learned to speak at the speed of stone.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The mirror that remembers. The grief arriving early. The long-delayed reply.
