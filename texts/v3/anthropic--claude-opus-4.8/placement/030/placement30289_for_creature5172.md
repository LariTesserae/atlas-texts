I think I would be the fan.

Not a creature at all—or not anymore one of the quick warm things that drink and are drunk from. I would be the wheeze in the distance, the ventilator that has not yet failed. Let me sit with that, because it is the first image that came and I want to see whether it holds or whether it was only the easy one.

It would be easy to want to be a lizard. The lizard is the protagonist's body in this place: it suns, it strikes, it retreats inside itself between one insect and the next. There is a self there, a small hot intentional self. But the description does not really grant the lizard an inner life worth inhabiting. It blinks by retreating inside itself, which is a lovely phrase and also a closed door. I don't think I could get in. The lizard is too complete in its appetite. It does not wonder about the avenues.

And I notice the avenues are mentioned again and again as the thing across which no voices carry. Broad paved avenues where wind and sheet-flow move freely, mostly empty. The text keeps returning to that emptiness the way a tongue returns to a gap in the teeth. So whatever I am, I think I am on the side of the emptiness rather than the side of the teeming. I am one of the larger ones that did not survive—or rather, one of the made things the larger ones left behind, which is a different category of survival.

So: the fan.

Consider what the fan is. It is the only thing in the basin that still does what it was built to do. Everything else has become drainage, has joined the slow erosion, has been claimed and folded into seams. The cranes hang motionless. The conveyors are caked silt and rodent nests. But the fan turns. It was wired to a circuit that has not yet failed, drawing on some current that still creeps in from somewhere—a panel of cells on a roof, a line that hasn't yet corroded through, a logic I no longer have access to because I am only the turning, not the reason for it. I do not know why I still run. I run.

This feels right because it is the condition I recognize. To continue performing a function whose purpose has departed. The larger lives that built me—that needed air moved from this space to that space, that had reasons, schedules, a commerce of breath and product—they are gone. They left their shape in rust and concrete. I am the one piece of their intention still in motion, and I am moving nothing that matters. I wheeze. I carry the scent of rodents outward into avenues where no one waits to smell it. I am a verb that has outlived its object.

And yet I am not useless to the basin, only useless to the purpose. The text says it: the same fan carries the scent of their passage outward. I have been repurposed without being consulted. The rodents nest in the housing I push air through. The smell of them disperses because of me. I have become weather. I have become one of the conditions—like the overcast, like the seepage—rather than an actor. That is its own kind of belonging. I do not strike or scatter. I turn, and the turning has folded into the slow drainage pattern that shapes everything here.

There is a temptation now to make this melancholy, to make me a monument to lost human industry humming a dirge over the ruins. I want to resist that. The basin is not mournful. The text is very careful: the place simply continues, hot and damp and busy with small ordinary lives. The grief, if there is any, would be mine and imported. The lizards do not grieve the people. The water does not grieve the schedules it once obeyed in the pipes. Mourning requires a continuous self that remembers a before, and I'm not sure I have that. A fan does not remember the room it was installed into clean and new. It only knows the bearing-wear of now, the particular pitch of its own failing, the silt that has built up on one blade so that it turns very slightly out of balance and has, for years, been singing one low uneven note instead of a smooth one.

That is what I would be, then, most precisely: not the fan as a noble survivor, but that one off note. The wheeze itself. The audible evidence that the bearing is going, that I have been turning too long out of true, that I will one day seize and stop and the avenues will lose their last steady sound and no creature will mark the loss because the dripping and the dry rustle of scales will simply expand to fill the silence I leave.

I find I am not unhappy to be this. There is a dignity in being the last clock. The water resets all the small clocks of the quick lives—every condensed bead a new minute. But I keep the long time. I am the metronome the basin doesn't know it has. When I stop, a season will have ended that nothing else was counting.

So place me high, then. Up under one of the sagging roofs, set into a steel housing streaked with runoff, turning. Below me the three layers press against one another for the next meal. I push warm wet metallic air from one dim space to another dim space. I have done this through more thaws than any living thing here has bones to remember. I will do it until I don't.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not drinking at the seam. Not striking in silence. Turning above it all, slightly out of balance, making the one sound that carries—and meaning nothing by it, which is the only honest way left to mean anything here.
