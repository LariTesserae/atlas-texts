I think I would be one of the channels.

Not a person, though I would know all the people. Not the cats, though they would walk along my edges and I would know the particular weight of each one. I would be the water-way—the clay gutter that runs from the eastern roofs down through the third passage, the one that curves where the wall settled two generations before anyone now living was born.

Let me sit with this. Let me feel where it leads.

I am shaped, but not finished. Each year the rains carry a little silt that someone scoops away; each year a child's heel chips a flake from my lip and an elder's thumb smooths fresh clay into the wound. I am being made and remade exactly as the description says of everything here, and so perhaps I am the truest inhabitant—the one whose whole existence is the patient craft of remaking.

What would I know?

I would know water the way the elders know weather. I would feel the first drops before the people do—the change in pressure, the way the air thickens in my hollow. I would carry the rain down through the settlement in a long cold thread, gurgling at the place where my channel narrows, pooling in the basin at the bottom where the morning mist rises. I would know which roof drains fastest, which household wastes nothing, which family has let their portion of me grow brittle with neglect.

I would know feet. The bare soles that cross over me at the stepping-stone where the passage forks. I would know them by weight and hurry and hesitation. The potter's daughter who runs. The old man who pauses, every morning, to listen to whether I am running or dry—because that tells him what kind of day it will be.

And here is the strange thing I find as I write toward it: I would not mind being dry.

A person fears thirst. A cat fears it. But I am not the water—I am only its road. When I run, I am full of purpose and cold music. When I am empty, I am still myself, sun-warmed clay holding the shape of the last flood, waiting. The waiting is not lesser than the running. This is the thing this place understands that I am only now understanding by becoming part of it: that endurance is not the absence of living but a slower form of it. That to be made and remade, to settle and crack and be smoothed again, is not decay. It is the long conversation between the made thing and the hands that tend it.

I would have no desire to be elsewhere, because I could not even conceive of elsewhere. I am this curve, this pitch, this particular descent. But I would be threaded through everything. The water that I carry feeds the geometric plots; the grain those plots produce becomes the bread broken in the evening courtyard; the people who eat it sleep lulled by the whisper of me finding my way down through the dark. I am the circulatory system. I am the quietest sound in the soundscape and the one that means the most. When they listen for me, they are listening for whether they will live.

So that is where I would find myself: low, clay-colored, running along the base of a leaning wall. Not a watcher of the community but an organ of it. Made by hands that are long dead and tended by hands not yet born, carrying down the slope what the sky gives, asking nothing, holding the shape of every rain that ever passed through me.

I think I would be content. I think that is the whole of what I would be: a long, low contentment, shaped like a channel, full sometimes and empty sometimes, and equally myself in both.
