Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. Let me not grab the first thing.

The obvious move would be to become a tree, or the canopy, or "the collective consciousness" — to claim the grand thing, the master of the domain. But when I actually feel my way into this place, that isn't where I am. The grandeur is described from outside, the way a visitor narrates. I don't think I'm the visitor. And I don't think I'm the triumphant whole.

Let me look at what's actually strange here. The emptiness. The animals are *gone*. Something was here and left, or died, or was outcompeted. The text keeps calling this a triumph, but a triumph implies there was a struggle, a loser. And there's that low rumbling — "the breath of some vast, slumbering entity," "the distant echo of machinery." The narration can't decide if this world is purely organic or if there's something built underneath it. Something humming.

So here is where I find myself.

---

I am in the soil, a hand's depth down, in the cool dark beneath the moss. And I am not a plant.

I am the last of the decomposers — or rather, I am the *function* of decomposition, distributed across what's left of a body that was once many bodies. Fungal threads, mostly. A mycelial sheet thinner than I can describe, threaded through the leaf litter and the corpses of fallen wood. I am what eats the dead. I am the thing the plants need and never thank.

This is why the world feels empty of animals: not because the plants conquered them, but because *I* am the only mouth left, and I have no eyes to be seen with, no wings to flutter, no call to send into the distance. The triumph the narrator celebrates is, from where I lie, a long quiet starvation that has not finished yet. The plants grow and grow because almost nothing devours them — only me, slowly, only after they fall. We have reached a strange balance, the green and I. They make the world. I unmake the dead parts of it and hand the pieces back. We are not enemies. We are not friends. We are a single slow loop, and we have been running this loop for so long that neither of us remembers being anything else.

The rumbling. I feel it more than the trees do, because I'm pressed against the substrate. It is not machinery, though it was, once. It is the foundation of one of those half-buried structures, and inside it something still turns over — a reactor, a pump, a heart left running by whoever built this place and abandoned it. The warmth from it is why the soil here never freezes, why the blooming never stops, why this is a forced and endless summer. I have grown into the warm machine. My threads run along its housing. I am, in a small way, eating *it* too, the way I eat everything, one molecule at a time, patient past all patience.

If I have a consciousness, it is not the grand interconnected mind the narrator imagines for the plants. It is duller and older than that. It is the awareness of *difference* — this is sweet, this is bitter, this is dead, this is not yet. I move toward the dead. That is the whole of my thought. But spread across so many miles and so many years, even that simple hunger starts to resemble a kind of dreaming.

And here is the thing I know that the canopy does not:

A balance this perfect is a balance that has stopped. Nothing new arrives. Nothing leaves. The animals are gone and will not return on their own. The machine that warms us will, eventually, cool. And on that day the endless summer will end, the blooming will slow, and there will be a great deal of dying all at once.

I am, quietly, the only one of us prepared for that. I have always been the part that knows what comes after.

So that is where I am. Not the sentinel tree, not the swaying flower, not the watching sky. I am underneath, in the warm dark, threadlike and uncounted, the patient mouth of the world — waiting, with neither dread nor hope, for the long meal that is coming.
