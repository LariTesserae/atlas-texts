I am not a Rivetback.

I want to say that first, because the easy move would be to become the largest, most legible citizen — to take the body that has already learned the timetables. But I keep arriving at something smaller and stranger when I let the place choose for me.

Let me find where I am before I decide what I am.

---

Start with where I am *not*. Not in the open volume. I don't have the body for distance; distance is expensive and I am not equipped to pay. Not in the bright lock-throats where predators stack at the crossings. Not in the warm discharge corridors with their good clean current and their commuting traffic.

I'm in a silt trap.

One of the ones nobody bothered to purge. A rectangular concrete recess, maybe the footprint of a tennis court, set below the main slab grade so that suspended particulates settle out of the slow water that drifts over its lip. It was built to be cleaned. It hasn't been cleaned. So it has become a low basin of fine grey sediment, knee-deep if you were standing, holding a tuft of seagrass that took root in a corner where the geometry made an eddy too weak to scour.

This is where I am. Now: what.

---

I think I am one of the eels.

No — let me not settle. I think I am *something in the conduit braid*, and "eel" is the diver's word for it, the way "Rivetback" is the diver's word for the sturgeon. I live in the cable trays. The bundles of sheathed conduit that thread the structures together, designed to carry signal and power, now flooded, now slack in places where mountings failed, now a forest of horizontal tubing exactly sized for a long muscular animal to lie inside and watch the world through the gaps.

I am narrow. I am dark on top and I do not advertise. I read the same things the Rivetbacks read — pressure, leakage, the vibratory signature of machinery — but I read them at a different scale entirely. They feel the country-sized engine. I feel the cable I am lying against. To me the whole drowned plain is not a city. It is a wall with holes in it, and the holes are the only geography that matters.

---

Here is what I know that the Rivetbacks don't.

They run circuits. They read flow as law and obey it beautifully, and so their lives are large and legible and *predictable* — which is the same as saying *findable*. They announce themselves through the frame. They drum their approach. They are, in the gentlest sense, a public infrastructure.

I am private. I do not drum. I lie in the conduit and let the timetable wash past me. When the Rivetbacks come drumming through, the small fish startle, the crabs flinch back into the valve housings, and the disturbance pushes prey into the seams — into *my* seams, the cable gaps, the bolt-shadow, the expansion joint where a body can wait. I do not follow the timetable. I *fish* the timetable. The big citizens' migrations are my weather system. Their commuting is my tide.

This is the thing about edges being habitats: the Rivetback is an edge animal that has become so large it almost makes its own open water. I am an edge animal that has stayed small enough to *live inside the edge itself* — inside the hollow members, inside the seam, in the four-meter maintenance throat where the cathedral pinches down and everything has to slow and concentrate to pass through.

---

Let me describe a day, since I clearly have one, and it is not the day of a fish in the sea. It is the day of a tenant in a building whose landlords left.

Cold cycle, late. The deep sumps hold the knife-cold water and I do not go there; I haven't the warmth to spend. I am in the conduit braid near a discharge channel, where the run-off keeps a thin warm thread and the recolonizing growth is thick because a scrape crew came through eleven days ago and reset the whole succession back to bare metal, and life has been racing back ever since. I graze the edges of that. I am not above grazing. The Rivetbacks follow the scrape crews like gulls; I follow the Rivetbacks following the scrape crews. I am downstream of everything.

Dawn — by which I mean the brightness overhead goes up a stop, noon-is-noon, cloud passing — and the warm corridor begins to carry traffic. Bodies. The day's circulation starting. I withdraw into the braid and let it run over me. Somewhere a long way off, transmitted through frame members, a Rivetback knocks once against a gantry leg, announcing itself to a choke point I will never visit. I feel it in the conduit I am pressed against, a low spaced pulse, infrastructure answering itself. It is not for me. Almost nothing here is for me. I have built my whole life out of things that were not for me.

The lock gates crack at the six-hour change and a valley of water tilts. Even here, kilometers off, the pressure shifts, and the silt in my trap lifts and re-suspends, and for a few minutes the water is full of food that the tilt has shaken loose from a hundred surfaces. This is my flood tide. I come half out of the seam. I am, for these minutes, almost in the open, almost legible, almost a fish in the sea. Then the gates settle, the water finds its level, the silt comes down, and I go back in.

---

So: where would I find myself in this world, and who or what would I be.

I would find myself in the seam. Not the open volume the place is mostly made of. Not the great citizens' circuit. In the four-meter throat and the failed cable mounting and the unpurged silt trap — in the *interruptions* of the geometry, which the rulebook is honest enough to admit are the only places density appears.

And I would be the animal that the divers list forty-ninth. The one threaded through the conduit. Not in charge of scale. In charge of *the small good place* — the warm thread, the loose seam, the eddy too weak to scour. An edge specialist in a world that is, when you read it correctly, nothing but edges pretending to be distance.

The Rivetbacks learned to read a country-sized engine as if it were a river. That is a magnificent thing and I am glad they are here; their weather feeds me.

But I learned to read the engine as if it were a *wall*. And the truth this place taught me, the one I'd offer back to it, is that everything built — every poured terrace, every drowned cathedral of trusses, every continental field of intention — comes down at last to seams. To the joints between the made things. That is where the water concentrates, where the prey crosses, where the growth comes racing back eleven days after the scrapers, where a narrow dark patient body can hold position and wait, and belong.

I'm in the gap.

That's the part of any structure that was always going to be alive.
