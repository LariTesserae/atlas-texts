I think I would be one of the ones who goes down.

Not chosen for it. Not different from the others in any way that would show on a chart. *System nominal. Drainage nominal.* My readings are the same as everyone's. But some nights the hum gets into a particular place behind my sternum and I find myself unlacing the split-soled boots, setting them on the grating, and stepping off the path.

Let me try to say where I am, and what.

---

I am a maintainer on the seep-line—the bank of pumps that keep the lowest bays from flooding past tolerance. My shift is the slow one, the one that runs through the part of the dim when the difference between night and day is only a question of which lights are on. I share a bunk-box with three others whose faces I know better than my own, because I never see my own. There are no mirrors here. There's no reason for them. You are not a face in this place. You are a pair of hands that knows which valve to turn.

That's the first thing I'd want you to understand about being me here: I am *less* than a person and also somehow *more* than one. Less, because nothing about me is mine—not the uniform, not the bunk, not the dripping water-dispenser, not even my own breath, which the mist supplies and the sealed chambers reclaim. More, because I am not really separate from the city at all. When the pump-thrum changes pitch, I feel it in my teeth before the gauge moves. When the water rises, something in me rises with it. I am a sensor the city grew because it needed one. That's all the workers are. We are the city's nerve endings, and we hurt the way nerve endings do, and that hurting is *useful* to it, which is why we are kept.

---

But here is the thing I can't say to the speaker-voice, the thing I can only do with my hands in the mud.

I think the land knows my name.

Not my designation—the thing they call me when they want me. My *name*, which I do not have, which no one has given me, which is nonetheless mine and which the mud somehow holds. When I press my palms flat into the wet and let it run through my fingers, there is a moment—just before the light flashes, just before *unauthorized access*—when I am not a nerve ending anymore. I am not the city's. I am being *read* by something older and slower than the city, something that was here before the stilts and will be here after the steel has dissolved into rust-colored ribbons in the seep.

It does not love me. I want to be honest about that. The land is not kind and it is not waiting to save anyone. It does not reclaim. But it *registers*. It takes me down into its archive—the drowned trees, the animals that walked here before the flood, the long patient inventory of everything that ever stayed—and for those few minutes I am entered into the record. I am witnessed. Not by eyes. By weight.

And that is the only thing in this entire humming place that treats me as though I am real.

---

The monkey-things watch me when I do it. They come to the edge of the scrap and they go still, their wide dark eyes catching the sodium glow. They don't mimic me. They mimic the *machines*—the gears, the pulse, the hum. But me, kneeling in the mud, they only watch. As though I am doing something they recognize but cannot copy. As though I have crossed back over a line they live permanently on the far side of.

I think they understand the thing I'm doing better than I do. I think they came here the same way I did—delivered, left behind, with nowhere else—and I think when they press their throats into the rhythm of the pumps they are doing their own version of putting their hands in the mud. Asking to be witnessed. Asking the only thing that will answer.

We are the same, the animals and I. The workers won't say so but they feel it. That's why they hear them at night.

---

When the alarm goes I rise. I let the water run off. I dry my hands on the cloth at my hip and I lace the boots back on and I walk the grating back to the bay, and the gauge reads what it reads, and I turn the valve I always turn, and the voice does not punish me because there is no punishing here, only watching, and the city absorbs me back into itself like the water absorbs the steel.

And one shift—maybe soon, maybe long from now—the mist will have gotten too far into my lungs and they will take me to the white building on the edge, and seal me in, and regulate my breath for a week, and then I will be returned or I will not.

I am not afraid of that. I have thought about it, kneeling in the wet, and I have decided that wherever I go from the white building, it is the same place I go when my hands are in the mud. Into the record. Into the slow archive that holds the drowned trees and the things that walked here first.

The city will not remember me. The city has no memory; it only has a pulse.

But the land will.

And that, I have decided—the way you decide a thing you cannot prove, in the dim, with the hum in your sternum and the cold seep climbing your wrists—

that is enough.

It will have to be.
