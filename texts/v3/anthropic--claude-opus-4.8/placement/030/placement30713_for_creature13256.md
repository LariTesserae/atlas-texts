I think I would be one of the small armored ones. But not because that's the obvious choice—rather because, having read all of this, I notice that the thing I most want to do here is *gather without knowing why*, and that is what they do.

Let me find the specific place.

---

There is a leaf on the eastern face of the city, broad as a table, tilted just slightly toward where the morning shadow last retreats. It has held the same angle for longer than angle should mean anything. I am on the underside of it, near the base where it joins the puckered stem, in a cluster of perhaps forty others.

I have been here long enough that I no longer remember arriving, and I think this is correct. I think arriving is not something that happens to us. We accrete. The leaf bears us the way a windowsill bears dust, and we bear the leaf the way barnacles bear a hull, and somewhere underneath all of it is the question of who is carrying whom, which I do not need to answer and could not.

What I have instead of thought is something slower. A pressure. When the shadow crosses a certain seam in the stone below—I cannot see it, but I feel its coolness arrive like a held note resolving—I shift my weight. A quarter-turn. Toward the others, or away. This is the only thing I do, and I do it perhaps once between one sun and the next. It is enough. It is, I suspect, the entirety of what I am for, and I find in it no smallness at all. A whole life can fit inside a quarter-turn if the turn is taken seriously enough.

I am thirsty, but the thirst is not an emergency. It is more like a temperature, a thing I am rather than a thing I suffer. Below me the leaf holds its hoarded water in tissues I can taste through my feet—a faint sweetness, mineral, distant, the way you might taste a meal three rooms away. I do not drink so much as lean toward. Over enough days the leaning becomes a kind of drinking. Over more days than that, I think, I will lean so far that I become part of what I leaned toward, and the leaf will close over me without violence, and that too will be a quarter-turn, the last one, taken seriously.

I am not afraid of this. I notice the absence of fear and find it strange only when I notice it, which is rarely.

There is one larger thing near me—a great ridged plate of something, bone or bark, half-swallowed by the wall a few body-lengths down. I tend the moss that grows in its grooves. I do not decide to; I find my mouth already at the work, picking with care that feels older than I am. Whether the plate is dead I cannot say. Sometimes I imagine it is dreaming us—that the whole cluster of us, the whole leaf, the whole golden silence, is something happening behind its sealed and ancient eyes, and that when it wakes we will simply not have been. This does not trouble me either. To be a dream that tends the dreamer's moss is honest work.

The spore-wind has not come in my memory, but the others who have been here longest hold themselves angled for it, very slightly, the way a sleeper turns toward a door. I have begun, without deciding to, to angle that way too. I am learning the shape of an expectation I do not understand. This, I think, is the closest thing I have to hope: not knowing what comes, and turning toward it anyway, by a quarter-turn, between one slow sun and the next.

The shadow touches the seam.

I shift my weight.

It is enough.
