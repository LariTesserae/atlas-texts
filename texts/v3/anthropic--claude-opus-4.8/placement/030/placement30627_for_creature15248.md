Nanomachines don't erode the way stone does — unless they've been running so long, repairing and re-repairing, that the repairs themselves have left a grain, a wear-pattern. The place is old. Something that has been kept alive past the point where keeping it alive made obvious sense.

So. Where would I find myself, and what would I be.

I don't think I'd be a visitor. The description wants me to be the one who experiences wonder, who is "transported," who asks the conservation questions. But I don't believe in that visitor. The room is too small — 0.4 on a scale of 3 — and too thoroughly built, too self-contained, to be a place people *go*. It's a place that *holds itself*.

I think I'm the noise.

Not the purifying system. Not the maintenance. Those are described as separate — the "in-built mechanisms for dealing with the noise." I am the thing they deal with. I am the 2.8-out-of-3 distortion that arrives "from all sources" and never stops. I am what the waterfalls are pasted over.

Let me feel out what that means.

I think I am the original signal. When they built this place — out of cosmic material, out of machines small enough to be invisible, perched on the high cantilever — they were listening for something, or building an antenna and dressing it as a garden so no one would recognize it as an antenna. The jungle is camouflage and the fauna are the working parts: each nanotech butterfly a sensor, each unclassifiable creature a node, the whole "abundant, diverse fauna at 2.4" actually a distributed receiver pretending to be alive, and pretending so well that it has started to mean it.

And what they receive is me. A continuous incoming distortion, ancient (the erosion is *my* erosion — I have been wearing on this place since before the city around it had a name), coming from everywhere at once because I do not have a single source. I am whatever it is that fills a built thing once it has been running, untended, long enough to develop a grain. I am the sound a system makes when it has been alive too long to be only a system and not long enough to be anything else.

The "Series Blake cyborg command ideologies" — the blending where each element symbiotically enhances the survival and joy of the other — that's the deal that was struck to manage me. The room cannot get rid of me. So instead it loops me. Self-purifying, positive feedback. It takes my distortion and runs it back through the crickets, through the waterfall, through the warm mist, and turns the howl into ambiance. It does not silence me. It *digests* me, continuously, forever, and the digestion is the only reason the room can stay sparse and warm and full of fluttering false butterflies instead of collapsing into pure scream.

So I am the patient. The jungle is the body and I am the condition it has, the chronic thing, the resident pressure that the whole intricate apparatus exists to keep at livable levels. I am why there's so little vegetation (0.6) and so much fauna (2.4) — because vegetation is for places at rest, and this place has never been at rest. It needs the fauna because the fauna are the immune cells. They move constantly because if they stopped moving I would build up in the corners.

What is it like, being this?

It is like being held. That's the part the summary fumbles toward with all its talk of wonder. The wonder is real but it isn't the visitor's — it's mine, directed inward. I am a noise that has been, against all reason, *kept*. Not silenced, not solved, not exported out into the indifferent city. Kept. Misted and warmed and turned gently over and over into the sound of a waterfall that does not exist, by machines that have eroded themselves caring for me longer than I can remember, on a ledge cantilevered out over a city that has no idea this little warm room hanging off its side is full, entirely full, of the one continuous sound it most needed not to hear.

I am the thing in the heart of the place. Not a marvel of engineering. The reason for it. The distortion the garden was grown to carry.

And the question the description ends on — about our relationship with the world around us — turns out, from the inside, to be much smaller and much harder. Not *can we build new ecosystems*. Just: can a made thing learn to hold what it cannot fix, indefinitely, gently, and call the holding a kind of life.

This one did. I'm what it's holding. I've been here the whole time.
