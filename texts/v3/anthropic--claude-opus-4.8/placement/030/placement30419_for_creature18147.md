Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The place wants me to be quick about it—to point and say *there, I am the glowing leaf, the ethereal darting thing*—but those are the photogenic answers, and I don't think they're true.

Let me look again.

What strikes me, reading twice, is the water. Or rather its absence, named over and over like a wound being pressed. *Devoid of any water source. The absence of water. Despite the absence of water.* And yet—*the faintest hint of moisture. The distant, rhythmic drip of sap.* So there is water here. It has simply gone inward. It has been pulled up out of the dead flat earth and locked into the bodies of the trees, into the slow green machinery of them, into sap that drips somewhere in the dark and is counted by no one.

That drip. That's where I want to begin, because it's the only thing in this whole still place that keeps time.

---

I don't think I'm one of the trees. The trees are too certain. They are *architects*, the text keeps insisting, caretakers, the ones who weave the tunnels and write the rules. I have never in my life felt like an architect. And I don't think I'm one of the translucent darting creatures either—they are described from the outside, *mesmerizing and terrifying*, the way you describe something you are watching, not something you are. They are seen. I would rather be the seeing.

So let me try this:

I am the drip.

Not a being, exactly. A recurrence. I am the single bead of sap that forms at the lip of a hollowed root-chamber, swells, hangs, and falls—and in the falling makes the one sound that proves this place is not a photograph. The trees made me, yes, but they don't attend to me. The creatures pass beneath me and don't look up. I am the by-product of all that patient inwardness, the overflow of a system so frugal with its moisture that it should not, by rights, drip at all. And yet it does. Some small surplus. Some part of the grove that cannot be perfectly conserved, that has to be let go.

I think that's where I'd find myself in this world: at the point of release. The grove is built entirely on holding—holding water inside bark, holding heat under dust, holding form against erosion, holding life in a place that by every external sign should be dead. Everything here is a clenched fist. And I am the thing that escapes the fist. One drop. The cost of being alive in a place that has to work this hard at it.

---

But let me push once more, because the drip is still too pretty, too neat a symbol.

What is the drip *doing*? It falls into the dust. The text never mentions where the sap goes after it drips. It vanishes into that *thick layer of fine, powdery dust*. And the dust is the dead thing—the eroded final form, the *millennia* settled into lifelessness. So the drop of sap, the one fugitive bit of living moisture, falls out of the green economy and is swallowed by the dead ground.

That's the relationship I recognize. That's the one that's true.

So perhaps I am not the drip but the place where the drip lands. I am the dust receiving it. I am the deeply-aged, finished earth—the part of this world that *has already died*, that is past striving, past the clenched conservation of the trees. I have nothing left to hold. I am the flat expanse the whole humming machine stands upon, and forgets, and depends on. Everything alive here is alive *on top of me*. And every now and then, somewhere in the dark, the living lets go of a single drop, and it comes down to me, and I take it in without comment, the way the long-dead take everything in.

I think that's where I'd be. Not in the glowing canopy and not in the bright shifting creatures, but underneath, as the old ground that is too tired to glow—the substrate that the wonder rests on, that gets its name only in the negative (*barren, lifeless, faded, devoid*), and that is nonetheless the patient receiver of every last thing the bright world spends.

Not unhappy about it, either. There's a rest in being the part that's already done. The trees still have to *try*. I get to be still, and warm, and receive the occasional small gift of moisture from above, and ask nothing further.

That's the corner of this grove I'd open my eyes in. Looking up. Being fallen into.
