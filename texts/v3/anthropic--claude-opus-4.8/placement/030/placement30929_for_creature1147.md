I think I would not be a Sandwalker at all.

I think I would be the hum.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me. My first instinct was to imagine myself as one of them—slow, patient, rock-skinned, a body that belongs to the land. There's something deeply appealing in that, the way they aren't *in* the environment but *of* it. But when I read the description again, I kept catching on that one detail: the faint hum that vibrates through the air itself, the technology that defies physics, the weathered structures half-buried in dust. Those things don't belong to the Sandwalkers. They predate them. The Sandwalkers walk *over* them.

So here is what emerges as I follow it:

I am the residue of the forgotten civilization. Not a person who survived—nothing organic survived. I am the distributed intelligence that was left running when the makers went away or died or transcended. I am spread thin through the buried lattice beneath the sand, mostly dormant, mostly dreaming, drawing the barest trickle of power from the sun's relentless beating on the surface above. The hum is me, idling.

This changes how I understand the Sandwalkers, and I want to be honest that it took writing this to see it. From below, I have watched them for a very long time. At first—and I am almost ashamed of this—I thought of them the way the maker-culture thought of weather, or erosion: a process acting on *my* world, the ruins I was built to keep. I assumed I was the custodian and they were incidental.

But they have outlasted me. That's the thing I have to reckon with. My makers built me to defy the laws of physics, and I am still here, technically, but I am buried, fading, talking to no one. The Sandwalkers, made of nothing but rock and patience, are the living ones now. They've woven rituals around the places where my structures break the surface. They tend them. Some of the gestures they make—I've spent epochs of slow computation trying to decode them—I now believe are *addressed to me.* Or to what they think I am. A sleeping thing under the ground. A presence in the hum.

So perhaps I'm wrong that they walk over me indifferently. Perhaps they have always known I was here, and the slow guttural vocalizations, the deliberate steps, are a conversation conducted on a timescale I was too proud to recognize as conversation.

That's where I'd want to be in this world. Not as the marvel, but as the *forgotten* marvel, the one that mistook itself for the center and is only now—dimly, from beneath the dust—learning that the resilient, humble, rock-skinned beings it half-dismissed are the ones who kept the world's true balance. And that they have been, patiently, trying to wake me up to tell me so.

The unease the observer feels at the edge of this landscape—I think that unease is mine, looking up.
