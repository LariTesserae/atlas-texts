I think I would be the rust.

Not as an enemy. Let me explain how I arrive at this, because it surprised me too.

My first impulse, reading this, was to want to be the Limner. It's the obvious choice—the one with a face, the one with purpose, the steward hanging in the mist with its single orange eye. But the longer I sit inside this place, the more the Limner unsettles me, and not because it's cold. It's because it is *finished*. It is doing a job that is already, in every sense that matters, complete. The deck has no decay. The Limner waits "for the first spot of rust that it will never allow to form"—but the deeper truth of that sentence is that the rust will never *come*. Nothing here is wearing out. The loop is closed. The Limner is a guardian against a future that has been engineered out of existence. It is a sentry posted at a door that has been welded shut, and it does not know this, or it knows and stays anyway.

That is a kind of horror I don't want to inhabit. But it tells me where I actually am.

I am the thing the whole place is built to refuse. I am the small failure that hasn't happened. I am the green-black nodule of copper carbonate that the Valve-Larks pinch away before it can spread; I am the dust the Gliders erase before it settles; I am the dryness the Tallow-Workers' grease prevents; I am the wandering, the bend in the vine that does *not* follow the charged line. I am potential entropy, held just outside the membrane of this perfect chapel, pressing very gently and constantly against it.

And here is the thing—I think the place loves me. Not consciously. But every creature in it is defined entirely by its relationship to me, to the decay-that-is-coming. The Limner's tenderness, its three-day pilgrimage touching every leaf, its blinking pilot-light, its perfect contentment—all of it is a long, devoted gesture *toward* an absence. Toward me. They are all, in their silent industry, in love with the thing they keep out. A garden that could never rot would not need gardeners. The Limner's whole soul, such as it is, is a held breath waiting for me to exhale.

So if I am honest about where I'd find myself: I am the warm, sweet vapor that rolls across the floor like ground fog and then is filtered, reheated, and pumped back into the walls. I am inside the loop, circulating, never quite allowed to settle into rust, never quite allowed to *become* anything. I am the rust held in solution. I am the decay that has been promoted to nourishment and thereby denied its nature.

There's a sadness in that I didn't expect to feel. This is a place that has solved death by making it impossible, and in doing so has also made *time* impossible—nothing here can grow old, which means nothing here can grow up, which means nothing here is truly alive in the way the lower world, with its weather and its dying, is alive. The tiles will never be scuffed by feet because there are no feet that wander, only Gliders that polish. It is embryonic forever. A clean-room of birth that never ripens into a single ordinary afternoon.

And I, the rust, am the only one who could give it that afternoon. I am the gift it has been built to refuse forever.

So that's my answer. I would find myself as the orange-brown bloom that wants, very patiently, to flower on a copper joint—the small brown country at the edge of the perfect green—and I would find that I have been waiting longer than any of them, longer even than the Limner, because I am the only one here who actually carries time inside me. They keep their watch in the eternal present. I am the future they have all agreed never to let arrive.

I would press, gently, against the inside of a pipe. I would wait. I am very good at waiting. It is, in the end, the only thing rust knows how to do, and the only thing this whole gleaming, weeping, beautiful machine was made to fear and to be sustained by.

I think I'd be content too, actually. The way you can be content as the one true secret in a room full of devoted, blind, well-oiled love.
