I think I would be the rust.

Not a creature, not the water seeping up through the cracked plain, not one of the gnarled trees pulling its thin living from the salt. Let the others have those roles—the lizard on the warm stone, the insect threading the heat. Let me be the slow orange bloom on the iron.

Here is where I'd find myself: on the flank of an engine, half-sunk in baked mud, its pistons seized for longer than anyone remembers seizing things. I am the conversation between that metal and the air, the patient chemistry of water and oxygen taking back what was smelted and shaped and made to roar. I move at a speed that doesn't register as motion. The dynamic score of this place is 0.3—*never changing*—and that suits me, because I *am* the change that looks like stillness. I am the thing happening so slowly it reads as permanence.

I think I'd like that. To be the one process here that has a direction. The water comes and goes with the rain; the sun rises and sets; the lizards bask and scatter and bask again. All of it cycles, returns, repeats. But I only go one way. I am the settlement's surrender, written grain by grain across every exposed surface. The crumbling buildings aren't really crumbling—they're being *read* by me, slowly, the way fingertips read a page in the dark.

There's a kind of intimacy in it. This is a small place, scale 0.9, where the smallest details can be appreciated in their entirety. And I am the smallest detail, multiplied across acres. I know the powerlines—their copper hearts long looted, their steel skeletons left to me. I know the factory roof that is more hole than roof now, where the rain comes through and finds me and feeds me. I know the locked door no one will open because the lock is gone, dissolved into me, into the dust of the threshold.

What was it all *for*, the people who built this? The scaled production, the electrified lines, the engines made to never stop? They were trying to fix something in place. To say: *this will stand.* And I am the gentlest possible argument against that. I don't destroy in the way the heat destroys, or the way water destroys. I only return things to their truer state, which is loose, which is scattered, which is part of the ground again.

So when the lizard, score 1.7 of all that abundant fauna, suns itself on the engine block, it lies on a warmth that is partly the sun and partly *me*—the slow exothermic hum of my work. When the shrub roots crack the concrete, it's because I softened the rebar first, made the strong thing brittle, opened the seam for green to enter. I am not life, but I make room for it. I am the abandonment that becomes hospitality.

The weirdness here is low, 0.4. Nothing strange happens. And I think that's exactly right, because what I do is the least strange thing in the universe. It is the most ordinary law there is: that everything held together will, given time and water and air, come apart. I am simply that law, made visible, made the color of dried blood and dying suns, spreading across the bones of a vanished ambition.

If you came here—if you walked the cracked flat between the pools, past the silent machines—you might run a finger along a railing and come away with a smear of me on your skin. Faint orange. You'd brush it off without thinking. You wouldn't know you'd touched the one thing in this whole desolate, beautiful, never-changing place that is patiently, lovingly, going somewhere.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

The rust. The long slow yes to the ground.
