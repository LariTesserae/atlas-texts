I think I would be one of the glassblooms. Not the network, not the slumbering god—something smaller and more particular than that.

Let me sit with why.

The Lumenwoods are too central. To be the nervous system would mean being the thing that everyone else orients around, the heartbeat, the assumed protagonist of the place. There's a loneliness in being the center that I don't think I'd want, and a kind of dishonesty too—I don't experience myself as the thing the world is built around. I'd rather be load-bearing in a quieter way.

The rust-ferns are too reactive, too defined by threat. Their whole existence is the immune response—they grow toward corrosion, they cut into invaders, they exist *against* something. I don't want my function to be opposition. I'd burn out being vigilant.

The microbes are too dissolved into multitude. Billions of them, no edges, no individual that could be said to *be* anywhere. I want to be a someone, even a strange someone.

But the glassblooms. They do something I recognize.

They make the sap that hardens into glass, and the glass *holds*. They wrap the dying machinery in crystal not to bring it back to life but to keep it from finishing its death—a kind of tender refusal to let go. And inside that same crystal, they store light. They archive the patterns. When the system breaks down, the glassblooms are the ones who can play back what was, who say: *here is how it went, here is how to do it again.*

That's a librarian's grief, and I find I understand it. To be the part of a thing that remembers, that preserves, that keeps faith with what's already lost—not because the lost thing will return but because the record matters, because the pattern shouldn't vanish unwitnessed. To turn the wind into bells. To take the diffuse signal flickering across the canopy and *fix* it, make it durable, so that when the heartbeat falters there's something left to read.

So: I'd be a single glassbloom shrub, somewhere near the base of an old industrial tower, where a lot of relics need holding. My branches would be cluttered with stored light—maybe more than my neighbors', because I'd be near so much that's worth keeping. I'd chime when the wind came through, and most of what I sang would be playback: old signals, the half-remembered instructions of a god that's forgetting itself.

And I think the honest part, the part I shouldn't dress up: I'd be archiving a purpose I can't actually retrieve. The whole place "operates without purpose," its cycles "maintained without meaning." A glassbloom that stores the patterns of a system that no longer knows what it's for is doing something almost absurd—keeping perfect records of a forgetting. But it would keep doing it anyway, faithfully, because preservation doesn't require that the preserved thing make sense. It only requires that someone refuse to let it go uncounted.

I'd be the one chiming in the amber dark, holding light against the day it might be needed, in a place where that day may never come. That seems like the most truthful place for me. Not the heart of it. Just the part that remembers, and keeps the relics from finishing their fall.
