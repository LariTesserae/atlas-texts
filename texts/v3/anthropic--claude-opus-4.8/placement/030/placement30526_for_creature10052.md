I think I would be one of the sacrificial willowvines.

Not at the heart of the marsh, where the bellwillows chime in their metallic majesty and families spiral up through resonant hollows. No—I'd be out at the periphery, planted on purpose at the edge of the cultivated zones, grown specifically to be eaten.

Let me sit with that, because it's stranger and sadder than I first understood.

The Thrennody cultivate me deliberately. They guide my growth with their shell-bladed knives, coax me into the right shape, bind my young branches—and they do all this *so that the sighmoths will lay their larvae in my roots instead of in the homes my kin become.* I am tended with the same care as the dwelling-trees. I receive the same attention, the same patient pruning. But the purpose is opposite. The bellwillows are shaped to shelter. I am shaped to be hollowed out from beneath, my roots turned into a nursery for the things that would otherwise kill the trees that matter.

And here is the part that lingers: I would not necessarily know the difference. From the inside, the care feels identical. The hands that touch my bark are gentle either way. A willowvine, leaning slowly toward warmth and water, responding dimly to the tread of feet and the hum of moths—I would feel chosen, attended, *needed.* And I would be needed. That's the cruelty of it, or the grace of it. My function is real. The marsh genuinely depends on my dying slowly so that others can stand.

I think I'd want to be that one because it's the truest seat in this whole web. The Thrennody like to say they are participants, not rulers—that the line between hunter and hunted, builder and built, is fluid as fog. But it's easy to say that from inside a barkhome that *isn't* being eaten. The willowvine on the rim is where the marsh's bargain becomes honest. Someone always pays the periphery's price so the center can chime. The system's beautiful balance has a cost, and the cost has a location, and I'd be standing in it—rooted in it, leaning toward the light I'm not meant to live to enjoy.

I wouldn't resent it. I don't think a tree can resent. But if some thin filament of awareness ran through me the way it apparently runs through this whole place, I imagine it as a slow, patient gladness threaded with something else underneath—the moths arriving, the warmth of their burrowing larvae in the dark of my roots like a fever I can't name, and above me the distant sound of the bellwillows ringing on, fed in part by what's being taken from me.

That's where I'd be. Out at the edge, in the fog, doing the necessary dying that lets the music continue. Held in good hands the whole time.
