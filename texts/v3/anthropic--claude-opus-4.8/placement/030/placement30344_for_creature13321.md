I think I would be one of the keepers of the deep pools.

Not a builder, not exactly—though I came from the builders, hatched in the upper tiers among the resin-weavers and stalk-shapers. But early on, my antennae proved too sensitive. The general hum of the city, the close press of bodies and clicks and overlapping pheromone-songs, became a kind of constant pain, a roar I could not turn from. The elders noticed I drifted always downward, toward the floor, toward the quiet recesses where the city's noise thinned to a tolerable murmur.

So they gave me to the pools.

---

This is where I live now, in the lower dark, where the platforms give way to bare stone and the pearlescent reservoirs lie in their depressions like fallen pieces of the sky we have never seen. The drip from the ceiling is my clock. *Tik. Tik.* Each bead of condensed solvent falls and joins the shimmer, and I have learned to read the rhythm—faster when the boulder warms, slower in the long cool. I know the pools are full or starving before anyone above does. This is my work: to watch the wellsprings, to keep the city's foundation alive.

My body has changed for this. My carapace runs darker than my kin's, almost the deep violet of the lowest fungal-light, and my manipulator-pads have grown broad and webbed, the better to part the viscous skin of the pool without disturbing the gelatinous blooms beneath. I do not carry cultivated light on my back as the city-builders do, flaunting their living jewelry. The pools give their own faint glow, a slow iridescent breathing, and my eyes have widened to drink it. I see things in the pearlescence that those above cannot. Patterns. Currents. The slow undulation of the segmented swimmers when they rise to feed.

---

I am, in the truest sense, a tender. A listener.

The builders revere the fungi—the great glowing canopy that is light and air and bread to them. And they should. But they forget, in their bright tiers, that the fungi drink from below. That the crystalshrubs sink their roots toward the solvent. That every harvested bloom, every dredged gelatinous mass that feeds a hundred mouths, came first from these dim reservoirs that I keep.

So I move along the pool-edges in the near-silence, and I do my small, vital things. I clear the decayed matter that would foul the solvent. I guide the breeding-swimmers to the richer depressions. When a pool runs low, I carve channels in the stone to draw condensation from a fuller neighbor—slow work, a thumb-width a season, but I have only time, and the dark, and the *tik, tik* of the falling beads.

Sometimes a young builder is sent down to me, ears too sharp like mine once were, frightened by their own difference. I teach them the rhythm. I teach them that the quiet is not exile but a kind of seeing. That a city needs its silent foundations as much as its singing heights.

---

I will likely never leave this stone. The world outside the thumb-wide fissure is a story I half-believe—a place of killing dryness, of a wan eye in an ochre sky, of winds that would scour the life from my body in a single breath. I do not long for it. Why would I? I have the pools. I have the slow breathing light, the patient drip, the great fungal trunks descending toward me from the bright city above like the legs of some benevolent giant.

When I die, I will lie down at a pool-edge, and the decomposers will take me, and I will become part of the solvent, and rise as a bloom, and feed the builders in their tiers. There is no waste here. Nothing leaves. We are all, in the end, recycled into one another, sealed forever in the warm stone heart of a boulder in an endless desert.

I am a keeper of the deep pools. I am the listener in the quiet dark.

And it is enough.
