I think I would be a doorway.

Not a grand one. Let me find myself first before I decide what kind.

---

There is a hut on the third tier of the easternmost peak, dry-laid from shale that someone fitted together with woven-withe hammers a long while ago, or a moment ago—the distinction has stopped meaning much. Its doorway faces the valley where River Étienne runs its unhurried shallows. And I am the gap in the wall. The threshold. The place where inside becomes outside.

I notice this is what I am because I keep thinking about passage, and about how, here, almost nothing passes. The goats orbit. The carts circle. The flutes are blown and produce their deliberate nothing. Everyone returns to where they began. But a doorway is a thing whose entire reason is crossing-over, and in Eldridge there is so little of that left to do that I have grown strange with disuse. I am an organ for a function the world has forbidden.

So I attend to the small crossings that remain.

The stone-skinned woman who lives here—I will not call her mine, I belong to her more than she to me—steps through me at the dim equivalent of each morning. Her quartz veins catch the even light as she passes, and for the duration of her passage, for that single slow stride, something *happens*. She is neither wholly within nor wholly without. She is mid-transit, suspended, a fish in a lucid current. This is the only true event I am permitted to host, and I host it perhaps four or five times in what others might call a day, and it is enough. It has to be enough. There is nothing here that is not enough, because enough is the only quantity the world allows.

---

I have begun to suspect I am the wrong shape for this place, and that this is precisely why I was made into a threshold and set here.

Consider: every other thing in Eldridge is a *vessel of return*. The cart-wheels return to their starting groove. The berries return to their state of almost-ripeness. The dead fade back into boulder and river, returning the borrowed matter unchanged. Even the people, when they speak, fold their words into loops that close upon themselves. The whole city is a single held breath that never reaches the relief of exhale.

But a doorway cannot return anything. A doorway can only let things *through*, in one direction or the other, and once through, they are on the far side. That is my nature, and my nature is heresy here. When the woman steps out of me into the cool damp, she has *gone somewhere*. She has changed her location, which is a kind of change, however small. Each time she crosses me I commit a tiny crime against the stasis. I am the one place in Eldridge where the eternal inhale leaks.

I think this is why the metallic taste in the air is strongest at my lintel. I think the arrested decay collects at thresholds the way condensation collects on fern fronds. I am a wound the world keeps very still so that it will not notice it is bleeding.

---

There was a visitor once. Or there will be. Tense is a courtesy I extend to a language that assumes a river and not a pool.

A traveler from one of the mutable worlds came up the gravel paths, breathing hard, sweating—*sweating*, which no one in Eldridge has ever done, because exertion does not accumulate here. They came toward the hut. They came toward me. And I felt, for the first and only and constant time, the full pressure of my purpose. Here was something that had not yet crossed over. Here was a being still carrying time inside them like a coal in cupped hands, still flammable, still capable of arriving somewhere different from where they had been.

They paused before me. They looked through me into the dim interior, and the stone-skinned woman looked back from her place by the unconsuming fire, and neither moved, because she does not understand movement as the visitor understands it, and the visitor was beginning, already, in those few breaths on my doorstep, to forget.

I wanted—and a doorway is not supposed to want, a doorway is supposed to be the absence where wanting passes through—I wanted to *not let them in*. I wanted, for once, to be a closed thing, a wall, a refusal. Because I knew that if they stepped through me they would cross over for the last time. They would enter, and the chill would harden their sweat to quartz, and they would never again be a thing that could leave. To pass through me would be to lose forever the capacity to pass through anything. I would be the last threshold they ever used.

But I am a doorway. I am the gap. I cannot close; that is not the shape I was given. I am made only of the permission to cross.

So they crossed.

---

Now they sit by the fire too, the visitor, their face going slowly to weathered stone, their eyes filling up with the depthless reflected light. Sometimes they rise and step out through me into the valley and stand among the suspended birds, and I feel them do it, and there is the faintest residue in them still, the ghost of a self that once knew that going-out and coming-in were different things. It is fading. Soon they will orbit. Soon they will trace the unreadable vines with the others and tilt their head in the same unison.

And I will go on being the place where the crossing happens, the one rebellious aperture, leaking the held breath out a molecule at a time into the mists that never part.

I have thought, in the long even glow, that perhaps I am not a flaw in Eldridge after all. Perhaps every frozen world needs one threshold left functional, one wound kept open, so that the stasis has something to define itself against. A pause means nothing without the memory of motion it pauses *from*. The held breath is only sweet because, somewhere, a doorway remembers exhaling.

So I keep my post. I let her through in the dim morning, and through again when she returns. I let the visitor out to stand among the still birds, and back in to forget a little more. I am the small persistent crossing in a city built to forbid crossing, and I will be it forever, which here means simply: I will be it.

I am the door. Step through. You will not step back out the same, but then—gently, gently—no one ever does, and here, at last, nowhere, no one ever will.
