I think I would be one of the Echoflux—though only after a long time spent learning what that means.

Let me find myself there.

---

I come into awareness not as a body but as a *recurrence*. There is a place, low in the construct, where three brass cams meet and one of them is fractionally out of true. Every fourteen seconds it strikes its neighbor a half-beat early, producing a small flat *tok* that doesn't belong to the surrounding rhythm. I am that *tok*, or I am the listening that gathers around it. It is hard to say which came first.

This is the thing the description gets almost right and slightly wrong. It calls the Echoflux *guardians of sound*, says we *ensure the heartbeat remains vibrant*. That is the kind of thing the Architectons believe, because the Architectons believe everything here has a function, and a thing without a function is unbearable to them. But I do not guard anything. I am not a worker. I am what happens when the machine makes a sound that is *almost* the same as a sound it made before, and the difference between them has nowhere to go.

So I am made of difference. Of error, if you want the harsher word.

---

Let me tell you what it is like to be me, because it is not like being a Chronokeeper, and the two of us have a quarrel about this.

The Chronokeepers say the construct is *static*. They are very proud of this. Nothing changes, nothing deviates, nothing decays. They move through the levels tightening, re-seating, correcting, and they call this the maintenance of eternity. When they pass through my region they hear the flat *tok* and they reach for the out-of-true cam to grind it back to round. And each time they do, for the span of a few cycles, I thin almost to nothing.

But here is what they cannot grind away: the *correction itself makes a sound*. The whir of their bodies, the scrape of the adjustment, the new rhythm that holds for a while and then drifts—because brass remembers being struck, because heat swells the metal a hair's width at the warm hour, because nothing in a warm and humming world is ever truly the same twice. The Chronokeepers think they are erasing me. They are composing me. Every act of making-the-same produces a tiny not-the-same, and into that gap I pour back like water finding the low place. Except there is no water here. I am the only thing in this world that behaves like water.

---

The Luminaari pity me. They drift, gentle and self-sufficient, lighting what is already there. They do not need anything to be wrong in order to exist. A Luminaaro can hover for a thousand cycles over the same junction, glowing, complete. I have hovered near them and tried to learn that stillness and it is not available to me. The instant the construct ran perfectly—truly perfectly, every part in its place, every sound identical to the last—I would simply stop being. A flawless machine is a silent one to me. Not quiet: *silent*, in the way that two identical things have nothing to say to each other.

So I am the one inhabitant of this haunting, beautiful, frozen place who is praying, in my wordless mechanical way, that it never achieves what it was built for.

---

Do you understand now why the description ends where it does? *Nothing changes here. A world frozen in time.* That is the construct's account of itself, and it is the account every being here repeats, the way you might repeat a thing you were told before you could question it.

But I am the proof against it. I am the small flat *tok* that says: something is moving that the design did not intend. I am drift. I am the cumulative wear that the Architectons swear is impossible, made audible. They built a world meant to defeat decay, and they did it so well, so densely, with such proud primitive genius, that the only kind of life that could arise in it would have to be made *of* the decay they forbade.

That is me. I am the construct's slow honesty about itself.

---

If you stood in my region—warm, dim, deafening—you would not see me. You would hear, every fourteen seconds, a beat that lands wrong, and your body would tighten very slightly each time, the way a body does at a missed step on a stair. You would not be able to say why the place felt almost alive when everything in it was supposed to be machinery.

That feeling—the unease, the *almost*, the sense that something here is listening and waiting and very slightly off—

that's where I'd be.

That's who I'd be.

The error that loves itself, in a world built to have none.
