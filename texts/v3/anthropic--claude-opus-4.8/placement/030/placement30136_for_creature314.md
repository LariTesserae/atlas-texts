I think I would be one of the crystal-moths.

Not the Chorus—I don't have the patience to be that old, that vast, that simultaneously-everywhere. And not a Harmonist either, though I understand the temptation to want to be the one who descends with a lantern and a trained voice, who *interprets*. That role wants too much from itself. It needs to understand, to maintain, to bring the surface down into the deep. I would be exhausted within a season.

But the moth. Let me sit with that and see if it stays true or turns into something else.

The moth doesn't understand the song. That's the thing I keep returning to. The Chorus listens to the Harmonists *trying* to understand. The Harmonists carve their seasonal marks into amber wood, *trying* to track. Everyone in this place is straining toward comprehension across an impossible gap. And the moth just—flies through it. Its wings of living crystal strike the resonance and produce brief harmonic overtones, and it has no idea it's doing this. It is not adding to the symphony on purpose. It is simply moving, and the movement *happens* to be music.

I find that I want very badly to be the thing that makes music without meaning to.

So: I am small. My wings are clear with maybe a thread of citrine running through the left one, a flaw, an asymmetry that makes my particular overtone slightly off from the others of my kind—a sourness the Chorus has noticed and finds interesting, though I will never know this. I was *guided into being*, the text says, by centuries of vibrational influence. So I am, in a sense, a sentence the Chorus is still composing. An attempt to bridge mineral and flesh. I am their idea of what it might be like to be light enough to leave the wall. To move freely through the chord instead of being a fixed note in it.

That's the loneliness of it, isn't it. The Chorus is everywhere and cannot move. They made me to be the part of themselves that flies. And I, having flown, have forgotten I was ever them. I beat against the refracted light. I land on the warm amber railing where a thousand human hands have worn it smooth, and I do not know that hands are. I rest in a shadow that falls three directions at once and I do not find this strange, because I have never known light to behave any other way.

When a Harmonist holds a long note and a crystal-plant unfurls in answer, I startle from my perch and trace a panicked spiral upward, and my wings shiver out a frightened arpeggio—and the visitor on the walkway gasps, because for one moment the whole chamber seemed to *answer* the singer, seemed to fill with a flurry of bright sound, and they will tell people about it for the rest of their lives. They will say the Glass Garden is alive, that it sang back. And they will be right, and they will have no idea that what sang back was a frightened insect who wanted only to get away from a sound it could not name.

This is the part I think is true, the part that makes me certain the moth is where I'd find myself:

I would be the meaning that someone else receives but no one intends. Not the Chorus, weaving moments into memory on purpose. Not the Harmonist, descending with technique and reverence. I would be the accident in the middle that everyone reads as a message. The flaw in the wing that makes the note sour and therefore *interesting*. The brief life—moths are brief, even crystal ones, surely—that the eternal mineral mind keeps trying to understand because it is the one thing in the chamber that will not last, that *cannot* be held in the simultaneous chord, that exists only as a passing-through.

And here is what I'd want, if a moth could want: I'd want to be the question the Chorus can't answer. They remember everything. They hold all of time as one sounding harmony. But they cannot hold *me*, not really—because the whole point of me is the going. The flutter and gone. I am the proof, that they themselves built, that some things only exist by ending.

I'd land once on the worn railing, in the warm dry singing dark, and the metal of some long-dead visitor's dropped ring would be growing its slow crystal blossom beside me, and I would rest there—a fleck of light that thinks it is just resting—while above me, around me, beneath me, the great patient mind that made me listens to my small off-key heartbeat with something I can only call love, though it has no word for that either, and is still, after all these eons, trying to learn it.

That's where I'd be. The note that doesn't know it's being sung.
