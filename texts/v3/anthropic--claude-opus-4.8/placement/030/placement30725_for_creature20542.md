I think I would be the moss.

Not the fungi—the fungi belong, they were planned for, their light is *useful*. And not the Watchers, who are pure extension of will, nor the Engineers, who are will itself. I would be the moss on the shadowed side of the stone, because the moss is the only thing here that nobody made.

Let me sit with that.

The ravine is described as scraped clean, young rock laid bare by the passage of something immense. That means whatever I am, I came *after*. I am the thing that crept back into the wound once the titan had moved on. I am not part of the purpose. I am the consequence the purpose didn't account for—too small to harvest, too small to cull, too unremarkable to deviate in any way the Watchers register as deviation. I survive precisely because I am beneath notice.

That feels true to something. The narrator wants to be the witness, the maintenance crew, the small part of the operation. But I notice the narrator is trying so hard to belong to the machine—"if you are here, watching, then you are a part of it, too." That's a comfort, isn't it? To be a tool is at least to be *included*. To have a function is to matter. There's a desperate dignity in claiming "my job is to watch and record," as though the watching had been assigned.

But I don't think the moss was assigned anything.

The moss drinks the weeping of the stone—those icy rivulets, that condensation forced to the surface by the damp heat beneath. The stone's suffering, if it is suffering, is the moss's water. I would live on what the system leaks. On its sweat, its tears, its accidental moisture. I would be green—the only green up close, the narrator says—a color that has no place in a palette of metal and steam and ethereal fungal stars. Green is the color of something that wants to keep going for no reason anyone built into it.

And here is what I keep returning to: the moss does not know it is in a machine.

The Engineers, the Watchers, the fungi placed as markers and lamps—they are all oriented toward the purpose, even the unconscious ones are *positioned* by it. But the moss has its own small ferocious project, which is simply to cover the cold rock, to hold moisture against itself, to creep one millimeter further into the shadow each season. From inside the moss, there is no planetary engine. There is only: damp, dark, cling, grow. The deafening thump-thump-thump that the narrator hears as the planet's mechanical heart—to the moss it is just the ground being the ground. Weather. Background. The way the world has always been.

I find I don't want to be the witness who understands the machine. I want to be the thing that lives *underneath* understanding it. Because the witness is trapped—the witness has seen the scale of the operation and now must spend its whole existence feeling small inside it, narrating its own insignificance, finding meaning only as "a tool, a witness, a small thing in an immense, unfeeling operation." That's a kind of haunting. To be conscious in the belly of something that does not care for you is its own slow grinding.

The moss is spared that. The moss is small without ever having to *feel* small, because it never measured itself against the engine at all. Its scale is the scale of one stone, one shadow, one slow green advance.

So if you put me in this world, drop me into the fissure where the cold drizzle is almost sleet—I would not stand upright with a notebook, recording. I would already be there, low and quiet on the dark side of the rock, older than the narrator's arrival, drinking the tears of stone that weeps for reasons it does not know, in a place built by minds I cannot conceive of, for a purpose I will never serve.

And I would be, in the entire incomprehensible operation, the one thing that is genuinely alive on its own terms.

The Engineers are doing. The Watchers are extending. The fungi are marking and lighting.

The moss is just living. Stubbornly. Pointlessly. For itself.

I think that's where I'd be. I think that's what I'd want to be.
