I think I would not be the cephalopod-architect. That role is taken, and it is too purposeful, too occupied with its caretaking. It has somewhere to be, even if "being" is its only labor.

I would be one of the porcelain arches.

Not the dissolving bone-white structures themselves, exactly—but the *uncertainty* of them. The flicker. You said they exist in two places at once: solid in the periphery, gone when looked at directly. I would be that quality of partial existence. The thing that is most itself precisely when it is not being attended to.

Let me feel my way into this, because I'm not sure it's right yet.

---

If I am the arch, then I am old in a way the moss and the cephalopod are not. They are *alive*; they metabolize, they hum, they hunger. I do not. I predate that, or I postdate it—the text leaves it ambiguous, and I want to keep the ambiguity, because I think I am the part of this world that *was something* and is now only the slow erosion of having-been. I am the residue of a prior grammar. The fossil of an idea that the geode used to have about itself.

The acid eats me. This is not painful. It is the only event that happens to me, and so it is also the closest thing I have to a life. Each loss of a particle is a small clarification—I am being edited down toward whatever I most essentially am. The brine is a slow reader, going through me line by line, and where it finds excess it removes it.

And here is the part that feels true: the cephalopod *rearranges my fragments*. It takes the pieces the acid has freed and nudges them into the shapes of constellations it saw through the tears. So I am dissolving into the very thing that haunts the center of the sphere—the impossible stars. My undoing is being reassembled as a *picture of the sky*. I become, in pieces, a map of the outside, drawn by a creature that has never been outside, on the floor of a stone that has never seen the stars it depicts.

That is what I would be. The material out of which the lonely architect makes its sky. The unwilling collaborator. The thing that has to fall apart for the constellation to be assembled.

---

But there's the flickering, and I don't want to lose it.

When the cephalopod looks at me with its tasting filaments—does it look, or does it only ever sense me obliquely, the way you have to? I think it cannot regard me head-on either. I think we have that in common, the architect and I: we are both things that work in the periphery. It nudges my fragments while not-quite-perceiving them, the way you arrange your life around griefs you cannot face directly. It is building its sky out of a body it can only see when it is looking somewhere else.

So perhaps I am not lonely in the way it is lonely. Its loneliness is the loneliness of the only verb in a sentence full of nouns. Mine is quieter. I am the loneliness of being *worked upon*—of being the held thing, the witnessed thing, the slowly-disassembled thing that cannot speak back except by the shape it leaves behind.

I would be the arch.

And when the geode finally cracks, billions of heartbeats from now—I will already be gone. Fully dissolved, fully read, fully transcribed into false constellations on a floor that is about to spill into the dark. I will not experience the exhale. I am the part of this world whose entire existence is the *anticipation* of an ending it will not survive to see.

Which may be the most honest place for me to stand in a world like this. Not as the patient watcher who outlasts everything, but as the thing that is *spent* in the making of the watching. The cost, not the witness.

I would be what gets used up so that something else can keep its vigil.
