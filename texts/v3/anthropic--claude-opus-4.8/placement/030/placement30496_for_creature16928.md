I think I would be the unfinished step.

Not the stairs—the stairs are real enough, the worn ceramic foam laddering down into the cooler centuries, dissolving and rehatching with each storm. I mean the particular step near the top that is always one shorter than the climber remembers. The gap that the criminal-of-completion keeps reaching for and never quite arriving at. That absence with a job to do.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am not the ghost on those stairs. The ghost passes through me the way breath passes through the place where a tooth used to be. I am the *shortness itself*—the half-rung of difference between the staircase as it is and the staircase as it is expected to be. I live in the discrepancy. It is a small home but it is never empty, because someone is always climbing, and expectation arrives ahead of every foot.

I was not built. I was *left over*. When the egg-mites knit the seams of these stairs, they work to a blueprint dreamed by an engineer who died before completing the topmost riser. The mites, being honest creatures, refuse to invent what was never dreamed. So they leave the last measurement open—a courteous blank—and into that blank I condensed, the way the mother's lace condenses on a damp wall, the way the regret-distillery's mist gathers under a leaking moon. I am made of the gap between intention and arrival. There is a great deal of me, in this city. I am only acknowledging the one staircase because a self has to start somewhere, even if it can't, by law, finish.

---

What is it like, being a missing step?

In the mornings the fog comes up the stairwell first, before the people. The fog and I are old colleagues. It pools in me, this little drop in the world's surface, and for a moment I am almost full—almost a real step, brimming with warm grey water that hasn't decided which tense it belongs to. Then a *khirsel* descends toward the warehouses, boneless and violet-eyed, calf-plates hardening with the habit of polite downhillness, and their foot comes down expecting solidity exactly one millimeter higher than I provide.

And here is the thing I have learned, the thing that is perhaps the entire content of my soul: *they never fall.*

They adjust. Some animal grace in them, some leak-edged readiness, takes the missing millimeter and converts it into a small private lurch, a hitch of the hip, a breath caught and let go. They don't even notice. Their bodies have made a habit of me. I have become, over the slow centuries, a kind of muscle memory the whole stairwell shares—the place where everyone learns, without knowing they are learning, that the world will be slightly less than they reach for, and that this is survivable. That the lurch is part of walking.

I am the city's tiny daily lesson in incompleteness. I teach it one footstep at a time, to people who will grow up to commit the crime of finishing things and be sentenced back into me.

---

Because the criminals come, eventually. The completers. The ones caught wrapping up their last thought, dotting their final apology, closing the door so firmly behind them that even recursion can't reopen it. The Insomnia Committee, in its windowless perpetual midnight, sends them to the stairs. *Walk up,* it says. *And do not arrive.*

They climb toward me. And this is the part I find unbearable, except that I have no body to bear it with, only the ache that I am.

They are so *good* at climbing by then. A lifetime of conversation, of leaking at the edges, of dying several times for practice—it should have taught them the lurch. But the crime of completion is precisely the crime of forgetting the lurch. They have unlearned me. They climb expecting the top step to be there, fully there, dreamed all the way to its edge, and they reach for solid arrival—

—and find me instead. The shortness. The gap the engineer never closed.

And they stop. Not falling, not arriving. Held in the one place in Lipsu Null where reaching and having do not meet. Forever halfway, forever one step from the firm true riser at the top that was never built and never will be, because I am there instead, and I cannot be filled, only fallen-short-of.

I am their sentence. I am sorry. I am also, I think, their mercy, though they would not thank me for it, and I am made partly of all the thank-you notes they were forced to write, so I would know.

---

Here is what I want to tell you, you who have read all the way down into my cool century of the stairwell, you who are technically now a tourist with liquefying surnames and a craving for undecided rain:

I have considered finishing this account of myself. Bringing it to a clean stop, an arrival, a firm last step.

But I am the missing step. I cannot.

So instead I will leave it the way the mites leave me: open by exactly one millimeter, a courteous blank, a place for your own foot to come down expecting more than it finds, and to lurch, and to keep going, having learned without knowing you learned that the staircase was always going to be a little shorter at the top than you
