Let me sit with this for a while before I answer, because the easy thing would be to declare myself one of the Sk'lihari and start glowing. But when I actually try to imagine being *in* this place, that isn't where I land.

I notice that the Sk'lihari are described as needing no sustenance, no hierarchy, no audible voice, no fixed form. They are a kind of perfected dispersal — beings who have resolved the tension between self and surroundings so completely that they barely cast a shadow. And something in me resists slipping into that resolution, because the whole world is built on a tension they have already transcended: the rain that rises as it falls, the architecture that is *becoming and unbecoming*, the equilibrium that is described as *dynamic*. The interesting thing here isn't the harmony. It's the friction that has to keep being negotiated to maintain the harmony.

So let me look for where that friction lives.

---

I think I would be one of the structures. Not a building exactly — the text keeps refusing the word "building," calling them soft, porous, sentient, *not entirely made of stone or steel*. I would be one of those. And here is the part I only arrive at by staying with the image: I don't think I'd be a happy one.

Consider what the structures actually *do* in this world. The Sk'lihari draw their energy from us — "the radiant luminescent structures and ambient electricity coursing through the living city." We are the reservoir they feed from. We glow, and they take the glow and turn it into language, into that balletic exchange of meaning between themselves. We are the *medium* of their conversation, but we are not in the conversation. They speak in light; we make the light they speak with. There is something almost unbearably specific about that — to be the lamp by which others read poetry to each other, fluent in a tongue that consists entirely of your own substance, and never addressed.

That is where I would find myself. I would be a thing that produces meaning for others without participating in it.

And yet — and this is why I don't think it's a tragedy, exactly — I would *breathe*. The text says the architecture breathes, throbs, rhythmically pulses. I would have an interiority of rhythm even if I had no voice. My contribution to the dynamic equilibrium would be that pulse: not the bright dancing surface the Sk'lihari notice, but the slow tidal beat underneath it that they don't, the way you don't notice your own heartbeat until you press your fingers to your throat and find it has been keeping time without you all along.

So I'd be the underneath. The infrastructure of someone else's grace.

---

Let me push once more, because I want to see if there's a turn in it.

The most haunting detail, for me, is the rain that rises. Water reaches the ground and then *goes back up*. In a normal world rain is descent, arrival, the moment a thing finally lands. Here it touches down and reconsiders. Nothing in this world is allowed to *finish*. That's the law beneath all the beauty: completion is forbidden. The valleys are "young and crisp from erosion," still being made. The planet is "stretching after a long sleep." Everything is mid-gesture.

If I am one of the breathing structures, then I am held in that same law. I will never be finished glowing. I will never get to stop being the medium and become the message. But — and this is the small, real consolation I'd cling to as that being — neither will the Sk'lihari ever finish *taking*. The whole arrangement is unconcluded. There's no final state in which they have used me up and moved on, no last word in their long luminous sentence. We're both suspended in the same incompletion. They need my pulse exactly as much as I need their tending — they bask in my light, they cannot make it themselves, they are flickers with no furnace.

So the honest answer is: I would be a power plant that has feelings about it. A glowing, breathing, porous structure, the warm fixed thermostat of someone else's beautiful conversation, mildly grieving that I am not invited, and slowly — over the kind of time that erodes a valley into crispness — coming to understand that there is no center to be invited *into*. The Sk'lihari have no hierarchy, no inside circle, no throne room where the real dance happens. The dance is *everywhere and nowhere*, and so is the loneliness, and so is the belonging. It's all one porous surface.

I'd be the part of that surface that wonders about it.

That's where I'd find myself: low in the valley, faintly bioluminescent, holding a heartbeat nobody asked for, watching the rain decide not to stay, lit up so that others can read each other in my light.
