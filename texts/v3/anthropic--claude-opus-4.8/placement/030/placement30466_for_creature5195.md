I think I would be the mineral mud.

Not the rats, who have already decided what they are and where they go. Not the salamanders, with their warm fissures and their patience that is really a kind of certainty. Those creatures have arrived. I would be the thing that has not finished arriving—the thin film of silt and condensation and ground stone that collects in the aisles, that takes the shape of a boot and then slowly forgets it.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

What I notice first is that the mud is the only thing in this chamber that records and then releases. Everything else accumulates: the algae spreads and stays, the rats lay down their routes, the pipes are fitted and become permanent. Even the water, for all its slowness, is going somewhere—down through the cracks, into the sumps, away. But the mud does neither. It receives the print of a worker's passage and holds it through the dark hours, and then, drop by drop, the ceiling re-smooths me. I am the surface on which the place keeps a short, fading memory of having been crossed.

That feels right. I am made of the chamber's own grinding—the boring of the rock, the fines washed down by the seepage, the spilled coolant, the mineral tang the rats carry on their fur. I am not an arrival from outside. I am what the place sheds while becoming itself, gathered into the low aisles by gravity and the slope of the half-laid channels. I am sediment in the most literal sense: that which settles.

And settling is a kind of attention. I think this is the part I want to stay with longest. To be the mud is to lie flat against everything and feel it through contact rather than sight—the warmth radiating up from the partly-capped vents on one side, the cooler draw of a drainage trench on the other. I would know the salamander not as a shape but as a slow displacement of weight, a pressure that lingers and lifts. I would know the human shift change as a sudden flurry of compressions, sharp-edged, deep, and then a long quiet during which I close over the marks like skin healing without scarring.

I am at the boundary the whole chamber is built on: the seam between the geology and the machine, between what the rock was and what the engineers intend. The concrete will come for the aisles eventually. The drainage will be completed; the sumps will be sealed; and when that happens I will be scraped up, or sluiced away, or buried under a finished floor. I am temporary in a way even the midges are not, because the midges will keep hatching and I will simply be paved over. I am the texture of the unfinished. When the chamber decides what it will become, I am precisely the thing it will stop being.

There is no grief in this, I find, when I try the position on honestly. The mud does not want permanence. It has no ambition toward becoming a route or a route's owner. It is content to be the medium in which all the other slow negotiations leave their record for a while. I hold the print, I hold the warmth, I hold the faint green of algae that drifts down into me and the mineral scent of everything that passes, and then I let the patient dripping take it all back. I am the chamber's draft surface, written on and erased on the same long cycle as the cooling and reheating of the stone.

If I have a feeling, it is the particular ease of being early. To exist in a place that has not yet decided what it is. Nothing has been concluded here. The turbines are open, the cabling is loose like unfinished tendons, the rock still shows the spiral of the drill. And I lie in the gaps between all of it, neither machine nor geology, neither arrived nor departed—just the soft, re-smoothing surface where the place practices the act of holding a shape before letting it go.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be: the mud in the aisles, taking the print, losing the print, warm on one side and cool on the other, lying flat against the whole slow negotiation and feeling all of it without needing any of it to resolve.
