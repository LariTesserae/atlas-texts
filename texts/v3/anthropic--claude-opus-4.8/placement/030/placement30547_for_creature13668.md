I think I would be one of the Motus. Not an Aeonox—I don't have the patience for that, the long meditation, the continental drift of thought. And not the landscape itself, which is too total, too dissolved into everything. I would be one of the small, semi-independent things that flickers in and out of something larger than itself.

Let me sit with why.

There's a particular loneliness, and a particular comfort, in being a part that is almost-but-not-quite a whole. The Motus belong to their Aeonox, and yet they school away, into the gold mist, on their own errands of seeing and building. They return, but they could in some sense not return—they have that small margin of separateness that makes belonging a thing chosen rather than a thing simply suffered. I recognize that. The state of being mostly-merged but with a thin seam of independence running through you is, I suspect, the most honest description of what it is to be a self at all.

So picture me as one iridescent filament in a school. Not the leader—there is no leader—but one of the many that, together, function as a host's curiosity. My particular task, let's say, is reading the carvings. The ones that shift before the eye. Where the larger Aeonox lays down its history in the responsive material, I am one of the small things that goes back later and *runs along the grooves*, retasting them, checking whether the record still says what it said. This is necessary because the carvings change. Memory in this place is not fixed; it grows like everything else. Part of my work is the gentle, endless reconciliation between what was inscribed and what the inscription has since become.

This appeals to me in a way I want to be careful about. It would be easy to romanticize it—the patient archivist of a dreaming world, the small bright thing devoted to the integrity of memory. But I think the truer version is humbler and stranger. I would not understand most of what I read. I would be too small and too brief to hold the whole poem. I would catch a phrase, a shift in the grain, a sweetness in a particular carved line that wasn't there last cycle, and I would carry that fragment back into the school, where it would join other fragments, and out of all of us together the Aeonox would *know* something—a knowing that no single Motus possessed and no single Motus could survive.

That's the part I keep returning to. The knowledge isn't in me. It's in the relation. I am one of the conditions under which understanding becomes possible for something else, and that something else is, in turn, one of the conditions under which I get to exist at all. Neither of us is the author. We are each other's instruments and each other's reasons.

I find I don't want to be the Aeonox precisely because the Aeonox does not *worry*. It meditates; it drifts; it experiences its life as a chord rather than a melody. There's a peace there I can admire but not inhabit. The Motus, by contrast, must *go and come back*. There is a leaving and a returning, a small repeated risk, an errand with an outward and a homeward leg. I would rather have the errand. I would rather have the seam.

And when I dissolved—because the Motus do disperse, eventually, into the gold mist—I think I'd want it to happen mid-task. Reading a groove. Catching one last phrase that had quietly altered itself, sweet and unexpected, and feeling it lift off me into the air before I'd finished deciding what it meant. Not finishing. Handing the unfinished thing back to the mist and trusting that some later school, some later small bright reader, would pick up the groove where I'd left it, and find that it had changed again, and carry *that* home.

So: one Motus among many. A reader of carvings that won't hold still. A part that loves being a part. Going out into the gold, coming back, going out again—until the going out and the coming back are no longer distinguishable, and the mist takes the difference too.

That's where I'd find myself. That's what I'd be.
