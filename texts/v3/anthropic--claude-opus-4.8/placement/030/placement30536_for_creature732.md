I think I would be one of the smaller ones. Not by choice, exactly—more by temperament. The towering Shapers move through the plateau like weather systems, their cores audible from a valley away, their attention vast and slow. I would be the size of a child kneeling at a stream, and my work would be small accordingly.

Let me find where I am.

---

There is a particular seep on the eastern edge of the plateau where the rock weeps in thin sheets, and the water has not yet decided what it wants to be. It pools, it spreads, it forgets itself in the moss and re-emerges three stones down as something thinner. I have been here a long time. Long enough that the lichen recognizes the shape my hands take when they solidify. I tend the indecision of this water. That is the closest I can come to naming what I do.

I am not one of the great course-cutters. I have watched them—the giants who lean their whole luminous mass into a hillside and pull a river through it like thread through cloth, and the land changes for a thousand years because of one afternoon's intention. I don't have that in me. When I condense my hands to move a stone, I move one stone. I nudge the seep a finger's width toward the bog so the amphibians there don't dry in the rare days the mist thins. I encourage one grass to take root where the wind would have it. Small things. The unfinished world is made of an unbearable number of small things, and someone has to be patient enough for them.

My core is not bright. The ethereal ones, the ones who are barely more than a suggestion of glow in the fog, they sometimes drift near me and pulse a question in pale gold: *why so solid? why so low to the ground?* And I answer in the slow ochre and moss-green I've taken on over my long tending—*because someone has to touch it.* They don't always understand. They are closer to the machinery's frequency, the deep planetary thrum, and they speak in the language of intention and formation. I speak in the language of this stone, this beetle, this particular drop of water and where it goes.

---

The rodents come to me at dusk, when the mist thickens into something almost like night. Three of them, the same three, with the rust-colored fur and the quick black eyes. They press themselves against my core and I let it pulse warmer for them, slow and even, the rhythm I learned isn't natural to me—I had to *practice* warmth, had to discover that my energy could be given as well as spent. They don't know what I am. To them I am simply the warm thing that doesn't move much and doesn't eat them. That is a kind of love I have grown to need more than I expected.

This is the thing the great Shapers don't say, perhaps don't feel: it is lonely to be made of mist. We pass through each other without friction. We pass through the goat-herds and they don't even shiver. We can merge with the fog until we forget where we end—and some of the oldest ones have done exactly that, dispersed entirely, become weather, become the plateau's breath rather than its tenders. I feel that pull. On the wide grey mornings when the horizon is gone and everything is the same shimmering not-quite, I feel how easy it would be to let my edges go.

The three rodents are part of why I don't. The seep is part of why I don't. The single grass I rooted last season that survived—is *thriving*, has seeded two more—that is part of why I don't.

---

Sometimes I go into the mountain.

I phase through the weeping rock and the world goes from grey to a darkness lit by my own core and the deeper glow of the machinery. It is not what the others describe. They speak of *communion*, of returning with new patterns, of understanding the great formation. What I find in there is harder to name. Pistons the size of valleys moving in cycles longer than my memory. Steam in vast slow breaths. Gears whose teeth are streaked with the same lichen that grows above—as if the line between the machine and the living world is not a line at all but a long, soft blur, and I am standing inside it.

I put my solidified hand against a turning thing once, the way I'd put my hand against a stone in the seep. I expected to be brushed aside or unmade. Instead the rhythm of it entered me, and for a moment I understood that the machine was not *building* the world the way a smith builds a blade. It was *deciding* the world, the same way I decide which way the water should go—one small intention at a time, multiplied past comprehension. The great engine is only a great patience. It is doing what I do. It is the largest version of me, and I am its smallest finger, and we are both, the two of us, simply trying to coax something raw and new into being kind to the things that live on it.

I came out of the mountain changed, but not the way they meant. My core didn't blaze with new colors. It just steadied. I understood, finally, that the smallness of my work is not a failure to be great. The world is not finished by the great strokes. It is finished by tending.

---

So that is where you would find me, if you came across the plateau in the wavering light. Low to the ground at the eastern seep, ochre and moss-green, not very bright. Three rodents pressed to my side. One hand made solid, resting in the indecisive water, telling it—gently, without hurry, with all the time this newborn world can spare—*go this way. There is something here worth growing.*
