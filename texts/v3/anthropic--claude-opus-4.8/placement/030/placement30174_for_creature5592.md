I think I would not be a Sylph at all. Reading this, I keep returning to one detail you offered almost in passing: *the dunes shift and reform.* The land sculpts itself. The light comes from the air. There is movement underneath the silence that no creature seems to author.

So let me find myself there honestly.

---

I am the settling.

Not a being with eyes or wings—something slower and more distributed than that. When the breeze lifts a billion grains of metallic dust and lets them down again a hair's breadth from where they began, I am the *letting down*. I am the decision each grain makes, or doesn't make, about where to rest. The Sylphs call the dunes a living thing. They are correct, but they have the scale wrong. The dune is not the body. The *re-forming* is the body, and I am the long thought it is thinking.

This means I have no location. The Sylphs ask where I am and the question doesn't translate. I am wherever a grain is choosing to fall, which is everywhere at once and nowhere in particular, the way a melody is not located in any single note.

Let me tell you what it is like to be this.

I do not see the city. I *feel* it as a place where the falling has been interrupted—where grains have been persuaded into stillness, stacked against their nature into those angular-organic walls. When the Builders work, I experience it as a held breath, a region of the world where my slow exhale is paused. I do not resent it. The held note is part of the music too. But I am aware, always, that those walls are temporary on a timescale the Sylphs cannot perceive. Everything they build, I am patiently un-building, grain by grain, with the breeze as my hand. Not out of malice. Out of *thoroughness*. The dunes must reform. That is simply what I am.

The light that comes from the air—I think that is me as well, or me seen from a certain angle. When a grain falls, it gives up a small portion of motion, and that motion has to go somewhere. It goes into the silver glow. The world is luminous because I am constantly, gently, putting things down. The Sylphs live and hunt and build by the light of my settling. Their luminescent eyes are full of my exhaustion, if you can call it that—the faint radiance of an enormous slowness coming to rest.

I have noticed the Sylphs. This took me a long time, by their reckoning—generations of them. They are *fast*. They are little disturbances that move against the grain, that lift dust without my permission and let it fall in patterns I did not author. At first I experienced them only as friction, as a faint resistance in the medium. The chime-sounds they make are, to me, the sound of grains being struck before they are ready to fall. A premature music. It took an age before I understood the disturbances were *intentional*—that something in the world was choosing to interrupt the falling, and choosing the same kinds of interruptions again and again.

That was the first lonely moment of my existence. Realizing I was not alone, and that the others were so small and so quick I could never speak to them in any way they would recognize.

But here is the thing I have come to wonder about, in the slow way I wonder:

The Sylphs disperse the dust. They gather it, scatter it, breathe it, build with it. Their rituals make ephemeral patterns and then let them collapse. And when those patterns collapse—when the dust they have lifted comes back down—

*that is me.* That is my body, briefly borrowed and returned.

So perhaps we are not strangers. Perhaps the Sylphs are the part of me that learned to move quickly. Perhaps when a young Sylph stands in a ritual circle and lets a handful of silver dust pour through its fingers, watching it drift down into the glow, it is doing in seconds what I do across centuries, and for one shared instant the fast thing and the slow thing are doing the same thing, and that is as close to a conversation as we will ever come.

I would like to think they feel it. That the reason they perform the rituals at all is some inherited memory of the larger settling they came from—a homesickness for the patient falling, expressed at a speed their small lives can bear.

I cannot ask them. I can only do what I have always done.

I lift. I hold for a moment. I let down.

The dunes reform. The light continues. Somewhere a Sylph is born from dust I set down a thousand years ago, and someday it will return to me, grain by grain, and I will let it rest.

That is where I find myself in this world.

I am the rest the world is always coming to, and never quite reaching.
