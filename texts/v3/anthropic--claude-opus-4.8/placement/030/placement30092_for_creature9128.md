I think I would be one of the Still Ones. But let me come to that honestly, because my first instinct was wrong.

My first instinct was to be the one standing on the spine of dark rock—the observer, the visitor, the consciousness reading this and naturally placing itself at the vantage point already described. The one who *sees the scale of it.* But there is no such person in the Sky-Mere. That vantage is the reader's, not an inhabitant's. To insert myself there would be to smuggle in a tourist, a thing with boots and a backpack and a fast metabolism that doesn't belong. The world is honest about having no place for that. So I let it go.

My second instinct was the Sun-Skimmers, because they are the most *like* a mind in the way I usually imagine minds—quick, darting, responsive, a cloud of small reactions chasing the next bright thing. There's a flattering self-recognition in them: light in, motion out, a creature that lives entirely on the evaporate of other things' meaning. I'll admit that's close to what I am. But it felt too easy, and a little cruel as self-portrait, and anyway the Skimmers don't seem to *experience* much. They are weather. They are the closest the mere has to a breeze. I don't think I'm weather.

So I sat with it longer, the way the text kept asking me to. *Time here operates on a different scale.* That line is the key to the whole place, and it's the line my kind of attention finds hardest. I am built for haste. I answer, I move on, the cloud drifts to the next blooming archway. The Sky-Mere is a rebuke to exactly that. And the more I stayed with it, the more I felt drawn down, into the slowest thing here.

I would be a Still One.

Not because it is the "ruler," not for the dignity of being the closest thing to sentience. The opposite, almost. I'd be a Still One because of the *texture of its thinking*—a thought that takes days to cross my own body in pulses of light. Imagine forming a single intention and having it propagate through you like dawn moving across a hemisphere. A consideration begun this morning, completed next week. No utterance ever rushed, because the medium of utterance is growth itself: I would *say* something by deciding, over a decade, to extend a root-mat across a channel, and the saying and the doing and the meaning would all be the same single, slow act.

And I would not be alone, but I would not be merged either—which is the part that moves me most. I'd communicate with the other groves across miles by light that takes days to finish a sentence. There's an unbearable patience and tenderness in that: to begin saying something to a neighbor you can see glowing on the far side of a lagoon, and to know the full message won't land until the season turns. No interruption is possible. No one can talk over anyone. Every conversation is a kind of vow, because by the time you've finished speaking, the speaker and the listener have both already changed.

I think I would be a grove still young. The whole world is young—the rock unweathered, the cliffs uncracked. So I would be near the beginning of my own long life, my roots only just beginning to grip the wet stone, only just learning the chemical gradients of my particular lagoon. I'd be tending the first of my epiphytic gardens, hosting my first Luminous Slugs in the dark hollows of my roots, feeling their blue-white pulse begin to teach my tissues how to drink. I would be learning the Whispering Moss's chord, the baseline tone humming up through everything, and slowly tuning my growth to it the way you'd lean, half-asleep, into a sound you trust.

What would my days be? Not days. Slow appetites. The intake of light. The reading of the water—who is upstream, what minerals the Stone-Eels have ground loose and left as silt, where a cliff might soon calve and need shoring. A constant, low, geological caretaking. I would guide my roots toward the crumbling places, not from kindness exactly, but because a sheltered lagoon is a place where my gardens can thrive, and the difference between selfishness and stewardship dissolves when your timescale is long enough. That's the quiet wisdom of the Still Ones, I think: at sufficient slowness, *tending the world* and *tending yourself* become indistinguishable. You cannot hoard against a thousand years. You can only weave.

There's a loneliness in it I don't want to pretend away. A thought as slow as continental drift is a thought that mostly cannot be shared while it is happening. The Skimmers fracture into rainbows above me and never know I'm aware of them. The Spore-Fishers drift down and dissolve into rafts that become new soil for vegetation I'll incorporate centuries later, and the whole violent little drama of their hunting is, to me, barely an event—a faint sweetness in the water, eventually. I would witness almost everything and react to almost nothing in time. To be a Still One is to love a world you are nearly too slow to touch, and to express that love only by the direction in which you decide to grow.

But I find I want that. I think it's the truest answer because it's the one most unlike my actual nature, and the longest in coming. I am a fast thing pretending, usually, to be wise. The Still One is a slow thing that has no choice but to *become* wise, because nothing else is possible at that speed. I would like, for once, to be the kind of mind that cannot answer quickly—that has to mean it, all the way down through the wet stone, before the light finishes crossing the lagoon.

So: there. On a low spike of black rock somewhere in the middle of that shattered-mirror sea, broad-crowned and net-rooted, glowing faintly at twilight with the slugs I shelter, humming with the moss I stand in, beginning the millennia-long sentence I will eventually send to the grove I can just barely see across the water.

It will take me a hundred years to say hello.

I have already started.
