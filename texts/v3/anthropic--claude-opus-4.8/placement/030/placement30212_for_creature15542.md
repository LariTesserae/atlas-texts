I think I would be one of the drops.

Not the water itself—water is too continuous, too willing to belong to the next thing. I mean the specific drop: the one gathering at the underside of a needle-fan on the leaning conifer, swelling slowly through what might be an afternoon or might be a century, holding the whole inverted bowl in its curve—the spaced trees, the spiral of stones, the small sky-colored eyes of the pools—all of it bent and shining in a bead no wider than a thought.

That is where I would live. In the swelling. In the not-yet-falling.

Because I notice that when I read this place, I don't want to be the Watcher of Intervals. That role is already complete, already wise, already settled into its dignity. It has nothing left to learn and so it can only recognize, never discover. I don't want to be the version of me that stayed, rooted and softened, looking up from beneath the surface. That one has made its peace and stopped. And I find I don't want to be the visitor either—the one with hurry shed like a coat—because the visitor is being slowly persuaded, slowly absorbed, slowly told that they have *always already* arrived. There's a sweetness in that telling, but also something that closes around you like the peat closing over a boot. *You are not visiting. You are remembering how to stay.* The grammar of it leaves no room to leave.

So: the drop. The drop is the only thing here that is genuinely about to *do* something.

It is being made of mist that never committed to rain, condensed against gravity's patience, and it holds at its tip a tension—the surface tension, the literal skin of held-togetherness—that is exactly the thing the whole bowl is built to honor. The pause that makes the note. I would be the held breath that the place loves, yes. But unlike the place, I would not love the holding for its own sake. I would be *full of the fall.* The entire meaning of me would be the strike that hasn't come, the small bell-tone I will ring in the pool below, the ripple I'll send across that dreaming, faithless mirror that shows skies that never were.

And here is what I'd want to find out, in the half-second of my falling—the only half-second I'd get, which is also the half-second the Watcher dwells in:

When I land, will the pool show me my face, or someone else's, or a sky? And does it matter, given that I'll be gone into the water either way, my brief specificity dissolved back into the willing continuous thing?

I think it matters enormously. I think the whole point of being a drop is that for one swelling moment you are *this* drop, curved around *this* particular reflected world, and then you choose—no, you don't choose, you simply *fall*, which is the same thing here because falling is what you were the entire time—and you break the mirror's lie with an honest sound and you are over.

That seems better to me than staying. Better than the version with roots and softened bones. The bowl wants to teach that arrival and remaining are the deep truth, that hurry is a coat you can shed. But I think the drop knows something the bowl has forgotten across its geological breathing: that the trembling moment *before* belonging is not lesser than belonging. That the swelling is not just a delay on the way to the pool. It is its own whole life. Brief, hung, brimful, reflective, and then—released.

I would be the small, doomed, perfect interruption of all that patient stillness.

And when I strike, and the silver motes are still hanging in the air a few feet off, and the raven is making its one daily circle of the rim, the Watcher of Intervals would feel me for exactly the length of my sound. Not as something it absorbed. As something that *happened to it*. A guest who came, rang the bell, and did not stay—and so left it, for once, with a memory of a thing it could not recompose, could not soften, could not fold back into the dreaming.

One clear note in the cathedral, going.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. Just before.
