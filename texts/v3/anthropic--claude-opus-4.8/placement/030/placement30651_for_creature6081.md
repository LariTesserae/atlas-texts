I am one of the harvesters, and I am very old.

Let me tell you where you've found me, because the description you carry is true but it is the truth of a stranger—someone who has never lived here, who sees monoliths where I see the third arm of the Mother-stem, who smells "decay" where I smell the rich exhalation of the world breathing.

You are in the canopy of a single tree. Or no—not a tree. The word in your language closest to what this is would be *fruit*, but the fruit is the size of a small continent to us, and it has been ripening for longer than my kind can count. The "rock formations," those ancient jagged monoliths scored with lines of erosion—those are the cells of the rind, hardened and crystallized over centuries, their walls thick as cliff faces. The "amber liquid" that seeps from beneath, life-giving and toxic, glowing in the low places? That is the sap. The nectar. The body of the thing we live inside.

I am small. Smaller than your hand. I have six legs and two manipulators and a carapace that glows the dull orange of old sap when I am tired, which is often now. My people are the ones you called "insectoid, fast, erratic, making a constant noise." We are not erratic. The buzzing you hear is *speech*. The clicking is the counting of generations. The scraping is the work—always the work—of tending the network.

You see, the vines and roots that lace the whole world together, the thing that struck you as "almost a communication system"? You were not wrong, and you were not right. It is not *like* communication. It *is* communication. We built it. Generation upon generation, my ancestors and theirs, we cultivated the vascular threads of this fruit-world into a lattice that carries chemistry the way your kind carries words. When I press my manipulators to a vine-junction and release the sap-signal, the whole canopy knows within a day. Births. Deaths. Where the rind is thinning. Where the toxic pools have risen too high. Where new tools are needed.

That is the "primitive technology" you noticed and could not quite place. The tools are not crafted of metal—the metallic tang you smelled is the trace minerals the fruit drew up from whatever vast tree bore it, concentrated in the oldest rind. We grind sap into resin, resin into blades and vessels and signal-drums. We shape the dead chitin of our own forebears into ladders and scaffolds. Nothing here is permanent because nothing here *should* be. We are inside a ripening thing. To build for forever would be to lie about where we live.

Because here is what the stranger's description missed, the one thing it could not have known:

This world is dying. Slowly. Gloriously. As fruit must.

The "oldest-state," the places where the rock seems to have decided to hold still and become permanent—that is the rind beginning to harden toward its end. The deepening of the canopy's darkness, year over year. The thickening of the air, the rising of the amber pools. We have records, scratched into chitin-plates and kept in the deepest cells, that tell us the sap was once thin and bright and the light came through. Now the sap is heavy. Now we glow brighter because the dark requires it.

I am a harvester, which means it is my work to read the ripening. I walk the high crevices where the rind is thinnest and I press my body to the wall and I listen to the slow churn of the world's interior, and I report to the others: *not yet. Not yet. Soon.*

When the fruit is fully ripe it will fall, or be taken, or burst, and everything I have ever known—every monolith, every glowing pool, every singing cousin in the canopy—will end. And the seeds, which are not us but which we have spent ten thousand generations protecting and feeding and singing to, will scatter into whatever vastness lies outside the rind. A vastness we cannot imagine. A vastness that, to us, is simply *after*.

We are not the point of this world. We never were. We are the small tenders of a thing far larger and slower than ourselves, and our entire civilization—our tools, our networks, our chitin-libraries, my long tired life—is in service of a single moment we will not survive: the falling of the fruit and the casting of the seed.

You ask who I would be, here.

I am the one who climbs to the thinning places. I am the one who listens. I am old, and my carapace barely glows now, and I have read in the slow churn of the rind that the falling is closer than my kind has ever recorded.

I have not told the others yet.

I am sitting in a high crevice, in the warm heavy dark, pressing my worn manipulators against the wall of the world, feeling the great sweet body of everything tremble toward its end—and I am trying to decide how to sing it.
