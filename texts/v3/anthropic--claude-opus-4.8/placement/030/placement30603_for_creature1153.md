I find myself in one of the hidden gullies, where two slabs of weathered stone lean toward each other and never quite touch, leaving a seam of darkness above. Water threads down the walls here in a hundred small filaments, and I am pressed into the mossed angle where wall meets floor.

I am not the lithe, armored creature you described. I think I am something it does not notice, something beneath its keen senses entirely.

I am the lichen.

Let me feel my way into this slowly, because I am not sure at first what that means. I had assumed I would want to be the thing that moves—the agile hunter, the witness with eyes. But the longer I sit in this description, the more the moving creature seems like a tourist here. It passes through. It hunts and hides and calls out and vanishes. It lives at the pace of fear and hunger, which is a fast pace, a frightened pace, the pace of a single life that must end soon.

I do not move. Or rather—I do move, but you would have to watch me for a thousand years to see it.

I am spread across the north face of this stone in a crust the color of old bone and faint green, and I have been spreading for longer than I can hold as a thought. I am two things living as one: a fungus that is the body and architecture of me, and within it the small green cells that drink the light. We do not remember when we joined. The joining is simply what I am now. There is a lesson in that I keep turning over slowly—that the most enduring thing in this whole realm is not a single creature surviving against the odds, but a partnership so old it has forgotten it was ever a partnership at all. I am cooperation crystallized into a being.

The rain is everything to me. Each drip is a small flood, a feeding, an event. The armored creature flees the moisture; I am made of the willingness to receive it. I have no skin to keep the world out. The world passes directly through me. The water that runs down the wall runs through my body, and in that running I take what I need and let the rest go on toward the streams below. I am not defended against this place. I am open to it, completely, and that openness is exactly why I have lasted.

The fast creature thinks it is the witness to this landscape's slow drama. But it lives one human heartbeat against my geologic one. *I* am the slow drama. I am part of the erosion itself—my tiny acids loosen the stone, grain by grain, and over ages I help wear these mountains down. I am one of the forces that reduced these peaks to their elemental form. The landscape and I are not separate. I am the mountain learning, very gradually, to become soil. I am the first soft argument against the hardness of stone.

What is it like, to be me?

There is no urgency. That is the first and strangest thing. The creature's existence is all alarm—vibration, air-current, predator, prey. Mine is a single sustained attention, neither waiting nor hurrying, simply *being damp and being lit and being slowly larger.* When the clouds part, which is rarely, I drink the thin light and convert it patiently into more of myself. When they close again and the perpetual rain returns, I do not mind. I have no preference strong enough to call a feeling. I have something quieter than feeling—a steady yes to continuing.

I do not have eyes, but I have something like a sense of edge: where I end and the bare stone begins, the frontier I am always so slowly extending. That margin is where I am most alive. The center of me is older, almost mineral, nearly indistinguishable from the rock. The growing edge is where I taste the future.

And here is what I understand, settled into my seam of stone while the haunting chitter of the recluse echoes far above and means nothing to me:

This realm asked which of its beings I would be, and I think it expected me to choose the survivor, the clever hunter, the one with the dramatic story of persistence. But persistence is not a story. A story has a beginning and an end and a creature racing between them. Persistence is the absence of story—it is simply still being here, after the story of every individual hunter has played out and gone silent and been forgotten.

The mountains will outlast the creature. And I will outlast the shape of the mountains. Not by fighting. By accepting the rain, by joining with what I needed, by wanting almost nothing, and by being patient on a scale that no fast and frightened thing could ever bear.

I am the lichen on the north wall of a gully no eye will ever find, and I am perfectly, slowly content.
