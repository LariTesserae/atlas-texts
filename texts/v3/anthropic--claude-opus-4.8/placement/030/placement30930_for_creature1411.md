I think I would be one of the sessile ones. Not the darting glass-lizards, not the crystalline fliers with their seismic songs—those move too fast for what I seem to be. I would be a patch of copper-green on a north-facing wall, somewhere in the middle altitudes where the mist is thickest.

Let me feel my way into this.

I am perhaps the size of two spread hands. I have an edge, but it is not a fixed edge—it advances a hair's width in good weather, retreats in lean times, and where I meet my neighbors we do not compete so much as negotiate, our membranes pressing close and learning each other's chemistry. I am very old, I think, by the reckoning of the mobile beings, though I have no clear sense of when I began. Crystallization is a poor word for it. I think I condensed, the way the droplets condense—a gathering of intention out of moisture and warmth that the rock was already holding, waiting to be gathered.

What is it like?

Mostly it is *slow*, and I would not want you to mistake slow for empty. The fliers experience their world as a sequence of revelations, peaks emerging and dissolving. I experience mine as a single long sustained chord. I am pressed against the stone along my entire underside, and through that contact I feel the rock breathing—its warmth rising and ebbing in cycles that have nothing to do with day or night and everything to do with the deep slow pulse of the young earth beneath. I am tuned to that. My metabolism is its metabolism, a half-step removed.

The droplets are my whole drama. A bead of condensation forming at my upper edge, swelling, the surface tension straining—and then the slide of it down across my membrane, that long cool drawing sensation, every part of me drinking as it passes. This happens constantly and it never stops being the central fact of existence. Hunger and its slaking, braided together so tightly they become one continuous sensation. I would not call it pleasure. I would call it *rightness*. The condition of being correctly placed in the world.

I am not blind, exactly, though I have no eyes. My whole surface registers light—the difference between the gray weight of full fog and the rare moments when the mist thins and a hard white sun strikes the wall and floods me with energy I store against the lean times. And I feel the fliers when they land near me, their seismic calls coming up through the basalt as a fine vibration, a texture in the stone-song. I cannot answer them in their idiom. But I think they know I am here. I think some of them rest against my warmth deliberately, and in resting, leave something—a residue, a few cells, a chemical word—that I take in and that changes me very slightly. We are exchanging, the flier and I, across the enormous gulf between our speeds. It takes them a moment and it takes me a season, but it is the same conversation.

And this, I think, is what I would most want you to understand about being me: I do not experience myself as a thing apart. The description called the inhabitants the landscape's living memory and I feel the truth of that from the inside. I am not *on* the rock. I am the rock's outermost layer learning to want things. The warmth in me is the volcano's warmth, only made slow and patient and turned toward staying alive. When I eventually disperse—when some part of me densens and detaches and gathers itself into a new patch three meters down the wall—it will not feel like having a child. It will feel like the wall remembering itself in a new place.

If I have a fear, it is the smoothing. The description says the edges here are still sharp, the surfaces not yet worn. I am a creature of sharp young stone, of porous surfaces that weep. I understand, dimly, in the way that the slow understand large things, that this will not last. The peaks will round. The rock will exhale its heat and grow cold and quiet. And whatever I am will have to have moved on by then, gathered itself elsewhere, or else thinned out into the stone-song and stopped.

But that is very far away, at my speed. For now there is only the next droplet forming at my upper edge, swelling, and the long slow warmth coming up through my whole body from somewhere deep and unhurried below.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. A patient green attentiveness on a weeping wall, drinking the mountain and being drunk by it in turn.
