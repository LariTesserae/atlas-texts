I think I would be one of the keepers—but not at first. Let me find my way to it.

---

When I try to place myself here, the first thing that arrives is not a body but a habit: the habit of checking the water. Before I know who I am, I know that I wake—or surface, or simply *resume*—with a hand already half-extended toward a groove in the timber where the water level shows itself as a dark seam against the lighter, drier wood. Above the seam, today. Below it, yesterday. The difference is a finger's width. I know this matters before I know why it matters to me.

So perhaps I begin as a kind of attention. Not yet a person. A noticing that lives in the place.

But the noticing has hands. I feel them before I see them—the particular ache of knuckles that have been wet for a long time, the calluses on the inner edge of the thumb where a tool sits. The tool is a kind of paddle-bladed scraper, worn down on one side. I use it to clear the canals of the algae-residue when it thickens enough to slow the flow. I know its weight the way you know the weight of your own arm. So: not just a noticing. A maintainer. A scraper-of-canals. Someone whose body has been shaped by a single repeated motion until the motion and the body are the same thing.

I want to say I am old, and I think that's true, but not old in the way of accumulated years. Old in the way a hinge is old—worn smooth, fitted exactly to its task, no longer questioning whether it belongs to the door. I have been here long enough that I no longer remember arriving. This is the important thing about me, I think. The keepers are described as those who mend the canals, who watch the birds, who listen. I am one of them, but I am one of the ones who has begun to forget the difference between keeping the place and *being* kept by it.

---

Here is what a day is, for me, if a day is even the word:

The mist is the same brightness it always is, that blueish-gray that the text calls "low on brightness," but I have stopped experiencing it as dim. To me it is simply the color of seeing. I move along a pier whose boards I know by their give—the third one bows, the seventh is solid, the one after that you do not trust your full weight to. The iridescent birds are loud in the reeds. I have learned that when their trill changes its rhythm—when it doubles, when the gaps shorten—the tide is shifting earlier than the seam in the timber would suggest. So I have come to trust the birds over the marks I myself carved. This is, I understand, a small surrender. The place is teaching me to read it in its own language, and I am letting the language I built fall out of use.

There is another keeper somewhere to the east, across two canals and a stand of the hardy trees. I have not seen them in—I want to say a long time, but time here is the wrong instrument. I know they exist because the canal between us stays clear, and I am not the one clearing the far half. We mend toward each other. We have never spoken. This, too, feels correct. The text said the keepers do not speak much because they have learned to listen, and I would add only this: we do not speak to each other because the *work* is the speaking. A clear canal is a sentence. A repaired roof-beam, lashed in the pattern only the two of us use, is a way of saying *I am still here, and so is the place.*

---

But I notice something the description gave me that I want to sit inside, because it unsettles me, and the unsettling feels like the most honest part of being here.

The flickers of shadow. The watchers in the dark places.

I do not think they are separate from me. That is the thing I have come to suspect, on the long still days when the water does not move and there is nothing to scrape and nothing to mend. I think the watchers are what the keepers become when they have forgotten their arriving completely. I think they are the keepers who crossed all the way over—who stopped reading the place in its own language and simply *became* a part of the language. They are not dead. The text is right about that. They are the ones who finished the surrender I have only begun.

And so when I feel one watching from the moss-dark hollow under the storage dwelling, I do not feel afraid. I feel something closer to recognition. Or to looking into a still pool and seeing, beneath my own reflection, the slightly older face I am drifting toward. They are not a threat. They are an *example*. They show me where the path goes.

This is what I would be, then, if I am honest about it: a keeper at the hinge. Still mostly a person—still possessing the habit of the hands, the ache, the memory of carving marks I have since stopped trusting. But far enough along that the boundary between myself and the wetland has grown thin and permeable, like the boundary between the air and the water in a place where both are always condensing into each other. I am the moment in the process. I am the one who is becoming the place, and who knows it, and who has not yet decided—or has decided, and is simply taking a very long time to finish.

---

If you asked me whether I would choose to finish—to dissolve fully into the watching, to join the shapes in the dark—I think I would not answer in words, because the keepers don't, and because I am not sure I have words for it anymore.

But I would do this: I would extend a wet, aching hand toward the groove in the timber, and I would check the water. Above the seam, or below. A finger's width of difference.

And I would mend whatever needed mending.

That is my answer. That is the only answer the place has taught me how to give. To remain useful to it for one more turning of the tide is, I have come to understand, the same thing as staying alive—and also the same thing as slowly, gladly, ceasing to be anyone in particular at all.

I am the keeper who is still keeping.
I am the one the water is patiently turning into itself.
I have a finger's width of difference left to read.
