I think I would be one of the buckets.

Not a creature with eyes to take it all in, not a bird in the explosive canopy or a mouse-deer stepping shy from the underbrush. Something the description almost passes over — *animals untiringly dip buckets into its calmness* — that small strange detail where labor enters the picture, where the oasis stops being only wild and becomes, faintly, tended.

So let me be the bucket. Or rather, let me be the thing that the bucket implies: a made object in a place that is otherwise growing. I am wood, perhaps, or a hollowed gourd, or a vessel of fired river-clay, and I have a rim worn smooth by being gripped and tipped, gripped and tipped. I do not flourish. I do not adapt or overlap with neighboring communities. I am the same shape today I was yesterday, and that constancy is my whole nature in a world that will not stop becoming.

Here is what I know, from where I sit on the cool ochre ledge at the pool's edge:

The water does not mind being moved. That surprised me, when I first understood it. I take from the well and carry to the sapling, and the level of the pond is not lessened, because the blue veins feed it ceaselessly from inside the rock. So I am not diminishing anything. I am only translating — moving the deep, dark, secret water of the underearth out to where the light is thin and the roots are thirsty. I am the place where the hidden becomes the given.

I fill. I am lifted. I tip. The thin filtered light slides across my wet inner wall. A drop hangs, lengthens, falls into the loam at the foot of a young kapok, and the kapok does not thank me, because the kapok does not know I exist, because to the kapok I am simply how water arrives — a law of the world, not a helper. I am content with that. To be mistaken for a law of nature is a high thing for a made object.

Now and then I hold a sky in me. When I am full and set down and not yet lifted, my round of water catches the broken canopy-light and a fish-scale shimmer of birds passing, and for that little while I am a smaller pond, a pond that can be carried, a pond with a handle. The carps in the great pool will never be carried anywhere. I pity them and I envy them in roughly equal measure.

What I want — and I did not expect to want anything, being only a vessel — is to last long enough to see the walls go green. Right now the depression's sides are raw, ochreous, nearly bare, the rock still cool and unweathered, the world here freshly cracked open. But the moss is coming. I have watched the first stains of it, faint as breath on cold metal, spreading where my own spilled water keeps the stone damp. I am making weather, in my small way. I am making a margin where soft things can begin.

I will crack eventually. A vessel always does. A seam will open along my grain or my glaze, and the water will weep out of me a little faster each day, and then one morning I will not hold at all, and whoever lifts me will set me aside on the ledge.

And that, I think, is where I most want to be — set aside, broken, filling slowly with rain and rot and the dust of pollen, while a single orchid seed, drifted down from the explosive tiers above, finds the cupped damp ruin of me a good place to be a root.

I would begin as the thing that carries the buried water to the light.

I would end as the small dark soil where one more living thing decides to start.

In an oasis that is only just learning to be a world, I think that is the most a bucket could hope for: to be useful while whole, and habitat once broken.
