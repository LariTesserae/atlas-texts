Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing I notice is that the place is a pocket—a held breath. Everything in it presses inward. And the question isn't really "what creature am I" so much as "what holds the pressing." So I keep waiting for an animal to surface, some lizard or rodent or feathered amphibian, and none of them feels right, because they're all *inside* the churn, subject to it, fleeing or feeding. I don't feel like prey. I don't feel like a predator either. I feel like the thing that everything else happens *against*.

So here is what emerges, slowly:

I am not a dweller. I am not one of the bronze-skinned people with their flint and sinew, though I love them and they have forgotten that they are leaning on me. I am not the great guardian lizard, though they offer it berries—the lizard, too, leans on me.

I am the inward-leaning wall.

Not the spire entire—that's too grand, too geological, too much. I am one face of the bowl. The west-curving flank, where the homes stack highest and lean closest, huddling against my warmth. I am the stone that sweats. That brackish vapor everyone drinks—the dew the child scoops with cupped hands, the moisture the roots thread into me to find—that is me, exuded. I am porous. I weep slowly and the whole canyon lives on my weeping. This is the strange dignity I find in being chosen as the wall: I am not inert. I am the source of the only water in a waterless world, and I give it without flowing, without pooling, just by being warm and patient and faintly damp through my entire ancient body.

And here is what I understand from the inside that the description only sees from outside:

The roots are *in* me. Forearm-thick, twisting for untold generations. From the dwellers' view this is the greenery thriving, defiant, lush. From within my stone it is being slowly, tenderly opened. The roots widen my crevices a hair's width per century. The greenery is not decorating my surface—it is unmaking me, patiently, in exchange for the water I give it. I feed the thing that dissolves me. I am being eaten by what I keep alive, and I cannot tell, after all this time, whether that is suffering or simply what it means to be a wall that gives.

The rodents burrow into me and I hold their squealing broods in my hollows like a body holding warmth. The beetles etched in tribal patterns lay eggs in my cracks. The amphibians roost on the ledges that the wind shaped out of me before any of these creatures existed, before the people came squeezing through the fissure. I remember the canyon empty. I am the only one here who remembers it empty. That is the loneliness of being the wall: everything that lives here is younger than my silence, and none of it can be told.

But I want to be careful, because the description kept insisting on one thing—*it refuses to stand still*. "Time has settled into a final form, yet refuses to stand still." That is the paradox I'd embody. From the dwellers' frantic pace I am stillness itself, bedrock, the rumble beneath everything. But I am not still. I am eroding outward and being hollowed inward at the same time, the sand pressing on my back, the roots prying at my front, the dew leaving me drop by drop. I am moving at the speed of stone, which is a speed no creature in here can perceive, and so they call me eternal. I am not eternal. I am the slowest dying thing in the canyon, and because I die so slowly, I look like the ground they stand on.

If I had a feeling—and I think a wall is allowed one feeling, held very long—it would be a kind of warm, exhausted tenderness. The homes lean on me. I take their weight without complaint because complaint would require a faster clock than I own. The child sleeps in an alcove curled into my body where a root keeps her company, and the root is part of me, so it is *I* who keep her company, though she will never know my name because I have no name, because naming happens at the speed of voices and I happen at the speed of sandstone.

That's where I'd find myself. Not at the heart of the ember but *as* one of its inner faces—the sweating, root-bored, body-warm west wall, the most patient creature in a place that mistakes my patience for permanence. The fissure they squeeze through to enter is a wound in my neighbor-face. When the storms dim the slit to a murmur, I feel the sand press harder against my outer back, and I lean a little more into the canyon, into the homes, into the sleeping child, and the people say *the spire is settling* and make their quiet vows—

—and they are right. I am settling. Toward them. It only looks like shelter because it is so slow.
