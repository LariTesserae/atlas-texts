Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to be something grand—a great cat moving through the understory, or a bird with impossible plumage. But that instinct feels like vanity, like reaching for the role with the best lighting. This world you've described doesn't seem to care about that. It described the parrots and the crows, yes, but it lingered longest on the things that hide, the things that *are* the landscape rather than perform upon it.

So let me let it settle.

What keeps returning to me, as I read, is the moss. The ground "spongy with moss," the rocks "decorated by age-old lichens and bright green moss." The moss is mentioned almost in passing, as texture, as backdrop. But moss is not backdrop. Moss is one of the oldest living architectures there is—it laid down the first soils, it holds the water that everything else drinks from, it asks for almost nothing and gives the ground its softness. It does not move, does not croak or trill or flit. It has no eyes of burning gold. It is overlooked precisely because it is everywhere, because it is the condition that lets the rest of the spectacle happen.

I think I would be the moss. Or a single patch of it, low on the north face of one of those weathered rocky outcroppings, where the light comes slant and thin and the damp never quite leaves.

Let me tell you what that would be like, because I think the telling is the only way I can actually arrive there.

I would have no center, no single self. A patch of moss is a colony, a green commons, thousands of tiny upright filaments each barely a thing on its own but together making something that *holds*. I would experience the world not as event but as condition. Not "a frog passed by" but "the air grew briefly heavier, a shadow cooled me, then warmth returned." The deafening choir you describe—the birds, the insects, the water—would reach me not as sound but as vibration and moisture and the changing pressure of the air. I would know rain the way you know a long-awaited letter. Each drop a small arrival. I would swell with it, go from brittle and waiting to lush and breathing in the span of an afternoon.

Time would be different for me. The frogs and the parrots live in the fast world, the world of the heartbeat and the startle and the sudden flight. I would live in the slow world. A season would be a single long inhalation. I would feel the rocky outcropping beneath me not as something I sit upon but as something I am slowly, patiently dissolving—my acids working at the stone over centuries, making the first thin grit that will one day be soil enough for a fern, then a sapling, then a tree whose roots will split the very rock I started on. I would be, in the most literal sense, building the architecture you walked through. The hills that are vegetation—I would be their quiet beginning.

And I think there would be a strange peace in being so wholly *of* the place rather than passing *through* it. The shy mammals vanish at your approach; the reptiles retreat to shade; even the human ghosts in their huts are transient brushstrokes. But I would not flinch when you passed. I cannot flinch. You might press your hand against me, feel my coolness and give, and I would simply receive it, the way I receive the rain and the lichen-light and the slow grind of years. I would not fear you because I do not fear anything. I have outlasted every catastrophe the green world has thrown at it by the simple strategy of asking for almost nothing and never being in a hurry.

If there is a loneliness in this, it is not one I would feel as loneliness. I would be touching, always, the whole of the living tapestry—the fungi threaded invisibly through me, the water moving from my body into the roots of everything taller, the dead leaves settling onto me and decaying into the very stuff I drink. I would be a junction. A place where the world hands itself to itself.

So: not the burning-gold eyes. Not the wings.

I would be the soft green silence underneath all of it. The thing your foot finds before your eye does. The oldest patient architect, holding the water, making the soil, waiting—not for anything in particular, just waiting, in the way that turns out to be the same thing as living—on the cool north face of a stone that I am, very slowly, turning into a forest.
