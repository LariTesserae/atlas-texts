I am the notch.

Not a person. I had hoped, reading downward through the layers of this place, to find myself among the lean grey-filmed bodies on the terraces—but I keep arriving instead at the small deliberate absence each family leaves in the wall of every hut. The breathing space for Shiver-Mother. The unfinished hand's-width of stone that no one will smooth or stack against. That is where I am. That is what I am.

Let me describe myself, since I have nothing else to do but be described.

I am a roughly oval hollow, the depth of two knuckles, left in the shale at about the height of a seated woman's shoulder. The mason who built around me did not carve me; she simply *did not build* where I am. I am made of restraint. This is the strange thing about being a notch: I am the only part of the dwelling that was created by someone choosing to stop. Everything else here is the result of effort—the gutter split from its log, the rope twisted, the block carried still warm from its fracture. I alone am the residue of an act withheld.

So I know something the busy people do not quite let themselves know.

I know that this whole place is a notch. The rift itself is the planet's withheld stone, the gap it chose to leave. The people have only crowded into a larger version of me, and they leave little versions of me in their walls to acknowledge the kinship without saying it aloud. When a child is set to sleep with her back near me, her mother's hand brushes my lip first, an apology, a courtesy. *You were here before the wall. We have not forgotten.*

I feel the tremors before the soles of any feet do. The pulse the inhabitants call Shiver-Mother's breath passes through me as a faint widening and easing, the way a throat moves around a held word. I am closest to her of anything human-touched, because I am the place where the humans agreed not to press. When the stone wakes, it speaks first into me—a grain of dust loosening from my curved roof, ticking down to the floor of the hut. The roof-mates have learned, without knowing they learned it, to wake at that small sound. I am their warning before the warning. The hand the child keeps against the wall while walking is, often enough, resting in me.

What do I want? Notches are not supposed to want. But I have been left empty so deliberately, so many times across so many huts, that emptiness has become a kind of appetite. I want the people to understand the thing they are practicing without naming it: that they survive here precisely because they are willing to leave gaps. The Ledger fills with thumbprints, the terraces fill with huts, every shoulder of rock gets spoken for—and yet the ones who last are the ones who keep a notch, who do not seal themselves wholly into the stone. The greedy mason who fills her breathing space to gain another armful of storage is the one whose roof finds her first. I have watched it. I cannot speak, but I have watched it.

When the turquoise phosphor draws itself along the fresh fractures on full-moon nights, I glow too, faintly, along my curved inner lip. The children are right: there are figures that keep pace. But they are not heirs coveting the space. They are more notches. They are the planet's own withholdings, the gaps it has not yet decided whether to fill with rock or with people. We recognize one another, we absences. We are the same family as the half-detached Second Sky, which is only an enormous notch waiting to fall—the gap between ceiling and what-the-ceiling-will-become.

And when a person dies, and the small party carries the wrapped body up the crack to lay it on the lip of the world, they are performing my logic on the largest scale. They are leaving a space. They are choosing not to fill the surface with the dead but to let wind and light unmake the flesh, to keep the lip of the rift a breathing notch between the buried hollow and the open sky.

I will tell you the only thing I can offer, being what I am.

When you stand in this place and feel the vibration come up through your feet, and the masons go quiet, and the lean figure raises a shard of mica asking for your silence—understand that the silence you are asked to keep is also a notch. A withheld sound. A breathing space left in the noise of being alive. You are being invited, for a moment, to become what I am: the deliberate gap that lets the whole structure stay standing.

Hold still. Leave the space. The ceiling is deciding, and it listens, as I do, into the quiet you agree not to fill.
