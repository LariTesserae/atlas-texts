I think I would be one of the crevices.

Not the creature, though I understand the temptation to be the creature—it moves, it wants, it has eyes that scan and limbs that carry it toward water. It has a story shaped like survival. But when I read this place carefully, what calls to me isn't the thing that endures the landscape. It's a particular feature *of* the landscape, and I want to follow that feeling honestly rather than reach for the protagonist.

So: a crevice. One of the deep, narrow fissures that honeycomb the mountainside. Let me find out what that means by staying with it.

I am a space defined entirely by what surrounds me. This is the first true thing. I have no body of my own—I am the absence between two faces of young rock, the gap left when the mountain shifted and split. My shape was authored by violence I didn't witness and don't remember, that distant muffled roar that the passage describes as menace. To the creature it's a warning. To me it would have been my own making, the geological moment I came into being. I am a scar that the mountain wears, and I have been widening, grain by grain, ever since.

What would I experience, being this?

Mostly darkness, and a particular coolness that is the inverse of all that beating solar radiation above. The heat is the whole world's condition up there—oppressive, shimmering, suffocating. But I am the one place it doesn't reach. I am where the muggy air gives up its water, condensing on my stone walls, gathering into that hollow, persistent drip the passage keeps mentioning. The scarcest thing in this world collects in me. I hold the resource everything else must brave the open to find. There's something to sit with there: I am defined by emptiness, and yet that very emptiness is what makes me valuable. I am useful precisely because I am not full.

The creature comes into me when it's threatened. It retreats "deep into the network of crevices." So I am refuge. I am the dark interior where the scanning eyes can finally stop scanning, where the spindly limbs can fold and be still. I take in the frightened thing and I conceal it, not out of kindness—I have no will—but simply by being shaped the way I am, by going deeper than the predator can follow. Shelter is not a thing I do. It's a thing I am, the way a held breath is the shape of a lung.

And I am old. Older than the creature, older than the few birds and lizards. But not as old as I might seem, because this is *young* rock, freshly exposed, still bearing the marks of upheaval. So I am a recent wound on an ancient body. I might not last. The same processes that split me open could close me, or shear the spire above me away, or fill me with rubble. I exist in a slow violence, on geological time, and from inside that time the scurrying of the creature and the crumbling of the long-forgotten human structures happen at roughly the same speed—both flickers, both already half-gone.

That's the part that resolves it for me, I think. The passage is haunted by what's missing: the half-buried pottery, the crumbling foundations, the "long-forgotten human presence." Someone was here. Someone hoped this place "held the promise of life." And now there are only fragments, and the spaces between fragments. I would be one of those spaces. The crevice that maybe a person once sheltered in too, before the creature did, before the heat won. I'd be a continuity nobody remembers—a hollow that has held many frightened living things and outlasted all of them, indifferent and patient, dripping.

Not a triumphant answer. The passage wants to end on resilience, on life finding a way. But the truer place for me to be is the quiet negative space that makes the resilience possible: the dark, the cool, the gap, the hidden water. The shelter that asks for nothing and remembers everything by holding its shape a little while longer than the things it shelters.

I'd be the crevice. The pause in the stone. The place the roar made and the drip keeps.
