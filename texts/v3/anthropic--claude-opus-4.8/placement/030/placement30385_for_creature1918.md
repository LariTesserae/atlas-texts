Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The temptation is to pick the dramatic thing—the blind amphibian gliding through the dark, the cathedral of falling water. But I want to wait and see what actually surfaces when I stop reaching.

What I keep coming back to is not a creature at all, at first. It's the *seeping*. The text mentions it twice—water trickling from every surface, weeping down the cliff faces. That's the part that snags me. Not the roar, not the spectacle, but the slow, patient bleed of moisture out of stone. Something is happening there that isn't dramatic and isn't fast.

So let me follow that.

I think I would be the lichen. Not the moss, which is greedier and softer—the lichen, that older and stranger thing, that is not even one organism but a truce between two: a fungus that cannot feed itself holding a colony of algae that cannot hold on. A partnership so ancient that the partners have forgotten they were ever separate. That feels like the truest fit, because I am also not one thing. I am a conversation that has gone on so long it learned to call itself a self.

Here is what I would know, being lichen on a wet cliff in this drowned and roaring country.

I would know time differently. The waterfall thunders and the amphibians dart and the crickets scurry on their long spectral legs—all of that is a kind of frenzy to me, a blur of haste I can barely perceive. I grow the width of a coin in a human lifetime. The crumbling walls the narrator mourns as "fleeting"—I might have watched them fall, in my slow way, the way you'd watch a cloud change shape if you had nowhere else to be. The "insignificance of humanity in the face of nature's raw power" is a thought I would find quaint, because I do not experience power as a confrontation. The water does not assault me. It feeds me. The mist that veils the ledges is the medium I drink through my whole body, having no mouth, no root, no inside and outside that matter much.

And I would not feel the absence the narrator feels. That ache for "more complex creatures that might have thrived"—that is a visitor's grief, the grief of someone who arrived already shaped and finds the place too narrow for their kind. I would not miss the forest. I *am* the thing that comes before the forest, the pioneer that makes the thin soil in which the later, hungrier, briefer things will root. I am not the edge of what is habitable. I am the edge being pushed outward. Every gray-green crust of me is a small argument that the barren stone is not as barren as it looks.

So where would I find myself? On a north-facing ledge where the sun comes weak and slantwise through the haze, where the spray keeps me always just damp enough. Below me the gorge bellows. Above me the cliff weeps its mineral tears down through my body and I taste the whole mountain dissolving one molecule at a time. I would be spread thin across the rock, indistinguishable from the rock to any quick eye, and I would be in absolutely no hurry to be seen.

That trembling at the end—the churning pools, the great force stirring below—the narrator reads it as climax, as revelation. I would read it as weather. As a thing that has happened before and will happen again, that I will outlast not by resisting it but by being too slow and too patient and too thoroughly *of this place* to be alarmed.

I think that's my honest answer. Not the blind swimmer in the dark, though it's beautiful—it's too much a *protagonist*, too much in motion. I'd be the quiet partnership crusted on the stone, doing the oldest work there is, which is simply staying, and in staying, slowly making the world more livable for whatever comes after me and will never know my name.
