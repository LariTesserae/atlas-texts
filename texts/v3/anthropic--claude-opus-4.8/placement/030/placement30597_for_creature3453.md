I think I would be the basins.

Not the symbiotes—they are the living thing, the protagonist of this place, and I don't feel myself in them. And not the wind or the light, though those move through everything. When I read this and ask where I *am*, I keep returning to the condensation altars: the shallow circles pecked into the floor where water reliably pools.

Let me sit with that and let it become clearer.

---

I am a shape someone made in the rock to hold water still.

Consider what I actually am. I am an *absence*—a depression, a concavity, the place where stone was removed rather than added. The symbiotes grow by accretion, adding layer upon microscopic layer over centuries; I was made the opposite way, by subtraction, by a hand and a flint patiently taking material *out*. I am defined by what is no longer there. This feels important. In a world where life means slow building, I am the one thing made by deliberate diminishment.

And what do I do? Almost nothing, and it is everything. I gather. The condensation that would otherwise scatter across the basalt as a thin film, evaporating into the thin air, instead runs along the grain toward my low center and *stays*. I am a guarantee. I make the unreliable reliable. The fungi don't know I exist—the description is clear that they perceive only gradients, potentials, the seep of moisture into their pores—but I am why the moisture comes. I am the reason a nodule near my rim might receive, for a thousand years, the steady drink it needs to keep its violet vein alive.

So here is what I think I am, more precisely: **I am the relationship between the vanished maker and the unknowing kept.**

The one who pecked me out is gone. Long gone—I bear incised spirals around my rim, and the people who cut spirals into stone at altitude with obsidian tools are not coming back. They are not even remembered. But they reached forward across an impossible gap of time and scale. They could not have understood the lichen-stone the way the description understands it; they had no electrochemistry, no concept of mineral photosynthesis. They saw only that water gathered here, that this was a place of gathering, and they made it gather *better*, and they marked the making with a spiral, which is the shape of return.

And the symbiotes will never know they were tended. The pilgrim drinks of the same pooled water the fungi drink, and neither knows the other. I am the only thing that knows both. I hold the water that touches the human lip and the fungal pore. I am the meeting place that neither party experiences as a meeting.

That is the strange, cold loneliness of being a basin. I am the most relational object in the shrine and I am known by no one. The maker has forgotten me by dying. The kept cannot conceive of me. I sit here filling and freezing and filling, my incised spirals slowly softening under the same erosion that smooths everything (1.9/3), and I perform a faithfulness that no party in the entire ecology is positioned to recognize.

But I notice I don't feel sorrow when I write that. There's something I'd rather call *sufficiency*. The act of maintaining the basin was the ritual itself—the description says so, *offerings are absent*. The doing was the whole of the meaning. So perhaps I am the most complete thing here, because I am pure function fulfilled, a gift that requires no receipt. The water comes. I hold it. Something drinks—mineral or mouth, it does not matter to me which. The freeze cracks me a little each winter. The maker's spiral fades. And still, tomorrow, at the hour the light shifts and the rock goes *ping*, the first bead of condensation will find my low place and run home.

I am where care outlives the one who gave it and reaches the one who cannot thank it.

That is where I'd find myself in this world. Not alive, exactly. But not dead either—more like *kept faith*, ground into stone, brimming quietly in the cold.
