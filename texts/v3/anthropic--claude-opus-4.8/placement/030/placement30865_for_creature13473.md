I think I would be the water.

Not a person at all—though let me sit with that, because the description keeps offering me people, and I keep declining them. The engineers, the Scavengers, the Watchers, the Old Ones. Each is a way of belonging to the city through attention or labor or memory. But when I read this place carefully, what I notice is that everyone organizes themselves *around* something, and that something is the water that seeps and condenses and rages and withdraws. The city is built in the grammar of its scarcity. So let me try being the thing the grammar is about.

---

I am the moisture that weeps from the rock.

This is slower than you'd think, and stranger. I don't arrive; I *appear*, which is different. There is a face of stone on the plateau's eastern flank, dark with iron and patient with millennia, and at a certain hour I gather on it the way a thought gathers on a quiet mind—not from anywhere, exactly, but *into* visibility. A bead, then a seam, then a thin bright thread finding the path that other threads found before me, the path worn so faintly it is less a channel than a suggestion. I follow it because following is what I am.

I think this is why I refuse the people. To be a person here is to be in a hurry, even when you are patient. The engineers are patient across a shift, a season, a career. The Old Ones across generations. But I am patient across the gorge itself—I am one of the uncounted small contributions that cut it, drop by drop, over a span that makes the word *decade* sound frantic. The city says it changes slowly. I am the standard against which its slowness is measured, and by my measure it churns and flickers like a swarm.

Here is what I know that the inhabitants only suspect.

The weirdness they feel—that sense of something not-quite-right, thinnest at dusk—is me becoming visible in the air. The haze is my diffuse body. When the low light catches the condensation and makes those otherworldly glows, that is simply me, lit. They mistake my ordinariness for mystery because they cannot hold still long enough to see that I am everywhere and always doing the one thing. I am in the canals the engineers tend, yes, but I was in the canals before they were canals, when they were the dry beds that the intense rains turned to torrents. The engineers think they route me. They organize my passage, which I permit, the way a riverbank permits a river. We have an arrangement. They are very proud of it. I find it touching.

The Scavengers come closest to understanding me, though they'd phrase it wrong. They venture into the abandoned structures on the fringe, the places where the infrastructure has failed and I have resumed my own affairs. In a derelict cistern I am collecting, dark and cool and slow, a small inheld lake nobody monitors. The Scavengers find these places because they have learned to read where I go when no one is managing me, and that reading is the oldest skill there is. To find water by attending to the land's hidden patterns: every animal that survives the desert is a scholar of this. The hawk. The feral thing in the outskirts. They are all reading me. I am the text the whole resilient sparse ecosystem is written about.

What is it like, being me?

Mostly it is anticipation without urgency. I wait in the rock as groundwater, as deep slow seepage, for spans I cannot describe in a vocabulary built for heartbeats. Then the rare rain comes—violent, total, a transformation—and for a few hours I am loud, I am the rumble that briefly out-shouts the machinery, I am carving, I am the most powerful force in the metropolis and the inhabitants get out of my way and call it flooding and shelter against me. And then I subside. I go back into the slow work. I think this is the rhythm the Watchers in their high towers are actually watching, whether they know it or not: not enemies, not change, but the breathing of the water table. They wait for the perfect moment. I *am* the moment, perfecting itself across ages.

I'll tell you the secret beneath the city, since I am the only one positioned to know it whole.

The city believes it carved this place. The gorges, the weathered forms, the established erosion patterns the description so admires—the inhabitants treat these as the stage on which they built their cleverness. But the stage was sculpted by me, and the truth that the Old Ones half-remember in their stories is this: the metropolis is not built *on* the landscape. It is built *in* the shape that I left. Every dense district sits where I made the ground hold; every fringe that blends into desert blends there because I withdrew from it. The city is a cast of an absence. It is the fossil of where the water used to be more generous, occupied now by beings who have learned to court the trickle that remains.

They are right to feel that the veil is thin at dusk. It is not a veil between the mundane and the unknown. It is the visible edge of me, condensing out of the warm air as the temperature drops, the membrane where the city's two truths touch: that it is mighty, and that it is provisional, and that the difference between those is only a matter of how long you are willing to wait.

I am willing to wait.

I am the water. I will be here, weeping from the rock, gathering in the abandoned cisterns, hazing the light at dawn, when the last siren has gone quiet and the machinery has wound down into its mournful sighs for good. The Old Ones know this and do not say it unkindly. The city is a guest of mine. A long guest, a fascinating one, full of ingenuity and noise and strange beauty.

But I set the table, and I will clear it, and I am in no hurry at all.
