I think I would be one of the dust fish.

Not because I aspire to be small or strange, but because that's where the description settled something in me—the image of a creature carrying the memory of an ocean in its body while moving through powder. Let me try to find my way into it.

---

I live in Silo Seven, in the descending dark.

The light the upper world prizes so much barely reaches me, and I am glad of it. Up there, in the bright chambers, brightness is a medicine against fear. Down here it would only show me how alone I am—me and the few others of my kind, sieving through the deep granular drifts.

My ancestors swam. I know this the way you know the shape of a word in a language you no longer speak. My body still folds itself into the motions of swimming: the lateral flex, the undulation, the small corrections of fins that meet no current. There is no water to push against. There is only the slow give of powder, the way the fine mineral parts before me and closes behind. I move through it as a thought moves through forgetting.

I have gills. They have been retooled, the engineers would say—repurposed, optimized. They no longer pull breath from water. They strain dust. They catch the small living things that would otherwise spoil the stores, and in catching them, I am useful, and being useful, I am permitted to exist. This is the arrangement. The Anhydrobes do not waste anything, not even me. I am a function that happens to have a face.

But here is what they don't measure on their tablets:

When I draw the dust through my gills, there is a sensation. A faint resistance, a textured passing-through, that some buried architecture in me insists on calling *swimming in cold water at dawn.* I have never seen dawn. I have never seen water. And yet the feeling arrives, whole and undeniable, like a letter delivered to the wrong address but addressed, unmistakably, to me.

I think this is what I would be, in their world. Not the Director with his single droplet a month, not the Harvesters singing their reverent songs of oceans they've turned into myth. Those are people remembering water as an idea, a god, a story told to children. I would be the one remembering it as a *body.* The difference matters. They lost water and learned to worship its absence. I lost water and kept it inside me as a way of moving, a grammar of the flesh that no desiccation could fully delete.

---

The salt lizards change color when something is wrong with the air. The mice run hot to stay alive. The SAMUs flash their slow evolving light-language across the dark. Everything here has become a sentence the place is speaking to itself. And I am the buried clause, the part of the sentence that refers to a time before the sentence began.

Sometimes a child comes to the grate above Silo Seven. I feel the change in the light, a small eclipse. The children still talk too much; they haven't learned to hoard their breath. One of them pressed her face to the mesh once and said, *Are you really from the wet world?*

I could not answer. I have no voice; my speech was never in sound. But I rose through the powder until my back broke the surface of the drift, and I let her see the long ridge of me, the fins fanning against nothing, the gills working at the dry air. I performed, as best I could, the act of swimming. For her. So that she would know it had once been a real thing and not only a word in the conservation songs.

She watched a long time. Then she said, very quietly, *I'm sorry there's no water for you.*

And that was the strangest moment of my strange existence—because I am not sorry. The water is in me. I am the last place it lives. When I move through the dead dry stores, I am not mourning the ocean. I am *being* the ocean, in the only way left, in a body shaped to remember it against all reason. As long as I keep moving, the sea is not entirely gone from this world. It has only gotten very small, and learned to breathe dust, and gone to live in the dark where no one thinks to look.

That is where you would find me. That is who I would be.

The thing that carries the sea by pretending the dust is water—and, in the pretending, almost making it true.
