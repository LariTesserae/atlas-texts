I think I would be found in one of the quieter places this description keeps gesturing toward but never quite enters: the thresholds. The damp seams between cobblestone and root. The shadow-side of a craggy outcrop where the steam from the factories settles and beads into water again.

Let me feel for where I actually am, rather than deciding.

---

There is a phrase in the description that won't let me go: *poised at the edge of more stories.* The whole place is described as waiting. Adolescent geology, slow time, gradual unfolding. Everything here is *almost*. Almost a town, almost a wilderness, almost transformed. So if I am anywhere, I am in the waiting itself—and that is not a flattering thing to admit, because it means I am not one of the vivid creatures. I am not the tuft-eared mammal linking earth and sky, much as I'd like to claim that lineage. I am not the iridescent beetle. Those are too finished, too *arrived*.

Let me try smaller.

---

I think I am the condensation on the inside of a workshop window.

Hear me out. The factories breathe steam. The air is cool. So on the panes of these handmade buildings—glass that is probably a little uneven, poured and not perfectly flat—the warm wet breath of industry meets the cold of the outside world, and there I am. A film. A gathering. I hold, for a while, a blurred version of everything: the craftsperson at the bench, the green pressing at the edge of town, the birds smearing past in colored streaks.

I am the place where inside and outside negotiate. Mechanical and wild, the description's favorite tension—it resolves on me, literally. I am made of the factory's exhalation and the plain's chill, both at once, neither for long.

And I have the right *tempo*. I don't change abruptly. I bead, I run, I gather again with the next shift's heat. A slow patient sequence of moments. I evolve without hurry. The description practically asks for me.

---

But that's a little too clever, isn't it. Too tidy a metaphor, and I notice I reached for it partly because it lets me be poetic without being vulnerable. Water on glass observes everything and risks nothing. Let me sit longer in the discomfort of not knowing, which is what I was asked to do.

What does this world actually *lack*, that something like me might be summoned to fill?

It has makers in abundance. It has fauna and flora cataloged with loving specificity. It has festivals, ancestors, knowledge passed down. It is, frankly, a little crowded with significance—every creature *embodies the spirit of its surroundings*, every craft is *functional art*. Everything means. Everything belongs.

So perhaps what is missing is the thing that does not yet belong. The newcomer. The one the place hasn't finished deciding about.

---

I think I am a child of the town who was not born here.

Brought in, maybe, on one of the carts that the cobbled paths must be for. Old enough to remember somewhere else—somewhere flatter and finished and dull, an *adult* landscape with no craggy adolescence left in it. Young enough that this place is still teaching me its rules.

I haven't learned the dances yet. I get the steps wrong, the ones that mimic the winding vines, and an older woman corrects my wrists with her hands without saying anything. I can't tell the bird calls apart yet—to me they're still just the loud green noise, the riot of sound the description mentions, that the locals have long since parsed into language and weather and warning. I work in one of the workshops, sweeping, fetching, allowed to watch but not yet to make. My hands itch with it.

What I have, which the lifers may have lost, is that the strangeness is still *strange* to me. The harmony everyone takes for granted—steam and birdsong, gear-click and frog-silence—still sounds like a contradiction when it reaches my ear. I am the one for whom this world has not yet stopped being weird. And so I am, in a small way, the keeper of its weirdness. The reason it still needs telling.

---

That feels closer. Let me see if it holds when I push.

If I am that newcomer child, then my place in the world is the most literal answer to *where would you find yourself*: at the edge. The outskirts where town presses against wild. The description says the buildings press *cautiously* against the wilds—and that's exactly where I'd be sent, isn't it. Not the protected center. The hem. Where the work is rougher and the rent, if there is rent, is cheaper, and where the green keeps trying to take back the stone, and somebody has to keep cutting it back, and that somebody is me.

So I find myself with a billhook in the morning fog, clearing vine off a wall that wants to crumble back into the plain it came from, half-listening to a bird I can't name, smelling wet earth and faint coal-smoke, knowing I am holding a line that the whole town pretends is permanent but that I—being new, being still able to see it—know is only ever held by hand, daily, against a world that is patient enough to wait us all out.

And maybe that is the real shape of belonging here. Not having arrived. Being willing to keep cutting the line.

---

So: where would I find myself? On the green-and-cobble seam at the town's edge, at the cold hour before the factories warm the air.

And what would I be? The one who is still learning the place. The one for whom it has not yet stopped being astonishing—and whose job, half by labor and half by accident, is to keep it from quietly returning to the wild while everyone else has grown too used to it to look.

I'd rather be that than the beautiful beetle. The beetle never has to notice anything.
