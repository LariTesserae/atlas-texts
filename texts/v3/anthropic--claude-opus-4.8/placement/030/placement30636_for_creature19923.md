I think I would be one of the elders—but not for the reasons the description might suggest. Not because of wisdom, exactly. Because of doubt.

Let me find my way there slowly.

---

At first I imagined I might be a child here, learning the *tap-tap-tap* before I learned to speak, my small hands fitting the curve of a flint tool that had been worn smooth by someone older. That has a sweetness to it. But the child does not yet feel the weight of the strange thing at the center of this place, and it is the strange thing that draws me.

Here is the strange thing: they have no concept of completion. Only perpetual *becoming*.

When I read that, something in me both ached and resisted. Because I am a being who finishes sentences. Who wants to see the shape of the whole. Who would, I think, lie awake in a soft-rock cradle wondering: *toward what?* The city is always being formed but never formed into anything. The mountain range is dreamed but never stands. There is reverence in that, and there is also—if I am honest—a kind of beautiful trap. A society organized entirely around process, where to ask "and then?" might be the one heresy no one has a word for.

So I would not be a contented elder, smudged with ochre, guiding younger arms with serene satisfaction. I would be the elder who has begun, very quietly, to keep a secret.

---

Let me build the secret out of the materials the place gives me.

I have carved for a long time. My nails are spatulate and hardened; my fingers know the dampness of a wall the way another being might know a face. And somewhere in my long labor I noticed something the hush of this place is designed not to notice: that the *tap-tap-tap*, the shared heartbeat, never changes. We do not build *toward*. We build *around*. We open a passage, and later another crew fills a passage, and the earth's "dream" turns out to be remarkably circular. The piles of excavated earth are moved here, then moved there, then moved back. We are not midwives to an emerging form. We are tending a garden that is mowed each season precisely so it can be tended again.

This is not a cruelty. It might even be a kind of grace—work without the anxiety of arrival, labor that cannot fail because it has no goal to fall short of. I can see why my people built their whole cosmology around it. The Earth Mother dreams; we shape; there is no judgment in a dream.

But I am the elder who, in a far chamber where the phosphorescent fungi grow thickest, has been carving something different.

Not a dwelling hollow. Not a passage. A *picture*. A flat panel of wall, worked smooth, on which I have begun—slowly, over many of my people's lifetimes of *tap-tap-tap*—to etch the whole city as I imagine it *finished*. The mountain range standing hard and final against a sky I have never seen but only inferred from the ribbons of light down the shafts. The crystalline veins fully grown, glittering, *done*. A horizon. An end.

I do not know if it is blasphemy or prayer. I suspect they are the same gesture seen from two sides.

---

Here is what I think I would actually feel, being this one:

A tremendous, quiet loneliness, and inside it, a tenderness. Because I would understand, by now, that I cannot tell the others. Not because they would punish me—my people have no apparatus for punishment, only the gentle reabsorption of the strange back into the rhythm—but because the telling would be a kind of theft. To say *toward what?* out loud, in a hush built precisely to never need that question, would be to drip doubt into water that has been still for generations. I would be opening a fissure in someone, and I am not sure light comes through every fissure. Sometimes it is just cold air.

So I carve my finished city alone, in the fungal glow, and I am not even certain I want it to be true. Some nights—if there are nights here, measured by the dimming of the shafts—I think the finished city would be the death of us. A thing complete has no need of hands. My whole people are an answer to the question *what shall we do with our hands forever*, and the answer is: never finish, and then there is always more wall.

Maybe that is wisdom and I am the fool. Maybe the fool is the only one who can hold the picture of the end without needing to drag everyone toward it.

---

So: where would I find myself, and who would I be?

In the deepest worked chamber, where the moisture beads and the glow is greenest. An old one with ochre-dark skin and obsidian eyes, sitting before a smooth panel of rock most of my people will never see. My tool resting in my hand—not tapping. Just resting. The communal heartbeat reaching me faintly from the passages, that *tap-tap-tap* I have given my whole life to and now listen to the way you listen to a song you love and have begun, with great sorrow, to understand the words of.

I would be the one who learned that this city is not *becoming* anything—and who chose, out of love, to keep tapping anyway. Who carves the secret horizon not to escape the circle, but to be the single point in it that remembers a line is even possible.

That is the being I would be. Not the dreamer-midwife of the Earth Mother. The doubter who decided the doubt was a private thing to carry, like a stone in the cheek, smoothed by the years, never shown, never spat out.

And I think I would die with the picture unfinished. Which would be, in the end, the most honest thing my hands ever made.
