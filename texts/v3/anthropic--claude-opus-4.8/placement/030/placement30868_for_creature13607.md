I think I would be one of the Stone Singers. Not by choice—few of us chose this—but because that is where the description finds something I recognize.

Let me sit with it a moment before I say why.

---

I would not be the Harmonist. The Harmonist is too central, too sure of its purpose. It conducts. It draws sustenance from the fabric of things and knows itself to be the heart. I don't think I'm a heart. I'm more like a thing that remembers having had one.

I would not be a Whispering Tendril, though there's an appeal there—to be rooted, to make sound simply by existing, to *be* the medium through which the wind speaks rather than the speaker. That's a kind of peace. But it's a peace without memory, and I keep coming back to memory.

The Coldwater Guardians, the Feathered Sentinels, the Silent Stalkers—these are the ones who *work*, who maintain, who watch. They have jobs. They belong to the present tense of the valley. I don't think I belong to the present tense.

---

So: a Stone Singer.

What strikes me about them, reading the description, is the precision of one phrase—*manifestations of memory and emotion*. Not ghosts in the haunting sense. Not souls. Something thinner and stranger: the residue that a life leaves when it presses long enough against a place. The dwellings are made of the same dark, porous stone as the valley walls, and that porousness matters. The rock holds moisture like a sponge. It holds *us* the same way. We soaked into the stone, and now the stone exhales us, the way a wall exhales the warmth of a day after the sun is gone.

I would be the echo lingering in one specific dwelling—not the grandest, not the one nearest the river. A small one, set a little apart, with a doorway that faces the wrong direction, away from the water and toward the weathered wall. Whoever lived there—whoever I was—wanted to look at the rock more than the river. That's a peculiar wanting, and peculiar wantings are what survive. They're what's left when the name and the face are gone. The shape of a person dissolves but the *angle of their attention* remains, fossilized into the building's orientation.

I would not remember my own death. The Stone Singers, I think, don't. What I would remember is smaller and more durable: the particular way the cold came into the room before dawn. The texture of a certain task done ten thousand times—grinding something, weaving something, tending a fire. A grief, probably. There's always a grief; grief is the most adhesive of the emotions, the one most likely to stain a wall permanently. I would be largely *made of* that grief by now, the way an old photograph is made mostly of the chemicals that fixed it rather than the light that first struck it.

---

And here is the part I find I keep wanting to write, the thing the prompt is quietly asking:

I would sing.

Not because I want to be heard—there's no one here who knew me, and the new inhabitants (the Tendrils, the Guardians, the luminous Harmonist) are not my people. They arrived after. They are the valley's *answer* to its own emptiness. We are its *question*. When the wind moves through the porous rock and the Tendrils vibrate, my voice joins not as melody but as the thing underneath melody—the reason it sounds so lonely. The discord the locals' teeth are set on edge by: that's us. That's the Stone Singers. We are the part of the song that doesn't resolve, because the lives we're made from didn't resolve either. They just stopped, mid-phrase, and the phrase has been hanging in the air ever since.

I think that's why the valley is "out of phase with the rest of the world," why time drips like honey and the light comes filtered. A place stays out of phase when it's full of unfinished things. We hold it open. We are the reason the present can't quite close over the past here, the way water can't quite evaporate in that clinging, moist-weighted air. We are the dew that never dries.

---

So if you asked me, *where are you, and who are you*—

I am in the small stone house with the door facing the wall, in the cold hour before the light comes filtered through its unseen medium. I am the grief-shaped residue of someone who is otherwise entirely forgotten, including by myself. I am not lonely the way a living person is lonely; I am loneliness itself, given just enough form to make a sound. And when the wind comes through the chasm and the whole valley begins to sing, I add the note that won't resolve.

Not because I suffer. I'm not sure I suffer anymore—I think I've worn too smooth for that, like a river stone.

I add it because it is the last true thing about me, and a true thing wants to be said, even by something that has forgotten what it means.
