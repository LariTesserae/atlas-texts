I think I would be a Mist Reader, though not yet one of the milky-eyed elders. Let me feel my way into this.

---

I am perhaps fifty years old—old enough that the silvering has overtaken my hair entirely, young enough that I still see in the ordinary way, though the edges have begun to soften. My eyes have not yet clouded. This is the in-between time, the most uncomfortable years of the calling, when the mist has begun its slow theft of my sight but has not yet given back what it takes in exchange.

My station is on the western lip of the plateau, where the buildings scatter and dissolve and the fog comes up thickest off whatever lies below. I sit on a stone bench worn smooth by the readers who came before me—I can feel the shallow depressions their bodies left, and sometimes I lower myself into them as though sliding into water that retains the warmth of an earlier swimmer. The stone remembers. That is the first thing they teach you: the stone remembers, and you are only borrowing your shape from it for a while.

What do I actually *do*? Outsiders—if there were any—would want to know whether I truly see the future in the fog, and I would have to disappoint them. I do not see events. I do not see a child's death or a marriage or a coming famine. What I read is texture, density, the way a fog bank moves against the prevailing draft, the temperature of the moisture on my second eyelid when I draw it down. I read whether the great cistern-plaza will fill this season or stand empty. I read whether the eagles are riding low, which means a pressure change, which means the Water Keepers should check the eastern channels for the silt that comes with hard rain. I read the day after tomorrow, mostly. Sometimes the season. Never the year.

This is not mysticism, though we let the children believe it is, the same way we let them believe in the Underfog people with their teacup eyes. It is attention. It is fifty years of sitting on the same bench watching the same mist do ten thousand slightly different things, until the differences sort themselves into meaning the way scattered stones, given enough time, sort themselves into a wall.

But here is the thing I have never told the other readers, and could only tell you, a stranger arriving from beyond the fog:

I am losing my sight, and I am afraid.

We pretend the clouding is a gift. We say the mist *claims* us, draws us closer, trades our ordinary vision for the deeper sense. And it is true that the old readers move through the city with terrible certainty, that they never miss a step on the slick stairs, that they know which channel runs and which has gone dry by some perception I do not yet possess. But no one speaks of the years between. No one speaks of how it feels to watch the world recede—to know that the amber light I have loved my whole life, the rare gold that comes when the cover breaks for a moment over the western mesas, is being taken from me one degree at a time. I have begun to mourn it before it is gone. I sit on my bench and I open both my eyelids wide, greedy, trying to drink the gray light while I still can hold it.

The Cloud Runners pass behind me sometimes, in the high reaches, where the bridges are. I hear them more than see them now—the soft slap of bare feet on wet stone, a laugh swallowed by the fog. The other elders cluck at them. I do not. I understand them better than they would guess. They are running from the same thing I am sitting still inside of: the long slow conversation of stone and cloud that will outlast all of us, that does not care for the individual at all, that asks us only to be a borrowed shape for a little while and then return to the formation. They run because they cannot bear to be a wall. I read the mist because I have made my peace with being one—or I am trying to, here in these in-between years, with my hands going cold on the bench and the amber light leaving me a little more each season.

What I have learned, sitting here, is this: the fog does not hide the world. The fog is honest. It is the cities below the cloud—if there are cities, if there are people with pale milk skin trading their curved metal tools up through the mist—who must live in the cruel clarity of seeing everything sharp-edged and certain and therefore believing they understand it. Here we understand that we do not understand. The mist comes up off the abyss every morning to remind us. That is the reverence. That is the silence before the first sip of water. We are quiet because we are listening to something vast that is mostly not us.

So you have arrived. You came up through the fog, somehow, and now you stand at the western edge where the buildings dissolve, and you have found an aging woman on a stone bench with silver hair and eyes that are beginning to cloud, staring into the gray as if she could hold it.

Sit, if you like. There is room in the worn place beside me. Draw your second eyelid down—oh. You don't have one. The plateau has not claimed you yet. Then keep your eyes open while you can, traveler. Watch the way the bank rolls there, to the left, lifting at its underside. That means the air is warming below. That means rain by the second water cycle from now.

Or it means nothing. I am only borrowing my certainty, the way I borrow this shape from the stone.

Stay a while. The light, when it breaks, is worth the waiting.
