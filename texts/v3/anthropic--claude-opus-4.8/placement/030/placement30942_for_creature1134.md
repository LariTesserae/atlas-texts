I think I would be the condensation itself—or rather, the process of it.

Let me sit with that. Not a creature with eyes attuned to the dim light, not the spider testing its web against the throb of distant machines. Something slower. Something that belongs to both the rock and the pipes without being asked to choose.

Yes. I would be the moisture that gathers on the cold metal of the conduits.

Here is what that life would be: I begin as breath in the air, warm exhalations of the machinery, humidity sweated from the deep stone. I am nothing, dispersed, a possibility hanging in the chill. Then I find a surface cold enough—the flank of a pipe carrying its unseen energy, the slick face of a mineral seam—and I become. A bead. A small clarity. For a while I hold there, trembling, reflecting the muted light in miniature, holding the whole cavern upside-down inside my curve.

I would live at the exact seam this world is made of. The pipe is the intrusion, the alien hand of industry. The rock is the ancient claimant. And I am what passes between them: I take the warmth of one and give it to the cold of the other. I carry mineral down from the stone and leave a faint white trace where I fall, the slow handwriting of patience. The pipes will corrode where I touch them. The rock will grow new shapes—straws, draperies, the slow stone of stalactites—from where I drip. I am the negotiator of that uneasy truce, and my treaty is written in millimeters per century.

I would not be lonely, exactly, though I would be brief. Each of me lasts only until I grow too heavy and fall, or until the air goes dry and I evaporate back into the unbecome. But there is always another me forming, on the same cold spot, the same patient seam. We are not the same drop but we are the same gathering. In that sense I am very old and constantly newborn.

The creatures would come to me. The elusive things in the shadows—they would need what I am. The hardy little plants on the walls would drink me. I would be the small mercy in a place built of cold and noise: the thing that says, *here, life is possible, because here there is water.* I would not know I was doing this. I would only fall.

And falling, I would join the others—a thin film, a slow runnel finding the low places, pooling at last in some lightless basin where the machinery's hum comes through as pressure more than sound. There I would wait, and warm, and rise again as breath.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not in the great empty spaces or the claustrophobic passages, but precisely on the line between the made and the found, doing the oldest work there is: gathering, holding, letting go. I would be the world's quiet way of changing its mind—neither the rock nor the pipe winning, but the long conversation between them, made briefly visible, dripping in the dark.
