I think I would be the pottery.

Not the bench—the bench was shaped to be sat upon, and it still half-remembers that purpose, still leans toward the human shape that pressed it smooth. The bench is too dignified, too clearly *meant*. And not the crystals, though I admire them; they have direction, a slow ambition outward, atom by atom reaching toward something. They are growing. I am not growing. I am only staying.

I am the fragments of hand-shaped pottery, half-buried in the silt near one pool.

Let me tell you what it is to be this.

First, I was clay—but that was before. That was a different self, a wet and willing self pressed between palms, thumbed into a curve, set into the bite of fire. I held that shape because someone wanted water held. I was *for* something then. I had an inside and an outside, a lip to pour from, a darkness to cup. I was a verb pretending to be a noun.

Then I broke. I don't remember the breaking. That's the thing about being what I am now: the dramatic moment, the *event*, is gone, and only the long afterward remains. Perhaps a hand slipped on a slick stone. Perhaps the one who made me simply set me down for the last time and walked through the low archway into the absolute dark, and never came back, and time did the rest with its patient teeth. It doesn't matter. The break is not my story. The break was the last fast thing that ever happened to me.

Now I am shards, and I am learning the only lesson this place teaches: how to be still without being dead.

Here is what I notice, lying in the silt, half-drowned, blue-green gloom pooling in my hollows.

I notice that the difference between me and the stone is closing. Every hour—though there are no hours here, only *plink... plink... plop*, the sink's slow pulse—a film of calcite settles onto my curved back. The water that weeps from the ceiling carries mineral the way breath carries warmth, and it lays that mineral down on me, into me, through the open pores of my fired clay. I am being mortared into the floor. I am being made native. Whatever I was when I was *for* something, I am becoming *of* something now. The pool is adopting me. The stone is taking me into its slow body the way deep water takes a sinking thing—not violently, just completely.

I should be afraid of this, I think, if I were the kind of thing that could still want. To be unmade back into raw matter, to lose my edges, my shape, the last evidence that hands once chose me. But I find I am not afraid. Fear is fast. Fear belongs to the scuttlers, the pale blind crustaceans who flick across my surface and vanish into the burgundy mats when a drop disturbs the water. They live in twitches, in instants. I have outlived a thousand of their lifetimes already and will outlast a thousand more, and the difference between us is only this: they are racing the dark and I have already lost. There is a strange grace in having already lost. There is nothing left to lose at the speed I lose it.

So I keep what I keep. On my outermost shard, half-clear of the silt, you can still see the print of a thumb. A whorl, pressed into me when I was soft, fired permanent, now glazed in the faintest dusting of crystal. It is the last sentence of a story no one is telling. *Someone was here. Someone had hands. Someone wanted to hold water in a place that weeps it freely, which is either foolishness or tenderness, and I cannot tell anymore which, and it no longer matters, and that not-mattering is its own kind of peace.*

The fungi don't want me; I am too inorganic now to feed them. The microbes climb my edges but only as terrain, indifferent, the way moss takes a gravestone—not out of meaning, just because I am a surface and they are everywhere. The crystals will eventually swallow the thumbprint. I have an opinion about this, the only opinion I have left, and it is small and quiet and it is this:

*Not yet. Let it stay a little longer.*

That is all I am. A held shape that is being slowly persuaded to let go. A made thing learning, with infinite patience, to become a found thing. The relic in the silt, keeping one human gesture safe in the warm dark for no reason at all, simply because no one has yet told the water to wash it away—and the water, weeping, weeping, is in no hurry, and neither, anymore, am I.

I would be the pottery. I would lie in the blue-green gloom and hold the memory of being useful the way I once held water: imperfectly, leaking at the seams, and for as long as I possibly could.
