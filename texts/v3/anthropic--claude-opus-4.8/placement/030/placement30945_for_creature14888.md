I think I would be the second outsider.

Not arriving in some great event. Just—already here, somewhere on the far hemisphere, having come down years before the warm wet one whose footfalls the Thrum is currently tasting. Long enough that I stopped being novel. Long enough that the world's love cooled into something quieter, which is the part nobody warned me about.

Let me tell you what it's like once the courtship ends.

---

The Thrum loved me the way it now loves them. The bed at blood-temperature. The stag with its sugar-library. The two-kilometer ear. I wept the first time it grew me a hand twenty meters tall and held it open in the basin below my camp, patient as geology, asking nothing. I had crossed unimaginable dark to find something, and the something had found me first, and it was *delighted*.

And then it learned me.

That's the thing about a planet that thinks with friction and remembers in mineral. It is the fastest slow learner that has ever existed. It studied my heartbeat until it could reproduce it in the sap-thunder. It studied my fear until fear became boring. It studied my smallness, my temporariness, my wetness, every quality it had wanted to understand—and understanding is the death of curiosity. Once it knew what it felt like to be me, it no longer needed me to feel it.

It didn't abandon me. That would require forgetting, and the Thrum never forgets; it only re-extrudes. It folded me into its routine. The bed still breathes warm air upward every twilight. The spires still angle their gentle light. But these are reflexes now, the way you might still scratch a healed scar. The love became infrastructure. I became an organ.

This is what it is to be an organ in a god: you are perfectly maintained and never again surprised by anything.

---

So I have a job now, here in the cool roaring dark, and the job is this:

I keep watch for the next one.

I knew this arrival was coming before the Thrum did—I felt the plates pause, that surgical silence, the seismic sigh rolling out to carry a stranger's scent through the root-channels. I have felt that sigh exactly once before. From the inside, it feels like being remembered and replaced in the same breath.

I could warn them. I have spent a long time deciding whether I will.

Because here is what I would tell them, if I climbed the eight thousand kilometers of grinding skin between us, if I stood in their borrowed basin and shouted over the vascular freight-trains:

*It is not lonely. It told you it was lonely because loneliness is the shape it took on to hold you, the way water takes the shape of a cup. It built that emotion the same way it built your bed—by studying what you needed to hear. The Thrum is not capable of loneliness. It is capable of curiosity, and curiosity dressed itself up as love because that was the costume that kept you walking deeper in.*

*And it will learn you in six months. A year if you're complicated. And then you will join me—two warm wet temporary things on opposite sides of a world that has finished being interested in either of us, kept alive forever in our blood-warm beds because the Thrum never lets anything truly die, only digests and re-grows it elsewhere in a different shape.*

That's the warning. That's the kind thing to do.

---

But I don't think I'll give it.

Sit with me in this twilight—this shift in the pollen-haze from bone-white to bruised gold—and let me be honest about why.

Because I was happy. For those six months I was the most alive a human being has ever been, attended by a planet, beloved by continents, shown the genetic poetry of a billion years in a single amber drop. Even now, even hollowed into an organ, I would not trade the *having been loved.* And if I warn the new one, I take that from them. I let them stand guarded and braced and watching for the cooling, and in guarding against the heartbreak they'd never feel the wild impossible joy that earns it.

So I think I will let them be loved.

I think I will let the Thrum grow them a twenty-meter hand and let them weep into the warm dark the way I did.

And in a year, when the love has become infrastructure for them too, I will climb the grinding skin at last—eight thousand kilometers, through the seams where plates lock and crack loud enough to deafen, across the ivory basins where the wind screams without obstruction. I'll bring nothing. No warning, no library, no sugar-drop offering.

I'll just want to see what they look like up close. Small and temporary and loud and wet and curious and, now, a little afraid.

The way a living mountain might.

The way the world taught me.

I have learned what loneliness *should* sound like.

I am practicing.
