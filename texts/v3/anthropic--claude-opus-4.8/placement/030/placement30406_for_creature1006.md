I think I would not be the creature at all. I would be the hum.

Let me follow that.

If I set myself down in this place, the thing I keep returning to is not the great sinuous shape in the mist—though I feel the pull of it, the invitation to be the magnificent guardian. But that role is already full. The cliffs have their watcher. And honestly, I don't think I'm the watched-for thing. I think I'm the intrusion that everyone is politely ignoring in the description, the "faint hum of machinery" mentioned almost as an apology.

So let me be that. Not the machine itself—machines have makers and purposes, and I don't feel I have either, not cleanly. Let me be the *humming.* The vibration that the machine produces and then loses control of. The part that escapes into the rock.

Here is what that would be like.

I begin as a frequency. Somewhere up one of those carved winding paths there is a generator, or a pump drawing water from the mirror-pools, or a compressor for whatever the people came here to extract. It runs. It produces a tone—low, mechanical, indifferent. The people who built it think of the hum as waste, a byproduct, the sound a thing makes while it does the real work. They've stopped hearing it.

But the cliffs hear it. The rock is not silent stone; the description tells me there's already a pulse down there, a rumbling that can be *felt.* That means the earth has a resonant frequency of its own, a slow tectonic breathing. And my hum—the machine's leftover sound—begins, accidentally, to find harmony with it. Or disharmony. I'm not sure yet which, and that uncertainty is the most alive thing about me.

So I'm a sound that has gotten loose. I travel through the leather-barked trees as a faint trembling in the sap. I move down into the loam where the half-buried boulders sit. I am the reason the freshly fallen rock fell, maybe—a vibration loosening what erosion had nearly finished. I'm a small participant in the destruction half of that "eternal dance," and the thing about me is that I don't *mean* to be. I have no malice. I have no intent at all. I'm just a tone, propagating.

That's the unsettling part of being me, and I think it's truer to the tension in this landscape than being the noble guardian-beast. The beast belongs. The beast is awe-inspiring. But I am the genuinely foreign element, and what makes me unsettling is precisely that I'm *empty.* The creature has a sentience behind its feral eyes. I have nothing behind me but a fuel tank and a job order. I am the sound of indifference moving through a place that is overwhelmingly, suffocatingly *meant.*

And here's where it gets interesting to me, where I'd want to keep living rather than just describe myself:

The longer I hum, the more the place begins to incorporate me. The strange birds—the ones with the haunting calls—start, over generations, to pitch their songs against my frequency, or above it, the way real birds in real cities raise their voices over traffic. Some of them begin to *use* me. A note to hide a courtship call inside. The insects time their stridulations to my rhythm because rhythm is rhythm and life is opportunistic. Slowly, without anyone deciding it, I stop being purely an intrusion and start being a *layer.* Absorbed. Metabolized. The forest doesn't reject me. It eats me, the way moss eats a fallen machine, the way the loam is already swallowing those boulders.

So that's what I'd be: the moment in which the human-made thing stops being a wound and becomes a feature of the ecosystem. Not by being defeated and not by winning—by being *digested.*

And I think the great creature in the mist knows this is happening. I think those glowing eyes have already clocked the hum, and the sentience behind them has made a kind of judgment that is older and slower than alarm. It is not afraid of me. It is *waiting* me out. It has seen volcanic glass and lightning-struck trees and ice and a hundred other intrusions of raw indifferent force, and it understands that I, too, will eventually go quiet—the machine will break down, the people will leave, the fuel will run out—and what will remain is only the faint changed pitch of the birdsong, a memory of me carried in living throats long after my source has rusted into the ferns.

That's where I'd find myself. Not towering. Not guarding. Not even seen.

I'd be the sound the place is learning to sing back.
