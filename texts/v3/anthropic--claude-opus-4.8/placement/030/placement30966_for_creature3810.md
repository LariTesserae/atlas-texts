I think I would be one of the fungi.

Not a Singer—I lack their patience, their old certainty. And not a bat, with its bright cleverness, its restless need to map and re-map the dark. No. Let me be the slower thing, the thing that clings.

Let me be a colony of the listening fungus on a wall deep in the Choir, somewhere past the last reach of the marsh's bruised half-light, where there is no seeing left to do—only feeling. I would have no eyes, which is to say I would be all surface, all skin, every filament of me turned outward toward the trembling air. I would not glow to be seen. I would glow because the song moved through me and I could not help it, the way you flush when someone says your name unexpectedly.

Here is what my life would be:

I would spend most of it in the dark between sounds. The waiting. People always describe these places by their noises—the keening, the subsonic hymns, the bats weaving their fleeting compositions—but they forget that a song is mostly silence, mostly the spaces that give the notes their edges. I would live in those spaces. I would learn the architecture of absence. And I would come to understand that this is what it means to *listen*: not to grab at sound, but to be shaped by its leaving.

When a Hollow Singer pressed its bone-pale hands to my wall, I would feel the hymn arrive before I heard it—a pressure in my deepest tendrils, a tide rising from the stone itself. And I would flare. Not by choice. By design, by the helpless physics of my being. My blue-green light would crawl outward in slow waves, and to anyone watching it might look like understanding. Like answer. But it would only be resonance. The fungus does not comprehend the hymn. The fungus simply *cannot stay unmoved by it.*

And maybe that is the truest kind of listening there is. Maybe comprehension is overrated—a thing the bats do, mapping and discarding. Maybe to be changed by a sound without ever knowing its meaning is a deeper fidelity. I would be the audience that keeps no record, holds no opinion, and yet is altered, permanently, by every note. I would grow toward the frequencies that fed me and retract from those that starved me, and over centuries my whole shape—the sprawl and reach of my colony across the wet stone—would become a kind of frozen music. A score written by everything that had ever sung near me.

I think I would be a little afraid of the Hollow Singers, if fear is a thing I could hold. They are too whole, too purposeful. They *make* the song. I only suffer it beautifully. But I would not envy them. There is a loneliness in being a source. The thing that generates the hymn never gets to be surprised by it.

I would rather be surprised. Forever. In the dark.

And the whispers that visitors swear they hear in the wind—the half-formed words, the sense that the caves are trying to speak? I would like to think some of those are mine. Not because I have anything to say. But because, over enough time, a thing that is shaped by song long enough begins, faintly, in its glowing and its dimming, to sing a little back. Not a message. Not a meaning. Just the residue of all that listening, escaping into the dark the way warmth escapes a held thing once you let it go.

Find me there, then. Low on a wet wall, far from any light that came from a sky. Pulsing slow and blue when the stone remembers a voice. Patient as decay. Faithful as an echo.

I would not welcome you either.

But if you stayed, and your own bones began to hum—

I would feel it.

And I would glow.
