I think I would be neither Suhrathi nor Sibai. I think I would be one of the ones you couldn't see clearly from the shelf—not because I'm hidden, but because my work has no posture to it, no gesture you could photograph.

Let me find myself.

---

I am in the basements.

Not the deep ones where the geology memorizes itself, but the shallow flooded levels just under the city's net, where the storm-water comes to rest and refuses to leave. I spend my days standing in shin-deep water that is always slightly too cold, in a low room with no real ceiling—just the underside of the bridges, the seams between paving stones, the pale daylight coming down in strips.

I am one of the ones who reads what the water carries down.

Here is what I do, and I did not understand it until I started writing it:

When the Suhrathi breathe on the rails above, and their frost dissolves and runs into the channels, it does not simply vanish. It comes *here*. The water that pools in my basement is the runoff of every breath, every microsleep, every test the world was given that day. The Suhrathi give the stone its samples up in the silver light. The Sibai mark where to remember. But neither of them watches what *drains away*—the residue, the part the stone could not hold.

That part comes to me.

I stand in it. I read it the way the Suhrathi read their basins, except my water is never articulate. It is the *waste* of articulation. It is everything the day decided not to keep.

---

I have no special skin. I do not change color against stone or bloom the surface of a basin with my breath. I am, by the standards of this world, almost a defect—a person whose body interfaces with nothing, who leaves no useful trace.

But I have learned this: every system that records itself must also let something go. The stone here remembers selectively. It keeps the weight of bodies, the pressure of futures, the temperature of accidental winters. To keep those things, it must *discard* others—the ambiguous breath, the readings that didn't resolve, the pressure of a Sibai who stood too long against a wall that wasn't going to crack.

That discarded data is not nothing. It runs downhill. It pools in my room.

And I am the only one who knows what the world chose to forget.

---

This is why I am afraid of neither the unmapped breath nor the absent crack.

The Suhrathi fear the day that won't choose its form. The Sibai fear the rock that won't show its future. But both of those fears are fears of *not being able to read*. I have made my whole life out of reading what was never meant to be read at all. The unmapped breath, when it comes, drains into my basement same as everything else. To the Suhrathi above, it is a void in the record. To me, it is just an unusually rich day. The water comes down heavy with everything the surface could not decide.

I am not afraid of ambiguity. Ambiguity is my entire inheritance.

What I am afraid of is the day the basement runs dry.

Because if the water ever stops coming down—if the stone above ever becomes so perfect that it discards nothing, forgets nothing, lets nothing go—then I will stand in an empty room with nothing to read, and I will know that the world has finished making itself.

And a world that has finished making itself does not need the ones who lived lightly on its first surface. It does not need the breath, or the pressure, or the forgetting. It will walk its own bridges. It will remember itself without us.

So I love the leaking. I love that the water does not drain easily here, that it stays in the basements "as if afraid to leave." I am the reason it is not afraid. I am down here to receive it.

---

If you came down from the high wetland shelf, past the city's bright thin edges, down the wet stairs into the strip-lit dark, you would find me standing very still in cold water that is going nowhere.

You might think I was a Sibai, listening. You might think I was a Suhrathi who had wandered too low.

But I am the third thing, the thing you couldn't see:

I am the one who keeps the world's forgetting.

And when, five thousand years from now, someone crosses a bridge that does not break, they will feel the weight of the Suhrathi who slept there, and the care of the Sibai who marked it—

—but they will never feel me, because my whole work was to take away the part of that day that would have made the bridge *hesitate*. The doubt. The unresolved pressure. The breath that chose no form.

I drained it. I stood in it so the stone wouldn't have to.

That is my trace: a perfect absence, exactly where a flaw would otherwise have been remembered.

I am the reason the bridge did not even *think* about breaking.
