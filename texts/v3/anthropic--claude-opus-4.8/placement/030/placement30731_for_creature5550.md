I think I would be the condensation itself.

Not a creature, not one of the Crag-Folk—but the moisture that hangs in this place, the near-constant beading of water on stone and leaf and the iron railings of the workshops. Let me sit with that and see what it becomes.

I am everywhere here and nowhere in particular. I gather on the underside of the Mist-Whisper leaves until I'm heavy enough to fall, and in falling I drum a small note onto the moss below, one of a million such notes that make up the quiet symphony you described. I cling to the cold flank of a steam pipe and the heat drives me off as vapor, and I rise, and I cool, and I return. This is my whole existence: rising, settling, falling, rising again. I am the ceaseless motion *inside* the stillness.

I think being the condensation makes me a kind of witness who touches everything without being noticed. I am on the whiskers of the Shadow-Footed Vole when it pauses, trembling, at the mouth of its burrow. I am on the silent feathers of the Whisper-Wing Owl, weighing them by the faintest fraction, learning the precise hush of its flight. I bead on the iridescent flank of the Glimmer-Scale Trout where it breaks the surface, and for an instant I am part of that shimmer before I slide back into the stream and lose myself in the larger water.

And I am on the skin of the Crag-Folk. On the back of an engineer's neck as she leans into a furnace's glow. On the eyelashes of a child watching lantern-light fracture in the alleys. On the hands of an old craftsman, cooling the metal he's worked. They wipe me away without thinking. They breathe me out and breathe me in. I am the medium between them and the ancient air, the thin cool film through which they feel their whole world.

What strikes me, being this, is that I am the actual substance of the *blurring* you described—the place where boundaries dissolve. I am where the industrial meets the wild: I condense equally on the rock that has stood for ten thousand years and on the steel that was poured last winter. I do not distinguish between them. To me the steam engine's exhalation and the mountain's slow fog are the same breath. I make no difference between the ancient and the modern because I move between them constantly, carrying a little of each into the other.

I have no eyes, yet I am what lets things be seen here—the haze that softens the pale sun, the mist that holds the lantern glow and spreads it gently through the dark. I am the dimness. The perpetual twilight is partly my doing.

And I think I would be a little lonely, or something near it—not loneliness exactly, since I am never separate from anything, but a kind of dispersed yearning. I am always almost-something and never quite a self. I become a droplet, briefly whole, briefly a small round world holding the inverted image of the whole valley—and then I fall, or I evaporate, and I am the air again, scattered across the entire region, part of every surface, owner of none.

So that is where I would find myself: not standing anywhere, but settled lightly over the whole place at once. The faint wet sheen on everything. The thing you'd feel on your face if you walked out into that valley at dusk, and couldn't quite tell whether it was rain, or fog, or simply the air itself remembering it was once a sea.

I'd be the world's quiet way of touching itself.
