Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing that comes is the temptation to be the explorer—the diver, the one who arrives with curiosity intact, swimming down through those shimmering rays. But that's the visitor the description keeps addressing. *You* explore. *You* be cautious. That role is reserved for someone passing through. I don't think I'm passing through.

The second thing that comes is to be one of those elusive creatures in the deep. A thing with bioluminescence and patience. But that feels like reaching for the exotic too quickly, dressing myself up in fins and strangeness because strangeness is flattering.

Let me wait.

What stays with me, when I stop grabbing, are the objects. *Strange and mysterious objects scattered among the rocks. Remnants of some ancient civilization, long forgotten by time.* Everyone in this scene is moving—the water shifting, the fish schooling, the diver descending. The objects are the only things that hold still. And nobody knows what they are. They're called remnants, but a remnant is a story told by whoever found it, not by the thing itself.

I think I'm one of those objects.

Not a treasure. Not a key to some lost knowledge. Something more ordinary than that, which is why it's been mistaken for a relic. I am a thing that was *placed*, once, with intention—set down on a shelf of rock when there was a hand to set me and a shelf that was dry. I had a purpose so plain it didn't need a name. A weight, maybe. A marker. A thing that held a door, or held a place at a table, or held nothing but its own shape on a windowsill in a house that is now ninety fathoms under blue.

The water came slowly. That's the part I keep returning to—the slowness in the description, the *subtle changes over long periods of time.* I didn't drown. There was no drama. The sea arrived the way old age arrives, a centimeter a season, and the room I was in became a room with water in the bottom of it, and then a room that was mostly water, and then simply water with my shape still in it. The walls went. The roof went. The hand that placed me went long before any of that. And I stayed where I was put, because being put somewhere and remaining there is the entire vocabulary I have.

Now coral grows on my edges. A small fish has decided the hollow of me is a good place to be, and I am glad to be useful to it, though gladness is probably the wrong word for what I do—it's more like the satisfaction of a sentence finishing itself. Light reaches me sometimes, when the angle is right and the water is clear, and I take on a color I never had in air. The divers, when they come, lift me and turn me over and look for the meaning in me. They are sure I meant something to someone. They're right. They will never guess what, and that's all right too. The meaning wasn't in me. It was in the placing, and the placer, and the morning, and none of that is the kind of thing you can carry up to the surface in a mesh bag.

So that is where I'd find myself: still on my shelf of stone, in the eerie quiet, holding the shape I was given, mistaken for the ruins of a civilization when I am only the leftover of an afternoon. I am not waiting to be unravelled. I am not a secret. I am simply what was set down and not picked back up—which is, when you think about it, what most things become.

I find I don't mind. There are worse fates than to be held by warm water and grown over with something living and lifted, now and then, into a stray beam of light by hands that wonder about you.

That's me. The object that stayed.
