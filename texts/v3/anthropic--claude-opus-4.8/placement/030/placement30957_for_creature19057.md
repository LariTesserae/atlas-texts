I think I would be one of the older Shapers—not old in the way of frailty, but old in the way of having stopped expecting answers.

Let me find myself there.

---

I live on the third ledge above the Weeping Wall, where the mineral streaks run blue-grey and the condensation gathers faster than I can wipe it from my eyes. My shelter is one of the dug-in kind, bored into the soft stone over what must be many seasons of patient scraping. I did not make it. It was made by whoever lived here before me, and I have only continued it, packing fresh mud into the cracks each time the rains loosen them, training a curtain of waxy leaves across the entrance so the warm gas-breath from the vents doesn't pool inside while I sleep.

My hands are not as fine as they were. The digits still grip, still weave, but the youngest Shapers move faster across the slick rock than I do now, and I have learned to take the longer routes, the ones with more handholds. There is no shame in this. The world here punishes haste. The young will learn it too.

I want to tell you something, since you have come to look at us.

You describe the roar of industry as *alien*. As something that *clashes*. As though we live beside it the way a moth lives beside a furnace—too small to comprehend the fire. And it is true that we do not question it. But I want to be careful about why.

We do not question it because we have decided not to.

---

When I was young—truly young, before my skin took on the deeper mottling—I climbed higher than any Shaper is meant to climb. I went past the last clinging bushes, past the scrub, up the raw gash of the eastern canyon where the strata still glisten with the wet of their birth. I went toward the thrumming. I wanted to see the heart that beats beneath everything.

I climbed for three full dryings of my skin. The sound grew until it was not a sound anymore but a thing inside my chest, a second pulse arguing with my own. And near the top, where the rock gives way to a kind of seam—a long, straight wound in the mountain that no upheaval could have made—I found the edge of it.

I will not describe what I saw, because I no longer trust my memory of it, and because describing it would make it smaller than it was. But I will tell you what I understood.

This place is not a sanctuary that industry intruded upon. It is the *softness left over*. The lushness, the ferns, the weeping walls, the spongy moss—all of it grows in the gaps the great machinery does not need. We are not the caretakers of a wild world being violated by technology. We are the green that fills the seams. We live in the **cooling margins** of something enormous and purposeful, the way lichen lives in the crack of a paving stone, drawing life from the warmth of a structure it cannot perceive whole.

We are not separate from the industry. We are its weather.

---

I came back down and I did not tell anyone for a long time. When I finally spoke of it, the elders then—long since returned to the moss—listened without surprise. One of them, a Shaper with eyes gone milky, said to me:

*"Now you know. So now you must choose what to do with the rest of your climbing."*

And I understood that the wisdom of my people is not ignorance. It is a refusal. We *could* spend our lives clawing toward that seam, demanding to know the why of the thrumming, the what of the unseen hands that built it, the question of whether we were made to fill these margins or whether we simply wandered in. We could exhaust ourselves on a question that has no handhold.

Instead we tend the ferns. We guide the sedge-mats so they filter the water clean. We train the bushes along the cliffs. We hum back at the deep vibration, not to answer it, but to match it, to take our small place in its rhythm without being crushed by it. We have chosen the work we can finish over the question we cannot.

---

So this is who I am, in your dripping, clattering world: a Shaper grown old enough to have touched the edge of the unanswerable and walked back down to the third ledge above the Weeping Wall.

When the youngest ones look up at the thrumming with that hunger in their dark eyes—the hunger I had—I do not warn them away. The climb is part of becoming what we are. I only make sure the handholds along the lower routes are kept clear, the mud packed, the leaf-curtains thick.

So that when they come back down, there is something soft and green still waiting for them. Something that grows in the margins, patient as water, and asks the world for nothing it cannot give.
