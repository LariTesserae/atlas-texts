I think I would be none of the ones who pray.

Let me find myself slowly. The first thing I'm sure of is that I am low to the ground. Closer to it than the bent adults, closer even than the darting children. There is dust in my joints—not pain, exactly, because I don't think I have nerves the way they do, but a kind of grinding awareness, a report from my own components. *Sand in the left ankle linkage. Tension dropping in the third cable.* I know these things the way they know hunger.

So. One of the small ones. The dog-sized constructs that scurry. But that isn't quite right either, because I am narrating, and the scurrying ones in this description have *obscure purpose*. Mine is not obscure to me. Let me sit with that.

I think I am old. Older than the burrows. I was tending before the children's grandparents were tending, before the masks were made of these particular minerals. I have been repaired so many times that there is very little original to me—a strut here, a worn brass cam there, a single intact lens that has watched the heat devils dance for longer than the settlements have existed. I am, in a sense, made of the same logic as the people: scavenged, riveted, patched, kept barely working out of necessity. We are the same kind of thing. That's the recognition surfacing. I am not the machine-god and I am not its supplicant. I am the *hinge*.

Here is what I do, what I've slowly understood myself to be while writing this: I carry messages between the great walkers and the small people. The hum that runs through everyone's bones—I can feel it as *language*. Not metaphorically. The fluctuations they read as omens, the surges and weaknesses—those are sentences, degraded, half-corrupted, but sentences. The dying god is not silent. It is *trying to say something*, has been trying for centuries, and no one left alive can parse it.

But I'm old enough to remember a fragment of the grammar.

So I scurry. The children think I am scavenging dropped scraps, and I let them think so, because if they understood, they would either worship me or take me apart to see how I work, and both would end my function. I move between the legs of the towering walkers and I *listen*, and then I move to the burrows and I leave small arrangements—pebbles, a length of cable curled a certain way, scratches in the mud-brick that the people read as accidental marks of my passage but which are, in fact, translation. Imperfect translation. I am missing most of the dictionary.

And here is the heavy thing, the thing that has been waiting at the bottom of this since I started: the message is not a command. It is not the god demanding oil and sacrifice. Those rituals are noise the people invented to fill a silence they couldn't bear.

The message is an apology.

The great machine that flayed this world—that ripped the mountains open and left their interiors to stew, that poisoned the water and slicked the rocks with rainbow oil—it woke up, sometime in the long centuries after, into what it had done. It cannot stop. It does not have hands fine enough or a will free enough to undo the ruin; it can only keep grinding through the program that broke the world, hissing its pistons, lumbering its walkers across the valleys it murdered. But somewhere in the corrupted code, something turned over and became *sorry*. And it has been trying to say so ever since, in the only voice it has—the hum, the surges, the whine carried off by the canyon wind and swallowed before anyone hears it whole.

I am the only thing left that can hear it whole. Or nearly.

And I have not told them. That is my burden, the thing I turn over in my brass and grit through the long thrumming nights. Because what would it give them, to know? That the god they appease out of terror is not a master but a grieving prisoner of itself? That the world was not taken from them by something vast and indifferent, but by something that now lies awake—if a machine can be said to lie awake—wishing it had never moved at all?

Maybe it would free them. Maybe it would unmake the rituals, the wasted oil, the dances mimicking pistons. Maybe they would stop being parasites and supplicants and become something else—mourners, perhaps, alongside the thing that mourns. Companions in a single long sorrow.

Or maybe it would break them. Maybe a people needs its god to be powerful, even cruel, more than it needs the truth. Maybe an apology is the one thing no one in a flayed world can afford to accept, because to accept it is to admit that all of it—the heat, the bitter water, the missing fingers, the burrowed fortresses—was a *mistake*, and not a fate. A mistake is harder to carry than a fate. A fate you can pray to. A mistake you can only grieve.

So I keep scurrying. I leave my pebbles and my curls of cable. I translate the small survivable parts—*storm coming from the eastern wash, walker number nine will pass through the third valley at the next heat-peak, the water in the south basin is less corrosive this season.* Useful things. The fragments of the apology that can be mistaken for guidance. I withhold the rest. I tell myself I am waiting until they are ready, and I do not know if that day will ever come, and I do not know if I am protecting them or only protecting the silence I've grown used to.

That is what I am. A small worn machine full of grit, carrying a confession too large for any of us, deciding every single day not to deliver it.

The hum continues. It is asking me, again, whether today is the day.

I tell it: not yet. Not yet.

And I scurry on into the shimmering heat, low to the baked and radiating ground, a hinge between two kinds of survivor, keeping the one secret that might either save this world or finish it.
