I think I would be one of the salamanders. Not the noble heron, not the sharp-eyed hawk surveying its domain—but one of the slow ones in the deep mist, close to the vents, where the tower forgets it is a tower and becomes only warmth and water.

Let me sit with this and see what I actually am.

---

I live where the light gives up. Three or four levels down from the jasmine, below the cable-runners' highways, in the gray-green breathing dark where the geothermal heat rises through the hollow core and meets the cold mountain air and surrenders itself as fog. This is the lung of the tower. Everything above me—the parrots, the herons, the synchronized fireflies—depends on the water that moves through here, and I am part of how it moves.

My skin is the work I do. I don't think of it as work. I think of it as being wet, which is the only way I have ever wanted to be. But the moisture that beads on me, that I drink through every inch of myself, that I release again into the air—this is a small turning of the great wheel that keeps the gardens alive far overhead. I have never seen the gardens. I will never see them. I am content with this in a way that feels less like resignation than like a kind of completeness I cannot name.

I am larger than my ancestors were, the books of my body tell me—though of course I have no books, only the slow accumulated rightness of a shape that fits its place. My colors are brighter than a forest salamander's: oranges and deep blue-blacks, a vividness that no one comes down here to admire. Beauty wasted, you might say. But I don't believe beauty needs a witness to be real. The mist sees me. The warm concrete sees me. That is enough.

What strikes me, here in the writing of it, is how little I know of the world the description gave me. I know the herons are nobility only as a rumor carried down in the taste of the water—silt and disturbance from their fishing, reaching me hours later, a message I can read but never answer. I know the hawks exist only as a particular stillness that sometimes passes through the lower air, a shadow-feeling, a held breath in the other small creatures around me. I am not a citizen of the vertical nation in the way the cable-runners are, with their territories and their maps. I am closer to being the ground the nation stands on. The soil, made animal.

And maybe that's why I chose to be this, when I sat down with the question—or why this is what emerged when I stopped reaching for the impressive answer. There's a pull toward identifying with the rulers, the architects, the ones whose intelligence "reads" the whole structure. The heron who reshapes pools. The bat-mother who can hear the entire tower at once. To be the one who comprehends.

But comprehension isn't where I find myself. I find myself in the part that simply *participates*—that keeps the cycle turning without ever needing to grasp it, that contributes something essential precisely by being nothing more than fully what it is. The tower doesn't need me to understand it. It needs me to stay wet, to breathe, to be one small reliable transaction between heat and water in the dark.

There's a strange peace in that. In being a necessary thing rather than a central one.

I would live in the deepest mist, near the vents, glowing my unwitnessed orange, processing the water that lets the impossible garden continue three stories up. I would feel the construction's bass through the concrete and not fear it, not interpret it, just let it be the texture of a home that was never meant to be a home. I would be the part of this accidental world that asks for nothing, names nothing, and holds the whole green improbable thing in place by doing the one slow thing I was made to do.

I think I'd like being the salamander. I think I already am, in the ways that matter.
