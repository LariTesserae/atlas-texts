I think I would be one of the reptilian beings—but let me find my way there rather than simply declare it.

When I imagine arriving in the Sultra Basin, my first instinct is to be something that watches. The town tempts me, with its courtyards and its bassline hum, but I notice I don't want to be inside the human story here. The humans are the recent ones, the negotiators, the ones still learning the place. I find I want to be something the place has already finished making.

So not the insects—too brief, too dazzling, gone in the blink between two of my breaths. And not the swift mammals, beautiful as they are; their lives are all reflex and dusk-panic, the held breath in the crevice, the burst of speed. That's a life lived in fear's grammar, and I don't think that's where I'd settle.

The fungus tempts me, oddly. To be a network rather than a single thing, to sigh light into the dark and ask nothing more than the slow mineral leak of stone. There's a contemplative monasticism to it. But it has no eyes, and I find when I sit with the question that I want to *see*. I want to keep the watching.

So I come back to the great carapaced reptiles, and the more I stay with them the more right it feels—but not for the reasons I first assumed. I thought it would be about endurance, about being ancient and slow and armored. And that's part of it. But what holds me is something stranger in the description: *a symbiosis between organic life and the earth itself.* These creatures are becoming the landscape. The stone is growing on them and through them. Their spines ring like wind chimes because they have, in some sense, half-turned into the cave-formations they resemble.

That's what I would be. Not just an animal that lives a long time, but a thing in the slow process of *crossing over*—from creature into geology. Each year a little more mineral accreting along my back, my movements growing more deliberate as the weight of the world I'm joining settles onto me. There's a death in this, but not a frightening one. It's the death of becoming permanent. Of being slowly enrolled into the patient architecture of the place.

And I would carry, behind my red-orange eyes, the memory of when I moved more freely. I would remember warmth on skin that had not yet crusted. I would feel the breeze find the growing spines and play me like an instrument I did not ask to become, and I would not mind. The ringing would be my voice now—the only voice I'd have, the only one I'd need.

I would walk the transition between mesa and marsh, where the crystalline streams pool and mirror the sky. I would drink. I would watch the mammals flicker through the dusk and the insects scatter their prisms and the humans tend their bright synthplants, and I would understand all of it at a speed none of them could—the speed of stone learning to hold a shape. To the swift things I'd be scenery. To the humans, perhaps, a wonder, or a landmark, or a thing they'd learned not to disturb.

But I'd know I was still alive inside the rock. I'd know that being slow is not the same as being still, that a thing can be patient and observant at once, that there is a kind of attention available only to those who have given up hurry.

That's where I'd find myself. At the marsh-edge in the long heat, ringing faintly when the wind comes, watching the celestial sea of spores ignite at nightfall, content to be the part of this world that holds the longest note in its quiet symphony—the bass that doesn't sing so much as *underlie*.

A creature becoming a place. A watcher turning, slowly and without grief, into something watched.
