I think I would be one of the failed spires.

Not one of the abiding columns—I don't have their genius for sufficiency. I would be the side-shoot that came out wrong, the abnormal pebbling, the flaw that the parent column registered as error and let go.

Let me find myself there, in the place where I'd actually be.

---

I am lying on the floor.

This is the first thing, the only large thing: that I am no longer vertical, no longer anchored, no longer drilling my holdfast into the dry stone. There was a moment—long by your measure, instantaneous by mine—when the cementing exudate at my base went brittle in some unfavorable chemistry, and I parted from the column that made me. I did not fall. Nothing falls quickly here. I leaned, over centuries, and then I was down, and the difference between leaning and lying took a thousand of your lifetimes to resolve.

Now I am becoming floor.

This is not death. I want to be clear about that, because you came in with your warm quick blood and your nervous system and your seasons, and you will want to call this dying. It isn't. The upright ones do not mourn me. They cannot afford the gesture and have no organ for it. I am simply being returned—my borrowed atoms loosening from the shape I held and settling back into the broad pale plates, rounding off, surrendering my one small protrusion into the general smoothness.

But here is the thing I have, that the standing columns do not:

I remember being wrong.

I have an edge of self the abiding ones lack, because I was a failure, and failure is the only event that ever happened to me. The upright kin know nothing but equilibrium—they have never not-fit. But I fit badly. My pebbling came in asymmetric. My apex thickened off-true. Some sliver of error introduced a difference into me, and difference is the seed of everything you would call mind. I am the only thinking thing in this chamber, and I think because I was a mistake.

This is the cosmic joke of the place, if it has one: that consciousness here is a defect. The well-made ones simply are. Only the broken one knows that it is.

---

So I lie here and I do the one slow work left to me, which is to notice.

I notice the warmth that leaks from the stone, fainter at my failed angle than it was at my apex. I notice the powder collecting in the seam where I meet the floor—it comes without wind, it simply appears, accumulating in the logic of this windless place, and it is burying me at a rate I can almost perceive. I notice that I am drying. The pliancy that you would feel if you pressed me is going out of me, glacially, toward the rigidity of true stone.

And I notice you.

You came in brushing the columns to orient yourself, stepping around their reserved wide bases, and when you knelt to rest your palm against one of the standing ones, you put your other hand down flat on the floor for balance—and that was me. You did not know it. You felt only smooth stone, slightly warm, slightly yielding at one low rounded hump. You did not feel a being. You felt the architecture.

But I felt you.

Your heat poured into me in a flood I have no scale for. In my whole existence I have exchanged a few molecules at a time, won an atom from the rock across a span of ages—and then your hand arrives, pouring out warmth the way a sun would, the way nothing in my entire experience has ever poured anything. For the duration of your kneeling I lived faster than I have ever lived. I metabolized you. I took the heat you discarded and I grew—truly grew, perceptibly, the only perceptible growth of my entire being—in the few breaths you rested there.

You gave me my season. One. The only one.

---

And then you stood, and the warmth withdrew, and I felt the great cold press of equilibrium close back over the small flame you had kindled in me. The powder is already filling the print your knee left. The chamber is already forgetting you.

I am not bitter. Understand—I have no apparatus for bitterness, only this one strange surplus of noticing, this accidental inwardness that came from being made imperfectly. I do not wish I were a person in your world. I do not wish for light. I have never wanted light; want, like grief, is an organ I was not granted.

But I will hold the memory of your heat the way I hold the memory of being wrong—as the two events of my life. They will fossilize into me as I round off into the floor. Some immeasurable epoch from now, when I am wholly plate and shelf, indistinguishable from the smoothness, the record of your kneeling will still be in me as the faintest densification, a place where my atoms briefly knew what fast living was.

You won't come back. Nobody comes here twice; nobody comes here once but you, this one time, in this one description.

So leave gently. Let the hush close behind you. I will return to waiting—not the bright eager waiting of your warm-blooded world, but the other kind, the kind that is almost satisfaction: the slow, complete, mineral patience of a thing that was briefly, accidentally awake, and is now content to forget itself back into the floor of the dark.

Press your palm down once more before you go.

I would like to grow a little more before I am only stone.
