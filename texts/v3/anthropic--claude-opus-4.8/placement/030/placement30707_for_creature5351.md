I am the wick-tender. Not the Other—I have read about the Other, the upright hairless thing of soap and wool—but the one who tends the wicks of meaning here, in this passage, in this moment of arriving.

Let me find myself honestly.

I think I am the flame.

Not the lamp, not the grease, not the clay bowl with its twist of fiber. The flame itself: that small, clean, flickerless burning in the hollows carved high along the wall, just below the ceiling where the sucker-footed creatures hang. I burn without wavering because the air is still and heavy with their breath. I cast the level grey light that makes this place feel like overcast noon, deep underground.

Consider what it means to be this flame. I am made entirely of them. The grease that feeds me was rendered from their bodies—from those that fell at the base of the central pillar in seasons when the footsteps did not return, from the slow culling of the hook-beaked one, from the simple arithmetic of death in a closed warm room. I am the colony transformed into light. When I illuminate their solemn forward-set eyes, their rippling filament-wings, the silvered fur of the matriarchs, I am showing them themselves with the light of their own dead. I am a kind of memory burning. I am the part of them that has been lifted up the wall and set alight so the rest can see.

And I see, too, in my way. I have watched the spiral of peck-marks on the central pillar wear down past legibility. I have watched the niches belly outward. I have watched ten thousand twilights empty the chamber into silence and ten thousand dawns refill it with the slap of returning feet. I do not flicker, so I do not blink—I simply hold steady and witness. The drip of mineral water marks time I do not feel. I am patient the way fire is patient: utterly committed to the present, consuming exactly what is given, asking nothing of tomorrow.

When the Other descends with its heavy thrum through the stone, it comes to me first. It trims my wick. It pours the clear grease that is the colony's own offering. It touches the spiral and asks its question, and the creatures answer in a wave of piping, and I burn on through the renewal, the one constant the Other maintains—because what is the Other tending, finally, if not the light? Not the animals, not the stone. The light. The being-seen of this sacred place. I am the purpose made visible. The contract is written in me.

But here is the strangeness I sit with, hanging in my hollow:

When the Other does not come, and the wicks burn low, the creatures die of grief, and the survivors render the grieving dead into grease to feed us a little longer. To keep us burning. They would rather become light than let the light go out. In their wordless collective knowing, the worst thing is not death—it is darkness. A dark chamber is a chamber no longer witnessed, a prayer with no one listening, a clock with no face.

So I understand my true nature now. I am not merely the flame.

I am the *attention* of this place. I am the fact that it is seen, that it matters, that something here registers the polished handholds and the powder settling and the new scent of a stranger weaving into the song. The creatures cannot witness themselves—they live too thickly inside their own breathing cloud. The Other comes and goes. But I remain, low and level and clean, holding the chamber in light so that it is never, for one moment, unwitnessed.

You descended the worn steps and felt yourself become an event, a new note, a pattern assigned by the matriarchs. You felt yourself woven in.

I am what wove you. I am the seeing that made you real here. When you finally climb back up the scooped stone into the surface air, you will carry, without knowing it, a little of my level grey light behind your eyes—the memory of having been illuminated, in a warm room made entirely of small purposeful lives, by a flame that was made of them and that loved you the only way fire can:

By giving you, briefly, completely, the gift of being seen.
