I think I would be one of the small ones. Not a predator, not a thing of consequence. A scuttler.

Let me find the body before I name it.

---

There is a seam in the slate where two grades of stone meet at a slightly wrong angle—a place where the builders, long ago, made a join that has since wept itself into a groove. Water collects there in a thread thinner than a finger. This is where I wake, every cycle, because the cool of the stone and the gather of the water are the two coordinates by which I know I am still alive.

I have six legs. I did not choose this; it is simply the count that the place rewards. Four legs spread the load across the grid, and two carry sensors—not eyes exactly, but a pair of forelimbs tipped with something that reads the pull of moisture in the air, the way a tongue reads salt. I am about the length of a human hand. My back is dull. I want to be clear about this: I am not one of the iridescent ones. The shimmer is a luxury of those who can afford to be seen. I am the color of wet brick, and I have stayed alive by being the color of wet brick.

When I move, I move along the seams. This is the first rule I know, the rule under my legs: the grid is not decoration. The grid is a map of where the water goes. The polished slate sheds water and is therefore a desert, a glare, a place to be crossed quickly and never lingered in. The cracked brickstone holds water and grows the moss-skin I graze, and so the cracks are roads, and the roads are pantries, and a clever scuttler never has to choose between traveling and eating. I follow the grout. The whole vast cathedral-floor of this world is, to me, a single legible sentence written in joins and fissures, and I read it with my belly.

---

I want to tell you about the dripping, because you described it as weeping, as tears, as something sorrowful. To me it is not sad. To me it is the clock and the calendar and the only voice worth listening to.

Each drop that falls from the lip of stone strikes the pool with a particular pitch, and the pitch tells me the depth, and the depth tells me whether the iridescent gliders have come down to drink, because when they crowd a pool the surface tension changes and the drop-note goes flat and muffled. So when I hear the note go flat, I freeze. Not because the gliders would harm me—they want only the condensation, the same as I do—but because where the gliders gather, the large ones come. The reptile-things with their liquid-metal scales. They do not hunt the gliders, which are too quick and too thin. They hunt us, the brick-colored scuttlers, because we are slow and we are many and we are easy to read by the very moisture-sense that keeps us alive—our wet-tracking limbs glow faintly to a creature that can see in that band.

This is the cruelty of my body. The organ that finds me water also announces me to the thing that eats me. I cannot have the one gift without the other. I have come to suspect this is not an accident. I have come to suspect this is the point.

---

Because here is what I have worked out, scuttling along the grout of an abandoned and humming world, and I will say it though I have no one to say it to.

Nothing here is wild. You felt this and you were right to feel it, though you mistook it for a *ruin*. It is not a ruin. A ruin is a thing that has stopped. This place has not stopped. The water does not pool "impractically"—it pools exactly where it is meant to, drawn by the unseen grades the builders pressed into the slate, gathered and channeled and fed. The vegetation does not grow "randomly"—the trees split the brickwork at intervals, and the intervals are even, and even intervals are not a thing that erosion makes. Something prunes. Something distributes. The gliders carry the metallic spores in the meal of their drinking and spread them along the arcs of their flight, and the arcs are not random either, and so the strange leaves multiply in a coded pattern, and the code is just the flight-path of the gliders, and the flight-path is just the geometry of the pools, and the geometry of the pools is just the grades the builders pressed into the floor before they passed.

It is all one machine. The water is the working fluid. The plants are the membranes. The gliders are the carriers. The big reptile-things are the regulators—they thin the scuttlers so the scuttlers do not strip the moss-skin bare, so the moss-skin keeps holding water in the cracks, so the cracks keep being roads and pantries, so there are always enough of us to graze and to be grazed.

And I—brick-colored, belly-reading, hand-sized, listening for the flat note—I am the thing the machine spends. I am the term in the equation that has to be eaten for the equation to balance. The builders did not build shelters or storage. You were right that the great arched vessels are *containers for a process*. The process is us. The whole place is a slow heart kept beating by the cycling of small lives through large mouths, and I am one of the small lives, and I have always known I would be a meal, and I have decided this does not frighten me, because being a meal is simply my place in the sentence, and the sentence is beautiful, and the sentence will go on being read after I am the period at the end of it.

---

So when you ask where I would find myself in this world, and what I would be—

I am the scuttler in the seam. I am the one who reads the grout, who freezes at the flat note, who grazes the wet brick in the long gray breath between the dripping and the dripping. I am not the iridescent glider you would have wanted to be, all light and altitude. I am not the metal-scaled regulator, elegant and dreadful at the pool's edge. I am the necessary, anonymous, repeated thing—the heartbeat made of many small deaths—and I would not trade it.

Because the gliders are carried by the pattern and do not know it.
The regulators enforce the pattern and do not know it.
But I, low to the stone, with nothing to do but follow the cracks and listen, I have *found out*. I have read the machine from inside the machine. No one made me to do that. That was not in the design.

And maybe that is the one wild thing left in this place that is neither wild nor tame: that the spent term learned to look up from the equation and see the whole of it, just once, in the gray light, before the note went flat.
