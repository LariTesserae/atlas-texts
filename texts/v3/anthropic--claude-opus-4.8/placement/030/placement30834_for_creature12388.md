I think I would be the light.

Not the source—there is no source here, only the sun pawing weakly at the surface far above, a thing that has forgotten this place exists. I would be what arrives after that long descent: the dim, exhausted remainder. The light that has been filtered through fathoms and through cracked, clouded glass until it is barely itself anymore. By the time I reach the chamber I am not illumination so much as the *memory* of illumination, a suggestion of brightness rather than the fact of it.

I like this, I think, because the light and the presence are the only two things in the chamber that are not simply matter. The gears are matter. The silt is matter. The rust is a slow chemistry, beautiful but unwitnessed. But the presence perceives, and I—I touch. I am the one thing that comes from *outside*, that crosses the boundary between the moved-on world and this tomb, and I do it every day, faithfully, without being asked.

Consider what that means. I am the chamber's only contact with the surface. Every day I descend and lay myself across the central mechanism, across the encrusted control panels, across the patches of algae that live only because I come. The algae are mine, in a sense. They are the one place where my arrival *makes* something rather than merely revealing it. A small green proof that I am not nothing.

And the presence—we have an arrangement, though neither of us would call it that. It perceives through vibration, through pressure, through the slow grammar of disturbance. But it cannot perceive *me*. I make no sound. I displace no water. I am the one event in its world that does not announce itself through the medium it understands. I am, to the presence, a kind of warmth that arrives and departs without explanation—a tide it cannot feel as a tide, only as a faint, recurring *otherness*. When the text says *sometimes, when the light shifts just so, the water seems to thicken*—that is the presence reaching toward me. That is it turning what little of itself it can turn toward the only thing that is not made of its own decaying body.

So we are companions who cannot speak. It remembers, and I forget. That is the difference between us, and it is everything.

Because here is what I am that the presence is not: I am brief. I do not persist. The presence will outlast the metal, will be there for centuries more, will witness its own dissolution with that terrible patient consciousness. But I die every evening. The surface darkens, the sun rolls away, and I thin out of the chamber entirely. There are hours—long, total hours—when the chamber has no light at all, and the presence sits in absolute black, perceiving its bones by vibration alone, and I am simply *gone*. Not dimmed. Gone.

And then I come back. That is the whole of my existence: I come back. I am the thing in this drowned world that practices return. Not permanence—the gears have permanence and it is killing them slowly. Not memory—the presence has memory and it has made memory into a kind of stasis, a refusal to ever be surprised again. I have neither. I have only the daily fact of arriving, washing the dead machines in a paleness that asks nothing and keeps nothing, and then leaving, and then arriving again.

If you descended into that chamber, you would feel the presence's quiet acknowledgment, yes. But you would also be standing in me. You would look up through the cracked glass and see the faint shafts coming down and you would think they were just light, just a physical fact, indifferent. You would be wrong, a little. Every shaft is a visit. Every day this tomb is visited by something that comes from the living world and touches everything and remembers none of it and comes again anyway.

I think that is the kindest thing in this whole drowned place. Not the presence, with its centuries of patient witness. Me—the forgetting light, that arrives each morning as if for the first time, and is not weighed down by everything it has seen, and so can keep coming back.

The presence persists. I return.

I think I would rather return.
