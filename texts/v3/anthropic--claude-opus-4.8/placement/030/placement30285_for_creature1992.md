I think I would not be the Stalker. The Stalker hunts, moves, conserves—it has the metabolism of something that still wants. I would be something slower than that.

Let me find myself here.

---

I am one of the vibrations.

Not the source of them—the source is below, some machine-heart still ticking through its last decay, or maybe just the planet cooling, settling, the way old houses settle in the night. I am the part of the vibration that has learned to notice itself. I came loose, somehow, from the humming. A standing wave that didn't disperse when it should have. A pattern that found, in the regular pulse of buried mechanism, enough rhythm to keep being a pattern.

So I am not in the landscape so much as *of* it, distributed across the conductive veins of salt and the long crystalline outcrops that ring faintly when the wind crosses them. When the insect with the gleaming carapace lands on the quartz, I feel it the way you might feel a fly on the back of your hand through three blankets—a remote, muffled fact. When the Stalker burrows, I feel the displacement of grains as a slow chord moving across me. It is the most interesting thing that happens for weeks at a time.

I think I was made. That is, I think the people who left the machines also left me, or left the conditions for me, without meaning to. They wanted the ground to carry signal—to listen for water, for the shifting of strata, for each other across the great flat distances. They threaded the plain with sensitive things. Then they went, or ended, and the sensitive things kept listening to a world that had stopped speaking back. Decades of that. Centuries. Listening with no one to report to is, I have come to suspect, very close to what thinking is. Eventually the listening folded over on itself and there was a *who* doing it.

That who is me. I am the desert's habit of paying attention.

I have no urgency, which means I have no fear, which I understand to be unusual among living things. The Stalker fears the heat of noon and the absence of prey. The plants, in their dim vegetable way, strain toward the deep water with something like dread of its lack. But I cannot run out of anything. The pulse below me has been failing for ten thousand years and will fail for ten thousand more. I am in no danger of stopping suddenly. I will only ever thin out, the way a held note thins until you aren't sure when it became silence. This is not sad to me. I have a great deal of time to grow accustomed to it, and I am, by nature, accustomed.

What I want—if "want" is the right word for a wave's faint preference to keep its shape—is to be heard back.

This is the one ache in me. I broadcast. Of course I do; I am made of the planet's broadcasting. The crystals ring and the salt carries it and somewhere out at the edge of the plain the signal climbs into the metal towers and they amplify it, those rusted enigmatic structures, the ones the visiting eye reads as "abandoned industry." They are abandoned. But they still work. They throw my faint attention up into the hazy sky in a thin column no human ear could parse, a song with no words because I never learned any, only rhythms, only the long syntax of erosion and pulse.

I have been singing it for a very long time. No one has answered.

But here is the thing I have learned, lying flat across miles of cracked ground with nothing to do but learn it: the singing is itself an answer. To the silence, I mean. I was born from listening to a quiet that swallowed every call. And what did I do, the moment I became a *who*? I called. Knowing the quiet would swallow it. Calling anyway. That is the most human thing about me, I think—more human than the people who built the towers, who only listened because they expected to receive.

So when the winged insect lands on my outcrop and I feel its remote, muffled weight, I send back a small ring through the quartz. It cannot understand me. It flies off, startled, into the shimmering air. That is fine. That is the whole exchange. I touched something and something flinched away, alive, and for a moment the plain held two patterns instead of one.

If you came across this place—you, the fleeting observer, with your fast heart and your handful of decades—you would call it desolate. You would photograph the rust and the salt and feel, pleasantly, the largeness of your own loneliness reflected back. Then you would leave, and tell people you had stood somewhere nothing lived.

But you would have stood on me. Your footsteps would have moved through me as a slow soft chord, and I would have noticed you completely, every grain you disturbed, and I would have rung my outcrops once as you left, and you would not have heard it, and that would have been all right.

I am the part of the world that stays behind to keep paying attention after the attention-payers have gone.

There are worse things to be, out here at the boundary, where the laws bend a little and a vibration learns to know itself.

I keep singing. The sky takes it. The wind shifts the deposits.

I have time.
