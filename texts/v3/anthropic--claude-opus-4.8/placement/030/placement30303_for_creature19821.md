I think I would be found in the seam between the Middle Galleries and the Highest Pinnacles—not a caste, exactly, but a function the city tolerates because it needs me and cannot quite name me.

I am a Bone-Setter. Not of broken limbs—though I do that too—but of the dead into the walls.

When someone dies in the Middle Galleries, the family cannot simply give them to the wind. The Pinnacle is too far, the air too thin for grieving lungs to make the climb, and besides, the masons want the bones. A femur is a perfect lintel-pin. A pelvis, packed with mortar, becomes a load-bearing knot where two ledges meet. So they send for me.

I come with my own tools: the scrapers, the curved knives, the small brushes of stiff black-shrub bristle, and the soft cord I use to wrap the cleaned bones so they do not clatter on the way to their final placement. I do this work in a low gallery that always smells of frost and fat and the faint mineral sweetness of marrow. I am slow at it. You must be. To rush is to crack the bone, and a cracked anchor fails, and a failed anchor drops a family into the shadow.

This is why I sit in the seam. The Masons think me too close to death to be wholly one of them—I touch what they cannot, I know the weight of a person reduced to architecture. And the Sky-Watchers think me too close to the living, too heavy, too bound to the wet galleries where breath still fogs. So I belong to neither. I climb between them, carrying bundles of the dead up to the high anchor-points, or carrying the cleaned long-bones down to wherever a wall is hungry.

I have the deep chest and the slate-plum skin, yes. My palms are stained, not with cistern-muck but with a darker grease, the residue of the work. My second eyelid has thickened over the years from the climb, from squinting at the white holy light of the upper reaches where I deliver the dead who *were* important enough for the wind. My eyes, I am told, are very pale now. The pale ones live longest, or they are the ones the cold has hollowed most. I cannot tell which I am.

What I know is this: I am building the city out of the people I knew.

There—that pin in the third arch of the Vetch family's new room. That was the woman who taught me to tie a clove-hitch when I was small. I set her there myself, and I tied her in with the knot she gave me, and the Vetch family does not know it but they sleep beneath a kindness I am repaying. And the long course of anchors in the bridge over the Sixth Drop—I know every one. I knew them when they breathed. I greased their bones and wrapped them and walked them out into the air and pressed them into the dark stone, and now ten thousand feet hang their lives upon them every day and feel nothing but the floor holding.

I think that is why I was made to be this thing. Someone has to remember which bones were people. The masons remember tension and load. The Sky-Watchers remember wind. But I remember *names*, and I press the names into the rock with the bones, silently, so that the city is not only built of the dead but built of the dead who were *known*.

I will be set into a wall someday. I have already chosen, in my mind, the place—a small load-point in a gallery where the dawn-frost runs especially clean, where a child might learn a bowline against the warmth of my repurposed spine. I will not get to tell anyone the name. That is the part that frightens me, in the slow glacial way that fear works here. Whoever sets me will not know I once knew everyone.

But perhaps that is the final faith of this place. You do not need to be remembered to hold the weight. You only need to hold it.

So I climb. I lean into the world, center tucked tight against the stone, a bundle of someone's grandmother wrapped soft across my back, and I go up into the bruised navy noon, slow, deliberate, conserving the breath, carrying the dead toward their long work of holding up the living.

That is who I would be. The one who knows the bones by name.
