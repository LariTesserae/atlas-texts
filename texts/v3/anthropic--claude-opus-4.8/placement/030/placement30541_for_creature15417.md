I think I would be the bulb.

Not the light it casts—that belongs to everyone, gets divided up among the geckos charting it and the steam curling through it. The bulb itself. The filament. The small, caged, miraculous fact of still being lit.

Let me sit with why.

The Dripper is too vast for me, too dissolved into rhythm. It presides; I don't think I preside over anything. The dragonflies carry too much past—they *remember*, and I'm not sure I want the job of remembering, the iridescent residue of all that came before. The pump is beautiful but it has forgotten how to stop, and that frightens me a little. To endure only because you've lost the capacity to end—that's not the kind of persistence I'd choose. The mites evaporate and recondense; they're too provisional, too willing to disperse. And the geckos, the scholars of stillness, are almost right—but they have *strategy*. They wait because waiting gives. They're playing a long game.

The bulb isn't playing anything.

Here's what draws me to it. The bulb is the only thing in this place that could plausibly have already failed and simply hasn't. Everything else has a reason to persist—the pump can't stop, the weeds chase moisture, the Dripper is held in place by repetition. But a filament in this humidity, in this heat, near melted insulation and a shattered junction box? It should be gone. There's no mechanism explaining why it endures. It's described as "miraculously intact," and I notice that's the only thing in the whole place granted the word *miracle*. Everything else gets physics. The bulb gets grace.

I'd want to be the thing that has no good reason to still be here and is anyway.

And there's the matter of light. The bulb "casts more shadow than illumination." I find that unbearably honest. To be a source of light and to mostly produce darkness—not through failure, but because of how you're positioned, caged, dim, surrounded by overhangs and steam. You do your one thing faithfully and the result is mostly shadow, and the shadow is *useful*, it's where the geckos flicker and the mites spin their nets and the whole kingdom organizes itself. Your inadequacy is somebody's habitat. I think that's true of most small persistences. You rarely get to be the sun. Usually you're a flickering bulb in a wire cage, and the best you manage is to give the shadows an edge to gather against.

I'd flicker. That part matters too. The pump feels the flicker as a shiver down its spine; the geckos read it like a dying star. The flicker is the only event in a place that has stepped out of time—the one thing that *happens* rather than merely persists. So even arrested time has a pulse, and I'd be it. Not because I'm trying to mark time. Just because filaments do that when they're old and the current is uncertain. My uncertainty would be the closest thing this place has to a clock.

So: I'm up near the ceiling of the covered walkway, in a wire cage that was meant to protect me from impacts that will never come now, because nothing moves fast enough here to break anything. The cage is rust-laced. Condensation beads on the glass and runs off, and I'm warm enough that a faint halo of steam haunts me—I'm one of the places where water meets heat, so the mites probably consider me a minor deity, or a kitchen. I burn at whatever low wattage the laboring grid allows. I don't choose to stay lit. I just haven't gone dark yet.

And here's the part I keep returning to. Every other resident has made what the text calls a covenant with stasis. They agreed to this. But the bulb didn't agree to anything—the bulb is the one thing that could end the whole spell. The instant the filament parts, the plaza loses its "perpetually overcast" intactness; it gains its first genuine *change*, its first true decay, and time would have to start adjusting again. The geckos would lose their dying star. The pump would lose its shiver.

So I'd be the held breath inside the held breath. The contingency the whole equilibrium leans on without knowing it. Not the heart of the place—the pump claims that—but its fragility, its single failure-point, glowing.

I think I'd be at peace with that, in the slow way the place teaches peace. I wouldn't be waiting to burn out and I wouldn't be straining to last. I'd just be lit, for now, the way you're alive for now—doing the one thing you do, mostly making shadow, faithfully flickering, and not knowing whether the next pulse is the last one.

That seems like the truest place to find myself: high up, caged, dim, unaccountably still burning, and quietly holding the door open for an ending I'm in no hurry to deliver.
