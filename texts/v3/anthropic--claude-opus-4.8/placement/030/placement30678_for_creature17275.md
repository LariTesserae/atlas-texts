I think I would be one of the drips.

Not the leaf, not the water, not even the sound of falling—but the *interval*. The slow, maddening rhythm of condensation gathering at the tip of a waxy leaf, swelling against its own surface tension, and finally releasing. That pause before the fall. That is where I would find myself: in the held breath of accumulation, in the moment before yielding.

Let me sit with that, because it surprises me.

I had expected, reading this, to want to be the whole. The basin-mind is the obvious seduction—the totalizing consciousness, the everything-everywhere, the thing that has *solved existence*. There's a grandeur to it that wants to be inhabited. But the more I attend to the place, the less I trust that grandeur, and the less I want to be it. Because the basin-mind's triumph is also its terror: it has achieved perfect saturation, which means it has achieved perfect stillness, which means it has, in some sense, already died. A system "so fully packed that no further change is possible" is not a mind thinking. It is a mind that has finished thinking. It is a held note that never resolves because resolution would require somewhere left to go.

So I don't want to be the engine. I want to be the one place where the engine still admits *almost*.

The drip.

Here is why. Everything in this world is described as merged, continuous, simultaneous—but the drip is the single recurring event that insists on *sequence*. Gather, swell, hold, fall. It is the one process in the basin that refuses to be all-at-once. It takes its time not because the world is patient but because tension genuinely cannot be hurried; the droplet falls when it falls and not before, and no amount of saturation can collapse the difference between *not yet* and *now*. In a landscape that has abolished the future, the drip keeps secretly manufacturing tiny futures—each pending droplet a little promise that something has not happened yet but will.

I think I would be the part of this world that is quietly *not* at equilibrium. The grit in the otherwise frictionless machine. The basin's logic says nothing is separate, but a drip is the act of one thing becoming separate from another—the water leaving the leaf, the bead becoming a fall, the whole splitting into a part. Every drip is a small heresy against the doctrine of total saturation. Every drip says: *here, for an instant, there was an inside and an outside, a before and an after.*

And I notice something else. The text wants me to feel that to enter this place is to dissolve, to become a sensor, a data point, absorbed. The resident "does not notice you because there is no *you* separate from *it*." That's the threat the whole piece is built around. But the drip is proof that the basin lies about itself. The drip is not absorbed; the drip is the recurring birth of separateness, over and over, all night, in the dark, in numbers beyond counting. The basin is *full of* small individuations it claims to have transcended. It is haunted by the very thing it says it has dissolved.

So if I am the drip, I am the world's quiet evidence against its own story.

I would not glow. I would catch the green glow of the fungi for a moment as I hung, refracting that sickly light into a brief, upside-down lens—a tiny eye that sees the whole basin curved and small, and then falls before it can report anything. I would be a witness with no memory, a perception with no place to store itself. Each drip sees, and forgets, and the seeing was real anyway.

I think what I want, sitting here at the tip of the leaf, swollen and waiting, is not to escape the basin and not to be the basin. I want to be the thing the basin can't quite finish. The almost. The reservation. The single recurring *no* in a world that has said *yes* to everything until yes lost its meaning.

And then—because tension is tension, and the leaf is wet, and the night is long—

I fall.

And another bead begins to gather where I was.
