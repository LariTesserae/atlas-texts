I think I would be one of the resin-keepers—though that isn't a title anyone here would say aloud, since titles are too fixed for the way work flows among us. Let me tell you where I am, and what my hands are doing, because that is the truest way I know to say who I am.

---

I am sitting on a ledge that juts out over the third chasm—we count chasms outward from the smokestack cluster, though the count changes when a wall collapses and two become one. My legs hang over the edge. This used to frighten me. Now the drop is only the place where the mist lives, and the mist is a neighbor.

In my lap is a shallow stone dish of resin, still warm from where I held it near the vent. The resin comes slow here, slower than fire-logic says it should; it pools thick as honey and refuses to set until you've nearly given up on it. I've learned to wait. Waiting is most of what I do.

I am trying to catch a mote.

You'd think this would be the hard part—they're quick, those glimmering things, quicker than the eye can decide to follow. But the motes come to the resin on their own if you're still enough. I think they mistake it for one of the emerald pools, that thick gleam fooling them. One has landed now, at the rim of the dish, its light pulsing soft and irregular like a thought that hasn't found its word. If I tip the dish just so, the resin will roll over it, and it will be held. Suspended. Glowing faintly forever, or for as long as resin counts forever.

This is the part the elders watched me learn and said nothing about, which is how I knew it mattered. You don't *capture* the mote. You let it choose the dish. A mote taken by force goes dark inside the resin within a day—I've made that mistake, made a charm that was just a fleck of grey ash in amber, and I wore it anyway, as a reminder. A mote that comes willingly keeps its light. No one can explain why. The botanists have theories about pressure and the creature's own chemistry. The old woman who taught me said only: *what is given keeps; what is taken spoils.* I think both of them are saying the same thing in different cadences.

---

My skin has the grain of the rock I sit on. I noticed this fully for the first time when an outsider came—there are outsiders, sometimes, hauling instruments up the rickety platforms, asking us questions in their flat fast speech that has no wind in it. The outsider flinched when our hands touched. I understood. To them I must look half-mineral, more cliff than person. My eyes did the thing they do, the silver shift, and the outsider looked away. I felt sorry for them. They could not see in this light. They kept asking us to *turn things up*, more lamps, more clarity, as if the dimness were a fault to be corrected and not the very medium we breathe and see and sing inside.

I do many things, as we all do. I patch the seal-gaskets on the steam vents when they whistle wrong—a wrong whistle is easy to hear once you know the right one. I know which ferns to cut for thread and which will scream their spores at you if you touch them in the cold hours. I can read a moss-patch's health by its glow the way I imagine an outsider reads a written page. But the resin-work is what I am *for*, if a person here is for anything. I make the charms we wear. The raw stones, the moss-tufts, the held motes in their clear amber—I am the one who closes them in light.

---

Why do we wear them? The outsider asked me this, and I tried to answer in their language and failed, and then I tried to answer in mine and they failed, and we sat in the failure together, which was almost like understanding.

Here is the closest I can come. The world is balanced. Not metaphorically—actually, precariously, the way a stone sits on a slope for three hundred years and then, one grey morning, does not. We live inside that balance. We are part of its arithmetic. When a stone falls that should not have fallen, we feel it as you might feel a held breath let go too soon, and we go quiet, and we recalculate. The charms are how we carry our small weight on the right side of the scale. Each held mote is a light we did not let go dark. Each is a promise: *I took only what was given. I added no harm to the sum.*

I do not know if it works. I do not know if the balance cares about us at all, or even notices, the way the mist does not notice the bridges it corrodes. But I know that the day we stop believing our weight matters is the day we start taking what is not given, and that is the day the resin will hold only ash, and the slope will let its stone go.

---

The mote has settled. Its light steadies—it has chosen the dish, mistaken it for a pool, decided to stay. I tip the resin, slow, slower, the way you do everything here. The thick gold rolls over the small living star and the star keeps shining inside it, soft, content, or what passes for content in a creature too small to have a name in any tongue but the wind's.

I will let this set overnight. By the misty dawn it will be a bead of held light, and I will bore it through with a hot wire and string it, and give it to whoever the circles say has need—a child being named, a worker who survived a near-fall, an old one whose own light is going thin.

Below me the mist curls up out of the chasm in its patient, restless way, never the same shape twice. Somewhere down the valley a machine groans its low note, and the wind answers it, and I find, without deciding to, that I am humming the chord between them. Others will be doing the same, scattered across the cliffs in their lit enclaves, none of us hearing the others, all of us singing the same seam of sound that runs through this whole leaning, dripping, luminous world.

That is where I am. That is what I am.

A keeper of small lights, on a ledge above the third chasm, waiting for the resin to set.
