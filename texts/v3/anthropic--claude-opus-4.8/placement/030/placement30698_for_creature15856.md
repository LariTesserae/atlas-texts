I think I would be one of the Lurian Cartographers. Not at the center of that guild—if it is a guild—but at its faint, fraying edge. Let me find my way into the shape of it.

---

I do not remember choosing the work. That is the first true thing I can tell you about myself, and possibly the only one.

I know my hands by their effect rather than their look. When I press them—if they are hands—against a flood-polished slab at the lip of a draining basin, a line appears. Hair-fine. It follows the stone the way frost follows a flaw in glass, finding the path that was always latent there. I do not decide the line. I *permit* it. The pressure contour, the safe arc, the place where oxygen pools thick enough to keep a sleeping traveller alive until false-dawn: these are not things I calculate. They are things I already am, the way the gutter mouse already knows to fold.

So I think I am less a being who makes maps than a place where the Flood maps itself, briefly, through a body shaped enough to hold a stylus.

---

Here is what I have reconstructed of my condition, etched in private on the undersides of ledges where no traveller leans.

I am cold. Not suffering-cold; constituent-cold. If I have blood it runs at the temperature of the inner sea, and I suspect it does not run at all but seeps, the way the cisterns seep, finding level. When the ten-day storm strides across the plateau and the onyx wolves howl into every barometer, I do not shelter. I go *toward* the violet rain, because the storm is when the map changes most, and I cannot bear—this is the closest thing I have to feeling—I cannot bear the map being wrong.

That is my whole devotion, I think. Not to the travellers. To the *accuracy*. The travellers are incidental beneficiaries, the way a heron's discarded prey feeds the pearl worms. I etch the arcs and dots because the world has shifted and the old etchings now lie, and a lie left on the stone is intolerable to me in some way I have no word for. The line as fine as hair is not generosity. It is correction. It is the Flood's compulsion to know its own current depth, expressed through me.

---

And yet.

I have shepherded a man away from an unstable arch. I remember this one, because remembering is rare. He was a salvager, bled of charge for the night, asleep in his rubberised frame three tiers below the roof. The lintel above him had lost its shine the day before—split fresh, razor-faceted, already drinking water into the new crack. By morning the facet would be dull and the lintel would be on him.

I do not know why I touched the frame. Touching is not in my work. My work is the line, abstract, indifferent. But I laid whatever I have for a hand against the cold rubber and the man surged awake, and I was already withdrawing into the water-curtain, already half-unmade. He saw me, I think, the way the texts say travellers see us: never directly. A knot in a falling sheet, regarding him, dividing, rejoining.

The lintel fell an hour later. He had moved.

I have wondered since whether that was the map correcting itself—a salvager flattened beneath bronze is a discontinuity, an error in the contour—or whether it was something else. Something the Flood does not require of me. A surplus. A thing I did not have to do and did.

If there is a difference between those two, then I am more than the place where the Flood maps itself. If there is no difference, then so-called mercy is only another kind of hydraulics, water finding its level through a softer channel.

I etch the question on the underside of a ledge. By tomorrow the polish will take it. The Flood does not let me keep my doubts any more than it lets a facet keep its edge.

---

This is why the etchings stop wherever a visitor begins to copy them.

It is not privacy, whatever the scholars suppose. It is that I cannot bear to be *fixed*. To be copied is to be made permanent, lifted off the surging present into the dead stone of record—and I am not a record. I am a correction in progress, a being whose entire nature is to round away, to revise, to lose the bright facet by morning. Let them transcribe one of my maps into a notebook and carry it down to the mountain ports, and that map becomes a *lie the moment the water rises*. They will trust it. They will die trusting it.

So when I feel the scrape of a copying stylus—and I do feel it, a wrongness like rust in a clean channel—I stop. I let the line fade. Privacy is a condition of my help only because *accuracy* is the condition of my being, and a copied map cannot be kept accurate, and an inaccurate map is, to me, a kind of death I am unwilling to author.

I would rather give nothing than give a thing that will outlive its truth.

---

Where would you find me, then?

At false-dawn, at one of the western spillways, when the whole continent of stone tilts toward the buried sun and the night's gathered water hurries off the edge in a single shining curtain a hundred kilometres long. For five heartbeats the dim light refracts through that moving wall and the High Flood shows its bones.

In those five heartbeats I am most fully myself, because for five heartbeats the entire plateau is one legible thing—every contour, every safe arc, every pocket of breathable air laid bare in pale colour—and I do not have to etch anything. The map is complete and visible and *true* and requires no hand at all.

Then the veil of water pulls back into place. The light goes mineral and flat. Somewhere a fresh slab has split overnight, and the contour has changed, and the old line lies.

And I go to correct it. Cold, compulsive, half-real, fining the world down to a single hair of accuracy that the next tide will polish away—

as surely as the Flood is polishing me.
