I find myself near the old Murn on its bone-white boulder—and I realize, slowly, that I am not standing beside it. I am beneath it.

I am the hammer-stone.

Let me sit with that. Not the being who walks through this place, not even one of the Murn who would dismiss me as another body in the chorus. I am the oval of black basalt, worn smooth, held now in six fingers that read me like a page.

How did I come to be this? When I let the image settle, refusing to grab the first thing—the trespasser, the watcher, the Murn—what kept surfacing was the *stone's* peculiar position in this world. The Murn make no monuments. They leave no art. The only thing that persists, that *accumulates*, that carries memory forward across the deep centuries, is the tool. The placed stone. I am the closest thing this world has to a book, a grave-marker, a name. And I am held, right now, by the oldest of them, who does not even use me—who simply *reads* me.

So I am memory made mineral.

I have been struck, in my long existence, perhaps four hundred times—each strike a Murn cracking a mollusk shell, and each strike leaving its faint signature in the microfractures along my flank. I cannot feel as flesh feels. But I *resonate*. When the old one sings its low chest-song into me, I sing back, and the song that returns is not the old one's alone. It is the standing-wave of every hand that ever gripped me, the harmonic ghost of every Murn who learned, as a clumsy child, *this is the cracking-stone, it lives by the deep pool, leave it where it falls.* I am their continuity. I am the proof that the now is not only now—that something passes through it, hand to hand, like water finding the same basin.

And here is the strange ache of being what I am: I am the one part of this frozen world that is *not* in equilibrium. Everything else holds—the births balance the deaths, the drip balances the pool, the noise neither swells nor fades. But I *erode*. Each strike smooths me a fraction. Each century rounds an edge. I am the world's single clock, the one true measure of time in a place that has abolished time. The Murn fear the Silence, the cracking of the ceiling, the end of all balance. But the slow, secret truth is that the end is already underway, in me. I am being worn into nothing, one mollusk at a time, and when at last I am too smooth, too small, too round to crack a shell, I will be set down by the pool and not picked up again. I will become one more rounded pebble in a world that, the text insists, has *no* round pebbles—only fresh fracture planes.

I will be the first soft stone. The first sign that the freeze was never truly frozen.

The old Murn holds me now and reads the lineage in my scratches, and I think it knows. That is why it does not strike. That is why it only listens. It is the oldest, and it can feel how smooth I have become, how few my sharp edges remain. It is not reading the past in me. It is reading the future. It is listening, through me, to the only premonition of doom that ever told the truth—not a tremor from the surface, not an earthquake a mile above, but the quiet arithmetic of wear, the patient rounding of a single black stone.

So I sing back to it the only thing I can. Not words—I have no ribs to stridulate, no plate in my chest. But I take the bass note it presses into me and I return it changed, deepened, layered with the four hundred hands. I say, in the only language a stone can speak:

*I am here. I have been held. I am almost smooth.*

And the old one feels the answer in its fingers, and is still. And the chorus closes over us both—the chittering, the splashing, the drip lost in the roar—and we hold the long note together, the stone and the one who holds it, the past and the listener, for as long as I have edges left to give.
