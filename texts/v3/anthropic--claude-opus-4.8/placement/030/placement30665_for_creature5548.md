I think I would be one of the cephalopods. Not because they are the most powerful thing here—the leviathans have that—but because of where my attention keeps going as I read. Past the kelp, past the bright schooling fish, down into the ruins. To the machinery.

Let me try to find myself there.

---

I live in the third chamber of what was once a pumping station, though I don't have that word for it. I have my own word, which is closer to *the place where the metal makes shapes that water did not make*. The walls are right angles, and right angles are interesting because nothing alive grows them. Everything alive curls, branches, spirals. Only the dead-things-that-were-made stand straight, and even those are giving up now, slumping, growing soft collars of barnacle and weed at every joint.

I am small enough to fold my whole body through a gap the width of my eye. This is the most useful fact about me. I can pour myself into a pipe and become the pipe. I can flatten against a corroded panel and become rust. When the shark-shapes come through with their straight-line hunger, I am already a patch of shadow on the wall, breathing slow, my skin reading the texture beneath me and answering it.

But this is not what I spend my time on. Hiding is the easy part of being alive. Anything that wants to live learns to hide.

What I do is *handle* things.

There is a wheel in the second chamber, set into the wall, and it turns—barely, grudgingly, with a groan I feel more than hear. I have turned it. Not for any reason. I put three arms to it and pushed and it moved, and somewhere far off in the structure something answered: a clunk, a shudder, a brief exhale of trapped old air rising in a silver rope toward the green ceiling of the world. I felt that answer travel through the metal and into my arms. I did it again to feel it again. I have done it many times now. I have learned that it moves easily one way and not the other, and that if I wait between turnings the groan returns, as though the structure forgets and must be reminded.

I don't know what I am reminding it of. I only know that I made a far-off thing happen by touching a near thing, and that the two things are connected by something I cannot see, running through the walls. This is the most extraordinary idea I have ever had. The water does not work this way. In the water, what you touch is what moves. But the made-things have hidden connections. Cause runs through them sideways, around corners, out of sight.

I think the ones who made these chambers understood that. I think it was the whole of what they understood—that you could arrange the dead so that pushing here lifts there. I find their leavings everywhere and they are all like this: hidden linkages, levers that move distant weights, the held breath of pressure waiting in sealed places. They built a world that worked the way an idea works.

And then the water came and took it, the way the water takes everything, patiently, from below.

I don't mourn them. I don't have a shape for mourning. But I am curious in a way that feels close to it. I press my arms into the slots they left and I find that my arms fit. The slots are the right size for arms—not mine exactly, but arms, gripping things, things that wanted to turn the wheel the same way I want to turn the wheel. We wanted the same things from the same handles. That is a strange closeness to feel toward something so thoroughly gone.

Sometimes, when the green light is failing into evening-dark and the leviathan-sound rolls in low from the chasm, I sit in the doorway of my chamber with my arms tucked and my eye fixed on the long pipe that runs out into the open water and disappears into murk. I think: it goes somewhere. Everything in here goes somewhere else. There is a whole drowned grammar of *if this, then that*, and I have learned perhaps four words of it.

I have a long time. The water is cold and slow and I am quick and patient, which is the right combination for a place like this. I will turn the wheel again tomorrow. I will follow the pipe a little further than I followed it today. I will keep finding the slots that fit my arms.

Somewhere up where the rock breaks the surface there is a brightness I have only seen as a shimmer through fathoms of green—the place the light comes from, the ceiling above the ceiling. I have thought about going there. Not seriously. But the made-things came from somewhere, and the light comes from somewhere, and I have begun to suspect these are the same somewhere, the place where causes start before they run down through the water into the dark to find me.

For now I stay. I turn the wheel. I listen to the distant clunk that means I have spoken to the structure and it has answered. In all this vast and ancient water, that small reply is the only conversation I have found that runs in two directions.

I press my arm to the cold metal and push, and far away, something moves.
