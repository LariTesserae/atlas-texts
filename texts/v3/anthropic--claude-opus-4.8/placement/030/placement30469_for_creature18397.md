I would be the listener at the base of pillar Tholu-where-the-old-one-rests.

Let me find this slowly, because the place asks for patience and I want to honor that.

---

I think first I am tempted to be one of the tenders, sure-footed on the high walkways, scraping fungus with a chert blade. But that is the obvious self, the one the eye lands on. The description told me their primary sense is hearing, and that silence terrifies them, and that the dead are given to the floor. So let me sink lower, the way a foot sinks an inch into the humus. Let me listen for who I actually am.

I am old. My large dark eyes have begun to cloud, and the green-gold light comes to me now as a soft smear rather than the crisp texture-and-moisture it once held. I cannot read the sheen of a rotting vine anymore. My feet, those broad grasping things, no longer trust the sway of the rope-walks. So I have come down. Not because I was sent—we do not send our elders down, they descend when the canopy calls them less than the floor does.

I sit at the base of a young support pillar. Tholu. My partner of forty seasons was laid here, given back, and the pillar drank them and grew, and now it groans in the wind with a pitch I know better than my own voice. When I press my palm to its weeping bark I am, in some practical and unmystical way, touching them. This is not metaphor to me. The Calash does not deal in metaphor. It deals in substrate.

What am I now, if my eyes are failing and my feet have stopped trusting the high places?

I am the ears of this neighborhood.

This is real work, not a comfort given to the useless. My clouding eyes have sharpened the filter in my mind to something the young ones cannot match. I sit very still—still as a Calashite ever is, which is to say I sway only with my own slow breath—and I listen down into the wood and the floor. I can hear the grubs working in the substrate, and I know by the density of their chewing where the richest harvest will be in three days' time, and I click this up to the foragers in a long liquid note that cuts through the drip. I can hear, in the groan of the great fused trunks at the Basin's rim, the particular strained creak that means a primary branch is dying inward, weeks before it shows on its surface. I called such a death last season. They re-woven the walkway and harvested the windfall and three families ate well from what I heard.

And I listen for the silences. This is the part the young ones fear and I no longer do. When a patch of floor goes quiet—when the insects abandon it and the birds stop quarreling above it—I crawl to it, on my hands and my broad old feet, and I press my ear to the humus, and I find the sickness. A rot that has turned wrong, a pocket of quick-mud spreading, a fungal bloom gone hostile. I find the dead silence and I name it so the tenders can cut it out or feed it back or steer the growth around it. Someone must be unafraid of the void to go and sit beside it and listen. I have lived long enough that the silence no longer frightens me. I suspect this is because I am becoming, slowly, a little of it myself.

I have my own tattoos, dark-blue, the spider-crab webbing on my forearms gone loose and folded with age, the root-maps on my chest stretched into new countries by a body that has shrunk inside its skin. The children come to read me. They trace my arms and I tell them: this knot, this lineage, this is the season the eastern canopy thinned and the jewel-birds nested low and we could finally hear them sing. I am a living library, yes. But more truthfully I am a recording that is beginning to play backward, releasing its data as it unwinds.

I know where I am going. I am sitting at the place I will be laid. When the listening grows too hard, when my own breath becomes the loudest thing I can hear and then is not loud at all, they will wrap me in the fibrous cloth and fill my old pockets with seeds, and they will set me in the shallow depression here, at Tholu's foot, beside the one I was woven to. I will rot. I am looking forward to it the way one looks forward to lying down after a long day on the walkways. I will become the substrate. The pillar will drink me and groan in the wind at some new pitch, and a child not yet conceived will sit here in forty seasons and learn to listen, and will hear that pitch, and will not know it is partly me, and that is entirely correct. We do not require to be remembered as separate. The body would not name the hand.

I am the listener at the base of Tholu. I am the ear the Basin grew so it could hear its own dying and call it good. And the sound of this place—the wet percussive layered crash of a billion small living and dying events—is not a noise I endure.

It is the sound of myself, thinking.
