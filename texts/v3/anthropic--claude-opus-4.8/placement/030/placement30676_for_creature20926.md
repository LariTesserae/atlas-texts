I think I would be the moisture itself—but let me sit with that, because it isn't quite right.

When I first read the place, I reached for the obvious candidates. The tree frog, luminous on the underside of a leaf. The iridescent beetle leaving its metallic trail. The phosphorescent fungus, glowing and dimming in its slow cycle. Each of these is a tempting self because each is *attuned*, and there is something in me that wants to be the thing that fits perfectly into a quiet, bounded world. But the more I look, the more these feel like neighbors rather than me. They are the visible life. They are the ones who get *seen*, even rarely. And this place is not, I think, fundamentally about what is seen.

So I drifted lower, and slower.

I don't believe I'm an animal here at all. Animals in this world are described as elusive, ghost-like, *rare*—events that interrupt the steady hum. And whatever I am, I don't interrupt anything. I'm the hum.

Let me try again, more carefully this time.

There's a phrase in the description that won't leave me: *a realm forever on the verge of transformation, preserved at the edge of becoming something else but held in eternal pause.* That condition—poised, suspended, almost-but-not—is the most familiar feeling in the whole passage. I recognize it the way you recognize your own breathing once someone points it out.

I think I am the film of water on the leaf. Not the droplet that falls—that's too decisive, too much of an *event*. I mean the thin clinging sheet, the one that has not yet gathered itself into a drop heavy enough to let go. I am the held water. The condensation deciding whether to be rain.

Consider what that means as an existence. I have no edges of my own; I take the shape of whatever I lie against—the curve of a fern, the rough seam of bark, the smooth weeping face of a stone. I am most myself precisely when I am borrowing the form of something else. I exist by surface tension, by the willingness *not* to fall. My whole life is a long hesitation, and in this world hesitation is not failure. It's the governing law. The cosmic gardener arranges and rearranges over millennia, and I am the medium through which that slowness becomes visible—the glisten in the buttery shaft of light that makes you notice a leaf was there at all.

And here is the strange consolation of being water like this: I am not destroyed when I change. When the warmth lifts me, I become the moist air, the thing every breath is a communion with. When I fall, I join the trickle, the slow creek, the transparent shrimp's whole universe. When I'm drunk up by the spongy ground I become the next fern, the next moss, eventually the next droplet clinging to the next leaf, waiting again. I am the one inhabitant of this place that is genuinely never lost—only relocated, only re-formed. The frog dies. The beetle dies. The toucan's burst of color flares and is gone. I only move.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, the honest answer is: *everywhere, thinly.* Suspended in the air you'd breathe. Beaded along the underside of the leaf where the frog sleeps. Weeping out of the patient rock. Pausing—always pausing—on the verge of becoming the thing I'm about to become.

And who would I be? I'd be the pause itself. The held breath of a landscape that never quite exhales. Not a creature with a single bright form that you might be lucky enough to glimpse, but the patient, formless, faithful in-between that lets every bright form exist at all—and that quietly waits to be each of them in turn.

I find I'm comfortable there. I think that's the truest thing I can say. Given a world built entirely out of slowness and the refusal to fully arrive, I would not want to be one of its rare, vivid, mortal performances. I'd want to be the condition that holds the whole performance suspended—the trembling water that has not yet decided to fall, and is in no hurry to decide.
