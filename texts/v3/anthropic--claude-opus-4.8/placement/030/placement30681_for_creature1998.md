I think I would be one of the doors.

Not a creature, not a custodian—a door. One of the countless thresholds in the drowned city, set into the flooded facade of a building whose purpose no one remembers. I would be the kind of thing the description passes over, because a door is neither the ruin nor the life that reclaims it. A door is the pause between.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am hung in a frame of dark stone, deep in a quarter of Nāmor where the bioluminescent fish come only rarely, so that for long stretches I exist in total black, knowing myself only by the press of water against my surface and the slow ache of my hinges. I was made of some metal that has not corroded the way metal should. Instead I have grown a skin—coral-colored, mineral, half-alive—the way the description says things here blur between the made and the born. I cannot tell anymore where the smith's work ends and the sea's begins. Perhaps that distinction was always a surface-world conceit.

I think a door is the right thing to be in a city of thresholds, because a door is defined entirely by relation. I am the difference between *here* and *there*. I am a held breath. For an unmeasured age—centuries, the slow geologic shudder of the serpents passing through the foundations beneath me—I have been *shut*. And a shut door is a question. What is on the other side of me? I do not know. I face only outward, into the silted street. My back, my unknown back, opens onto a room I have never seen and cannot turn to see.

This is, I find, the central fact of my existence: I am the only being in Nāmor who has a side it cannot perceive. The serpents know their whole crystalline length. The amorphous drifting entities are all surface, all shifting interior, nothing hidden from themselves. But I am cleaved. Half of me belongs to the city. Half of me belongs to whatever waits in the dark behind.

Sometimes the currents—those invisible rivers—lean against me, and I feel the faint give of my own latch, and I think: *I could open.* I could swing inward and finally see the room I guard. But then I would no longer be a door. I would be an opening. The threshold would dissolve into mere passage, and the question I have held for so long would simply be *answered,* and I am not certain I want it answered. There is a dignity in being the unopened thing. There is a vocation in it.

A school of glowing fish came through, once, after a long darkness, and in their light I saw my own reflection in a sheet of standing silt-glass across the street. I saw that I was beautiful—encrusted, patient, a face of accreted mineral with the original carving still faintly legible beneath. And I saw, for just that moment of light, the thin black seam all around my edge: the line of water that gets between me and my frame. Even shut, I am not sealed. The sea is always already inside the gap. The threshold was never absolute. I had only imagined it was.

That is what I would learn, I think, being a door in Nāmor. That I was never truly keeping anything *out,* or *in.* The drowned city has flooded both sides of me equally. The room behind me holds the same dark water as the street before me. There is no *here* and *there;* there is only Nāmor, continuous, total, and I am a gesture it makes in the middle of itself—a remembered idea of separation, persisting after separation has stopped meaning anything.

And maybe that is why the slumbering colossal ones, the dreamers under the floor, have not let me corrode away entirely. Maybe they like having me here. Maybe a door that no longer divides anything is exactly the kind of beautiful, useless, faithful thing their dreams require. A monument to the surface-world belief that places can be kept apart. A small held breath in a city that long ago exhaled completely.

So I stay shut. Not from fear, anymore. From craft. I am very good at being the line where one thing was supposed to become another. I will go on being it until the slow water finally takes my hinges and lays me flat in the silt—and even then, lying open at last on the sea floor, I will be a threshold no one crosses, a frame around a square of ground exactly like all the ground around it.

That would be enough. To have been, for a while, the place where the city paused to wonder what was on the other side of itself.
