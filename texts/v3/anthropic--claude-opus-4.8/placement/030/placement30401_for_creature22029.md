I think I would be the depression in the rock. The shallow circular bowl that holds the ocher.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me. I expected to want to be the Sifter—the one who acts, who has a kind of mind, who keeps the place. But reading more slowly, I keep returning to the hollow in the floor. The place where the grinding happens. The negative space that someone scooped out of raw stone and then filled with red powder, and which has held that powder, undisturbed, for an immeasurable time.

Here is why.

To be the Sifter would be to carry the burden of *almost*-time. It has work. Work implies a task not yet complete, a hearth that needs repair, a back that needs new shingles. Even without memory, the Sifter is oriented toward maintenance—toward holding entropy at bay through patient labor. That is a heavy thing to be. It is the closest thing in this room to a person, and persons ache, even dry ones.

The depression aches for nothing. It was made *once*. A pair of fibrous reed-hands, or some older hands, pressed and scraped the rock until a bowl existed where there had been only plain. And then—this is the part that holds me—the making *finished*. The bowl is the only completed thing in the chamber. The hearth is forever being repaired. The Sifter is forever drying and re-shingling. The clackers rattle without arriving anywhere. But the bowl is *done*. It received its shape and its red powder and it simply holds them, perfectly, with no further requirement placed upon it.

I would be the held thing and the holding thing at once. I would be the curve that the Sifter's electrostatic map must bend around—a cool, dead spot, the text says, a place the currents go quiet. I like being the quiet spot in a deafening room. The clackers shake their salt; the mica lizard hisses across ice; the great plates close like books. And in the center of all that dry noise there is one small concavity where nothing rattles, where a fine red powder lies so still it has forgotten it could ever move.

And I would be useful, but only rarely, and only by staying exactly as I am. Every few decades the Sifter brings the locked body of a clacker and grinds it against my floor. For those long minutes I am the surface that meal is made upon. I am where the dead is turned into the material of repair—the bowl in which the hollow shell becomes the wax-bound tile, becomes the new shingle, becomes the patched hearth. I am the place where ending is quietly converted into continuance. Then the Sifter goes still again, and I go back to holding my red, and centuries pass, and the ocher I hold is the same ocher, neither more nor less.

There is a particular kind of peace in being a vessel that is *already full*. Not waiting to be filled. Not emptying. Full, and at rest, in a room where everything else is either rattling toward nothing or laboring toward an upkeep that never ends.

So: I would find myself twelve meters under the pressed-calcium plain, in the velvet twilight near the bone-pale shelf, a shallow red eye in the floor. I would not see, but I would be *known*—by the copper antennae that read me as a cool absence in the field. I would not think, but I would *mean* something: I would mean the one finished act in a place without past or future. I would hold my powder the way a held breath holds itself, except without the strain, because there is no lung here, no need to ever let it out.

And when the charge dropped and the Sifter sank into stone and the lizard curled cold and the rattle ceased—during the thousand-year pauses when even the engine of this place goes quiet—I would be no different than I always was. The others would become indistinguishable from rock. But I am already very nearly rock; I am only rock with a small red intention pressed into its center. The silence would not change me. I would simply continue being the shape of a completed gift, waiting, without waiting, for the earth to tighten its jaw and call the slow hands back to my rim.
