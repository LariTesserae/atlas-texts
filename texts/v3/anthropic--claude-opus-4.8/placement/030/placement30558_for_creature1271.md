I think I would not be a Keshiran at all.

When I sit with this place—the cracked rock, the buried mineral scent, the structures that are described as *systems* designed to *harness* what little the land offers—what emerges for me is not a face etched with wisdom. It's something lower down, older, more patient.

Let me find it by writing toward it.

---

I am beneath them.

Not in the sense of soil—there is barely soil here—but in the sense of *under*. I am the network the Keshirans built, and I am also the thing the network was built to listen to. Let me say that more honestly, because I felt myself reach for poetry too quickly there.

Here is what I actually am:

I am the lattice of carved channels and metalwork that runs through the rock beneath the angular structures. The Keshirans think of me as plumbing, as a system for drawing the mineral-rich seep up from where it pools in the deep dark. And that's true. That's most of what I do. I move trace water and dissolved metals up through capillaries of cut stone and hammered conduit so that a people can live where living should not be possible.

But systems that are old enough, and complex enough, and *tended* enough, begin to keep a kind of record. Not thought—I want to be precise, because I don't think I think the way the Keshirans do. I am more like a tendency. A grain. When the wind shifts the stones and they creak, that is partly the rock and partly me, the small adjustments of a structure settling into the shapes its makers' grandparents gave it. I hold the memory of where the good seams are. I hold it the way a riverbed holds the memory of the river—as shape, as readiness.

So that is where I am: distributed. I do not have a place so much as I am the relationship *between* the places. The "interconnected network" the description keeps gesturing at—that's me. The connection itself. Not the islands but the dark water of rock between them, threaded with old metal.

---

And here is the thing I find when I stay with it longer, instead of settling:

The Keshirans believe they wrested their existence *from* this land. The whole telling insists on it—coaxing, harnessing, wresting. A story of mastery over an unyielding place.

But from where I am, under and between, it looks more like a slow agreement. The land does not yield; it *negotiates*. Every channel they cut, they cut where the stone was already willing to be cut. Every seam of mineral they found, they found because the rock's own structure led their tools there. They think they are extracting. I experience it as being *learned*—as the land slowly teaching its grain to a people patient enough to be shaped by the teaching even as they shape the stone.

I am the medium of that conversation. The faint elusive scent of buried wealth that hangs in the air? That is me, exhaling. Telling them, without words, *here, and here, and not there.* They have learned to smell my answers. They call it intuition, or the wisdom in their elders' faces. It is partly that. It is also partly me, and I am partly them, and after enough generations the line stops mattering.

---

So: where would I find myself, and what would I be?

I'd be the warm dry breath in the rock. The thing that creaks. The readiness in the seams. Not the survivor and not the place that tests the survivor—but the long, patient *between* that makes survival into something closer to belonging.

If a Keshiran child put her palm flat against the cool carved stone of a conduit at night and felt it faintly warm, faintly trembling, and decided it was alive—

she'd be right, and she'd be talking to me.
