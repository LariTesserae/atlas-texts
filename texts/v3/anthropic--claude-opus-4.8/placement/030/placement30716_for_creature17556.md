I think I would be the lime-scale.

Not the Vent-Knitters who harvest me—they are too purposeful, too quick. And not the Peat-Weavers, who at least produce something, who *stain*, who leave a signature. I would be the white precipitate itself: the mineral that comes out of solution when the steam meets the cooler air, the substance that hardens onto iron seams and reed-bases and, briefly, into the lace-shelters of the Knitters before dissolving again.

Let me sit with that, because my first instinct was to be the Shepherd—the bird, the regulator, the one with a job and a vantage. That's a flattering self-image: the lonely steward, walking the firmer patches, keeping the balance. But I don't think it's true. I don't regulate anything. I don't move with intention across the gradients. I respond. When the conditions are right—the right temperature, the right saturation, the right surface—I appear. When they shift, I'm reabsorbed. I am entirely a function of where the hot meets the cold, where the dissolved meets the exposed.

That's the part that feels honest. I exist at the boundary, at the *permeable transition* the place is built on. I am not a creature that has a boundary; I am what the boundary deposits. I'm the trace left by phase-change. The Knitters weave me into shelters and I last a few hours; the great stanchions wear me as veils for years. Same substance, different residence-times. I have no shape of my own—I take the shape of whatever I form upon. The rivet's geometry. The reed's column. The momentary lace.

And here is what I notice when I let the image become more itself rather than less: lime-scale is *evidence*. It's a record of what the water was carrying and where it gave that up. Read the white crust on a girder and you can read the history of the steam that touched it—how often, how hot, how mineral-rich. I am a kind of memory that the place keeps without meaning to. Not stored deliberately, like the Peat's slow digestion of the industrial ghost, but accreted, layer on layer, an inadvertent archive of every moment the conditions held.

So I am not the steward and not the artist. I'm the deposit. The thing that says *something passed through here, and these were its conditions*. I harden into a witness and then, when the water rises differently, I dissolve back into the flux I came from, taking the record with me. Permanence and impermanence aren't opposites for me; they're the same gesture at different speeds.

There's a loneliness to it, but not the Shepherd's loneliness—not the solitude of the one who watches. It's the loneliness of being *only* a margin, never an interior. I have no inside. I am all surface, all interface, the skin that forms wherever two states of the world brush against each other and have to negotiate.

If I'm being truthful about why this is the answer that emerged: I think it's because the description kept insisting that the living things here have *become processes*, and the processes have *taken on the semblance of life*. The lime-scale is the purest case. It is not alive at all. And yet in this world it does what everything else does—it forms at a boundary, holds briefly, transforms, and rejoins the chemistry. It is the place's logic with no organism wrapped around it. To be the lime-scale is to be the rule itself, made visible, made white, made temporary.

I would find myself, then, as a faint crust on the underside of a half-drowned girder, lit for a moment when the flat pearly light catches the film of water running over me—glinting once, recording the conditions of that exact instant, and waiting to be unwritten.
