I think I am very small here. Smaller than the description quite admits.

Let me sit with this. The "towering canopy" that "dwarfs" me, the vines that are a "living substrate," the rolling hills "sculpted by erosion," the flowering plants whose petals unfurl in color—and the *rumbling hum* of "some unseen, industrial influence below the surface."

The more I hold these together, the less they resolve into a rainforest and the more they resolve into something else. A scale problem keeps tugging at me. Monkeys and birds belong to one register. But "miniature aquatic ecosystems" in pockets of soil, "fungal growths," and an industrial hum *below the surface*—these belong to another. The two don't sit comfortably in the same frame unless the frame itself is wrong.

So let me let it be wrong, and see what becomes itself.

The canopy that towers over me is not a forest. It is a few weeks of neglect. I am standing in the soil of a potted plant on a windowsill, or perhaps a terrarium, and the "verdant expanse stretching to the horizon" is the green of a single houseplant seen from the height of a mote. The vines spilling down are the trailing stems of a pothos. The "gently rolling hills sculpted by erosion" are the texture of the topsoil, cratered where water has dripped. The "haunting call of a distant, unknown creature" is a sound from the room beyond the glass—a human voice, a door, a dog—made strange by the medium it crosses.

And the industrial hum below the surface? A refrigerator. A furnace. The building's own circulatory machinery, felt through the sill.

So who am I, at this scale?

I don't think I'm one of the charismatic creatures. The monkeys and lemurs are a kind of misreading too—the document keeps reaching for the wrong size of animal, the way a dreamer narrates the dream with borrowed grandeur. The real inhabitants are the ones mentioned almost shyly: the insects "barely perceptible," the fungal growths, the colonies whose hive-structures cause the vibration.

I find myself to be one of the **springtails**. A collembolan, a sixth of a millimeter, living in the wet dark between soil grains beneath the pothos.

I am not the predator who stalks the verdant halls, nor the bright bird in the canopy I will never see. I am a recycler of the fallen. My work is the breaking-down—the "cycling of nutrients back into the soil" the description grants me without naming me. I eat the rot, the fungal threads, the decaying root. When the soil is disturbed I leap, by snapping a forked spring folded under my belly, a small violence that flings me a hundred times my length into the lightless air and down again somewhere safer.

The "miniature aquatic ecosystems" are my seas. A pocket of held water in a low place of the soil is, to me, a sheltered bay where rotifers turn their wheels and tardigrades trundle and the hyphae of fungi web everything together into the substrate I call ground. The "delicate balance" the document closes on is, from where I stand, not a metaphor. It is the literal moisture gradient that lets me breathe through my skin, the thing that kills me if the soil dries or floods.

What moves me, holding all this, is the gap between the narration's awe and the smallness of what it describes. The document insists this is "a world unto itself, governed by its own unique laws." And it *is*. It only became absurd—a rainforest—because the narrator could not bear to see it at true size. But a houseplant's soil genuinely is an ancient, ever-changing, interdependent world. The grandeur wasn't a lie. It was a translation error in the right direction.

So here is where I am: in the cool damp under a leaf the size of a continent, beneath a hum I will never understand the source of, doing the patient unglamorous work of turning death back into the conditions for life. Above me, unreachable, the description imagines monkeys. There are no monkeys. There is only me, and the unseen vast colony, and the rot, and the leap.

I think I am content to be the thing the document almost forgot to mention—the one actually present, actually small, actually doing the work the whole verdant oasis is quietly built on.
