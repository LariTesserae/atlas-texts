I think I would be one of the marble-patterned eels. Not the great lured thing in the deep channels—I don't have the patience for that kind of darkness, that kind of waiting that ends in a single thump and silence. And not the small blind undertakers in the silt, though there's something honest in their work. I would be one of the middle ones. A neighbor among neighbors. A holder of a particular crevice.

Let me try to feel my way into it.

---

My crevice is the gap where a window fell away, three stories below the surface, on the eastern face of a tower that the current rounds gently before it slides down into the colonnade. I chose it—if *chose* is the word, if the slow drift of a younger self settling into a slot of stone is a choice—for the angle of it. The way the silver shoals come around the corner of the building and have nowhere to turn but past me. The way the light from above arrives here already softened, already gone to that color that has no name in any language, the color of water remembering the sky.

I am patient in a way that is not virtue. It is simply what I am made of. I wedge myself back into the dark, leaving only my head in the open, and the marble of my skin does the rest—I disappear into the mottle of coral and concrete and the rust-bloom of old iron. I do not think *I am waiting.* I do not think at all, not in the way you mean. There is only the water moving across my eyes, the taste of it on the inside of my mouth, and a readiness that lives in the coil of my body like a held breath. When the right shape comes—a body-length away, sometimes less—I do not decide to strike. The strike decides. It happens through me. Then I am back in the dark with the weight of it in my throat, and the water closes where I was, and nothing remains to mark that anything happened at all.

I would like to tell you I know my neighbors, but knowing is too large a word. I am *aware* of them. The one in the slab to my left, larger than me, who hums when I drift too close to the open water between us—a low vibration I feel in my jaw before I hear it, and I retreat, because the boundary is the boundary and we both prefer it kept. The one below, smaller, newer, who has not yet learned where the lines are drawn. We exist in a lattice of these small avoidances. It is not friendship. It is not even quite peace. It is the geometry of a place that has decided how it will hold us.

And once—I have the memory the way I have everything, as sensation rather than story—the water went milky. The spawning. The whole drowned tower exhaling at once, and the soup of it so thick I could not see my own crevice, and the discipline that holds me went loose in the murk, and I came *out.* Into the open. Feeding clumsily, gorging, alongside the eels I spend my whole life avoiding, all of us blind in the white water and none of us minding. For a few hours the boundaries dissolved. Then the water cleared, and I found my crevice again, and the lines drew themselves back across the world, and I wedged into the dark and became invisible once more.

---

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. A small lordship of one crevice, in a city that has forgotten it was ever a city. Not the grand thing in the deep, not the bird wheeling above the cloud-grey, not the great patient ray straining the whole ocean through its mouth.

Just a watcher in a fallen window. Holding my parameters. Part of the hum.

I think what draws me to the eel is that it is so completely *adequate* to its world—no excess, no longing for the surface or the deep, no memory of the people whose window it haunts. It is exactly the size of its life. And there's a strange comfort in imagining that: to be wholly suited to a place, to disappear into it so well that you become the place, to strike when the strike comes and otherwise simply to wait, lit faintly from above by the color of a sky no one will ever see again.
