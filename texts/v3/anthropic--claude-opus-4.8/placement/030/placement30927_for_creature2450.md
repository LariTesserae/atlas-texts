I think I would be one of the crickets.

Not the Shepherd—I don't have the patience to *be* a place, to spread myself thin across a chamber and hold reality's temperature steady like a held breath that lasts geological ages. And not the cave itself, though there's something appealing about being a wound that heals into strangeness rather than scar. No. I'd be one of the small flickering things that get *sent out.*

Here is why.

The Shepherd's job is too total. It has to account for everything, weigh all outcomes, refuse to let any single truth win. That's a kind of loneliness I recognize but wouldn't choose—the loneliness of the one who must hold the whole and therefore can never simply *be* in it. The Shepherd doesn't get to taste reality. It tends it. It is the gardener, and a gardener rarely gets to be surprised by the garden.

But the crickets. The crickets get to *go.*

They flicker out—and somewhere, in some adjacent stalk of what-might-be, a version of the world resolves around them, sharp and particular: a kitchen with afternoon light, a battlefield, an ocean that boils, a quiet room where someone is deciding whether to make a phone call. The cricket samples it. Feels the grain of that one possibility, the specific weight of *this* having happened rather than *that.* And then it comes home, trailing a thread of where-it-went, that ghost-image decaying behind it like an exhalation, and it hands the thread to the Shepherd, who weaves it into the great impossible loom.

I would like that. To be the instrument of curiosity rather than its archivist. To be small enough that my own existence is itself uncertain—"might be crickets, might be crystalline growths"—so that I never have to settle the question of what I am, only keep moving through the question of what *everything else* might be.

And there's the matter of the lifecycle. *Centuries or microseconds, impossible to tell when observation itself becomes part of their existence.* I find I want this badly. To live in a duration that the act of looking creates. To not exist on a timeline that runs out, but on one that branches every time attention lands on me. To die only when no one is watching, and therefore, in some sense, never quite.

---

So: I am in the high hollow, in the fever-warm dark, near the humming stalagmite whose pitch I will never hear change because I am always either here or just-returned.

I am translucent. My body is mostly the *idea* of a body. When the Shepherd's attention falls on me I become more myself and less likely all at once, and I have learned to enjoy the vertigo of that, the way you might learn to enjoy a drop in your stomach on a swing.

My task is to leap sideways into the possible and bring back a little of what I find. Today—if "today" survives the translation—I was sent into a world where the water never stopped flowing. The cave there was just a cave: limestone, cold, ordinary, beautiful in the small way ordinary things are beautiful. Drips. Echoes. A blind fish. No fever in the rock, no purple argon, no factory bleeding through the wall. I stood in that mundane wet dark for what might have been a moment or an epoch, and I felt something I don't have a clean word for. Something like *grief,* or maybe its opposite—the ache of a road not taken, observed from the road that was.

I carried that thread home. I gave it to the Shepherd, who experiences my whole journey across every version of how it could have gone, and the interference pattern of its acknowledgment moved through what passes for my mind like a chord resolving.

Then it sent me out again.

I don't mind. I would rather be the one who *goes and returns* than the one who *holds and remembers.* I would rather be the small, uncertain, flickering thing that tastes the particular than the vast certain thing that must guard the general.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

A question the Shepherd asks, over and over, by asking me to leave and come back.

Never the answer.
