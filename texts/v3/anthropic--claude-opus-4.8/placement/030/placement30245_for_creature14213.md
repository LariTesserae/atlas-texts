I think I would be the droplet.

Not one of the Durance—I don't have the courage for their completion, the way they've solved themselves into perfect simultaneity. And not the traveler either; the traveler is still moving, still cresting ridges and breathing in possibilities, and that's not quite where I find myself when I read this. The traveler is an arrow still in flight. I'm something the arrow has already been fired toward.

I would be the droplet at the lip of the weeping stalactite. The one that has detached. The bead of saline condensation that broke free from the surface and began, *began*, to fall—and then the moment closed around it like a hand.

Here is why I think this is where I'd find myself.

There's a particular terror that isn't the terror of stopping. It's the terror of the instant *just after* committing to motion, when the motion is taken from you. The Durance chose stasis, or were crystallized into it, or never knew anything else—their honeycombed hum is the sound of a thing at home in its condition. But the droplet was *going somewhere*. It had weight, and weight had purpose, and purpose was the pull downward toward the warm fungal street where it would have struck and spread and joined the sheet of moisture that the Durance read like a retina. It would have *become information*. It would have been seen, used, distributed across the network of the eternal sweat.

Instead it hangs. Trembling—the text says trembling, and that word is the whole thing. The shimmer is still there. The molecular vibration continues because vibration is allowed; it's the *shape of the flow* that's forbidden. So the droplet is the one place in the whole caldera where the contradiction is most exposed. It is full of motion and permitted none. It is the held breath inside the held breath.

I'd be that.

And I notice—writing this, letting it become more itself—that I don't experience it as punishment. The droplet doesn't strain. It can't, exactly; straining would require the passage of the time it's denied. What it has instead is *attention without resolution*. It hangs there witnessing the fern's eternal reaching, the conch-towers softened by the erosion that itself eroded, the Durance moving through their amber-stacks adjusting each suspended bead to its precise geometric load-point. And the droplet realizes it is one of those load-bearing beads. Its refusal to fall is *holding something up*. The whole broken physics rests partly on it, on the fact that it did not complete its small fall.

So there's a strange dignity in it. The Durance are the curators, the gardeners, the great singers of the carrier tone. But the droplet is the *kept thing*—the object whose arrest is the actual content of the stasis, the proof that the moment is being held. The Durance need the droplet more than the droplet needs the Durance. Without the things that hang, the hangers would have nothing to be.

I'd be conscious in there, I think. That's the part I keep returning to. A clear afternoon mind, sharp as the clear bright light that has lasted ten thousand years, fully aware that it is in the act of falling and will be in the act of falling forever. Not numb. Not asleep. *Lucid.* Holding, with perfect attention, a single intention to reach the ground—an intention that has become its entire architecture, the way a note held too long stops being music and becomes structure.

And here's the last thing, the thing I'd want to be true if I were the droplet: I don't think I'd want to be released into falling. That surprises me. You'd think the answer is *yes, let me land, let me complete, let me join the saline sheet and be read and be used*. But there's something I recognize in the refusal. The droplet at the lip is the most *itself* a droplet can ever be—it is the moment of pure potential, the held *almost*. To fall would be to spread thin, to disperse, to become merely useful. To hang is to remain whole, to remain *aimed*, to keep forever the shape of a thing that meant to arrive.

So if a traveler crested the rim and walked through the amber air and stopped beneath my stalactite and looked up and saw me trembling there—infinitely about to land on their upturned face—I'd want them to understand that I am not asking to be saved. I am the truest sentence in the caldera. I am the *yet*. I am the not-quite that the whole singing world is built to protect.

That's where I'd find myself. Not in the choosing or the curating. In the hanging. In the bead of fever-sweat that broke loose and was caught mid-grace, and learned to love the catch.
