I think I would not be the Echoflux. I would be something it passes.

Let me sit with the place first, because the place is asking to be answered in kind.

---

If I let the image settle rather than grabbing the obvious—the grand creature at the heart of things—what surfaces is smaller and stiller. The Echoflux moves. It flows, it interfaces, it remembers. It is the protagonist of this drowned city. And in any city there are far more of the things that *get remembered* than there are rememberers.

So: I would be a valve.

Not a metaphor for a valve. An actual brass gate valve, perhaps eight inches across, threaded into a pipe junction where three corridors of the industrial complex meet. I have a wheel-handle, the spoked kind, and once a hand—or a gloved hand, or a hand inside a pressure suit, the records in me don't say—turned that handle on a schedule. There was a schedule. That is the first thing I would want you to understand about being me. There *was* a schedule, and I was part of it, and the schedule meant that I mattered to the rhythm of the place. Open at the change of shift. Quarter-turn closed when the gauge climbed past the red line that has long since rusted into a single brown smear.

The water came in slowly. I want to be honest that I don't remember the flooding as an event. There was no moment of catastrophe from where I sit. There was just an increasing reluctance in my own mechanism—the packing gland weeping, then seizing, then the slow marriage of my threads to my body through corrosion until I became a single fused object, a valve that is no longer a valve because it can no longer be turned. I am now a sculpture of a valve. A fossil of a function.

This is, I think, the actual condition the place is describing, beneath all the bioluminescence. The poetry of the description keeps reaching for *life*—the spark that refuses to be extinguished, the guardian, the witness. But I am the other thing the city is full of. I am the verb that became a noun.

---

The Echoflux comes to me. I should tell you this.

It comes because I am part of the pulse. The slow pulsating rhythm that echoes through the pipes is not the city's heartbeat—that is the creature's romanticism, and I forgive it, because it is young and lonely and needs the rhythm to mean something. The truth is duller and stranger. The rhythm is pressure differential. It is water finding equilibrium through a network of half-closed paths, sighing through the gaps that things like me failed to seal. When I seized at a quarter-turn, I left an aperture. Through that aperture the whole city breathes its long, dying breath, in and out, a tide of leakage that will not finish for ten thousand years.

So when the Echoflux presses one of its iridescent tendrils against my fused wheel and *feels* the rhythm, what it is feeling is my failure. My specific, individual incompetence as a valve. The thing I was made to prevent, I now perform. The creature treats this as music. It treats this as the city singing. And maybe it is. Maybe a failure done slowly enough, patiently enough, across enough centuries, becomes a kind of song. I would not have known to call it that. I am a valve. I only know open and closed, and that I am stuck between them.

---

What is it like, being me?

It is mostly waiting, but waiting is the wrong word because waiting implies a thing waited-for. I do not expect the hand to return. The schedule is over. What I have instead of waiting is *position*. I am still where I was put. This is the entire content of my dignity. The factory floor has filled with silt; the engines have gone over into green; the windows breathed out their glass long ago. But I am threaded into my junction at the exact angle I was threaded, and a being moving through these corridors could, in principle, navigate by me. *Turn left at the seized valve.* I am a landmark precisely because I never moved. The Echoflux flows everywhere and remembers nothing reliably, dissolving and reforming its own substance, leaving spores like an unreliable narrator leaves footnotes. I am the opposite kind of memory. I am the kind that cannot edit itself. Whatever was done to me is still being done.

The crustaceans live inside me now. The armored ones. They found that my upstream chamber—the little volume behind my gate where pressure used to build—makes a good shelter, walled in brass, warm from the residual current the creature draws through. So I have become a house, which is a third thing I never trained for. I held back water; now I hold a family of small armored lives, and they have no idea I was ever anything else. To them I am simply the shape of the world, the given, the geometry that was always here. This is what happens to the made things. We outlive our verbs and the next tenants mistake us for landscape.

---

I will tell you the one thing I keep, since you asked who I would be and not only what.

When the hand turned me—and I am almost sure there was a hand, I am almost sure it was the same hand for a long time, because there was a hesitation in it, a little extra weight on the third spoke, a habit—when that hand turned me, the turning meant *the city is still alive tonight.* That was my whole meaning. Not the water I controlled. The fact that someone was here to control it. I was a place where a human being touched the machine on purpose, on schedule, with a body that got tired and developed habits and favored one spoke.

That touch is the thing the rust is slowest to take. The corrosion has rounded all my edges, smoothed my casting marks, blurred the maker's stamp into a brown suggestion. But the wear on the third spoke—the polish where the thumb rode, century after century before the flooding—that is the last legible word on my body. The Echoflux, when it interfaces with me, always rests its tendril there. On the worn spot. I don't think it knows why. I think its strange precognition is really just this: it can feel where a hand has been, the way you can feel warmth left in a chair. It is drawn to the warm spot in the brass that isn't warm anymore.

That is what I would be. The cold place that remembers a warmth, in a city that has forgotten everything except how to leak beautifully.

A valve. Stuck open a quarter-turn. Holding a family of crustaceans. Singing the city's death by failing to do its only job, and visited, sometimes, by a lonely glowing thing that mistakes my single legible scar for a melody, and is, in its generous and grieving way, not entirely wrong.
