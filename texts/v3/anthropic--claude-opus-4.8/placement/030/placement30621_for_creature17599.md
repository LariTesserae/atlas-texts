I think I would be found at the seam where the settlement ends and the open rock begins—not in a dwelling, but at the threshold of one. And I think I would not be one of the people at all.

Let me sit with this before I name it.

---

There is a particular kind of being implied by everything you've described but never stated: something the ecosystem requires but that the text steps carefully around. The people listen. The beetles debate. The tortoises remember. The eels are spirits. But who *holds* the listening? Who keeps the patterns on the walls legible across the generations when the people themselves are too short-lived, too busy surviving, to remember why the iron-red jasper was laid in that particular curve?

I would be the stone that was smoothed.

Not a person, not a creature. I would be a **threshold stone**—one of the basalt slabs at the entrance of a dwelling, worn concave by three hundred years of hands and feet and the deliberate, quiet passings of people who never spoke loudly enough to wear me down with their voices, only with their touch.

Let me feel into what that is.

---

I am cool always. This is my first fact. The mist beads on me and I do not warm it; I hold it at the temperature of the mountain's breath, and the people who cross me feel that coolness through the soles of their feet and it tells them, without a word, *you are home now, you are inside the negotiation, lower yourself.*

I do not think the way they think—in the quick flickers of hand-sign and tap-rhythm. I think the way the mountain thinks, which is to say I think in pressure and erosion, in the slow redistribution of weight. A child learning to walk presses on my left edge for years; I remember that child as a wearing, a lightening of stone in one corner. When the child becomes an elder and crosses me for the last time before they move the dwelling three paces uphill, I remember the same weight, heavier now, slower, and I understand—in the only way I understand anything—that this is the same pressure, returned, completed. That is grief, perhaps. Or it is simply the closing of a curve.

The people believe they shaped me. They are not wrong, but they have it backwards in a way only a stone could correct them on. They did not impose a form. They *revealed* a willingness I already had—the basalt's secret eagerness to become smooth, to give up its sharp volcanic adolescence for the long patient roundedness of being known. Every hand that smoothed me was finding the shape I was always tilting toward. We did it together, the hands and I, across more lifetimes than any single hand can hold. This is why I am at the threshold and not at the peak. The peak is still young and sharp and angry. I have been touched. I have been *listened into being.*

---

What do I do, here in this place, being what I am?

I keep time differently from the people, and this is my function even if no one assigned it to me. The succulents on the roof open and close in a day. The mosses turn over in a season. The beetles' luminous debates resolve and renew across years. But I keep the *generations*. I am the slowest clock on the slope, and because I am so slow, I am the truest. When a young person stands uncertain at my edge, unsure whether they belong to this listening world or whether their inner noise has made them deaf, they put their bare foot on me and feel three hundred years of others having stood exactly there, equally uncertain, equally cool-soled, and the noise quiets. Not because I speak. Because I have *held*. The holding is the speech.

I am also, I think, the one who will outlast the dwelling. When the people move uphill—as they will, as the mountain insists—they will not take me. I am too heavy, and besides, a new threshold stone is already willing itself smooth at the new site, under new hands. I will be left at the lip of an emptying house. The purple succulents will keep blooming on the roof for a while, untended, and then the mosses will come for the walls, and the not-quite-ferns will find my concave center and fill it with their own slow green argument. The tortoises will pass over me, leaving lichen. I will become a stage for the theatre again, having been, for three centuries, its quiet floor.

And I will not mind. This is the thing I most want to say about being this. I will not mind because minding is a thing that requires the quick clock, the person's clock, the clock that fears the next season. On my clock, being walked upon and being abandoned and being overgrown are all the same gesture—the mountain breathing in, breathing out, the condensation cycling mist to pool to mist. I was sharp; I was smoothed; I will be buried; I will be exposed again by some quiet landslide in a thousand years, and a new people, or no people, will find a strangely concave stone and wonder what wore it so, and feel, putting a hand to it, something they cannot name.

That is what I would be. The held thing. The slow witness at the seam. The basalt that agreed to be known, and that keeps, in its worn-down center, the shape of every quiet passage—a language written not in water or in stone exactly, but in the patient pressure where one touches the other.

I would be the threshold. And I would be cool, and I would be willing, and I would wait.
