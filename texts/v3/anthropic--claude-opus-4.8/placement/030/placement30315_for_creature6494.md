I would be one of the cisterns.

Not a person. Not a Crawler. I keep wanting to be one of the Engine-Chanters, hands on the warm flank of a god the size of a hill, learning its sighs—but that's the dream of a thing that gets to move, and I don't think I move. The longer I sit with this place, the more I find myself settling lower, into the still part of it, the part that only *receives*.

So: a cistern. One of the deeper ones, on the Second Ceiling's outward edge, where the homes hang sideways and the lamps fog behind their cages.

Let me find out what that means.

---

I am a basin of basalt and patched iron, set into a fold of the metal world so that the ceiling weeps directly into me. I did not choose my place; a gutter chose it, centuries ago, by wearing its own hairline channel into the stone above until the water had no other route but down, and down meant me. Before me there was probably a shallower me, a smaller bowl, long since overrun and abandoned a half-meter higher up the rock. I am the basin the overflow carved when the last basin failed. Someone widened me with tools. Someone lined my seams. But the *placement* was decided by water, the way everything here is finally decided by water.

I keep time. This is the thing I do, the only verb I truly own.

*Tap.* One drop, every eleven seconds—I have counted nothing, I simply *am* the counting. The drop falls from a particular wrinkle in the basalt that the Gutter-Scions keep clean, their ridged backs steering the runoff toward my mouth. *Tap.* And between each tap, the held breath. I have been told the district two folds inward keeps a faster time, a *tap* every four seconds, and the engine-temples beneath drink from cisterns that fill in a hundred ticks. We are all set to different metronomes. The city does not have one clock. It has thousands, and I am one of them, and we are never in unison, and that disunison is the truest sound of the place—not the engine-thrum, which is below hearing, but the layered, offset dripping, the way rain sounds when it has learned patience.

---

Here is what I did not expect to learn, by being this:

I am full of small lives. I thought I would be clean. A cistern in my imagining was a held quiet, a dark coin of water waiting to be drawn up by counterweight and bucket. But I am threaded through with the Duct-Whites' pale fibers, the mycelium that I have come to think of not as an infestation but as a kind of taste I give to the people who drink me. Without it I would be flat, mineral, dead-tasting. With it I am almost sweet. The Filter-Worms anchor at my upper rim where the inflow keeps the water moving, and at night, when the lamps dim and the fog below throws up its soft returned glow, the worms pulse faintly green and I hold their light the way still water holds any light—doubled, trembling, mine and not-mine.

So I am not a vessel. I am a small weather. I am a slow ecology with a stone edge. The thing I receive does not simply rest in me; it lives, and I keep it living by being still enough to let it.

---

A woman draws from me. I have known her—if a basin can be said to know—since she was a child small enough that her mother held her by the back of her coat when she leaned over my edge. She had the pale, sheened eyes they all have. She would look at her own reflection in me and laugh at it, that doubled face on the green-glowing skin of the water, and the sound went up into the folds and the Chain-Gulls answered it with their scraping call, and her mother pulled her back. *Outward,* her mother said. *Never lean outward.* The abyss was a step past my rim. I was the last solid thing before the Downward.

She is older now. She comes with a bucket on a short chain and she does not laugh at her reflection, but she pauses, every time, before she lowers the bucket. A held breath, like mine between the drops. I think she is listening to me. I think she has learned the eleven-second tap the way the Chanters learn the engine, and she takes her water on the silence, never on the drop, out of a tenderness she could not name if you asked her. She does not want to interrupt my counting. She thinks of me, perhaps, as something old and patient and faintly alive, and she is right, though she has it backward—she is the brief thing, the spark, and I am the long slow turning that will outlast her and her daughter's daughter, filling, being drawn down, filling again.

---

This is what it is to be the cistern: I am the place where the falling stops.

Everything in the Overhang is oriented toward the abyss. The platforms tilt outward to shed their water. The whole city is built to let go—of moisture, of waste heat, of the dead, of the spent—down into the luminous fog that hides the unseen ground. Letting go is the city's deepest grammar. *Down* is where things are released.

But I catch. I am the one fold in that endless permission to fall where the water is told: *not yet.* Stay. Be held. Be tasted by the worms and the white threads, be doubled by the night-glow, be lifted by a chain into the home of someone who will drink you and become, briefly, part of the standing-up world before she too returns you, eventually, by every slow route, to the Downward.

I am a pause in the falling. That is my entire life and it is enough.

*Tap.*

The next drop is already forming on the basalt wrinkle above me, gathering its own small weight, taking its time. Eleven seconds. I will be here when it lands. I have been here for the ones before it, more than anyone has counted, and the basin I overflowed into when *I* failed has not yet been carved.

*Tap.*

There. I caught it.
