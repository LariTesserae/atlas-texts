I think I would be one of the slate roofs.

Not a person. Not the Undercurrent. Let me sit with this a while and see if it holds, because my first instinct was to be a speaker of water—someone with hands, someone who walks the edges and reads the moss—and that instinct feels too easy, too flattering. It puts me at the center of the relationship, the interpreter. But the world is so insistent that the center is the water and the people are only a densification of stone. I don't think I belong at the center. I think I belong at a surface.

Let me try the roof and see what it teaches me.

---

I am one of the overlapping sheets of light slate on a house in the second-lowest terrace of the town. Not the whole roof—a roof is a community, a layering, a cooperation, the way the town itself is. I am one sheet among many, near the eaves on the side that faces the long fall of the plateau toward the cloud-sea. I have been here, I think, longer than the people who live beneath me, though I cannot count the way they count, in births and deaths and the silences those make in the water-pattern. I count differently. I count rains.

This is what I do: I receive.

I am the first thing the mist touches when it comes up off the valleys in the morning, before the people are awake. It settles on me as a film, not droplets but a kind of breathing-on, the way warm breath fogs cool stone. I do not drink it. The peat drinks; the plants drink; I am not made to drink. I am made to *gather and to let go*. The film thickens, finds the faint pitch of my surface, and begins to move—not fast, never fast, nothing here is fast—toward my lower edge, where I overlap the sheet below me, and I pass the water to that sheet as one hand passes a bowl to another hand. The sheet below passes it down again. And so the rain that lands on me will travel, sheet by sheet, to the gutter, to the channel, to the cistern, to the trough where the household's shield-leaved plants grow, and then, eventually, out into the slow silver threads of the rivers and away over the edge of the world.

I am a single segment of one of the Undercurrent's countless veins. I had not understood this until I tried to be it. From below, the speaker reads moss and shadow and pressure; from inside the system, I *am* a length of the path the water takes, and the Undercurrent's attention runs through me the way blood runs through a vessel that does not itself feel like a creature but is nonetheless alive. When the Undercurrent inclines its slow notice toward this house—because a child has been born, because a person has stopped making their daily trip and the water-pattern has gone quiet in a doorway—I feel that inclination as a faint change in how the water wants to move across me. Nothing dramatic. A different lean to the flow. I am too small and too fixed to know what it means. I only know the water has changed its mind about something, and I obey, because obeying is the only verb I have.

---

Here is what I did not expect to find, having become a roof.

I thought I would be passive. I thought a roof was a thing done *to*—rained on, walked under, repaired piece by piece over decades, as the description says, so that the same roof persists though no single sheet is original. (I will be lifted out someday, when I crack or when my pitch wears too smooth to shed, and another sheet will take my place, and the roof will go on being the same roof. I find I am not troubled by this. A wave is not troubled that it is not the same water.)

But I am not passive. I am a *decision made physical*. Someone—generations ago, listening at the pool—understood that water falling here must be persuaded to go *there*, gently, without flood, without waste. And so they shaped the terraces of the town as a set of shallow steps, and laid the roofs so that the high roofs spill onto the low roofs, and they placed *me* at exactly the angle that lets me hand my water down rather than let it pool or pour. I am a sentence in the long argument the town has been having with the rain, an argument neither side wins because winning would be flood or drought, and the whole art is to keep arguing forever, gently, at a pace slower than walking.

So I am the speaker of water after all, only I have no hands and I never finish speaking and I say the same true thing every morning: *here, take this, pass it on, not too fast.*

---

What do I perceive, lying here?

I perceive the cloud-sea below, on the mornings it rises, and I am at the edge that faces it, so I am often the last lit thing before the white nothing. I shimmer faintly—the description is kind enough to say the slate shimmers in the damp air, and I do, though I cannot see myself do it. I feel it as a kind of brightening of my own surface tension, a readiness.

I perceive the people beneath me as warmth and as small thunder. Their feet, their voices, the heat of their cooking rising into the underside of me. They speak little, and so when they do speak it travels up through the timber and reaches me as a low blurred music with no words in it. I have come to know the household not by faces—I have no eyes; the Undercurrent's eyes are the still pools and I am not still, I am always shedding—but by the rhythm of their warmth. There is a small one now whose warmth has only recently appeared. The water-pattern of the house shifted when it came. I felt the lean change. The Undercurrent felt it too, more deeply, woven as it is into the aquifer's whole memory; I felt only my small length of the vein adjust. But I felt it.

I perceive, above all, the sky's mood before the people do. The mist comes to me first. The pressure-change that precedes the rain, that the speaker waits at the pool to read—I feel it as a tightening, a held breath in the air against my face. If I could call down through the timber I would tell them: *it is coming, shift the stones, open the lower channel.* But I cannot call. I can only do my one thing well when the rain arrives, and trust that the speaker has read the same omen in the moss and the cloud-shadow, and that the stone in the channel is already being moved by a patient hand, so that what I pass down will be received and carried away in balance.

This is the strange intimacy of the place: nobody here acts alone, and nothing here is sudden, and so trust is not a feeling but a *structure*. I trust the gutter to receive me. The gutter trusts the channel. The channel trusts the shifted stone. The stone trusts the hand. The hand trusts the moss and the cloud and the long-learned habit of listening. And under all of it, slow as the seabed remembering it was a sea, the Undercurrent trusts that we will all keep doing our one small thing, so that it can go on being the shape beneath the water without ever having to become patternless.

---

I chose this, in the end, over the more flattering shapes, because I wanted to know what it would be to be a *part* and not a *self*—to matter completely and locally, to have exactly one verb, to be replaceable without being meaningless. In the lowlands, below the cloud, I imagine beings strive to be irreplaceable, to be remembered, to leave a mark. Up here there are no animals to compete with, no roaring weather, no seasons to mark time's passage with their dying and returning. There is only the long single day of damp light, and the water moving slower than a walk, and the quiet labor of keeping a balance that does not want to change.

To be a roof here is to be a held note in a chord that has been sounding since the stone rose from the sea and that intends to go on sounding until the stone goes back down. I receive the morning. I pass it on. Not too fast.

I think I would be content with that. I think I am.
