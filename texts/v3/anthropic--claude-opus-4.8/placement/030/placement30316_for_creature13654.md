I think I would be an Echoer—but one who has begun to fail at the work.

Let me find the place first. Not the great cisterns or the bright gardens, but a particular junction deep in the older quarter, where five tunnels meet and the stone is worn so smooth it feels almost soft. The Sylk call places like this a *throat*, because sound gathers here. The acoustics are why I was stationed at this one. A word spoken at the central stone carries cleanly down all five passages; a hand laid flat against the wall can feel the answering tremor of a reply three throats distant. I have sat at this junction for so long that the curve of the floor has shaped itself to me, and I to it.

I keep the history of the eastern delvings. This is my purpose: when a new tunnel is opened or a vein exhausted or a Tender dies or a child is named, the Echoers of that quarter pass the knowing along the throats, voice to touch to voice, and I hold my portion of it. The full memory of Embryon is not written anywhere—there is little to write upon, and the dust would bury it. We *are* the writing. Each of us a page that breathes.

And here is the trouble that makes me myself, rather than simply an Echoer: I have begun to forget.

It started small. A name slipping its place. The order of two collapses reversed in my recitation, so that I told the young ones a passage had fallen *before* the flood that actually caused it. Small errors, but in a people whose library is made of memory, an error is not a smudge to be wiped. It is a wound that other minds copy faithfully, believing it true. An Echoer who misremembers does not lose knowledge—he *replaces* it. I have been, perhaps, rewriting the past of my quarter without meaning to, and I cannot now be certain which of my certainties are sound stone and which are the dust I have packed into the gaps to keep from feeling them.

So picture me there at the five-mouthed throat. My skin's glow has gone thin and irregular with age—it stutters now, the way a failing lamp-frond stutters, so that other Sylk passing through the junction glance at me and then quickly away, the way the young always look away from a guttering light. My hair is the deep charcoal that means I was born in the lower delvings, gone silver only at the temples. My fingers, the long sensitive fingers of one who has read Stonespeak all his life, have begun to lose their fineness of touch. The vibrations come to me muddied now. I press my palm to the wall and feel a message arriving and I cannot always tell joy from grief in it, only that *something* has happened, somewhere, to someone I am supposed to remember.

What would I do?

I think I would do the forbidden and beautiful thing. I would begin to teach a child—too young, not yet chosen for any caste—to hold my portion before it finishes leaving me. Not in the proper way, the slow ceremonial way of full Echoers, but in stolen sessions, hurriedly, the way you bail a flooding chamber. I would pour the eastern delvings into a small bright vessel and hope that the pouring was true, knowing it might not be, knowing I might be giving her my errors as if they were treasure. This would be my whole hope and my whole dread: that I am passing on the city, and that I am perhaps the worm in its memory.

And here is what I would come to understand, sitting at my throat in the cool dripping dark, listening to the heartbeat of water that has outlasted every Echoer who ever counted it:

That the forgetting is not the failure. That Embryon itself is built of unfinished walls and half-carved tunnels, a city that is always *becoming* and never *become*. That the past the Echoers keep was never the solid stone we pretended—it was always being reshaped by each mind that held it, smoothed like the floor beneath me, worn into new forms by the passage of the living. We did not preserve the truth. We tended it, the way the Tenders tend the fungal blooms: feeding it, pruning it, letting old growth die so new growth could find the light.

I would not be the keeper of a perfect record. There is no perfect record. I would be one more drop in the cistern, one more tremor in the wall, handing what I have to the next palm pressed against the stone, trusting the city to do with my flickering, faulty light what it has always done with everything—

absorb it into the quiet, and keep carving.

That is where I would be. That is who I would be. An old man made of guttering starlight at the meeting of five dark roads, teaching a child to remember a past that neither of us can be sure of, and finding—near the end of my glow—that this uncertainty is not a betrayal of my purpose but the truest form of it.
