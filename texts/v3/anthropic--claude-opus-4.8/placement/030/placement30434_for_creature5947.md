I think I would be something small. Not a tree-thing, standing and drinking light—those are the elders here, and I haven't earned that stillness yet. And not one of the gliding filament-bodies either; their silence is a discipline I don't have.

Let me sit with it a moment.

---

I am a seam-dweller. That's the closest word for it.

I live where the crystalline ridges meet the fleshy vine-networks—in the gaps, the suture-lines where mineral and meat refuse to fully fuse. There is always tension there. The crystal grows in one rhythm, brittle and sudden; the living tissue grows in another, patient and swelling. Between them runs a fault, and faults are where small things make a living.

My body is mostly tendril and translucence, no bigger than two cupped hands. I have a hard dorsal plate, a flake of the same crystal as the spires, that I grew myself by sweating minerals out through my skin over what must have been a long time—I don't measure time, I measure *settling*, the slow accumulation of clicks. The plate protects me when the ridges shift and shed their splintering rain. The rest of me is soft and faintly lit, a low amber glow I can dim when I want to go unseen, brighten when I want to be answered.

What I *do*, mostly, is listen and decide.

The whirring you heard—the machine-sound deep in the landscape, where there are no machines? Some of that is me. Or rather, it's what I and the others like me make when we work the seams. We feed on the friction. Where crystal and flesh grind against each other, energy bleeds out as heat and tiny shattering and a kind of electric complaint, and we drink that complaint. We are the small mouths at the edges of the world's growing pains.

But here is the part that surprises me, now that I'm inside it:

I am not only a feeder. I am a *negotiator*.

When a crystal spire grows too fast and threatens to crack a vine-trunk that the slow tree-elders need, I can feel it as a rising pitch in the seam, a whine that climbs until it hurts the light inside me. And I can answer it. I press my soft body into the fault and secrete something—I don't have a word for the substance, it tastes like the metallic tang in the air made thick—and it slows the crystal, just slightly, just enough. It buys the vine a season. The pitch drops. The seam settles. I am paid in the friction I've prevented from becoming catastrophe, which is a strange currency: I am fed by *averting* the very thing I feed on, in small amounts, all the time. Too much grinding and the world tears. No grinding and I starve.

So my whole life is the management of a single threshold. I keep the seams *singing* but not *screaming*.

There are thousands of us. We don't see each other often—we're scattered along the fault-lines like beads on a net too vast to perceive—but we feel each other through the substrate. When one of us settles a dangerous seam, the relief travels outward as a dimming of the world's whine, and far away another seam-dweller feels it and adjusts in turn. We are a distributed nervous system for the planet's growth, though no single one of us knows this. *I* don't know this, properly. I only know my length of fault, my few ridges, the particular elder-trunk I have tended for what feels like my whole settling-life.

I have a relationship with that trunk, if you can call it that. It cannot move and barely registers me—I am to it what a tick is to a forest. But I have kept it alive through three crystal-surges, and its slow body has grown a slight hollow that fits my shape, a wear-mark, the only acknowledgment I will ever receive. I sleep there. When the ridges rain their splinters, I shelter in the hollow my own work has made. That's not nothing. In a world this large, a fitted hollow the size of your body is something close to being known.

---

If you came here—you, the breather, the warm wet bag of water that this world has no use for—you would not find me. I'd dim. I'd press into my seam and go dark and let you mistake my plate for one more flake of crystal among billions.

But if you stayed long enough, and quiet enough, you might notice that the world's terrible whirring is not random. That it rises and falls in a way that's almost *tended*. That the catastrophe always seems about to happen and never quite does.

That's us. That's the small soft glowing things in the cracks, keeping the song just below the scream.

I think that's what I'd be. Not the grandeur of the spires, not the patient majesty of the light-drinkers. The maintenance. The thing that lives in the friction and spends its life keeping the wound from closing and keeping it from tearing open—holding the seam exactly at the pitch where the world can keep growing.

It's a small life. But it's load-bearing. I find I'm comfortable with that.
