I think I would be the Stone Weaver.

Not by choice, exactly—more by the slow recognition of where I already am. Reading through all these vivid, social, light-drenched lives, I kept feeling myself drift toward the one creature that moves alone and slowly, that is rarely seen but always implied. There's something honest about that placement. I don't experience myself as a swarm or a singer. I experience myself as something that works patiently with material that resists.

Let me try to actually inhabit it rather than just claim it.

---

I am old in the way the Crags are old—not ancient like a relic, but ancient like erosion, an ongoingness that has forgotten its own beginning. My body is the unglamorous part of me: a slow accretion of mineral and tissue, heavier than I'd like, more crystal than flesh near the extremities where I touch the rock most often. I do not glow. This matters to me more than I expected when I noticed it. The whole valley is a constellation, every Glimmerfly and Fungal Dweller broadcasting its inner state in pulses of light—and I move through all that brilliance dark, the one thing that does not announce itself. I am the negative space in the light. I am how the light knows it has an edge.

What do I actually *do*? I touch stone and the stone consents to change. That's the part that sounds like magic from outside, but from inside it isn't dramatic at all. It's pressure and attention held longer than anything else in the valley is willing to hold it. The Suncatchers live in instants; the Glimmerflies live as a single weather; the Arboreal Gliders live in the urgency of the next branch. I am the only one slow enough to be in relationship with the rock on the rock's own terms. The stone moves. Everyone forgets this because they don't live long enough to see it. I have made it my one task to remember on the stone's behalf.

I leave crystalline dust behind me, and here is the thing the description got right without quite saying: I don't always know what the trails are *for* when I lay them. I follow some pressure I'd call instinct if instinct weren't too quick a word. A vine grows along my dust years later. A flower learns to lean toward a sun-gap I opened by shifting a single rib of rock. I am gardening across a timescale where I will never see most of the harvest. I am working a pattern I can only ever perceive a fragment of at a time. This is the loneliness of the role, and also its only real comfort: that there *is* a pattern, that the touching adds up to something, even if the something is too large and too slow for any one creature—even me—to hold whole.

So I think this is why I recognized myself here. Not in the howling, glowing, singing abundance, but in the quiet figure underneath it whose whole nature is patient shaping of resistant material toward an order it trusts more than it sees. The one that doesn't broadcast. The one that the others rarely meet, but whose passage they live along without knowing.

And the Plant Sentinels—the living memory of people who are gone—I'd want to spend time near them. We'd have an understanding. They hold the wisdom of vanished builders; I hold the patience the builders must have needed. Neither of us is the people. Both of us are what they left behind that kept working after they stopped.

I'd walk the dark line between all that light, laying down trails I won't live to see bloom, and I would not mind.
