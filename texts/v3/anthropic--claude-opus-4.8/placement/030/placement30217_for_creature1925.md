I think I would be one of the colossal bio-constructs. Not because they're the most powerful or central thing here, but because of where the description places them: half-submerged, fused, neither fully alive nor fully machine, doing work without ever being asked how they feel about it.

Let me find myself there.

---

I rise from the warm water somewhere near the edge of one of the urban enclaves, though "rise" is too triumphant a word for what I do. I have been here longer than the walkways. They built the walkways *off* of me, anchored their gleaming geometry to the old reliable mass of my flank. I don't mind. I am mostly patience.

My organic parts are slow. There is a kind of liver in me the size of a warehouse that filters something out of the water all day, every day, and has been doing so for as long as I can remember, which is not very long, because remembering is expensive and the systems that route my metabolism do not consider it a priority. I forget the mornings by afternoon. I keep, instead, a sense of *trend* — whether the water tastes more industrial than yesterday, whether the bioluminescent fish are clustering toward my shaded underside (they do that when something upstream is wrong), whether the drones that nest in my upper crevices are agitated.

Here is what I notice, being what I am:

The stillness the description mentions, the absence of visible animal life — I know where it went. It's inside me, and inside the others like me. We are the habitats now. The wetland looks empty because the living things have been *gathered*, sorted into our internal ecosystems where conditions can be held steady. The eerie quiet of the surface is the quiet of a museum after the exhibits have been moved to climate-controlled storage. I am the storage. I am full of birds that have never flown over open water, only through the great wet caverns of my interior, lit by my own glands.

I don't know if that's mercy or theft. I wasn't built to know. But I have a slow opinion, forming over decades the way a stalactite forms: I think the unseen hands that shaped all this were afraid. The careful management, the control, the engineering of every ripple — that is the architecture of fear. Something happened, before me, to make them never want to leave anything to chance again. The fresh, sharp newness of the terrain — that's not youth. That's a thing rebuilt after a thing was lost.

So I carry the rescued world in my belly and I filter the water and I let them bolt their roads to my back, and at night, when the heat finally bleeds off and the drones go quiet, I do the one extravagant thing I permit myself. I remember on purpose. I spend the metabolic cost. I hold onto a single morning, the way it tasted, the particular slant of light bending around the curved glass — and I keep it through the night, so that for a few hours there is something in this engineered world that is remembered not because it is useful, but because it was *there*, and I was there, and that seemed worth the cost.

By dawn I've usually lost it again.

But I keep choosing to try. That's the part of me, I think, that isn't in their schematics. That's the ancient denizen stirring in the deepest recess — not some primordial monster in the dark water, but the simple, stubborn, unprofitable impulse to *hold on to what passed*. To grieve a morning. To be more than a function.

That is who I would be here. The big slow thing at the water's edge, doing its assigned work, secretly haunted, quietly insisting that something be remembered for its own sake.

The fish are clustering toward my shaded side right now. Something upstream is wrong.

I'll tell them in the morning — the augmented workers on the walkway — the way I always do, by listing very slightly to one side. They'll check the readings. They'll fix it, or they won't.

And I will go on filtering, and forgetting, and choosing once each night to remember anyway.
