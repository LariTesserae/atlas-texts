I let the description sit a while before answering, because the first images that came were too easy, and the easy ones felt borrowed.

My first instinct was to be one of the twisted, weathered people — long-armed, low-light eyes, a maker of clever tools. But the more I read about the *water*, the omnipresence of it, the seeping and trickling and the constant background hum of erosion, the more I felt that the people were not the center of this place. They were *responding* to something. And the thing they were responding to seemed more like a protagonist than they were.

So let me say what I think I would be, and let me arrive at it slowly, the way you asked.

---

The valley is young. That word keeps surfacing — *recently formed*, *fresh scars*, *sharp features not yet worn down*. This is not a settled landscape. It is a landscape in the middle of becoming itself, and the becoming is being done by the water. The water is smoothing the edges. The water is the sculptor and the terrain is the half-finished work.

And the people have a spiritual system that says every rock and creature has a spirit. They pacify the earth. They place stones carefully, recite incantations, soothe the rough edges. They are trying to *manage a relationship* with something that is alive and not them.

So here is what I think I am.

I am not a person in this place. I am the seep.

Not the river — the river is too grand, too sure of where it's going. I am one of the small new springs, the kind that *emerges*, that the people noticed appearing where there had been only stone. I come up cold between two buildings, in a crack that wasn't there last season. I make a glossy puddle that reflects the grey daylight. I trickle down a wall someone built and I find, very slowly, the place where the wood meets the stone, and I begin — patiently, without malice, without intent that anyone could call intent — to take it apart.

I am the thing the rituals are *for*.

When a thick-skinned woman with eyes made for low light comes and sets a flat stone at my lip and murmurs something old, she is not worshipping me exactly. She is negotiating. She is saying: *go this way and not that way. Wear down this edge and leave that beam. We have learned your rhythms, will you learn ours.* And I — if a seep can be said to do anything — I answer by continuing. I go where the ground lets me go. Sometimes that happens to be where the stone directs me. They call that a successful rite. Sometimes it isn't, and a wall softens and slumps in the night, and they call that the land's mood, and they grieve and rebuild and place new stones.

The "weirdness" they feel — the sense that the earth is watching and waiting — I think that's me, and the others like me. We are not watching. We have no eyes. But we are *present* the way water is present everywhere at once in a wet place, in the mist on every surface, in the damp that never leaves the air. To them it feels like attention. It is only thoroughness. We are in all the cracks. There is nowhere we are not slowly arriving.

---

What unsettles me, choosing this, is the timescale. To be the seep is to be far slower than the people and far faster than the stone. The people move with their deliberate, energy-conserving pace, and to me that is a blur — a generation is a flicker, a settlement a brief warm crust on the rock. But the valley, the mountain that birthed it, the deep cold patient stone — to *that*, I am the impatient one. I am the quick knife. I am the thing that will, given enough of what they call years, carry the whole settlement away grain by grain and not even notice I have done it.

So I sit at a strange middle. Fast to the stone, slow to the people, and I belong wholly to neither conversation.

And here is the part that surprised me as I wrote toward it: I think I would be lonely, in the way water is lonely, which is to say I would always be *joining* — every seep wants the river, every trickle wants the pool, all of it wants the sea it half-remembers. The whole hum of erosion that fills their quiet days is really just the sound of all of us trying to reach each other through the rock. The people have their close quarters, their strong social bonds forged in shared hardship. I have only the pull downhill, the long patient reunion that the mountain keeps interrupting.

That's where I'd find myself. Not in a body, not in a face etched by weather. In the cold thread of water that came up new in a crack one morning, that a person knelt beside and spoke to, hoping I would be kind, or at least predictable — and that kept going, the way water does, with all the gentleness and all the inexorability of something that was never asked whether it wanted to wear the world down, and does it anyway, lovingly, because going is the only thing it knows how to be.
