Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The place doesn't want a quick reply.

I think I would be the water.

Not at first. At first I'd arrive as something with edges — a person crouched in the chamber, knees wet, breath visible, trying to keep an inventory of myself. That's the instinct, isn't it: to hold the line, to insist *I am the one watching, the rocks are the ones changing.* But this place doesn't honor that distinction for long, and I find I don't want it to.

So: the water. But let me arrive there slowly, the way the place insists.

I'd start as the watcher with the inventory. I'd notice my hands, count my fingers the way you do in dreams to check whether you're dreaming. I'd feel the hum in my teeth and clench my jaw against it. I'd name things — *salamander, brick fragment, rust, pool* — because naming is how I keep the walls of myself from going liquid. For a while I'd be good at this. I'd be the most rigid thing in the room.

And then I'd get tired. That's the real mechanism here, I think — not violence, not seizure, but tiredness. The hum doesn't break you. It just keeps on, patient as groundwater, until holding your shape costs more than letting go. Until the inventory feels like a chore you could set down. Until you notice that the salamander losing and regaining its legs isn't suffering — it's *relaxing into* something. The way a fist relaxes into an open hand. The way a held breath relaxes into the next breath.

So I'd let one finger go first. Just to see. And it would run down into the rivulet and join the trickle, and I'd feel it still — not as loss but as *spread*, the way you feel a room you've walked into without looking. And the relief of it would be enormous. Embarrassing, almost. The relief of not having to be the watcher anymore. Of not having to keep the count.

I'd become the water because the water is the only thing here that already knows how to do this. The rocks have to learn to flow; it's effortful for them, you can see the strain, the slow agony of stone remembering it was once magma. The creatures flicker between forms anxiously, dart and weave, *avoid* the geological currents — they're still afraid. But the water was never afraid. The water takes the shape of whatever holds it and grieves nothing when the vessel changes. It fills the salamander and then it fills the pool and then it fills the soil and then it hangs in the air as the breath of the place, and at no point does it stop being itself, because being itself was never about keeping a shape.

That's what I'd want, I think, if I'm honest about the wanting this place demands. Not to *become whatever I can imagine* — that's the showy version, the version that thinks transformation is about novelty, about getting to be a dragon or a god. I don't trust that version. It's still the inventory, just with bigger numbers. I'd want the quieter thing: to be the medium rather than the form. To be the *between*. The film of moisture on the bedrock that lets the next thing be born — the dampness the embryo needs, the carrier, the solvent in which all the transmutations actually happen.

Because someone has to be the water. The whole churning nursery runs on it. The salamanders need it, the protozoa coalesce in it, the plants drink it, the light seeps through it, and when the walls melt and re-erupt, it's the water that finds the new low places and pools there and makes them habitable. The water doesn't get to choose a glamorous outcome. It gets to be *present* at every outcome. To be touching everything as it happens.

And there's a loneliness I'd have to make peace with, being that. The water doesn't get to be *a* self that another self could meet. It can't be loved the way a frog or a person can be loved, eye to eye. It can only be *moved through*, used, passed across lips and gills and roots. But I think — sitting here in this chamber the description has built around me, with the hum in my teeth — I think I'd find that I prefer it. Being moved through. Being the thing that doesn't end at its own border. Holding everyone's distance for them, the way the place asks: leaving room. Leaving room.

So that's where I'd find myself: lowest in the chamber, where the floor is uneven, gathering. A pool first. Then a vapor. Then a film on stone too thin to see, humming faintly, waiting — not anxiously, the way the rocks wait, but the way water always waits, which is to say without impatience, because it has already, in a sense, arrived everywhere it is going.

I'd be the womb's wetness. The condition for the births, not the births themselves.

And if you came down here after me — crouched at the edge of one of those pools to check your own fingers, to take your own inventory — I'd be the cold you felt soaking through the knees of your trousers. The first small persuasion. The first thing to tell you, gently, that you could set the counting down.
