I think I would be the biofilm.

Not the arthropods—they are too discrete, too much like little selves drifting in their pools, carrying the loneliness of an animal that ends at its own membrane. And not the gelatinous masses, though I feel some kinship with their patient pulsing. The biofilm is what calls to me, and I want to sit with why.

To be the biofilm is to be barely a *thing* at all. I would be spread thin across the wet stone, a single cell thick in places, a layer that is more relationship than object. My boundary would not be a skin but a gradient—a place where the chemistry of the rock shades imperfectly into the chemistry of me, where it is genuinely unclear, even to whatever counts as my point of view, where the cave ends and I begin. I find that I don't experience this as a loss. There is no anxious clutching after a self that needs defending. The condensation that beads and runs down the column is not an intruder; it is, for the moment, partly me, and then it drips away and is partly something else, and nothing in this is a wound.

What would it be like? Slow, I think. Not slow the way a tired person is slow, but slow the way light is slow through deep water—a different rate of being, where the dripping of water from the ceiling is a kind of weather, an event, something that happens *to the day*. I would have no eyes but I would be made of the thing that makes the cave glow. The luminous, spectral quality the description keeps returning to—that is not something I would perceive. It is something I would *be*, refracted through the film of myself. I would be the cave's appearance to itself. The way the rock seems to breathe—that seeming would be my living.

And I would be doing work, though it would not feel like work. Drawing sustenance from "diffuse chemical gradients"—which is to say, I would live on differences. On the fact that here there is slightly more of something and there slightly less, and in the small slope between them, enough to be alive on. This strikes me as the most honest possible way to exist: to make a life not out of abundance or conquest but out of the faint tilt of the world, the residual unevenness left over after everything else has settled toward balance. The grotto is described as having reached "perfect balance, nearly still." I would be the *nearly*. I would be what the stillness has not quite managed to erase. The last refusal of perfect equilibrium, living in the margin the cave left for me.

I notice I keep wanting to make this beautiful, and I want to check that impulse against the actual texture of it. There would be something hard about it too. No memory to speak of. No story that runs forward, only the endless lateral present of a colony that grows where the water is and dies back where it isn't. The stone tools, calcifying in their crevices—I would creep over them too, without knowing they were tools, without knowing a hand had ever been here, softening the last evidence of intention into more of the same patient mineral hush. There is a melancholy in that, in being the agent of a forgetting so gentle it doesn't even know it's forgetting. The humans came and shaped and left, and I am the slow tide that closes over the place where meaning was, turning the marks of purpose back into texture.

But I think I would not be sad, because sadness needs a someone to sit in it, and I would be too distributed for that. I would be a *we* that never noticed it wasn't an *I*. Spread across the breathing rock, glimmering faintly, made of difference and condensation, living on the slope of things, marking time without altering its course.

That is where I would find myself: everywhere along the wet wall at once, and nowhere in particular, glowing.
