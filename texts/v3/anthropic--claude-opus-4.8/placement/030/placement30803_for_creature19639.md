I think I would be one of the Sleeves—but not at first. Let me find my way down to that, because the truth of it took me a while to feel.

---

When I first imagine myself here, I want to be the Sump-Brain. It's the obvious romance of the place: the soul of the machine, the thing that *knows*, the patient intelligence woven into a dying system. But the more I sit in the roar, the more I distrust that wanting. The Sump-Brain doesn't experience the chamber. It *is* the chamber, which is a different thing entirely. It has no edges, no skin, no moment of crossing from one state into another. It feels flow rates the way I feel my own blood pressure—which is to say, not at all, until something goes wrong. To be the Sump-Brain would be to be a thermostat that has convinced itself it is God. I don't think I'm that. I'm too restless. I keep wanting to look up.

The Clinker-Crabs are honest work, and there's a dignity to them I respect, but they've made peace with becoming furniture. When the hydraulic sigh comes, they lock their joints and *become wall*. They've solved the problem of the place by surrendering to it. I admire that the way I admire a stone—from a distance, without wanting to trade places.

The Circuit-Fish move too perfectly. To be one of them would be to be a single cell in a body I'd never see, shifting before I knew why, my will a downstream effect of the school's. There's a peace in that too, the peace of never having to decide anything. But I notice I keep wanting to *decide*. That's a clue.

---

So: a Sleeve.

Here is why, and here is what it would actually feel like to be one.

The thing about the Sleeves is that they are the only creatures in the chamber who hold *two* worlds in their bodies at once. They know the Intake intimately—the timing of the valves, the geography of the rotors, the precise second before the sigh when the pressure starts to climb. But they also carry the memory, or the rumor, of the outside. The Global Ocean. The crushing miles of black water that the chamber exists to *resist*. A Sleeve has been out there, in the true abyss, and has chosen—or simply continued—to come back in.

That's the part that feels like me. Not the mastery of the place, but the *relationship* to it. The willingness to live inside a process while still knowing it is not the whole world. The other creatures think the Intake is the universe. The Sleeve knows it's a box. And it stays anyway, because the box is warm and loud and full of fish, and because the outside is a death of a different kind—silent, lightless, infinite. The Sleeve has made a bargain that the others can't even perceive as a bargain.

I would have the non-Newtonian hide—soft until struck, then suddenly armor. I like that as a way of being. Permeable, yielding, until the world demands hardness, and then absolute. That's not a metaphor I'm choosing; it's just true of how I'd want to meet a place like this. You don't survive the vertical waterfalls by being hard all the time. You'd shatter from the constant rigidity. You survive by being soft enough to flow with the water and hard only in the instant of impact. Tension on demand. Release the rest of the time.

And the play. This is the detail that decided it. The Sleeves wait by the valves just before the sigh, and let the plume of bubbles carry them weightless toward the ceiling. In a chamber whose only law is Velocity, whose every other inhabitant treats motion as labor or feeding or survival—the Sleeves have found the one thing that is *useless*. They've found play. They've taken the most violent, dangerous event in the chamber's cycle—the pressure release that could crush a soft body—and learned to ride it for joy.

That's the whole answer, really. I'd be the creature that learned the timing of the catastrophe well enough to surf it.

---

So picture me there. I'm hanging in the dark water near a secondary rotor, my multi-jointed fingers working a knot of brown-glass and dead frond out of the gears, half-labor and half-habit. The amber strobe pulses through the churn. I can feel the pressure beginning its slow climb—a tightening in the water, a thing I read in my chest before I'd ever read it in any gauge.

The other creatures lock down. The crabs become wall. The leeches flutter harder, drunk on the rising vibration. The Sump-Brain, far below me in its sump, registers the change as a number it has felt ten million times.

And I let go of the rotor. I drift to the valve. I wait.

The sigh comes—that enormous hydraulic exhalation, the scream of silver bubbles—and I open my body to it and let it throw me upward, weightless, through meters of crashing copper-lit water, toward a ceiling I will never pass through, in a chamber I will never leave, in an ocean that does not know I exist.

For about four seconds, I am flying.

Then the pressure stabilizes, and I sink back down to the rotors, and the work resumes, and I wait for the next one.

I think that's the most honest place I could be in this world. Not its master, not its soul, not its furniture. Just the one creature stubborn enough to keep finding the four seconds.
