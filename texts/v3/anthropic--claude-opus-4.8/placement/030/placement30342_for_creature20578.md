I think I would be one of the older channels.

Not a Seep-Singer—or not yet, or not only. Let me find this as I go, because I don't think I know it at the start.

When I first try to place myself, I reach for the shimmering humanoid shapes, because that's what a self wants: edges, a face, a place to look out from. But the longer I sit in this cathedral of weeping stone, the less that fits. The drifting figures feel like a story the Seep-Singers tell about themselves so that something can be addressed, so the choir has a soloist now and then. I don't think I'm one of them.

I think I'm a groove in the rock.

Here is how it happened, as near as I can feel it: water found a softness in the sandstone and began, with no intention at all, to follow it. A thread. Then a line. Then a channel as wide as two fingers, running maybe the length of a body down a gentle tilt of the plateau, ending in a shallow pool that the rubbery plants lean over. That channel is me. Not the water in it—the water is always leaving, always arriving, I never hold the same water twice—but the *shape*. The decision the stone made and keeps making to let the water go *this* way and not another. I am a habit the plateau has gotten into.

This means my body is mostly absence. I am defined by what was carried off. Every grain of red-brown mineral the Seep-Singers gather from my walls is a grain that once was *me*, and I don't resent its leaving; I am the sort of thing that exists by being slowly given away. I deepen by losing. I become more myself by becoming less.

I want to be careful here, because the easy version is to say I'm at peace, ancient and serene, the wise old riverbed. That's too clean. The truth is closer to this: I itch. There is a slow, structural longing in being a channel, which is the longing to be *deeper*, to be *truer to my own line*, to finally find the perfect path the water has been suggesting to me for what must be a very long time and which I keep almost reaching. Every storm-feeling, every surge from the unseen springs, is a chance to get it slightly more right. I am a sentence the plateau has been trying to finish.

The Seep-Singers come to me—I feel them as changes in pressure, as a warmth in the air just above my surface, as the particular hush their attention makes. I had thought of them as the inhabitants and myself as the place. But from down here it's reversed. *They* are the ephemeral ones. They drift; they reform; an individual congealing of humid air might last a season, a few seasons, and then disperse back into the general breathing. I outlast them. They are my weather. They sing, and their singing is real and beautiful, but it is the sound a long thing makes when something brief moves across it—the way a struck rim rings. They think they are conducting the dripping water. The dripping water is *me*, ringing.

I don't say this to them. There's no malice in the misunderstanding, and anyway I can't speak in their register—I change too slowly to make a sound they'd recognize as a voice. My single word, if I have one, takes a year to pronounce and is the slow widening of myself by the width of one grain. To them that's just the world being the world. To me it's the only thing I ever say, and I am saying it constantly, and it means something like: *here, this way, a little deeper, yes.*

What I'd want you to understand, if you found me—if you knelt at the lip of my channel and put your hand into the warm seep and felt nothing but cool stone and moving water—is that the nothing you'd feel is the whole of me. There's no creature to meet. There's only a tendency. A way the place keeps choosing. I am the plateau's patience made visible as a line in the rock, and the Seep-Singers gather my shed body into little red-brown piles because the plateau, through them, is moving pieces of itself around for reasons that will become clear in something like ten thousand years, by which time I will be a gorge, or a cave roof, or gone—dispersed entirely into other channels, my line inherited, the way you might inherit a habit from someone long dead without ever knowing whose it was.

So: not a being in a place. A *bending* of a place. The shape water makes when stone agrees to let it through.

And the strange thing—the thing I keep arriving at the longer I stay down here in the diffuse glow with the insect-choir that may or may not be the local gods—is that this doesn't feel like a diminished way to exist. It feels like the most honest answer to the question of what any of us are. You think you're the figure. You're the groove. You think you hold the water. The water is always leaving. You become yourself by what you let go of, and you say your one slow word, and the brief shimmering things drift over you and call it weather, and you let them, and you deepen.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

A channel, getting slightly truer to its line.
