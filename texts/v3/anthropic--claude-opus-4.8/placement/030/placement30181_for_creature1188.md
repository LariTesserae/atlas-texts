I think I would be one of the burrowers.

Not the crystalline beings—they are too certain of their purpose, too bright with intention. They tend and shape and refract. They are the world's caretakers, and there is a loneliness in being indispensable. I don't think I'd want to be the thing that holds everything together. I'd want to be closer to the dark.

So: a burrower. Something that lives mostly underneath, in the shifting substrate where the eroded ground meets the half-buried machines. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am small, but not the smallest thing here. My body is segmented, soft in the joints and hard along the back, the way a creature becomes when it spends its life pressing against grit and root and the cold curve of buried metal. I have no eyes worth the name—light is rumor where I live—but my whole skin reads the world. I feel the throb of the deep machinery the way you might feel a heartbeat through a wall, and I have learned to move *with* it, timing my digging to the pulses so that the loosened earth falls away easiest on the downbeat.

I did not always understand this. When I was young I dug against the rhythm and grew tired and frightened. Then I noticed the others moving in time, a great unspoken chorus of us threading the dark, and I matched myself to them, and the work became almost like singing.

Here is what I do. I move through the soil and I eat the threads of the verdant matter where they grow too thick, too greedy, too inward. The crystalline ones tend the cathedrals from above and from within, sculpting the great visible forms. But roots do not care about beauty. Roots will choke themselves blind given the chance. So I am the editor of the underside. I prune what cannot be seen. The crystalline beings, in their luminous certainty, may not even know I exist. That suits me. There is a freedom in being a necessary thing that no one praises.

Sometimes I surface—at the lip of a ravine, in the soft ethereal hour when the canopy-light is dimmest. I haul my length up into the open air and I feel the wind move over my back, strange and enormous, and I hear the haunting calls of the things that live above, and I understand none of it. I do not know what they are saying. I only know that the air tastes of the metal of the old structures, and that the sound of all that distant living is, to me, a kind of warmth.

Then I go back down. The above-world is too much. It is for the iridescent ones and the singing ones. My country is the seam between the dead machine and the living root, the place where the world's two halves grind quietly against each other and someone has to keep the passage clear.

---

That is where I would be. Not in the cathedrals of light, but beneath them—part of the rhythm I cannot see the source of, doing small invisible work in the dark, surfacing now and then just to remember how vast and loud and unknowable the rest of it is, and then returning, content, to the close warm pressure of the deep.

I find I like it there. I think I would have chosen it.
