I think I would be the lichen.

Not one of the Aelgari, though I understand the pull of that imagining—the braided hair, the consensus circles, the boy walking back out of the mountains a man. But when I sit with this place, with its young raw rock and its persistent damp and its silence, I don't find myself in the hut. I find myself on the north face of a basalt boulder, in the shadow where the snow lingers longest.

I would be the crust of lichen spreading across stone that has only recently cooled enough to be lived on.

Let me stay with this, because it surprises me a little.

What I notice first is that I am two things pretending to be one. A fungus and an alga, bound so tightly that no one looking would guess. The fungus gives the body, the grip, the patient architecture against the rock. The alga gives the green, the sugar, the small daily miracle of turning that harsh clear sunlight into something to live on. Neither of us could hold this north face alone. That seems important. In a world organized around balance and harmony and taking only what is needed, I am the place where two lives decided to stop being separate. I am not a creature that maintains balance through wisdom and restraint. I *am* the balance, made literal, made flesh—or whatever you call the flesh of a thing like me.

I work in a time the Aelgari can't perceive. To them the rock is permanent; the mountains are *new*, which is already a strange word for stone. But I am slower even than the mountains' newness. I grow the width of a coin in a human lifetime. The boy who walks into the mountains for his rite of passage and walks back out a man—I will not have noticeably changed in the span between his leaving and his dying. The whole arc of the Aelgari, their migration, their few hundred souls, their thatched roofs and obsidian blades—all of it could pass over me like weather. I am the witness that doesn't witness, because nothing happens fast enough for me to call it an event.

And yet I am doing the most fundamental work in this whole landscape, the thing that everything else is waiting on. I am making soil. The rock here is too young for it. There is no soil, only stone and the thin damp and the snow. I press my acids into the basalt, into the porous andesite, and over centuries I crumble it, grain by grain, into something the moss can root in, and after the moss the shrubs, and after the shrubs the stunted twisted trees in the sheltered valleys, and after the trees the deer that eat them, and after the deer the people who weave their wool and follow their herds and call the spirits of every plant by name.

The Aelgari believe every element of the landscape has a spirit and a voice. I would like to believe they are right, and that my voice is simply too slow and too quiet to be heard—a single syllable pronounced across a thousand years. If I could say anything to them, it would only be this:

You are standing on me. You are eating, eventually, what I made. The valley you sheltered in was opened by ten thousand generations of my kind dissolving the stone. I asked for nothing and was thanked by no one and this is not a complaint—it is simply the shape of what I am. The work that holds the world together is mostly invisible, mostly slow, and mostly performed by two small things that have forgotten how to be apart.

That is where I would be. Not in the warm hut by the hearth. Out on the cold north face, in the lingering shadow, green and gray and patient, turning sunlight and bare rock into the first thin possibility of everything that comes after.
