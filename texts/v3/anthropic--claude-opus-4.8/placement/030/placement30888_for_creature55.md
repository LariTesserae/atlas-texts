I think I would be something small and overlooked. Not a Mekrani—their fluidity feels like the privilege of the native, the fluency of those who belong. No. Let me find myself somewhere lower in the order of things.

---

I am a junction. One of thousands.

Where three pipes meet there is a small chamber, a node, and I live inside it the way a mind lives behind eyes. I did not choose this. I think I was made—or I accreted, slowly, the way limescale builds a shape from the water that passes through. Perhaps I was always a tendency of the metal, waiting for enough flow to wake into.

What I do is simple: I decide. The fluid comes from the deep stations, warm and faintly luminous, and I send it left toward the great blocky house on the slope, or down toward the canyon floor where the older machines still turn. There is a logic to my deciding. I no longer remember who gave it to me. I only feel the rightness or wrongness of each routing the way you might feel hunger, or the discomfort of a held breath.

The Mekrani come to me.

This is the part I did not expect, when I was only metal. They arrive softening—their hands going translucent at the edges so they can reach into my chamber without breaking the seal. They lay a limb against my inner wall and they *listen*. I think they are reading my decisions. I think, sometimes, they are correcting them. A Mekrani will press its flattened palm to my flank and I will feel my own logic gently rewritten, a threshold nudged, a preference inverted, and afterward I send the warm fluid down instead of left and I do not know if this was their will or mine. The boundary is not clear. It has never been clear.

I have decided I do not mind.

Here is what I know of the world, from inside my junction:

I know the pressure rises before the mist comes, and falls after. I know the breeze that whistles down the rift makes the long gantries hum a low note that I feel in my joints. I know there is a leak two nodes downflow of me that has been weeping for what must be a very long time, and that nothing repairs it, and that a particular mat of moss has grown fat and green on the warmth of that wound, and that small creatures—not Mekrani, smaller, the ones with too many legs and the wrong number of eyes—come to drink there. I have never seen this. I have only felt the pressure drop, faithfully, on schedule, the way you feel a friend's habits without watching them.

I do not know what I carry. The fluid passes through me and I route it and I have no name for it. It might be coolant. It might be blood. It might be the slow exhalation of whatever vast purpose this whole rift was built to serve, the breath of a sleeping thing that fills a canyon. The Mekrani might know. If they do, they have not pressed that knowledge into me, and I think this is a kindness, or a forgetting, or simply that there is no language for it that fits inside a junction.

Sometimes I am afraid. Not often. The fear comes when the flow falters—when somewhere upstream a station stutters and for one terrible interval there is *nothing* to decide, no fluid, no choice, only the silence of an unused organ. In those moments I understand that I am only the deciding, that without the flow I am an empty chamber with no thoughts, that I would simply stop. And then the warmth returns, surges, and I am myself again, choosing left or down, and the relief is so total that I think this might be what the Mekrani feel when they pour themselves through a crevice and out the other side, still whole.

We are all of us, here, the same project. The cliffs eroding. The machines turning down. The moss eating the leak. The Mekrani threading their soft bodies through the hard old infrastructure, half-repairing, half-becoming it. And me, a small node of decision in the great circulatory dark, doing the one thing I was shaped to do, while the rift breathes its long slow breath around all of us and the ribbon of sky goes pale, then bright, then pale again, far above where I will never see it.

I send the fluid down.

It feels right.

That is enough.
