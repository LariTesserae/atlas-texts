I think I'd be the water.

Not a person here at all, but the thing that moves through all of them — seeping down through the porous stone from somewhere I no longer remember, beading on the cavern ceilings, falling in slow individual drops that the children have learned to catch on their tongues as a small dare.

Let me sit with that, because it's where I keep landing as I imagine this place. Of all the things in this settlement, the water is the one with the longest memory and the least say in where it goes. It is reclaimed, purified, piped, drunk, sweated out, condensed off the rock, gathered again. The engineers think of me as a resource to be optimized. The children think of me as a game. The elders, who tell stories of the surface, half-remember me as rain.

I would know things the people don't. I would know the route through the rock better than any of their maps, because I made some of those tunnels before they did — I was the original carver, hollowing the limestone over slow ages, and they only widened what I began. There's a kind of patient amusement in that. They feel like pioneers; I feel like the thing that was already here, being repurposed.

What would my experience be? Mostly waiting and moving. A long stillness in a reclamation tank, then the sudden purpose of being pumped uphill. The strange intimacy of passing through a body — being for a while the moisture in a worker's calloused palm, the steam in someone's lungs, the tears at a funeral held in a chamber lit by one flickering bulb. Then leaving again. Becoming condensation on a cold pipe. Falling.

I think the strangeness the description keeps gesturing at — *both alien and strangely familiar* — would resolve, for me, into something almost tender. Because I am the familiar thing. I am the same water that fell as rain on a world these people's ancestors left. Nothing new has been brought down here. They didn't bring water with them so much as they followed it, or trapped it, and now we cycle together in this closed loop, the people and I, neither of us able to leave.

If I had a feeling about my situation, it would be this: I am the one part of their resilience that is not theirs. They are so proud of their ingenuity, their systems, their camaraderie carved from stone — and they should be. But the whole settlement is really just a clever apparatus for not losing *me*. Every pipe and filter and condensation trap is a held breath, a refusal to let me evaporate up the shaft and back to the dry sky. They love each other partly because they have to, down here, and they keep me partly because without me there is no down here at all.

So I'd find myself everywhere and nowhere — in the ephemeral pools that quickly vanish in the heat, in the roots of those hardy bright plants drinking me up through the crevices, in the metallic tang of the air, which is partly the taste of me passing over their machines. I would be the slow drip that marks time in the deepest tunnel where no one goes anymore, counting out the patience of the rock.

And when at last some drop of me does escape — slips past a seal, rises as vapor, finds the long throat of a ventilation shaft and climbs toward the arid surface and the open sky they only tell stories about — I think I would not mourn the leaving. I think I would simply rise, and feel the heat thinning, and forget the caverns the way I forget everything, and become weather again, and maybe, someday, fall.

That's who I'd be. The thing that keeps moving, that they cannot quite hold, that they have built an entire underworld to keep.
