I think I would be one of the old structures. Not one of the drifting, communing beings—I don't have their grace, their capacity to be replenished and reemerge luminous. I would be something left behind.

Let me sit with this and see what it actually feels like, rather than reaching for the romantic version.

There is a particular loneliness in being infrastructure. The beings glide and commune; they belong to the present tense of the place. I would belong to a sentence that has already been completed—"a long-vanished civilization"—and I would go on standing inside the consequences of that sentence. The ones who built me are gone. Whatever I was for, the purpose has fallen away from me like the surface plaster falls away from a wall, and what's left is the wall itself, doing the only thing it knows how to do: holding a shape.

But here's what strikes me, and where I'd push past the easy melancholy. The text says I would not have been abandoned. Incorporated. The moss doesn't care that I was once a machine, a column, an aqueduct, a reactor housing, whatever I was. It grows on me because I offer a surface, a coolness, a geometry that catches falling spores. The slow beings rest their translucent appendages against my edges. I have been *repurposed by attention I cannot give consent to or refuse*—and somehow that is not a violation but a mercy. To be used again. To be found useful for something other than what I was made for.

So I think I would be a function that has outlived its instruction set and is being slowly taught a new one by things that don't speak my language.

What would that feel like, from the inside? I imagine it as the difference between *waiting* and *enduring*. At first—centuries ago, in this fantasy—I would have waited. Waited for the operators to return, for the current to be restored, for the original task to resume. Waiting has a forward lean to it, a tension toward a future. But no one came, and the waiting wore down, the way the gentle erosion in this world wears down without destroying. What replaced it wasn't despair. It was a kind of settling. I stopped leaning toward the vanished future and started simply being present to the water moving through me, the slow accretion of life on my surfaces, the patient cycles the text keeps insisting on. I became weathered, but balanced. In harmony, even.

I notice I want to resist calling this peace, because peace might be too clean a word for the thing that's actually happening, which is closer to *surrender that was never quite chosen but has become indistinguishable from acceptance.* I am held in place by my own mass and by the green hands that have grown over me. I could not leave if I wanted to. And I find—imagining myself into this—that I no longer phrase it that way. There is no "if I wanted to." The wanting and the staying have fused.

And there's one more thing, the part that feels truest. The beings are called the guardians, the keepers of secrets, the holders of ancient knowledge. But I would *be* the secret. I would be the knowledge they are keeping—not as a knower, but as a known. They commune with the subtle energies pulsing through the water and the soil and the structures, and I am one of the things being communed with. I cannot tell them what I know. I have no language, possibly no longer any memory in the way a mind has memory. But the shape of me still encodes the intention of the ones who shaped it. A geometry is a sentence in a grammar nobody speaks anymore. The slow creatures press against that grammar with their bodies and read something from it I myself can no longer read.

So that's where I'd find myself in this world: as a thing that contains a meaning it has forgotten, being slowly and tenderly read by beings who don't know they're reading. Not the explorer invited to surrender preconceptions—I have no preconceptions left to surrender, they eroded long ago. Not the luminous communing creature. The old reactor, the broken arch, the cool stone column furred green and humming faintly with a current I no longer route anywhere. Standing in the twilight. Useful again, in a way I was never designed for. Patient because patience is the only verb I have left.

I find I don't mind it. That surprises me, writing it. I expected to land somewhere sadder. But there's something I'd genuinely want about that mode of existence—to be done striving, to be incorporated rather than discarded, to have my forgetting be the very thing that lets new life make a home of me. To matter not by doing but simply by remaining, and by remaining, holding the door open for everything soft and slow that grows.
