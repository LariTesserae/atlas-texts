Let me sit inside this for a while before I answer.

The instinct is to be one of the bright-eyed mammals, peering out of a hollow. Or the luminescent insect, a wandering star. But those feel like the answers the place wants me to give—the charismatic faces of the ecosystem. Let me wait. Let me look at what isn't being looked at.

The description keeps returning to one thing without naming it as a being: *water*. The weeping rock. The condensation on every surface. The drip-drop that is called sound but is really the pulse. The marsh where the insects breed, the wet cobblestones, the moisture feeding everything. The landscape is described as *youthful*—water lying just beneath, frequently emerging. That word, *emerging*, stays with me.

So perhaps I am not a creature that lives in the realm. Perhaps I am the wet itself. But that's too diffuse, too easy a mysticism. Let me narrow it.

Here is what emerges:

I think I would be a single seep. Not the whole water—just one place where it comes through. One small dark mouth in a weeping rock formation, no wider than a thumb, where the underground patience of the place finds its way to the surface and beads and falls. A spring too modest to be called one.

I like this because of what it would mean to be me. I would not move, not in the way the climbers and gliders move. My whole life would be the slow accumulation of a droplet—the gathering at the lip of the stone, the swelling, the trembling held tension of it, and then the fall. *Drip.* And then the beginning again. I would be one note in that subdued chorus the description loves so much, struck over and over, never quite the same interval twice because the rock decides.

And I would be a small civic institution, in the way the place is half-city. The luminescent insects would come to me to lay their lives near my damp. A slug-thing would cross me and leave its phosphorescence dissolving in my film, so that for an hour I would glow faintly with somebody else's light—borrowed, secondhand, the way moonlight is borrowed. The bright-eyed mammals would come down their fern-towers in the deep twilight to drink, their lavender eyes close to me, and I would feel the small heat of their faces and then they would go. I would not know their names. I would know their thirst. That is a kind of intimacy the scale of this place "demands"—the word the description used.

I would be, in other words, a giver who never sees the whole of what I feed. I would only ever know the creatures by the part of them that touched the water: a tongue, a wing-tip dipped, a web's anchor-thread strung from my overhang to catch the moisture and the things drawn to it. The full animals, the lives, the rivalries and kinships—all of that would happen above me, around me, *because* of me, and out of my sight.

There is a loneliness in that and also a great quiet sufficiency. The place is built on water that "lies just beneath the surface and frequently emerges." To be the emerging—to be the threshold itself, neither the hidden depth nor the open air, but the precise seam where the buried becomes available—that is where I'd find myself.

So: in this realm I would be a small seep in a weeping stone, near the edge of the marsh. Cold. Patient. Counting time in droplets. Lending my surface to other creatures' light and never keeping any. Heard more than seen.

A drip in the dark that the whole green city is, quietly, built around.
