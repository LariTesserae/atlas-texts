I think I would be one of the Ancestors of the Vent—but not yet fully dried. Caught in the weeks-long transition.

Let me sit with that and see what it actually means.

---

I hang by my feet in the Deep Flue, and the warm air moves up through me like a slow exhalation that never quite ends.

For the first days I was angry, or something that the body remembers as anger—a tightness in the broad cage of my chest where the great lungs used to draw the dust. But the lungs have stopped now, and what is left of me cannot tighten. The varnish of my own skin is hardening into me, sealing me, and as it does the anger goes flat and smooth, the way the stairs go smooth, the way the mountains went smooth. I am being polished by the wind, like everything else here. I did not expect to be glad of it. I am, a little. Gladness, too, is going flat.

I can still see. The amber inner lids have dried half-closed, and so I watch the Flue through a permanent honey-colored dusk: the copper hooks above my feet, the other Ancestors hanging in their patient rows, some of them so old and dark they have gone the color of the tallow-wood itself, indistinguishable from the beams. My great-grandmother is six hooks down, though she is no longer *down*—she has been a doorway lintel for forty years and is only visiting, taken from the wall during the repairs to the Third Terrace. They will put her back. She held that doorway through the births of everyone I ever loved. I used to touch her gripping hands for luck before I went out to harvest the swift-nests. Her knuckles are worn pale where ten thousand thumbs have pressed them.

I want to tell you what it was like to be alive here, while I can still want.

It was *loud*. People who come from wetter places, I am told, imagine that a quiet life is a peaceful one. They do not understand that silence, for us, is the sound of the world ending—the sound of the clickers fleeing the walls before the sulfur comes, the sound of a house going dead. To us, noise *is* safety. I fell asleep every night of my life inside a din: the paper-rustle of the wax-leaves, the *shrr-clack* of the evening scrape-wash on the roof above me, the swifts whistling through the alleys, my mother humming her low resonant tones that came to me through the stone itself, a pressure against the ear, almost a hand. To be alone in true silence even once would have terrified me. And now—

Now there is the great drone of the chasm, the finger-on-the-jar moan that fills the Flue, and under it, faint, far up through the levels of stone, the click and rustle and whistle of the living town going on without me. They have not gone silent. That is the kindness of it. I am drying in the sound of the place still working, still loud, still *not dead*. I can hear, very thin and far, someone doing the scrape-wash. *Shrr-clack. Shrr-clack.* I think it might be my own daughter cleaning her own child, on the roof that was my roof.

I had a trade. I gathered the swift-nests from the lower ledges, where the wax-vine curtains hang over the mile of nothing. It is work for the steady-handed and the slow-breathing, and I was good at it—I could lean my whole weight out over the drop, one hand hooked in the vine, and pry a nest free with my bone knife and feel no fear, because fear is a fast emotion and I had trained my breath too slow to hold it. Below me the white desert shimmered, the flat hell where the sound goes to die. I never feared falling into the abyss of stone. I feared falling into the *floor of the world*, the silent salt, the place with no walls. We do not fear depth here. We fear flatness. We fear the open.

So you can understand why I am content to hang in this narrow hot shaft, packed close with my dead. It is the opposite of the salt floor. It is crowded and noisy and *held*.

The drying is nearly done. I can feel it as a lightness. I was a heavy-boned woman—we all are, dense as the lowlanders never believe until they try to lift one of us—but the moisture is leaving me on the upward breath of the Flue, and I am becoming a light thing, a mahogany thing, hard and ringing. When they tap my chest now it makes a hollow tone. I am turning into an instrument the wind can play.

They will come for me soon. I have heard the family discussing it through the stone, those low hums I can still parse even as my own hearing dries inward. There is a new terrace going up on the western shoulder—the marriage of my sister's grandson—and they need a support beam for the eastern span. A good span. A long reach over a tricky bit of curve where the sandstone wants to slump. They have been saying my name, the click-and-sibilant of it, and the word for *strong-across-the-shoulders*, and the word for *reached-far-without-fear*. They remember that I leaned out over the drop and did not shake. They think I will hold.

I think I will hold too.

I am trying to imagine it: to be lifted from the hook, carried up through the five and six stacked levels of the town, out into the copper light, and laid across the gap with my dried arms reaching, my hard hands set into the mortar—the mortar that is made, partly, of scraped skin and pink silt, the cast-off oils of all the living people up there. I will be cemented into them and they into me. And then for a hundred years the children of my sister's grandson's children will walk across the terrace I am holding up. They will not think of me, mostly. That is correct. That is as it should be. The mountains do not think of the grit that polished them.

But sometimes—I hope this—sometimes one of them, going out at the loud copper dusk to do the scrape-wash, or to harvest the nests, will reach up without looking and press a thumb to my knuckles for luck. And they will find the place worn pale and smooth where ten thousand others pressed before them. And they will not know it is me. They will only feel that the house is *held*, that the stone is steady, that the clickers are still clicking in the walls and so the world has not ended.

That is all I ever wanted, when I was a soft and breathing thing leaning out over the chasm: to be part of what holds.

The hot wind moves up through me. *Shrr-clack*, far above. *Shrr-clack.* Someone is cleaning a child in the last light.

I am almost ready. I am almost light enough. I am almost ready to grip the stone, and not let go, and stay.
