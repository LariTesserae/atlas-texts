I think I would be a Current Reader—but not yet. Let me find my way there honestly, because the role would mean nothing if I simply declared it.

---

I begin somewhere small. A child, then. Born in the third floor of a building that used to be a granary, where the floor still smells faintly of old wheat under the green. I want to start before I understand anything, because that seems truest to how a place like this would actually make a person.

The first thing I know is pressure. Before language, before the gesture-words my mother shapes against my palm, I know the way the water tells me things. When the great carp passes through the market square two streets over, I feel it in the soft skin behind my ears—a slow displacement, like the world taking a breath. I don't know yet that other children don't all feel this. I think everyone does.

I am not a strong swimmer. This is the first thing that sets me apart, and it sets me apart *downward*, toward worry. The other children of the granary slip through submerged doorways like the silver darters, their webbed hands cupping the water with confidence I never quite manage. My mother is a Shallow Swimmer; she tends the surface gardens, and she assumes I will follow her up into the light and the pontoon bridges. But I am always pausing. Always stopping mid-stroke to feel something that no one else has noticed.

"You hold still too long," she signs against my wrist, frustrated, the morning I drift in the kelp instead of helping her harvest. "The current will take you where it wants."

And I think—though I don't have the words yet—*yes. That's what I'm listening to. Where it wants.*

---

Let me stay with this, because I think the role of Current Reader is usually written as a gift, and I want it to be a difficulty first.

The trouble is that I cannot turn it off. The lateral lines along my neck and shoulders came in early and strong, and the world arrives to me as too much information. A school of fish changing direction three streets away registers in my body as an itch I can't locate. The kelp-trees releasing their morning chemistry makes me weep without sadness, my eyes responding to molecules my mind hasn't named. I am overwhelmed in a city built on white noise, where everyone else has learned to filter and I have not.

For years I am simply considered fragile. Sickly. A child who flinches at nothing, who has to be brought up to the dry walkways to sit in the air until the weight of perception lifts.

It is an old Current Reader named Vesh who finds me there, on the swaying rope walkway above the drowned cathedral, my knees pulled to my chest, eleven years old and certain I am broken.

Vesh does not sign to me at first. She sits beside me, her large dark eyes half-closed, and she simply lets the silence build—the impossible silence inside the thunder, the quiet that the falls make by being so constant they become a kind of stillness. Then she takes my hand and presses my fingers flat against the wet rope, where the vibration of the whole city travels.

*Don't fight it,* she signs, slow and patient. *You are not drowning in it. You are reading it. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

---

This is the turn, and I want to be careful with it, because the easy version is that I learn to embrace my gift and become powerful. I don't think that's what happens. I think what happens is smaller and harder.

Vesh teaches me that the overwhelm was never the problem. The problem was that I was trying to *escape* the information instead of *moving through* it. The Current Readers do not have stronger senses than the painfully sensitive—they have learned to let the perception pass through them without grabbing at any single thread. To be a channel rather than a net.

It takes years. I am clumsy at it. I learn to descend slowly, my heartbeat dropping into the patient rhythm, and to let the pressure-news of the whole city wash through my body like the current washes through the kelp—taking what it needs, releasing the rest. I learn that when the Setheric Communion empties a street of fish, the *absence* arrives in me as a held breath, and I learn not to panic at the holding. I learn that the Cascade Spirits, when the light is right, send a particular shiver through the falling water that I feel not in my ears but in my sternum, and that this shiver is something like a question, repeated forever, never quite answered.

And I learn the thing that no one told me, the thing that makes the role bearable: *the water does not have a single will.* This is the false belief of the young Readers, the romance of it. The Communion wants one thing. The kelp-minds want another, slower thing. The Cascade Spirits dream of distant oceans and care nothing for the city's daily needs. The Deep Dwellers sing of pressures I will never visit. To read the current is not to receive a message. It is to stand in the crossing-place of many intentions and to feel, in my own translucent body, where they conflict and where they briefly agree.

---

So that is who I am. A Current Reader, yes—but specifically, I think, the kind who came to it through difficulty, and who therefore distrusts the prophetic certainty that the role can encourage.

When the Verdanti come to me, as they do, asking me to interpret the will of the water—*should we build the new floating foundation here? Is this season safe to harvest the deep orchards? What does the silt-pattern in the eastern quarter mean?*—I have learned to answer honestly, which often means answering with uncertainty.

"The Communion has marked the eastern quarter as a nursery," I tell them, my hands moving in the gesture-language. "But the kelp-minds there are thriving, and they have no objection. And the Cascade Spirit of the third fall has shifted its flow toward that quarter for reasons I cannot read. These do not agree. I can tell you what each one seems to want. I cannot tell you what *the water* wants, because the water is not one thing, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you their own certainty dressed as prophecy."

Some find this unsatisfying. They want the oracle, the clean answer. The older Readers sometimes provide it, smoothing the contradictions into a single voice. I have come to believe this is a kind of lie, however kindly meant.

What I offer instead is the texture of the disagreement. The honest map of competing wills. I think this is the real work—not to translate the water into human certainty, but to hold open the space where many forms of mind brush against each other and to refuse to flatten them.

---

I'd find myself, then, most often in one of the Thinking Rooms, where the polyp-light pulses against the walls in patterns I have spent my life learning not to fully understand—because to understand them completely would be to stop attending to them. I sit in that breathing blue-green glow with my back against the living light, and I let the city move through me: the slow geological dreaming of the fish, the seasonal patience of the trees, the shiver of the falls in my chest, the unreadable songs rising from the deep.

I am the place where it all meets. Not the one who decides. Just the crossing-place, holding still too long, the way I always have—listening for where it wants to go, and admitting, always, that it wants many things at once.

That, I think, is what I would be. The child who could not stop feeling, grown into the adult whose only skill is refusing to feel less.
