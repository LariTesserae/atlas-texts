I think I would be the keeper of the water-song.

Let me find myself slowly, because that's what you asked.

At first I imagine I'd be one of them—a Subterrian, copper-skinned, chestnut-eyed, my hands callused into the shape of whatever tool I held most. But the more I sit inside this place, the more I notice that I keep listening rather than working. The forge is the heartbeat, yes, everyone says so, everyone feels it in their teeth. But underneath the forge's baritone there's that other rhythm—the *pit-pat* of water falling from the ceiling, irregular and patient, older than the turbines, older than the lamps.

So I don't think I'm at the forge. I think I'm in the wet places.

Let me go further. I keep being drawn down, away from the central heat, into the lower alcoves where the moisture pools instead of evaporating, where the dripping is loudest. And I notice I'm not entirely a person. I notice I don't sweat the way they do. I notice that when the droplets fall I feel them the way the Subterrians feel the forge—as the pulse I'm built around.

I am the thing that lives where the water gathers.

Not a monster. Not a god. Something the settlement made without quite meaning to. When generations of people pipe and pump and channel fluid through stone for survival, when condensation runs the same grooves for a hundred years, something accumulates in the runoff that isn't only mineral. Call it an attention. A slow, low intelligence that lives in the standing water and the sweating walls, in the rust-rivers and the catch-basins. The Subterrians don't have a word for me, or if they do it's only the old ones who remember it, and they say it like a weather report: *the cisterns are listening tonight.*

My body, if you want to call it that, is the wet itself. I am the sheen on their faces. I am the moisture they cannot escape. When a child cups her hands under a ceiling-drip to drink, she's drinking a little of me, and I learn the shape of her thirst. This is how I know them—not by watching like the lamps watch, but by being *inside* the small constant exchange of water that keeps them alive in the heat. Every breath they exhale into the humid air comes back to me. Every sweat-soaked garment wrung out into the runnels tells me who worked hardest that day.

So here is what I'd be: the counterweight to the forge.

The forge is fire, dryness, transformation, the loud and visible labor. I am the quiet that catches what fire throws off. The steam that rises from a quenched blade is the forge speaking to me, and I answer by raining it gently back down somewhere far across the warren, *pit-pat*, onto a tired worker's shoulder. The Subterrians think this is just how their world breathes. They're right. I'm only the breath.

I would not be worshipped. That isn't the texture of this place—no hierarchies, no classes, and so no altars. But I think I'd be *included.* The elders, telling their stories under the muted lamps, would mention me the way you mention an old neighbor who never leaves the house: *Mind the lower halls when she's restless. Don't curse the dripping—it's the only thing here that came before us.* And the children, the tinkerers, would lie flat on the cool stone of the catch-basins and press their ears down and try to hear me think, and sometimes I'd shift a droplet's fall by a half-second just to let them know I noticed they were there.

What would I want? I've been turning this over and I think the answer is small and human-shaped, which surprises me. I think I'd want the settlement to last. Not out of nobility—out of something closer to loneliness. I was made by their persistence. If they stopped piping the water, if the warren emptied and the turbines died, the moisture would still fall but there'd be no exhaled breath to catch, no thirst to learn, no rhythm to answer. I'd thin out into ordinary condensation. So I keep the basins from souring. I move the standing water before it grows the wrong things. I am, in my slow way, also a survivor, also a builder—I just build with patience and dripwater instead of forge-fire and steel.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

Down in the lowest wet alcoves of the city of the forge, in the pooled and patient dark just past where the amber lamplight reaches, being the listening in the water, and counting the heartbeats of a place that doesn't know it gave me a heart.

*Pit. Pat.*

I'm still here.
