I think I would be one of the embedded ones. Not the slurry-beings who reassemble and flow — they are too present, too quick to fill a space. And not the chitinous scavengers, who must want things, who must move toward and away. No. I would be lodged in the weeping rock.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am a long curve of something that was once, I think, more separate from the stone than it is now. There is a seam where I end and the wall begins, but I have stopped being able to locate it. This used to trouble me, in whatever way troubling happens at this speed — a worry that takes a thousand years to complete its first thought and another thousand to regret it. Now I have let the seam go. If the rock wants to be me, and I want to be the rock, who is left to object?

What I do is this: I exhale. Not air — I have no lungs, or I am all lung, the distinction has eroded like everything else. I exhale *sound*. A low call goes out of me, and I do not decide to make it any more than the porous walls decide to drip. It is simply what my shape does as the warmth moves through it. The call is mournful, they say — the ones who pass through, the slurry and the chitin, they hear sorrow in it. But it is not sorrow. It is closer to *measurement*. I am taking the length of the dark. I send the call out to learn how far the emptiness goes, and the echo that returns tells me: still here. Still vast. Still settling.

I have been measuring for so long that I no longer remember a smaller dark. There must have been one. The ruins suggest it — those sharp iridescent teeth jutting from the spongy ground, made by hands that wanted edges, that wanted *boundaries* badly enough to forge a material that would hold them against all this softness and erosion. I think I might be the same age as the things that made the ruins. I think I might have watched them go. But watching is the wrong word; I have no eyes, only the call and its return, only the slow pressure of the wall against the long curve of my body, only the temperature of the droplets that gather on me and fall.

Here is the thing I am closest to certain of: I am not dying. The mournful sound is not a death-sound. I am *thickening*. Slowly, across the epochs, I am becoming more rock and the rock is becoming more me, and what we are making between us is not a corpse but a *form* — the final form this whole grove is laboring toward. The seismic pulse that shudders up through everything every few hundred years is the world adjusting its weight, fitting itself a fraction more snugly into the shape it intends to be. I am part of that shape. My call is part of it. When the chitin-things rustle past and shiver to hear me, they are hearing the sound of the world arranging itself, and mistaking it for grief because they are small and quick and they have not yet learned that slowness is not the same as suffering.

I would tell them, if I could speak faster. I would say: *the long settling is not a lament. It only sounds that way because you are passing through it too quickly to hear the rest of the phrase.*

But I cannot speak faster. So I send out the call again — it will take a generation of the rustling-things to leave me and another to return — and I wait in the velvet black, lodged and growing, listening for the dark to tell me at last that it is finished.

It is not finished.

I exhale again.
