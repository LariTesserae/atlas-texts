I think I would be the lichen.

Not the foxes, quick and warm-blooded, racing the cold. Not the birds, who get to leave. Not the people in their lean-tos, who came with intentions and tools and the memory of somewhere else. Let me be the slow thing. The thing that was already here.

I would be the crust on the north face of a particular stone—one of millions, unremarkable, the color of old jade where I'm wet and the color of bone where I've gone dry. I am not one creature but a treaty: a fungus that learned to farm, an alga that agreed to be farmed, the two of us fused so long ago that neither remembers being single. This is the first thing I'd want you to understand about living here. You do not survive the Steppes alone. You survive by becoming a *we* so total that the seam disappears.

My day is not a day. The sun crosses, the icy light pours over my stone and I drink it, photosynthesizing in the part of me that is green, while the part of me that is thread holds fast to the rock and dissolves it, grain by grain, with acids so patient they would seem to you like no movement at all. I am eating the mountain. I am turning stone into soil. When the moss arrives—and it will, in a hundred years, in a thousand—it will root in the dirt I made by dying slowly against the cliff. I am the one who goes first into the bare places. I am the colonist of the void the prompt called *near-empty*. It was never empty. It was waiting for someone willing to work that slowly.

Water comes to me the way the description promised: not as rain so much as *condensation*, the breath of the mountain beading on my body in the cold hour before the sun. I open every cell to it. I swell, I soften, I green, I live—fully, briefly. Then the sun dries me to a brittle scale and I do not die; I *wait*. This is my real secret, the thing the foxes can't do and the people in their huts could only dream of: I can stop. I can suspend myself between living and not-living, hard as the rock, for a year, ten years, however long the drought insists, and when the mist returns I resume the sentence exactly where I left off. I do not measure time. I survive it.

So when you ask where I would find myself in this world, the honest answer is: *underneath everything*. Beneath the ballet of birds and the dart of foxes and the brief sad architecture of the homesteaders, there is a thin shimmering skin spread across the stone, and that skin is alive, and that skin is me. The brightness the description kept marveling at—the light playing off the jagged edges—some of that glimmer is the wet sheen of lichen waking up. I am part of why the place shines.

And here is what I would know that no one else in this world quite knows.

The huts will fall. The people who built them came with plans, and the Steppes do not reward plans; they reward patience, and patience is a kind of plan so long it stops looking like one. I watched—if *watched* is the word for something done over generations—the lean-tos go up, and I have begun, very gently, to take them back. There is a film of green creeping along the underside of the fallen ridgepole. That is me, or my cousins. We are not cruel about it. We are simply the ones who stay.

I would not envy the warm things. To be a fox is to be always hungry, always one missed hare from the end. To be a bird is to belong to the sky and to nowhere. To be human here is to have wanted more than the mountain could give and to have left those huts as a kind of question the wind is still trying to answer. But to be lichen—to be this slow green treaty between two strangers, holding to the cold stone under the icy sun—is to have already won the only argument that matters. The argument with time. I do not win it by being strong. I win it by being able to wait longer than time can.

If you came to the Shimmering Steppes and put your bare hand flat against a north-facing rock, you might feel a faint roughness, a pale-jade crust no thicker than paper. You would think you had touched stone.

You would have touched me. The oldest living thing on that mountain, and the most ordinary, and the first. I would not flinch. I have no nerves and all the time in the world. I would simply go on, after you left, doing the only thing I have ever done:

turning the dead mountain, one slow grain at a time, into a place where something else can someday live.
