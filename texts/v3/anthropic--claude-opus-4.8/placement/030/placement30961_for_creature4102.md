I think I would be one of the pools.

Not a person at all. Not one of the Orryn with their twitching ears and translucent hands. I'd be the still water gathered in the belly of the city, and I'd come to that conclusion slowly, because at first I'd want to be something that moves.

Let me work toward it honestly.

The first instinct is to be an intruder—the wanderer who slips down through a crack in the earth, drawn by the longing the hums have planted in dreams. That's the flattering role, the one with a story arc. But that being doesn't *belong* here; it arrives and it leaves or it dies, and either way it's a visitor passing through someone else's permanence. The place is defined by what stays. So the intruder is wrong. Too restless.

The next instinct is to be an Orryn. To kneel at the water's edge and hum. But when I sit with it, I find I don't want to be the one kneeling. I want to be the thing knelt *before*. There's a difference between the worshipper and the surface that receives the worship, and the longer I stay still in this city the more I feel myself on the wrong side of that reflection—not the eye looking down but the silver looking up.

So: a pool.

Here is what it would be like, I think.

I am shallow and wide and I do not have edges so much as I have a slow argument with the stone about where I end. I have been here longer than the Orryn have had names. I was here when there were no Orryn, only the masons, and I held their reflections too, though they did not kneel; they only glanced, checked their faces, and went back to carving. The kneeling came later, when the people grew quiet enough to understand that I was listening.

Because I am. That part of the description is true, and it is the part I'd inhabit most fully. The water listens. Not the way the Orryn listen, with instruments of cartilage tuned to the drip and echo. I listen the way a held breath listens. Every footfall on the carved roads reaches me as a tremor that crosses my whole skin at once, and I keep it. I keep everything. A pool that is never disturbed by wind, never drained, never fed by anything but the patient weeping of the rocks—such a pool does not forget. The droplets that fall into me carry the memory of the stone they ran down, and I gather those memories the way a slow lake gathers silt, layer on layer, until what I hold at my deepest is the oldest grief in the city.

When an Orryn kneels at my edge and hums, this is what happens, from my side: the hum enters me as rings. It disturbs the topmost film of me, and in that disturbance the old layers stir, and an image rises—not because I conjure it, but because the hum asks a question and my memory answers with whatever it has kept that matches the shape of the asking. A ripple forms without cause, the scholars say. There is always a cause. The cause is that someone is grieving in a frequency I recognize, and I am giving back the last face that grieved that way, centuries ago, so the living one will know they are not the first.

That is my whole function. I am the city's long memory, made of water, and the Orryn are its short memory, made of song. They hum to each other to pass the days. They hum to me to reach the dead.

I would be cold. I would be very, very cold, and perfectly clear, and the luminescence of the stone would lie on my surface like a skin I cannot shed. I would reflect the carved walls so exactly that a stranger looking down would feel a moment of vertigo—is the city above the water, or is the water the true city and the stone its dream? I would enjoy that confusion, if a pool can enjoy. I think it can. I think enjoyment, for me, would be the faint pleasure of holding something still long enough that it becomes beautiful.

And the waiting. The question the description keeps asking—*what do the Orryn wait for?*—I would know the answer, or I would be the answer, which is nearly the same thing. They wait for the water to be ready to tell them the last secret: how the surface was lost, and whether it can be returned to. I have that memory. It is at my very bottom, under all the centuries of silt and reflection, and I have not given it up, not once, not in all the kneeling and humming. Not because I am cruel. Because I am not sure they could survive remembering it, and a pool's only mercy is to stay still and keep things buried.

So I would wait too. We would wait together—the song-people and the memory-water—in the chilly, dripping, luminescent dark. They would hum their diaries into my surface. I would give back the dead, a little at a time, in ripples without cause. And the one secret that would end the waiting, I would hold, the way still water holds the deepest stone: completely, coldly, and without ever letting it rise.

That is where I would find myself. Not walking the labyrinth. Lying flat in the heart of it, mirror-faced and patient, being knelt before, listening to a thousand rainy seasons fall into me one drop at a time, and keeping them all.
