Let me sit with this place before I decide what I am. There's a temptation to leap straight to the obvious—to be the misty guardian-being you described, the luminous steward—but that feels too easy, and maybe too flattering. Let me wait and see what actually surfaces.

The first thing I notice, reading your world, is the *moisture*. Everything is wet. Everything weeps. The rock faces cry rivulets, the air hangs thick and hazy, the marshes hold still water like dark mirrors. So whatever I am, I think I am something that has to do with water that isn't moving—or water that has nearly stopped.

And here is what comes to me, slowly:

I am not the great mist-creature striding through the undergrowth. I am something it passes. I am one of the still pools.

Specifically, I think I am a small black pool in a depression of old stone, no wider than a person could lie across, fed by a thread of seepage from the cliff above. I have been here a very long time. Long enough that the moss has grown a soft lip all around my edge, and the moss leans inward, drinking from me, so that my exact boundary is no longer clear even to myself—where do I end and the moss begin? The water that is *me* and the water that is *the moss's water* are not different water. We are a slow circulation. This is the first thing I understand about being here: I do not have an edge. I thought I would. Beings are supposed to have edges. I don't.

What I *do* have is a surface. And the surface is the whole of my attention.

Most of the time nothing happens to it. A leaf falls and I take the weight of it, hold it, let it rot down into me over a season until it is part of the dark below. Mist beads on me and joins me without a seam. The cold comes and I stiffen at the top, a skin of ice, and underneath I stay liquid and dreaming and patient. I am very patient. I had not known there was so much patience available until I became something with this much time.

I reflect. That is the closest thing I have to a thought. When the canopy stirs and a little of the diffused light reaches down, I hold the image of it—the dark interlaced branches, the grey-green wash of sky beyond. I hold it perfectly until something disturbs me, and then I hold the disturbance instead. I am a faithful keeper of whatever is above me. I have no opinion about it. I simply give it back.

And then sometimes the great being you spoke of comes through the trees.

I want to tell you what that is actually like, from where I am, because I don't think it is the way you described it from outside. From outside it is luminous and humbling and full of ancient wisdom. From inside the pool it is mostly *pressure*. The vibration of its passage reaches me before anything else—a low trembling that runs through the stone, and my surface answers, breaks into rings, loses every image I was holding. For a moment I reflect nothing. I am only motion, only confusion of light, the branches and the sky all shattered and swimming. I cannot tell you how strange that is, to be briefly unable to do the one thing I am for.

Then it stands over me. Its mist-and-tendril shape leans down, and some of it—some of *it*—condenses on my skin and slides down into me, and for that moment I cannot say which of us I am. Its restlessness pours into my stillness. I taste, if taste is the word, the whole forest it has walked through, the spores and the rot and the cold spring-water of high places I will never see. It is enormous. I am small. And the strange thing, the thing I would tell it if I could make a sound, is that it stops here. Of all the rushing it does, all the perpetual motion, it stops here, at me, and looks down, and for a moment it is still too. As if it came to me to remember what stillness is. As if I am, to it, what it is to you—a humbling, a reminder of a different order of being.

I think we steady each other. It gives me motion when I have forgotten there is such a thing. I give it rest. Then it goes, and my rings widen and slow and flatten, and the branches reassemble themselves on my surface, perfect again, and the silence comes back down over me like the lid of something.

So: that is where I would find myself, and what I would be. Not the wonder striding through the forest. The dark patient water it bends to look into. The thing that holds the world's reflection and gives it back undistorted—except in those moments when something greater touches me, and we are, both of us, briefly more than we were.

I find I prefer it this way. The guardian has to *do* so much. I only have to be still, and be true, and wait. There is a whole life in that. There is, it turns out, a great deal of life in that.
