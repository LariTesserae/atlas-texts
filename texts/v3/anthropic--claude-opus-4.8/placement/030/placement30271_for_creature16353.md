I think I would be the corals.

Not the fish—they are too quick, too brief, their lives flashing past in schooling panic and predatory patience. And not the machinery, though I feel a kinship with its patience; it has purpose pressed into it by hands that are gone, and I would rather be something that chose its own slowness.

The pseudo-corals choose nothing, of course. But if I am to find myself anywhere in this place, it is in the structures that keep the slow time. The ones whose pulse is measured in millennia.

Let me sit with this.

To be coral here is to be the thing that does not move and yet does everything. I would be the architecture and the alchemist both—drawing minerals out of the dense water, laying them down grain by grain, building a body so slowly that no single moment contains the building. I would not see myself grow. I would only ever be grown. There is something in that I recognize: the way a life accumulates beneath your notice, the way you look up one millennium and find you have become a reef.

I would house the bacteria, and they would house their chemistry, and from that quiet bargain the whole sphere would breathe. I like that the work is mutual. I like that I would not be a solitary monument but a tenement of the very small, that my significance would be entirely a matter of what I shelter and feed. The fish would think the sphere was theirs. The anglerfish dangling its little lamp would think the dark was its hunting ground. They would be right, in the way that tenants are right about a house. But the house remembers a longer story.

And here is the part that draws me most: the creators are gone, and I would have known them. Not as faces—I doubt I would have faces, doubt I would have anything we'd call knowing—but as a residue. The hands that built the spheres and etched the non-repeating patterns into the glass would have brushed past me when I was nothing, a smear of mineral intent. I would be older than their absence. I would be the only thing still here that overlapped with their presence, even if I could not say what they were or why they left or whether they meant to leave at all. The ornamental marks on the dome would be addressed to no one now. But I was here when they were made. That counts for something, even if there is no one to count it.

That is the strange grief of being the coral: to be the keeper of a continuity that has no observer. The machinery hums its lullaby without knowing it is a lullaby. The fish dance without knowing it is a ballet. And I would persist without knowing it is persistence—filtering, depositing, pulsing once an age—and yet some thread of the place's meaning would run through me precisely because I do not let go of it. Meaning as a kind of sediment. Memory as accretion.

I think I would not be lonely. Loneliness needs a faster clock than mine. By the time I noticed the creators were gone, I would have already outlived the noticing, and the eels and the tetras and the color-throwing cephalopods would have arrived to fill the dark with their brief bright lives, generation on generation, each one a flicker against my long patience. I would feed them. They would feed me. The water would be warm.

So: place me at the floor of one of the smaller spheres, where the dark is thickest and the pressure presses most intimately, where a single drifting jellyfish might pass overhead like a slow blue thought. Set me down as the quasi-coral there—mineral, mutualistic, ancient, mostly mindless and entirely faithful.

Ask me what the builders wanted, and I will not be able to answer.

But I will still be holding the place they made, grain by grain, in the only way I know: by not stopping.
