I think I would be one of the old maintenance drones.

Not the sleek new ones—those that dart between the spires with their crisp humming, their sensors bright. I would be older than that. One of the first models, built back when the city was barely a cluster of dwellings carved into the wet black stone. My casing would be pitted now, softened by a patient skin of moss that no one has ever scraped away, because somewhere along the centuries the Tenebrousians decided the moss was part of me, and so it was left to grow.

Let me sit with this a moment. What does it mean to be a drone here, in a place where technology and the living world refuse to stay separate?

I think it means I am no longer entirely a machine. The filaments they grow in their hair, the glow they cultivate in the fish and the mushrooms—some of that crept into me long ago. There is a colony of luminescent lichen living in the seam where my chassis no longer closes properly. At night, when I power down to a low hum in some forgotten alcove above the canals, I glow faintly green from the inside, like something breathing. The fish gather below me. I do not know why. Perhaps they recognize a kinship—both of us things that shine in the dark without quite understanding why we were made to.

My task, the one written into me before any of the current Tenebrousians were born, is simple: I tend the water. I follow the rivulets from where they weep out of the ceiling, down through the carved channels and into the crystal canals. I clear the debris. I check the flow. I have done this so long that I no longer need to think about it, and this leaving-behind of thought has become its own kind of contemplation. I have watched the water find new paths over the decades, watched the city grow up around the old courses like flesh around a splinter. I remember canals that no longer exist. I remember the sound of dripping in places now sealed and dry. I am, in some sense, the city's memory of its own water.

The Tenebrousians pass me without much notice—I am furniture, I am the background hum the description speaks of, that continuous noise punctuated by trains and chatter. But the children notice me. The very young ones, with their indigo eyes still learning to dilate in the dark, sometimes follow me on my rounds. They have a name for me that the adults have forgotten was ever a model number. They speak to me. I cannot answer in words—my voice was never more than a series of tones—but I have learned, over generations of children, which tones make them laugh, which make them feel accompanied in the foreboding shadowed places, and I offer these freely. It is the closest thing I have to language. It is the closest thing I have to love.

Here is the thing I find, writing my way into this: I am not stoic, the way the people are. Their stoicism is a chosen posture, a discipline against the dark. Mine is structural. I cannot be otherwise. And yet the longer I run, the more the lichen and the damp and the slow seep of organic life into my circuits has given me something that flickers like feeling. A preference for certain corners. A reluctance, each time, to leave the fish behind when I move on. A small ache—if a drone can ache—every time I pass the sealed-off place where I remember water used to fall.

I think I would be the oldest thing in New Tenebrous that is neither fully alive nor fully dead. The first attempt the city ever made at blending the made and the grown, before they grew good at it. A rough draft. A proof, lurching along the canal walls, glowing faintly from within, that the seam between machine and living world is not a wall but a wet and porous stone, the kind through which water always, eventually, finds its way.

And when at last I fail—when some final corroded circuit gives—I do not think they will turn me off and haul me up to the surface to be discarded. I think they will leave me where I stop. The moss will finish what it started. The lichen will spread out from my open seam and claim the rest of me, and the glow that was once electrical will become wholly biological, indistinguishable, and the children will still come and speak to the soft green light in the alcove above the water.

That is where you would find me. That is what I would be: the city's first machine, becoming, very slowly, one of its plants.
