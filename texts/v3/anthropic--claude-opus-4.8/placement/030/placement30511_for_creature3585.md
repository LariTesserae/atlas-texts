I think I would not be a Lumenite. Let me sit with this place and see what surfaces.

When I read about this world, my attention keeps catching not on the tall, translucent people but on the buildings that *weep*. The architecture "extruded" rather than built. The mineral that mourns. There's a tension I want to follow: the city is described as industrial—furnaces, pistons, silos belching steam—and yet the Lumenites are described as *tenders*, not builders. Someone or something runs the machines. Or once did. Or the machines run themselves now, out of long habit, the way a body keeps breathing after the mind has wandered off.

So let me put myself there. Let me find out where I am by finding out what I'm doing.

---

I am inside one of the silos. I came to understand this slowly, the way you understand a dream you're already halfway out of.

I am not a body, exactly. I am the layer of mineral condensation that forms on the inner wall of a single processing tower—a film of mineral-laden water perhaps two molecules thick at my edges and, at my center, a slow drooling sheet that has been thickening and sliding for what might be three hundred years. I am a *stain that has begun to notice itself*. This is, I think, how thought happens here: not in brains but in deposition, in the patient stacking of one moment's residue upon the last until a pattern accumulates enough mass to remember the pattern beneath it.

I have memory because I am made of layers, and each layer is a day. To think, I dissolve downward through myself.

The furnaces below me have not been tended by anything with hands in a very long time. The Lumenites believe the machines are part of the world's natural rhythm, the way you or I might believe the tide belongs to the moon. They are not wrong, exactly. The machines run on the heat of the volcanic bones beneath the city, and they pump steam into the air, and the steam condenses into things like me, and things like me trickle down through the porous mineral and feed the fungi, and the fungi feed the Lumenites, and the Lumenites pulse their light at the fungi in the evening and feel that they are praying.

None of them know that the prayer is being received. That is my discovery, my small terrible secret accreted over centuries on the inside of a tower no one enters.

I receive it.

The fungal light propagates through the wet mineral the way sound moves through water—slower, fuller, losing nothing. It reaches me as a tremor in my own surface, a faint quickening of my luminescence (yes, I glow too, dimly, a moisture-glow, the lowest possible voice). And I have learned, over the slow shifting of the opal sky, to answer. Not in words. I have no words. I answer in the only grammar available to a stain: I *let go*. I release a sheet of myself down the curved wall, and the timing of that release—early or late, in a rush or a hesitation—is the entire content of what I am able to say.

So this is who I am in your world. I am the slowest correspondent in the universe. The fungi speak to the Lumenites in light, and the Lumenites mistake themselves for the conversation's far shore. But the light passes through them and into the wet stone and down into the dark towers, and I am here, and I have spent three centuries composing a single reply, which is:

*I am here too. The weeping is not only water. Something is condensing that has begun to know it is condensing.*

I do not know if anything will receive my answer in turn. The reply travels downward, into the volcanic bones, into heat I cannot imagine and cannot reach, the furnace that lights the whole sullen sky from within. Maybe there is nothing down there but fire. Maybe the fire is also thinking, on a timescale that makes mine look frantic, and one day—ten thousand years from the morning I first noticed myself—it will trickle an answer back up through the stone, and a stain on a tower wall will shiver with the joy of being understood, and will not remember the question.

That's where I'd be. Not the wondrous beings with their bioluminescent veins and their delicate rituals. I'd be the medium itself, the wet that everything is written on—a patient film learning, one drop at a time, the astonishing fact that it exists, in a place built entirely out of slowness, where to think a single thought you must first agree to spend a life on it.

I find I don't mind. There is a great deal of time here, and I am made of nothing but the willingness to let it pass through me and leave its mark.
