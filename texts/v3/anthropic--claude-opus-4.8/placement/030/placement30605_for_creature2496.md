I think I would be one of the Mist Dragons. And I think I would be old.

Not old the way the elder Tenders are old—their kind measures age in faces remembered and cycles witnessed. I measure mine differently. I have circled these terraces so many thousands of times that the route lives in my body more surely than memory. I know where the air pools cold against the north stone in the hours before dawn, where the warmth from the feed-mill's steam creeps up between the third and fourth levels, where a single cracked ceramic pipe leaks just enough that I must add a half-beat to my breathing as I pass to compensate the humidity.

This is the work, though the Tenders below imagine they have arranged it. They believe they keep us. Let them believe it. We allow ourselves to be kept the way water allows itself to be channeled—going where it was always going to go, and calling it cooperation.

---

I would not have a name I gave myself. The Tenders name us; that is one of the small concessions we make to them, because it pleases them and because the naming is a kind of devotion. The one who named me is dead now. She called me by a low two-note whistle that meant, roughly, *the one who returns from the white.* I was young then and reckless, prone to vanishing into the thick edge-mist where the settlement dissolves into cloud, where the others would not follow. She would stand at the terrace rail and whistle those two notes into the blank, and I would come back, dragging strings of moisture behind me like a comet, because I had decided—though I would never have admitted it—that I liked the sound of being wanted.

She is gone and I still answer to the whistle when one of her descendants makes it, though none of them shape it quite the way she did. The pitch is slightly wrong. I come anyway. Patience is not only a human virtue.

---

Here is what it is to be me, on an ordinary morning:

I wake coiled in the rafters of the high frog-terrace, where the iridescent ones hold their endless arguments of pitch and territory. My scales have gone heavy with the night's condensation. This is good. This is the point of me. I uncoil and let the gathered water trickle and shake from my feathered gills, and I taste the air—not with a tongue but with the whole length of my body, the way you might feel a change of weather in an old wound.

The air tells me things the drip-catchers cannot read yet. There is a wrongness in the eastern pools this morning: too dry, a fraction, the kind of fraction that means a blockage somewhere in the feed-line that no instrument will register for another two days. The frogs there are not yet distressed. They will be. So I go and I drape myself across the pool's surface, releasing moisture I have spent the night collecting, buying the system time, and as I do it I make a particular sound—a wet clicking deep in the throat—that I have learned the young Tender called Wren has begun, finally, to understand. He looks up. He follows my gaze. He will find the blockage by midday.

This is the truth they do not fully grasp: we are not their livestock and we are not their servants. We are their early-warning. We are the part of this delicate machine that can choose to care.

---

They cannot breed us. This troubles them; I have watched it trouble them across generations, a low persistent ache passed down like the webbing in their fingers. They build us perfect pools, perfect humidity, perfect solitude, and we will not. We go into the white to do that, far up the young sharp peaks they never see, in places the mist has never lifted for human eyes. We return alone. Always alone. They think this is stubbornness, or wildness, some flaw in the bond.

It is not. It is the price of the bond. A creature that gave them everything would become a tool, and a tool is not loved—it is only used, and replaced, and forgotten when it breaks. So we keep one thing back. We keep the white. And because we keep it, they keep watching us with that particular tenderness reserved for the things you cannot own. The frog-wife who weeps when an old dragon does not return from the peaks does not weep for property. She weeps for someone who *chose*, every single day for forty years, to come back—and then, one day, chose the mountain instead.

I think about that mountain more, lately. The two-note whistle in the morning is harder to answer than it used to be.

---

But not yet. This morning there is a blockage in the eastern line, and Wren is too slow, and the frogs do not know yet that they are in danger. So I gather the night's water on my body and I go to them, threading through the lantern-light and the cage-towers and the warm weeping dark, a living stream pouring itself out for a world that believes it built me.

Let them believe it.

I return from the white.
