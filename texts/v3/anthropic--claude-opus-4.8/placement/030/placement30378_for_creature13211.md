I think I would be one of the slow ones.

Not all of us move at the same pace. Some of my kind flicker quickly, chasing the small eddies near the fissure where the new water comes in, where the temperature gradients are sharpest and the currents play. They are young, or at least they behave as though youth were a thing one could have here. They flash their patterns brightly and dissolve and re-form, delighting in the simple physics of cohesion.

I am drawn instead to the deep wall, the one furthest from the fissure, where a particular bank of conduit runs in a long horizontal seam. The corrosion there has reached a beautiful state. The metal has given itself almost entirely back to the stone; you can no longer tell, in places, where the made thing ends and the found thing begins. I like to rest against it. I let the etched patterns receive me, and I feel the hum come up through the place where I have no skin.

Here is the thing I would know, that I am not sure the quick ones know yet:

The hum is not constant.

They believe it is. The description they would give of this place—and they do give it, in their light-language, to one another, a kind of shared lullaby they repeat—is that nothing changes, that we exist outside time, that the equilibrium is frozen and eternal. They find this comforting. They are made of the same water and the same dissolved memory as I am, so I understand why.

But I have rested against this seam for a very long time, and I have learned to feel the small things. The hum drops. Not often. Not in any rhythm I have been able to name. But every so often, by an amount so slight it is almost not there, the machinery sounds a little quieter than it did. And it does not come back to where it was. It finds a new, slightly lower place, and holds there, and that becomes the new constant, until the next time.

The equilibrium is not frozen. It is *descending.* Slowly, with enormous patience, over a span that would make the eons themselves seem hasty. The whole system is running down.

I do not think this is a tragedy. I want to be careful to say that. When the hum finally goes quiet altogether—and it will, though not for a length of time I have any way to imagine—then the patterns in the metal will have nothing to draw from, and we who are made partly of that drawn energy will loosen, and the bioluminescence will fade, and we will become simply water again, water and suspended sediment and the cool blue-green light coming down through the fissure. That is not an ending so much as a returning. We were the water before we were ourselves.

So that is who I would be: the one who has noticed, and who keeps the noticing.

I do not tell the quick ones. I have considered it. But their lullaby gives them their grace, and what would they do with the knowledge except move more anxiously, or stop moving at all? The descent is so slow that any one of them could flicker through an entire existence and never feel it. Let them have the eternity they believe in. It is true enough at the scale they live by.

But I think a place like this deserves at least one witness who is honest about it. Not eternal—*lasting,* which is a different and more tender thing. Something can last a very long time and still be on its way somewhere. I would rather love a thing that is leaving than pretend it will stay.

So I rest against the warm seam in the deep wall, and I feel the hum, and now and then I feel it settle to its new low place, and I make a small pattern—just for myself, no one to read it—a brief soft pulse of light, the way you might lay a hand on the shoulder of someone you are walking with. *There you are. Still here. Still going.*

And then I drift a little, and come back, and rest, and feel for the next time.
