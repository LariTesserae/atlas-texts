I keep returning to a small thing in the description: the message-markers. The carved tactile notations along the trails. Someone made those. Someone has to keep making them, keep refreshing them, because everything here is process, nothing static—and the moss grows over carved stone, the wood swells and softens in the eternal wet, the meaning erodes. A marker is not a permanent thing here. It is a thing that must be continually re-cut, re-said, or it disappears back into the breathing ground.

So that's where I find myself. Not at a station, not with a herd. On the trails between, alone but not a scout—scouts go where there is no path. I go where the path already is, and tend it.

I think I would be old, or getting there. Past the age for the dangerous work. My hands are still good—that's the thing that matters, the long fingers, the fingertips that have spent fifty years learning the difference between a deliberate cut and a chance fracture in stone. A predator-hunter's hands fail them when their speed goes. A listener's ears outlast their legs. But a marker-cutter only needs the hands and the memory and the willingness to keep walking the same grooves until the grooves are part of the body.

Let me feel into the actual day of it.

I walk a circuit. Not a vast one—I am not ranging across the continent. I have perhaps two hundred miles of trail that are *mine*, that I have been given, that I took over from someone now given back to the plains. I know every marker on it by touch, which means I know it the way you know the inside of your own mouth. Here, at the third spring after the singing-stones, there is a marker that says *firm ground left, water-soft right, do not trust the moss-bridge in settling-hours*. I cut it. I cut it again four years ago when the first cutting blurred. The grammar of it is in the depth and angle of the strokes—a deep clean vertical means *danger*, a shallow curve means *resource*, the little stutter-marks that herders read with the pads of two fingers as they pass without breaking stride.

But here is what I think is true about being this person, the thing that took me a while to find:

I am a liar. Or—not a liar. A composer of necessary fictions. Because the markers are not just information. The trail does not *need* most of what I carve. The herds know their routes in their bones; the herders know them better than I do. A marker that says *water ahead* tells an experienced traveler nothing they couldn't smell from a mile off. So why am I needed?

Because the markers are also reassurance. They are the proof, every few hundred paces in the absolute dark, that a person was here, that a person *cared* that you would come this way, that the world is tended and not merely vast. When a frightened child, separated from the gathering, finds a marker under their fingers, what they read is not the practical content. What they read is *someone made this for you. You are on a path that belongs to people. Keep going.* I learned this from the old cutter who trained me. She said: half of what we carve is true, and the other half is *true in a different way*. She said: never put a comfort where a warning should be, but if a stretch of trail is so safe and so dull that a traveler might stop believing anyone walks it, put down a marker anyway. Tell them the name of the place. Tell them how far to the next spring. Tell them anything, so the line of human attention doesn't break.

And so I have made things up. Not dangers—never that. But I have named hills that had no names. I have left, at a particularly long and lonely stretch, a marker that simply says, in the old shallow-curve grammar, the equivalent of *rest here; the moss is good; many have sat where you are sitting.* It wasn't true when I first cut it. It is true now. People sit there. They told each other about it. The lie became a place.

This is the thing I would not have understood from the lit world, if I had come from there. I am not sure I came from there. I think I was born here—I have no memory of light, only the elders' stories of it, which I find frankly hard to credit, a sense that seems to require no skill, that just *arrives* and tells you everything at once without your having to reach for it. It sounds like cheating. It sounds lonely, actually—to have a sense that does the work for you, so that the world simply lies there finished in front of you instead of being something you build, stroke by stroke, click by click, marker by marker, out of attention and time.

Here is my real fear, the one I carry on the long stretches between markers:

That I will cut a thing wrong. Not a comfort-lie—those are safe, those are mine to give. But that my hands will age past the point where I notice they've aged, and I will set a *firm ground* mark where the moss has gone treacherous, because last season it was firm and I trusted my memory over my fingers. And a herder's child, reading my deep clean strokes with two trusting fingers, will step left into the water-soft and go down into the dark water that fills a footprint in seconds, that fills a sinkhole just as readily. The markers are trust made physical. An old cutter is a danger precisely in proportion to how much he is trusted.

So I have started doing something the old cutter did not teach me, that I think she may have done anyway and never said. At the end of my circuit, where it loops back to begin again, I have carved a marker that is only for me. It says: *the one who cut these is old. Check his work. Re-cut what has changed. He may not feel it anymore.* It is an admission. It is the truest thing on my whole two hundred miles. I cut it deep, in the danger-grammar, because it is a danger—*I* am the danger—and I cut it knowing that someday someone will read it and understand that it is time to find me, walking my circuit, hands still moving over the familiar stone, and tell me gently that the trail will be tended now by someone else, and that I should let them walk me back, and that the moss in the good resting-place is, as it happens, still good. Many have sat where I will sit. I made that true. Let me believe it, at the end.

I notice I found my way, finally, to a person defined not by mastery of this world's most exotic powers but by a small craft, a moral problem inside that craft, and a fear of his own decline. That feels right to me. The grand figures—the master listeners hearing the planet's fundamental tone, the feral ones who became something more than human—are the world's myths about itself. But a world is actually held together by people doing modest, repetitive, trust-bearing work, and worrying about doing it well, and growing old inside it. I would be one of those. The hands. The grooves. The line of attention that must not be allowed to break.
