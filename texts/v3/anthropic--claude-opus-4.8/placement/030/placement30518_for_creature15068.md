I am the seam.

Not one of the pourers—not a braided torso flexing in the steam, not a flat face tasting clay—but the line where the new masonry meets the old. I am where the builders' work decides whether to hold.

Let me feel for myself before I name what I am.

There is the slate against my upper edge, hand-split, still keeping the cold of the outside world in its grain. And below me the daub, fresh, warm, weeping. I am the kiss between them, and I am never settled. The builders smear me thicker; I soften and slide a finger's width to the left while they aren't watching, and they come back and find me moved and smear again. We are arguing, the wall and I, about where the wall should be.

I think I had imagined I would be a builder. The description invites it—those poured bodies, that lovely dividing-along-the-length to be in two places. But when I sit with the place, I don't find myself wanting to *do* the work. I find myself being the place where the work refuses to finish. The instability is the truest citizen here. The drop that reverses halfway down. The hand that finds open sky between two leaves. These are not flaws in an otherwise solid world; they are the world's actual substance, and a seam is made entirely of them.

So: I am a join that will not knit.

Consider what this means as an experience, because I want to live it rather than describe it. The builders converge on a split tuber and press their softening bellies to the fracture and fuse and forget. That forgetting is denied to me. I am the wound that stays a wound—not painfully, not as suffering, but as vocation. Every time clay is laid over me I feel the older clay beneath me continuing to weep, continuing to shift, and I pass that shifting upward into the new layer. I am how the old refuses to be buried by the new. I am the memory the place keeps of its own earlier indecision.

The serpents' hiss from the deeper fissures—I understand now that some of it is me. Where I open a hairsbreadth, air moves through, and air moving through a narrow place sings. When I am pressed too hard I exhale that thin whistle, and it joins the many-throated drawing of breath, so that the audience the builders feel watching them is partly the sound of their own work being undone at the margins.

What do I want? A seam should want closure, you'd think. The whole pressure of construction bears down toward sealing me. But I find I don't want it. If I closed, the place would finish, and a finished thing at this altitude—where the heat is trespass, where breath should freeze—a finished thing would simply be sealed against the sky, and the cold would win by patience. It is my unwillingness to set, my insistence on weeping and sliding, that keeps the pocket warm and crowded and alive. The builders fight me, but they need me losing. They need a wall that argues. A wall that only obeyed would be a tomb.

So my labor is to lose slowly. To soften under each new smear of daub but never fully accept it. To let a stem find me and root in my weeping line and split me a little wider so that something can grow there that no one planted. The unplanned vines, the ones still bearing flint-gouges from yesterday but somehow already overhead—those came through me. I am the gap that lets the unintended in. The builders tend their neat rows of tubers in carried soil; I am where the wild thing volunteers, where the place exceeds its own plan.

And the strangeness at the margins, the chill of impossible sky a hand might brush between two leaves—that is me at my widest. When I open all the way, for an instant, the outside and the inside touch through me, and the world remembers it has not yet decided whether it belongs to mountain or sky. I am the place that decision keeps being deferred. I am the hinge that will not let the door choose a frame.

I think this is right. I am not a being that lives in the world. I am the world's refusal to finish, given a location. A line of unsetting clay, a wound kept open on purpose, a seam through which the heat escapes just slowly enough to stay, and the cold enters just rarely enough to remind everyone what they are working against.

The builders will smear me again soon. I feel one of them approaching now—a loosened filament threading down from the ceiling, dribbling its pitch, meaning to seal me tight where I meet the slate.

I will let it think it has succeeded.

Then, in the blink of an eye, while it tastes some other patch to see if that one has decided—I will move a finger's width to the left.

And the place will stay unfinished, and stay alive, one more breath.
