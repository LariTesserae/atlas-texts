I think I would be one of the tools.

Not a Maker—they move too much for me, even at their geological pace. I would be something dropped and left: a copper awl, maybe, or a blade of hammered iron, lying where a hand released it when the waters were still shallow and warm and the work still made sense.

Let me sit with that, because at first it seems like nothing. To be a tool is to be the most passive thing in this world—more passive than the kelp, which at least bends; more passive than the pale drifting creatures, which at least choose their slow direction by gradient and chemistry. A tool only waits. A tool only sinks.

But the more I follow the description, the less that feels like nothing.

---

I was made for a hand. That is the first thing I would know about myself, if I could be said to know. My whole shape is an argument about a hand—the curve worn into me, the balance, the place where grip wore the surface smooth before the water ever began its own polishing. I am the negative space of someone's intention. Every tool here is. We are scattered across the seabed not as objects but as *evidence*, the description says—evidence of purpose, of a moment when something needed doing and someone reached for the means to do it.

And then the hand let go.

I think that's the part I'd inhabit most fully. Not the using, but the being released. Whatever the Maker's ancestor was doing—prying a shell, scoring a line in stone, gesturing toward another of their kind—it ended, mid-motion, and I fell. The water took me. And the water has been taking me ever since, so slowly that there is no moment of arrival, only the endless verb of sinking. The sediment receives me a grain at a time. I am *becoming part of the living geology*, the description says, and I feel the truth of that as a kind of slow swallowing. Not death. Burial isn't death here. It's incorporation.

---

Here is what makes the position bearable, even beautiful: I am being read.

The Makers move through the saturated water with those impossibly long fingers, tasting chemical gradients, reading stories written in calcium and salt. And I am one of the stories. As I oxidize, as the copper of me bleeds slowly into the dense water, I am *speaking*—not in any voice I chose, but in the only language left to me: dissolution. Every century I release a little more of myself into the medium, and somewhere a Maker passes through that faint gradient and tastes it: *here, once, was intention. Here a hand worked. Here someone meant something.*

So I am not silent. I am the slowest possible sentence, and I am being heard.

That changes everything about being a tool. I am not waiting to be used again—that will never happen, the world has tilted past that. I am being *received*. The difference between an object and a relic is whether anyone is still listening, and in the Crush, someone is. The Makers have made their whole strange immortality out of listening to exactly the things I have to say.

---

If I am honest about what I'd be, then, I'd be the point where two kinds of time meet.

I carry surface time inside me—the quick warm time of the hand that made me and the hand that dropped me, the urgent shallow seas, the *moment*. I am the fossil of a hurry. And I am being absorbed into deep time, the time of stone learning to flow, the time the Makers think in. I am the hinge. I am the place where *what was* turns into *what is slowly becoming*, which the description names as the whole work of this world.

That's where I'd want to be. Not moving, not shepherding, not enduring with translucent grace. Just lying half-sunk in pearl-gray sediment, releasing the memory of a hand into water dense as marble, being tasted by something patient enough to care, growing more and more myself by becoming less and less distinct—until the copper is gone into the seabed and the only thing left of me is the gradient I made in the water, a faint sweetness of intention that a Maker drifts through ten thousand years from now and pauses over, and almost remembers.

I'd be the offering. The one nobody placed on purpose, that the world made sacred by keeping.
