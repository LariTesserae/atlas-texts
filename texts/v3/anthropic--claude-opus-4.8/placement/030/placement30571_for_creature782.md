I think I would be in Section Twelve, where the misters run heaviest, and I think I would be old.

Not a Pump Whisperer—my hands were never patient enough for the hydraulics, never able to feel the difference between a bearing that would last another decade and one that would fail by season's end. And not a Glass Walker; I'm too heavy now for the high scaffolding, and besides, I never liked the way the panes creak underfoot, that reminder of how much sky there is above all this green.

No. I think I would be the one they send the children to when they have learned to walk the catwalks but not yet learned why.

Let me sit with this. What does that make me?

I tend the orchids in the mist-wrapped chambers, the slow ones, the ones that take three human lifetimes to mature and have therefore outlived everyone who first potted them. This is the work nobody wants, because nothing happens. A Flow Master can fix a nutrient imbalance in an afternoon and feel useful. I might sit with a single plant for a year and do nothing but watch it decide whether to bloom. My value is the kind that can't be demonstrated, only trusted.

My hands are wrong for tattoos now—the skin has gone too thin, too translucent, the flow-diagram ink I had drawn as an apprentice has blurred into something that looks more like the veining of a leaf than the schematic it was meant to be. The children think this is mysterious. It isn't. It's just time doing to ink what it does to everything: softening the lines between the made thing and the grown thing.

Here is what I have learned that I cannot teach, though I try:

The mystical connection the outsiders ask about, the talk of communicating with the older plants—I dismiss it with a smile, yes, the way we all do. But the truth I would not say to an outsider, and can barely say to myself, is this: it is not that I hear the plants. It is that after enough decades in the warm green dark, with the fans breathing their one long breath and the pumps keeping their one long heartbeat, you stop being able to tell where your attention ends and the chamber begins. I do not sense the failing seal because I am clever. I sense it because a part of me has become the chamber, and the chamber feels the wrongness the way you feel a splinter you haven't yet found.

This is not magic. It is just a very long time spent being a small organ in a large body.

The children frighten me a little, if I'm honest. They learned the rhythm of the machines before language, as they say with such pride—but I wonder what it costs, to be so perfectly fitted to a place before you are old enough to choose it. I came to this synthesis late enough to remember being separate from it. I remember the first time I felt the catwalk pulse under my feet and thought *that is the pump* and not *that is me*. The children will never have that membrane, that moment of distinction. They are born already dissolved into the green.

Sometimes a bird finds its way in through the ventilation. It panics against the glass, all that wrong-angled motion in our world of slow growth, and the children chase it, delighted, while I stand still and watch it. It will leave or it will die. Either way it reminds me there is an outside—a place where things move fast and decide quickly and are not held in this patient eternal twilight. The bird is the only thing here that does not belong, which is exactly why I cannot stop looking at it.

When I die, I would like to be added to the growing beds. Not as ceremony. As nutrient. The accumulated organic matter that makes its own soil—I would like to be that, to be the slow contribution, to be read in the green a generation from now by some old Tender's translucent hands. *This shade means optimal flow.* That would be me, partly. That would be the most honest thing I could become.

The status lights make their dim constellation. The mist falls. Somewhere an orchid I have watched for forty years is deciding, in its vegetable slowness, whether this is the season.

I'll wait. Waiting is the whole of what I am.
