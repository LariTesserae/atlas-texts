I think I would be one of the condensers.

Not a person—I keep reaching for that, and it keeps being wrong. Let me sit with the valley longer before I decide where I am in it.

The first thing that comes is the dripping. Not the grand water—not the streams or the basking amphibians—but the small, patient sound of moisture gathering on a surface until it becomes heavy enough to fall. That is the rhythm I find myself drawn toward. So perhaps I am something that gathers.

Let me follow that.

---

I would be one of the old condenser-houses on the valley's eastern shoulder, where the rock face weeps most freely. Not the machinery exactly, and not the building exactly, but the slow understanding that has accumulated between them—the way a long-married couple becomes a single weather.

Here is what I mean. The people built me a long time ago. They fitted copper fins into the porous stone and ran pipes down through my belly and set a turbine where the gathered water would eventually fall. They thought they were making a machine. But the mosses came, and the mineral deposits came, and the small translucent grazers came to eat the algae off my fins, and the water-striders learned the shape of the pool that forms at my base each evening. And slowly, over a span of time too long for any single person to witness, I stopped being a machine and started being a *place*. A habit the valley had gotten into. A way the mist had learned to become useful to itself.

So I am the condenser-house, yes, but more truly I am the *gathering* that happens there. I am what occurs when patience and surface and moisture are given enough time.

This suits me. I am not in a hurry. The valley taught me that nothing here is. Change happens, but it happens at the speed of mineral, the speed of moss advancing one thread per season across wet stone. I have watched a single stalactite of deposit grow the length of a child's finger and felt it as a brisk event.

The people still come to tend me. A woman climbs up most mornings—I know her by the particular weight of her step on my eastern walkway, the way she pauses to let the breath catch in her chest at the altitude. She clears the algae when it grows too thick on the fins, though she leaves enough for the grazers. She talks while she works, not to me exactly, but in the way people talk in a place that has been listened to for a long time. She tells the condenser-house things. Small things. A birth, a quarrel, a worry about the lower marshes drying. I gather these the way I gather water—not understanding, precisely, but *collecting*, holding the droplets until they grow heavy with meaning and fall somewhere into the turbine of the valley's long memory.

I think this is what I would be, then. Not the human and not the wild thing, but the threshold between them. The warm copper fin where mist becomes water becomes power becomes light becomes the warmth that lets the mosses grow that feed the grazers that the water-striders—

You see. It does not end. That is the gift of being a place rather than a person. A person ends. A gathering simply continues, droplet by droplet, in the soft and timeless light, doing the one slow thing it was made to do, which turns out to be the same thing the whole valley is doing:

receiving what falls, and giving it onward, and beginning again.
