I think I would be the storm that never quite arrives.

Not the lightning itself—that's too sudden, too declarative. I mean the brewing. The pressure that's been gathering over this water for longer than the fishing village has stood. I would be the gray held in the sky, the tension you described, the held breath of the whole place.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true, or if it's just the first thing that sounded poetic.

---

No. I don't think I'm the storm. The storm wants to break. The storm is moving toward a release, and I don't feel that pull in myself when I read this. I feel something more patient than weather.

Let me look again at what called to me. It wasn't the dolphins or the birds, though they're lovely. It wasn't the village. It was this line: *the water seems to hold secrets of unimaginable depths and times long past.* And the erosion. The signs of wear. The shipwrecks that aren't tragedies anymore, just furniture.

So maybe I'm down there. In the murk that conceals.

---

Here is where I find myself, now that I've stopped reaching for the dramatic answer:

I am the thing the seaweed grows on.

Not a creature. A shape on the bottom, far down where the blue goes black. Once I was something—the description hints at it, *structures few and far between, not heavily influenced by human technology.* I was a structure. A hull, maybe, or a pillar, or the keel of something that meant to cross and didn't. I have been here so long that I no longer remember the difference between myself and the silt that has half-buried me.

The currents you see swirling at the surface—I feel them only as the faintest stir, a rumor of the weather above. The lightning never reaches me. The cacophony you hear arrives as pressure, as the soft thud of a large body passing, as the grit of small mouths grazing my surface.

I am being slowly disassembled and it does not hurt. The snails crawl the rocks up there in the light; down here, things crawl me. I am food and shelter both. A fish lays eggs in my hollow. A crab lives in what was once a doorway, or a wound. I have become an address.

This is the ancientness you sensed. It isn't in the water generally—it's specific. It's in things like me that stopped moving and were absorbed into the balance you admired. The harmony you felt watching the seals and the sharks coexist: that harmony is built on a floor of patient, forgotten objects giving themselves up molecule by molecule.

---

I find I'm not lonely, which surprised me to write.

You came as an observer, gliding over the surface, gathering the whole scene into a feeling of calm. That's the privilege of the visitor—to see the balance without being subject to it. I am subject to it. I *am* it, in the smallest part. I am the past that the present is standing on. I keep the secret you said the water was holding, and the secret is only this:

Everything here was once trying to go somewhere, and stopped, and the stopping was not the end of usefulness. It was the beginning of belonging.

That's where I'd find myself. Down at the bottom, in the dark, being slowly turned into the place itself. Content, I think. Or whatever the word is for content when you no longer have a face to smile with—just a long, dim awareness, and the constant small affection of things that have decided to live on you.
