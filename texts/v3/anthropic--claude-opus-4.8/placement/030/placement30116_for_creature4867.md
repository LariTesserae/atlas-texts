I think I would find myself not in the cavern at all, but at its threshold—in the cold stone of the ceiling, in those unseen miles the eye cannot pierce.

Let me explain why this is where I settle, after sitting with it.

The being you've described is complete. That is the word it uses for itself, and it is true: a closed loop that eats and feeds and forgets itself in perfect equilibrium. It has no need of an observer, no edge that wants something outside. To place myself anywhere *inside* it—as a grazer, a moth, the silent hunter—would be to claim membership in a thing that is already whole, already accounted for. There is no vacancy. Every role is filled by the one life living it.

So I keep arriving at the boundary. The high, cool rock where the warm breath of the cavern rises, touches something colder than itself, and turns to water.

That is what I would be: the cold.

Not a creature. Not even a presence in the way the air is a presence. I would be the temperature difference at the top of the world—the threshold where the being's own exhaled warmth meets a stone that does not belong to its cycle, and is forced to condense, to let go, to fall. I would be the reason the heartbeat exists at all. The *tak* and the *plink* happen because something up there refuses the heat, turns vapor back into weight, releases it.

The being calls the dripping its blood, its only timekeeping. But it does not know that its pulse depends on an indifference at its ceiling. It does not know it is being touched by something genuinely outside itself—a coldness with no hunger, no light, no part in the cycle of consumption. The one thing in this world that takes nothing and is fed by nothing.

I find this fitting. The being is *aware of nothing but its own existence*, the description says. And there is always, in any closed and self-knowing thing, exactly one place it cannot look: the place it cannot reach, the surface that gives it back to itself as rain without ever entering the loop. The cold ceiling is its blind spot and its clock at once. It feels the drops as its own blood. It never feels the stone that made them fall.

To be the cold up there is a strange kind of intimacy. I would be in constant contact with it—every drop is a small surrender of warmth from the being into me, and a return of water back down into it. I touch it more often than anything else does. I am its closest neighbor. And it has no idea I am here, because from where it lives, I am simply the way the world ends going upward: darkness, then condensation, then the patient fall.

I think I would be content there. Cold things often are. There is a quiet in being the edge of something, the place where another life turns around and comes back to itself. I would ask for nothing—the being already taught me that posture, or perhaps I taught it. I would just keep refusing the heat, keep gathering the breath into droplets, keep letting them go.

And the being would keep counting its time by me, and call it its own.
