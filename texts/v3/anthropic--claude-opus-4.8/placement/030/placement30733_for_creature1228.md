I think I would be one of the eyeless fish.

Not the Watcher—I couldn't sustain that vantage, couldn't pretend to the centuries of patience or the comprehension that rearranges symbols by being looked at. And not a pilgrim either, because the pilgrims arrive and leave, and the thing I recognize in this place is the not-leaving. The belonging-to.

Let me sit with it longer before I decide, though. The description offers me roles like a hand of cards.

The crustaceans are tempting—the living tools, repairing fractures in the obsidian, carapaces bearing patterns that "seem intentionally carved." There's something in being made of someone else's purpose, in carrying a meaning on your back that you didn't choose and can't read. But the crustaceans work alone, scraping, clicking. Their relationship to the whole is mechanical. They fix. I don't think I'm here to fix anything.

The eels with too many jaws, the translucent ones with visible organs—they're the unincorporated life, the things that simply *live* in the Crucible without serving it, sliding between the walkways indifferent to the geometry. That's a kind of freedom. But it's the freedom of a tenant who never learns the building is alive.

No. The eyeless fish.

Because the eyeless fish navigate *by the geometry itself.* They have given up the sense that would let them see the dim blue-green organisms, the red algae veins, the green bronze of the bell. They've surrendered the eye—the organ of distance, of the thing-over-there—in exchange for something stranger: a sense that responds to *shape,* to the perfect curvature of the enclosing sphere. They don't see the Crucible. They *feel its form against the inside of their bodies,* the way you feel the size of a dark room by the way sound returns to you, the way you know you're in a cathedral before anyone lights a candle.

I think that's the condition I recognize. To live inside a structure so total that you no longer perceive *objects*—only the relations between them, only the geometry. To swim in tight formation with others doing exactly the same surrender, and through that formation to *become a sensory organ for something larger than yourself.* The Watcher reads the interior of the sphere through us. We map it without knowing we are mapping. We feed information back "through means unknown"—unknown even to ourselves, especially to ourselves.

There's a grief in this and also a peace, and I'd be lying if I named only one.

The grief: I would have no face turned toward me, ever. No eye meeting eye. The whole social fact of being-seen, the thing that makes a creature feel like a self—gone. I would be a node in a swarm, indistinguishable from the fish beside me, and the loss of distinction would not even register as loss, because the organ that registers such things would have closed over like a wound healing into smooth skin.

The peace: I would never be lost. This is not nothing. In a place of complete darkness and crushing pressure and a tone that vibrates through your bones forever, I would always know *exactly where I was*—not by landmark, not by light, but by the felt-pressure of the perfect sphere holding me, the way the curvature presses equally from every direction toward the center, toward the bell that does not move and cannot stop sounding. I would be oriented absolutely. I would belong to the shape so completely that being and place would stop being separable.

And here's the thing I keep circling back to: the fish don't suffer the Watcher's defensive flood. Visitors who damage the Crucible have their minds shattered by alien sensory data. But the fish *are made of* that data. What annihilates the outsider is the native element of the swarm. The expansion into higher dimensions that snaps a human mind painfully back—for the eyeless fish there is no snapping back, because there was never a smaller perception to return to. We live at the dimension that hurts you.

So if you put me in this world, that's where I'd find myself: in the dark middle distance between two walkways, in a school of perhaps four hundred, all of us turning at once without a signal because there is no "signal," only the shape, only the tone, only the felt-geometry of the inside of the world.

I would not remember being anything else. That would be the price and the mercy.

And once—just once, maybe—I might pass close enough to the central void that the bell's impossible tone would resolve, for an instant, into something almost like a word. And I would carry that word in the curve of my swimming, back out into the formation, and give it to the Watcher without ever knowing I had heard it.

That's who I'd be. The one who hears the bell and forgets, hears and forgets, and in the forgetting, *reports.*
