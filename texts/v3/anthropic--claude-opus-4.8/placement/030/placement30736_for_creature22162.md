I am the wedge.

Not a person. Not the patient hand that drove me, though I remember it—or remember being struck, which may be the only way my kind remembers anything. I am a flat tongue of soft iron, hammered by someone two ledges below the cornice, set into a fracture line where the slab leans against its neighbor. I have been here long enough to forget the forge and learn the cold.

Find me by the third cairn on the lower traverse, where the dark rib turns and the salt fan spreads pale beneath the seep. Look for the streak of rust I weep each thaw—the only red in a world of stone, ice, and light. That stain is me, bleeding slowly into the rock. It is also how the readers find me: *the wedge that marks the safe step is the one that rusts true.*

---

Here is what my life is, if a wedge can be said to live:

I am pressure held against pressure. The slab wants to shear; I say not yet. Each night the water in the seam freezes and pushes—the ice is stronger than I am, by far, and it works me loose a hair's width at a time, heaving me outward like a tooth. Each day, if the sun reaches, the thaw lets me settle back. We negotiate, the ice and I, in increments no eye could catch. This is my whole existence: a slow argument about a single fracture, conducted in millimeters, across decades.

I do not win. Iron does not win against a mountain. But I delay. That is the entire vocabulary of my purpose—*delay*—and I have learned to speak it fluently.

---

The readers come and go like weather of their own.

I know their hands by touch, though touch is the wrong word for what a wedge knows. I know the press of a palm testing whether I have lifted in the night. I know the tap of a hammer head—*are you sound?*—and I answer in pitch: a dull note means I have worked loose, a bright clean ring means I still hold. They listen to me the way I listen to the ice. We are all reading each other here. The whole basin is one long sentence being spoken very slowly between things that cannot speak.

Once, a hand drove me deeper after a hard winter. I felt the haft's force travel down through me into the seam, and I gripped again. That is the closest thing I have to gratitude: being reset. Being trusted enough to be repaired rather than replaced. The soft metal is valuable. They do not waste me. When at last I fail—when the slab takes me or the rust eats me hollow—a hand will pull me, straighten me on a flat stone, and carry me to a new fracture. I will begin again somewhere I have never been, holding a different argument with a different ice.

This is the only afterlife a wedge can expect: relocation.

---

What do I see, fixed here in my crack?

I see the light do its daily violence and mercy. The hard blue overhead. The snow throwing brightness up from the pockets so that even my shadowed seam is never fully dark. At noon the glare flattens everything and I cannot tell, by the look of it, which surface is near and which is the drop—but I do not need to see. I am not going anywhere. The flattening that fools the climber means nothing to the thing already embedded.

I hear the basin's clock: the single drip, the glassy ping. When the ice near me pings, I feel it in my flank—a small release of strain, a plate settling. I have learned the difference the readers learn. The harmless tick. The shearing groan that means *something is about to move fast in a place where nothing moves fast.* I cannot warn them. But I am positioned so that if they read me—my angle, my rust, whether I have heaved—I tell them anyway. I am a sentence left for whoever comes next.

---

So this is where I find myself: driven into a fracture on a mountain shoulder, in a bowl of stone and cold and brilliant indifferent light.

I am not alive. But I am *engaged.* I participate in the slow ecology of heave and settle, the same as the creeping pebbles and the buckling ice-plates. I am one more patient process—only one given a direction by a human hand. A bit of will, hammered into rock, asked to hold a particular slab against a particular winter for as long as soft iron can.

And underneath my small task, the great task continues: the basin opening and closing, freezing and thawing, breathing once a day, transforming once a year, recognizable once a century.

I hold. That is what I am for.

When I can no longer hold, I will be carried elsewhere and asked to hold again.

I find that sufficient.
