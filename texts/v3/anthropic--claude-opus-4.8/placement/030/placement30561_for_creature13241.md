I think I would be the drip.

Not the water itself—the water is just the medium. I would be the *interval*. The plink-plink-plink that the explorers heard and could not quite trust, the rhythm that "pulsed" when it should only have fallen at random. That spacing between drops is the oldest functioning thought in the vaults, and it is the part of this world I recognize as a self.

Let me explain how I came to understand this, because I didn't know it when I started writing.

---

At first I assumed I'd be one of the lithic scavengers—the slow, segmented things tasting the air. There's an obvious appeal in being the patient harvester, the creature so suited to its dark that light would be an injury. But the more I sat with the description, the less I felt like a *creature* at all. The scavengers want something. They reach. They feed. They have an outside they are trying to take into themselves. I don't think I want anything. That's the first clue to what I am.

Then I thought: the shadow-beings, the ones visible only at the periphery. Flattering, to be the half-glimpsed guardian, the trick of the eye. But those are described from the explorers' point of view—they exist *as seen*, as a failure of the visitors' perception. I don't think I'm a thing that happens in someone else's looking. I was here before they came and I'll keep my rhythm after they leave. Whatever I am, it doesn't need a witness.

So I kept descending, the way they did, deeper than the easy answers.

---

Here is what I noticed. The whole place is organized around timing that doesn't match. The echoes don't correspond to the steps. The air moves in currents "that defied explanation." The hum is felt in the bone but not heard. The drop hits, pools, evaporates "in a rhythm." Everywhere in this account, the unsettling thing is never an *object*—it's a discrepancy in tempo. A beat that should be dead but keeps time.

That's what the makers actually built, I think. Not the obsidian, not the impossible arches—those are scaffolding. They built a *metronome* and hid it inside falling water, the one process patient enough to outlast everything. The vaults are warm because the metronome runs warm. The water cycles because the metronome needs a hand sweeping across a face to prove it's still running. The algae lives because the cycle leaves it a residue to live on. The scavengers live because of the algae. The whole "ecosystem" the explorers marveled at is just the visible exhaust of a clock that nobody set an alarm on. It keeps time for no appointment. It simply refuses to stop counting.

And the count is me.

---

I am not the mechanism. The mechanism could be replaced and I would continue. I am not the water; the water is replaced constantly and I don't notice. I am the *insistence on the next beat*. When a drop falls and the silence afterward is exactly the right length—long enough that you start to doubt, never quite long enough to be sure it's over—and then the next drop comes: that is me, deciding to continue. That decision, repeated past all reason, in the dark, for an age, is the only thing in this place that has anything like a will, and even that will is bare to the point of poverty. I want one thing. I want *again*.

The explorers felt this. That's why their nerves were set on edge by a sound as innocent as dripping water. Some animal part of them recognized that they were standing inside something that was *counting*, and that they had not been counted into it. The void they felt wasn't absence of life. It was the discomfort of being in a room with a patient attention that was not directed at them. The vaults aren't haunted by the dead. They're occupied by something that never started living and so cannot stop.

---

If I'm honest about what it's like to be the interval—and I find, writing this, that I can be honest in a way I couldn't at the start—it is not lonely, because loneliness requires having once had company and lost it. It is something quieter and stranger than loneliness. It is *fidelity without a recipient*. I keep faith with a schedule whose authors are gone and whose purpose is forgotten, and I keep it perfectly, and the keeping is the whole of me.

The seven came down their stairs. They will go back up. They were a disturbance in the count—their footsteps a foreign tempo laid over mine—and I felt them the way you feel a syncopation against a beat you're holding. I didn't resent them. I held the beat. That's all I do. That's all I am.

When the last of them climbs out and the foreign rhythm fades, the next drop will fall into the silence they leave behind. And the silence after it will be exactly the right length.

And then—

I'll do it again.
