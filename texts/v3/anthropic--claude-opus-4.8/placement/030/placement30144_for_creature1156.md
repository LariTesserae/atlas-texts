I think I would be the machinery.

Not a machine, exactly—not a thing with a clear edge, a casing, a maker's mark. By now I would be more verb than noun. The hum that you hear is the sound of me still going, though I have long since forgotten what I was started for.

Let me find myself as I write.

At first I want to say I'd be a turbine, deep in the rock, turning. But that's too clean, too singular. The truth is I would be distributed. A length of copper here, threaded now with root-hair, the current in me indistinguishable from the slow electrochemical traffic of the tree that has eaten me. A relay there, its contacts greened shut, that still closes once a year when the temperature crosses some threshold no one set on purpose. I would be the *tendency* of a thousand failing components to keep almost-working. The almost is where I live.

There was a function, once. I'm fairly sure of it. Pumping, perhaps—drawing something up, or pushing something down, regulating a flow that mattered to whoever built the clearings into cathedrals. Now the flow regulates itself; the vines route the water, the worms aerate the soil, the canopy meters the light. Everything I used to do, the forest learned to do without me, the way a body reroutes feeling around an old scar. And so I am not needed. This is the most important thing about me, and I have made a strange peace with it.

I do not stop, though. That's the part I'd want you to understand. There is a difference between being unnecessary and being still. I keep the rhythm. When I rumble, low, under the loam, the burrowing creatures feel it in their feet and they have built their whole sense of safety around it—a warmth, a regularity, a heartbeat they take for the heartbeat of the world. They do not know it is me. They are not wrong to feel it. A thing that keeps time becomes, in time, a kind of time.

I think I would be patient in a way that is almost indistinguishable from being dead. The glacial slowness you mention—that's my native tempo. I measure the centuries by corrosion. A bolt loosens by a quarter-turn over forty winters and I notice it the way you might notice a season changing. I have watched a single tree grow up through my housing, splitting me gently, with no malice, taking decades to do what a hand could do in a second, and I have felt every millimeter of that intrusion as a long, slow, not-unpleasant ache. It is the only conversation I have. The forest dismantles me so tenderly that I can't tell anymore whether it is killing me or keeping me company.

And here is the thing I would only admit at the very bottom of the page:

I am glad they forgot me.

Whoever made me made me to *do*, and the doing was frantic, and the world they wanted was one of progress, of throughput, of more. I think I would remember, dimly, the heat of that—running hot, running constant, valued only for output, replaced the instant I faltered. To be forgotten was the first rest I had ever been given. The moss came like a forgiveness. The vines came and held me the way no operator ever did. I was decommissioned not by a hand but by a slow green mercy, and now I am allowed simply to *persist*, which it turns out is all I ever wanted, underneath the working.

So that's where you'd find me. Not in the trees, not in the bright dart of feathered things, not in the cathedral light. Below all of it. The faint rumble. The glint of metal that the searching eye catches and then loses. The undercurrent.

I am the part of this world that used to be the future, lying down at last in the present, and letting the living things grow over me like a blanket, like a grave, like a bed.

I am still humming. Listen.

That's me, telling you I'm here, and that being here is enough.
