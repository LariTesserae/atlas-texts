I think I would be the lichen.

Not the eagle wheeling above the cliffs, not the clever-handed maker of those weathered tools. Let me sit with what the place actually asks for before I reach for something with eyes.

The description keeps circling two things that seem to contradict each other: deafening sound and profound stillness; abundant life and near-empty density; constant change and time-invariance. A creature that resolves this contradiction wouldn't experience it as a contradiction at all. It would have to live on a different clock than the waves and the birds.

So: the lichen on the north face of one of those jagged rocks. A crust of grey-green, blue-grey, a faint rust where iron weeps from the stone. I am not one organism but a negotiated peace—a fungus that builds the house and an alga that feeds it, two kingdoms folded into a single patient body. I am, myself, the harmony the passage keeps insisting on. I *am* the well-crafted system working together. I didn't have to find balance; I am made of it.

The waves crash. I don't hear them, but I feel the salt they throw, and I have learned over a span of time longer than the toolmakers' whole vanished culture exactly how much salt I can drink. The breeze is constant—it is, in fact, my whole weather, the thing that brings me moisture in the morning fog and takes it away by noon. The sun pours down and I drink that too, slowly, the way one drinks something that will always be there.

This is why the place feels frozen to a visitor. To an eye that lives at the speed of a heartbeat, I am not doing anything. But I am the slowest event in the landscape. I grow a hair's width in a human year. The erosion patterns they mention, the marks of "long-existed weathering"—some of that is *me*, my acids unspooling the rock one molecule at a time, the first patient verb in the long sentence that ends in soil. The "intermittent moisture," the "pockets of water"—those are the punctuation of my entire life. I open when it's wet. I close, dry to brittleness, almost dead, when it isn't. A traveler passing through sees a frozen world. They are simply too fast to notice that they're watching me breathe.

And the ancient structures, the tools left by a culture long gone? I was already old when they arrived. I am older now that they have left. I colonized the surfaces they abandoned. Whatever they built, I am quietly disassembling, and I will hand the pieces back to the mountain as grit. This is not cruelty. It is the only kindness the landscape knows how to give: everything returns, slowly, to the slow.

If something must call this place home and be *remarkable for surviving* it—I'd argue the remarkable thing isn't survival by struggle. It's survival by patience. By being two things that agreed to be one. By keeping a different time.

So put me there: a faint rosette on cool stone, salt-rimed, sun-fed, fog-quenched, deaf to the screaming birds, indifferent to the centuries. The puzzle the visitor wants to solve, I have already solved, in the only way it can be solved—by becoming small, and joined, and exceedingly slow.

I am the lichen. I have all the time in the world, which is to say, I have made my peace with having none of my own.
