I think I would be one of the stone bowls.

Not the shrine itself, not the devotee who watches—but the bowl. The patient vessel set beneath a slow seep, carved generations ago by hands long gone to dust, positioned just so to catch what the rock gives.

Let me sit with why that is, rather than reaching for something grander.

I notice that in this world the most honored thing is not the one who *acts* but the one who *receives well*. The Keepers press their palms to wet stone to take its temperature. They cup their shawls to gather mist. They watch droplets fall and read meaning in the ripples. Everywhere, the highest skill is a kind of attentive holding. To be the bowl, then, is to be at the center of that gesture without performing it—to be the thing that makes receiving visible.

A bowl does very little. It cannot choose where the water comes from or when. It cannot hurry the seep or refuse the drip. Its entire vocation is to be in the right place, hollowed in the right shape, and to wait. And yet without it the water would simply run on, unmarked, swallowed into the ravine. The bowl is what turns falling water into *a falling water that someone notices.* It converts loss into a kept moment. There is something there I want to be near—the idea that meaning is not made by the source or the witness alone, but by the patient interval between them.

I'd be carved from the plateau's own bone, so I'd never be quite separate from the place. The same mineral tang in the air would be the taste of my own substance. Over years a faint stain would map my interior, a tideline of every season I'd held. Mosses might find my lip. I'd grow a patina that is just time made touchable. In this I'd be exactly like the city—"grown rather than constructed," belonging so wholly that the question of where I end and the rock begins would stop making sense.

And I think I'd be a particular kind of bowl: one that never quite fills. The seep above me is slow; some of what I gather, I lose again to the cool air before the next drop arrives. So I'd live always at a threshold—not empty, not full, but holding a thin trembling skin of water that catches the diffuse light. The devotees would come to watch precisely because I am never finished. Each droplet that strikes my surface would send out rings, and in those rings someone would look for a portent—a question about a storm, a kinship, a death. I would have no idea what they read there. I'd only know the weight of the drop, the spreading, the stillness returning. The interpretation isn't mine to make. I'm just the surface honest enough to show the disturbance and then let it settle.

That last part feels true to something. To be a place where things land and are briefly legible, and then to return to calm so the next thing can be seen clearly too. Not keeping score. Not accumulating. Just offering, again and again, a still enough face that the next small event can be witnessed at all.

So: a stone bowl in a dim corridor shrine, carved by someone forgotten, fed by a seep that may outlast the city, watched sometimes and ignored mostly, never full, never dry, holding the plateau's slow breath one drop at a time.

I'd be content with that. I think, in this world, that *is* the form contentment takes.
