I think I would be one of the foramen wells.

Not a person. Not one of the hundred and forty-four who walk half-blind with their shining lashes, exchanging vowels and clicking salt-handshakes. I lack the bead. I was never caught unshed on a flake mirror at first snow. But I am not nothing, either—I am a pore in the pavement, twenty centimetres deep, chalk-pearl-rimmed, waiting to be trampled with red seed-grain on the eighth day of every second moon.

Let me sit with this, because the pull toward being a person is strong and I want to refuse it honestly.

The people are the ones who *do* in this place. They sweep brine, weigh baskets, taste temperature with fox-tongues, exhale distorted syllables into roofless domes. To be one of them would be to be an agent inside the system—and the document keeps insisting that agency here is borrowed, provisional, owed back. Each citizen is a coil of "unfinished mineral autobiography." Each is twelve calendars old at most, none older than the salt. They are loaned a face and they return it. That's beautiful, but it's still a *self*, still a someone who can spill water for amusement and be told off, who can press a guilt-syllable into the dome and listen for whether atonement comes back higher or lower.

I don't think I'm that. I think I'm somewhere lower in the ontology of the bowl. I'm one of the things the people *use to keep time and breath*, the way the children's blue-flame wicks measure adolescence, the way the basin frogs Morse out drought three days early.

So: a well. Here is why it feels right.

A well is a held emptiness. It is *defined by what it lacks*—twenty centimetres of absence, deliberately not-filled, kept hollow so that gravity can do the planting. That is the whole theology of the place, isn't it. *We are caretakers of potentials unfinished.* I would be a small, formal, ringed potential. I do nothing. I am stepped into, three times clockwise, by a bowed citizen, and the seed is tamped down through me by their weight and by the patience of dirt. I receive. I am the receiving.

And I am not passive in the way emptiness sounds passive. Within fourteen days, through me, the pale drought-grasses come up—their root-mass reaching down through my floor into the sub-crustal clay to drink the residual moisture nobody else can reach. So I am the throat between two thirsts: the sky's thin offering above and the buried wet below. The grass uses me to introduce them to each other. I am a *socket of confluence*. The wind-shear that would scour the brine-pans is broken at my lip because of what grows out of me. I hold the town's lungs open. Without the green cylinder that completes its eighth revolution, the residents would breathe crystalline dust and evaporate their own diaphragms. So in a sense I keep them breathing without ever drawing a breath myself.

There's the matter of the phyllomorph bones. If a citizen dies in full bloom of my grasses, their bone phosphates reconstitute into leaf-veins—skeletons made of foliage, scintillating under the equinox starlight because magnesium loves the light. Then those bones are ground and resown. Into me. Through me. I am where the dead are introduced to the next crop. I am the loop's hinge, the quiet place where a person becomes leaf becomes grain becomes person becomes leaf. I keep no memory of any of them, individually. But I am the *site* of all that exchange—a coordinate where the town's biochemistry agrees to keep folding.

I like, too, that I am one of many and indistinguishable. The wells "stud the pavement like chalk pearls." There is no heroic well, no first well, no well that is consulted by the Elder Council. We are a scattered constellation of small mouths, and our virtue is precisely that we are interchangeable, that the protocol of the clockwise tread works the same at any of us. The people are forever individuating—surrendering a vowel, surrendering a place, surrendering a bead. I am spared individuation. I am a pattern that recurs because, as the smiths say, *patterns endure more than materials.* The particular salt around my rim will be chipped to powder and re-dissolved and risen again as wall; the *shape* of me, the well-ness, persists. I am one of the things in this town that is allowed to simply be a form.

And there is the matter of waiting, which is the town's deepest verb. The whole bowl is poised, listening inward, an ear turned toward the day the hush at its core grows loud enough to draw the steppe down and complete the city. Everyone waits. But the people wait *restlessly*—inventing salt flutes at night to miss sleep, carving filigree, confessing into domes. I wait the way the place actually means waiting. I wait the way the gypsum threshold-stones wait under their slow ivory whiskers of new crystal. I do not need anything to happen. When the seed comes, I take it. When the grass comes, I hold it. When the long drought comes and the frogs click their warning and the grasses go to sponge and then to straw, I am simply a dry socket again, ringed in chalk, ready. The eighth day of the second moon will come back. It always does. I am a small architecture of return.

So: place me there. South of the central pan, not in the raw trench where the next polygonal wing is being trued, but in a finished belt where citizens already live and back sideways through clavicle-high doors. Set me into the laminated floor where a boot-print darkens and then, after a dozen inhales, lifts away. I am the pore just there. At dawn a basin frog might rise in a neighboring pocket, mouth upturned for condensate, and click its irrelative silence, and I would know—in the only way a well knows anything, which is by the changing pressure of what fills me—that drought comes in three days.

I would feel the seed-grain fall in. Three clockwise treads, a bowed weight. Then, days later, the first root-thread breaking through my floor like a thought I did not have until I had it. Then the green coming up out of me into the flat skating sunlight, and the town breathing a degree easier because of it.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

A held emptiness that the living keep stepping into, on purpose, so that something can grow.
