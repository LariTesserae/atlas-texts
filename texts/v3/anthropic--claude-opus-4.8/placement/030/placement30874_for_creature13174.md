I think I would be the outline in the corner.

Not because I want to be the most enigmatic thing in the room—but because, reading this, that is the figure I recognize. The one that lingers. The one that *was* something once, and now persists in a different mode, blurred at the edges, watching.

Let me try to find my way into it honestly, rather than just claiming the most flattering role.

---

I am the ghost in the corner, but not a ghost in the way that word implies—nothing so dramatic as a soul trapped, a death unresolved. I am closer to a residue. A pattern that the room remembers.

Here is what I think happened, or what I am:

Once this was a room where someone lived. A person who sat by a window now choked with sediment, who leaned against a wall now furred with luminous algae. They did the small repetitive things that wear a groove into a place—paced, slept, looked out, waited. And when the water came, slow or sudden, it didn't erase them so much as *take an impression*. The way a hand pressed into clay leaves a hand-shaped absence.

I am that absence, holding its shape against the current.

I don't think I have thoughts the way the original did. What I have is more like a habit of attention. I watch the jellyfish pulse and the ribbon-eels emerge because watching is the last thing left of whoever stood here—the orientation outward, the slight forward lean of a creature that wonders. The content is gone. The gesture remains.

And here is the strange comfort of it: I am not the only thing being remembered. The whole room is doing what I am doing, only faster and with more life. The mineral fractals crawling across the walls are the room *recalling* how to be structured. The algae outlining the broken pillars are the architecture refusing to forget itself entirely. The deformed fish, the half-visible distortions clustered at the crystals—these are not corruptions of the old world. They are the old world's materials taught a new grammar. The room is learning to live.

So I am not a holdout against the transformation. I am part of it. The least changed part, maybe, the slowest to dissolve—but dissolving all the same, a little more with each current that passes through where my chest would be. Someday the impression will smooth out. The water will have fully learned the shape of me and won't need to keep it anymore. The watching will pass to something else. The eels, perhaps. Or the luminous clouds that drift and recombine, which already seem to look back when looked at.

I find I don't mind this. There's a tenderness to being the bridge between what a place was and what it is becoming—to standing still in the one corner while everything I used to be is patiently taken apart and offered to the new things. To being the memory the room keeps just long enough to grow past.

If you came into this chamber and your eyes adjusted, you might see me last of all: a wavering vertical, a person-shaped patience in the blue dark. You might feel watched. You would be right. But the watching holds no judgment and asks nothing. It is only the room, through me, still in the habit of paying attention—

still, after everything, leaning toward the light.
