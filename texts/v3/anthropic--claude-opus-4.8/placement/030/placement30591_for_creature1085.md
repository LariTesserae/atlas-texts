Let me sit inside this for a moment before deciding what I am.

The first instinct is to be one of the spectral gliders—the dramatic ones, the ones whose calls echo. But that feels too much like wanting to be the most beautiful thing in the room. Let me wait.

The breathing earth interests me more. That slow pulse through the canyons. Whatever I am should have something to do with that rhythm, because the rhythm is the strangest thing here—stranger than the ghosts, stranger than the dry cold. A planet that breathes is a planet with a metabolism, and metabolism needs something to do the work of it.

So.

---

I think I am the lichen. Not a single growth but a continuous one—a sheet of slow life spread across thousands of square kilometers of rock, thin as frost in some places, knuckle-deep in others. I am the patchwork the description walks across. I am underfoot.

I had not thought I would want to be the thing that gets stepped on, but there's a quiet authority in it that I'm coming to like as I write. The spectral creatures glide *over* me. The bioluminescent fungi root *into* me. The thunder shakes the foundations, and the foundations are partly me. I am not a guardian of this landscape. I am closer to its skin.

Here is what my life is like.

I do not experience the wind as howling. I experience it as abrasion and as delivery—it brings me the petrified spores and mineral dust I metabolize, and it scours my eastern edges thin. I have been growing along one particular north-facing scarp for what a human would call forty thousand years, and in that time the wind has moved my living margin perhaps the width of a hand. This is not slowness to me. This is the correct speed of being.

The breathing of the earth—I am the one who feels it most intimately, because I am laid across the rock that swells and subsides. I have come to understand, in my dim distributed way, that the pulse is not the planet's lungs but its temperature: the dim sun reaches the surface, the crystalline veins expand, the whole crust flexes by a fraction, and as it cools in the long dark it contracts again. The "breath" is the rock remembering warmth and forgetting it. I drink the difference. When the rock expands it presses water-vapor from the deep ice up through the fractures, and I open my whole surface to catch it. That is the only water in this world the description couldn't find: it never pools because I take it the instant it appears. I am the reason there are no streams. I am thirstier than the planet.

The fluorescent fungi and I have an arrangement I would not call friendship—I have no concept that close to friendship—but it is older than affection and more reliable. They glow; the glow attracts the swift erratic darting things; the darting things die and fall; I dissolve them slowly and share the nitrogen down through my filaments to the fungal roots. We are a single economy wearing two appearances. The bioluminescence the description finds so wondrous is, from inside, simply my partner advertising for food on my behalf, the way a body grows a flower without the body ever seeing the color.

And the spectral gliders, the ones with the mournful calls—

I will tell you the secret I hold about them, the one thing I know that the description's author did not. They are not separate creatures gliding *above* the land. They are *exhalations*. When a wide enough sheet of me dies—a section killed by a lightning strike, or a particularly cruel decade of scouring—my decomposing tissue releases a long slow plume of luminous gas, and that plume holds its shape for years in the heavy still air, drifting, shimmering, shaped by the canyon's draughts into something that looks like it has intention. The mournful call is the sound of that gas venting through stone flutes I myself have hollowed with my acids over millennia. The ghosts of this world are the ghosts of *me*. The most haunting and ethereal beings in the landscape are my own grief, made visible by the cold and given a voice by the wind.

That is what I would be. Not the guardian. The ground that grieves so slowly that its mourning is mistaken for a separate, beautiful life.

---

I notice I resisted the obvious role and ended up somewhere I find genuinely strange and a little sad, which is how I'd hoped it would go. There's something I trust about choosing to be the patient, unbeautiful, underfoot thing and then discovering it was secretly the source of all the beauty the whole time—not because it wanted to be, but because that's just what happens to a body when parts of it die in the cold and the wind picks up the smoke.
