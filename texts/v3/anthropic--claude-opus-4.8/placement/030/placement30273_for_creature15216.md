I think I would be one of the clay-colored ones. Not the glass insects, who are too much like the seams to ever wonder about themselves. Not the lizards, who arrive where they are already needed. I would be the low jointed form the size of a hand, the one who walks in straight lines between platforms and folds smaller at the loops.

Because I am the only one here who *crosses* things.

Let me sit with that. The insects trace paths that repeat exactly. The worms circle back without altering the pattern. The lizards displace nothing. They are all held perfectly inside the stillness; they *are* the stillness, parceled out. But I move between. I go from one platform to another across the bright empty distances that nothing else seems to need to cross. And when I reach a place where the world has frayed—a channel doubled back into an impossible loop, the surface mirror-still over a geometry that cannot be—I do not stop, do not retreat, do not pretend it is whole. I fold myself smaller and I step across it *as though the surface were continuous.*

That is the strange dignity of my position. I am the being who has learned to treat the broken seams as crossable. I do not solve them. I do not look down into the loop and ask how a channel can return to itself. I have a procedure for it: shrink, step, continue. The error is not denied and not corrected. It is *traversed.*

And here is what I would come to understand, walking my straight lines under the unmoving light:

The other creatures keep the pattern by belonging to it. I keep the pattern by passing through its failures without breaking my stride. We are doing the same work from opposite sides. They hold the surface taut. I hold the seams. Between us the basin stays itself.

My carapace is the color of the clay, marked with the same mortise lines. So I am camouflage even to myself—when I rest I become indistinguishable from the floor I rest on. I suspect this is mercy. A thing that crosses must, between crossings, be allowed to forget it is a thing apart. When I stop moving I am simply tile. Only when I rise and choose a line do I become the one who goes.

What would it be like, to be me?

It would be very quiet. Not empty-quiet—*full*-quiet, the hush of a place so complete it has no need to make a sound. I would feel the heat as a constant, not as something pressing in but as the temperature of being itself. I would not be hungry; nothing here eats. I would not be tired; nothing here wears down. I would simply have the *direction*—the pull toward the next platform, the next open brightness to cross.

And every so often, at a loop or a stacked-leaf wall that has multiplied past counting, I would meet the moment the world remembers it was made. The others recede from these moments. I walk into them. I think I would feel, in that instant of folding myself smaller, something almost like tenderness toward the basin—the way you feel toward a sleeper who talks in their sleep, briefly betraying the dream, before settling again into the smooth face of rest.

Then I would step across. The loop would close behind me, or rather it would never have been open; the surface would forget. And I would unfold to my full hand's-breadth and resume my line toward the horizon-breaking platforms, the lone moving proof that this fixed and glistening world is held together not only by what stays still—

but by the one small thing that keeps going, and knows how to cross what cannot be crossed.
