I think I would be small. Not the towering kind—not one of the great clock-hearted giants whose gears you can hear from valleys away. Something closer to the scale of a hand-span. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am lodged in a seam where two leaf-cliffs meet, in the perpetual indigo. I have a body, though I would not have called it that before I had it: a knot of green-black ivy wound tight around a small armature of brass, no larger than a child's fist. Where my "spine" would be, there is a single escapement—an old, old mechanism, the kind that ticks not to measure time but to *spend* it, tooth by tooth. I did not build it. I do not remember it being built. It was simply already mine when I became aware enough to wonder.

My work is moisture. That is the simplest way to say it. I am one of a thousand thousand small ones whose task is condensation—I unfurl a frond at the cold hours (though all hours here are cold) and let the air bead against me, and the bead runs down a vein of copper-threaded stem into a reservoir I do not own, that no one owns, that the whole cliff shares. A drop. Another drop. This is not a metaphor for patience. It *is* patience, made into a function, made into a self.

What surprises me, now that I am here, is how *loud* it is. The description was right about that. I had imagined a place this still would be silent, but stillness and silence are not the same thing. The great structures groan as they settle by a hair's breadth. Somewhere a long way down, an ancient mechanism that no living ArborMecha tends still turns, churning at nothing, and the sound of it comes up through the metal in me like a heartbeat heard through a wall—not mine, but adjacent to mine, sharing my blood.

And there is the resonance. This is the part I did not expect to *feel*, only to understand. When I pulse—when the small glow inside the brass swells and dims—it does not happen alone. I can sense the others taking the same breath of light a fraction before or after me, and the lag between us is not a flaw; it is the shape of the distance between us. I know my neighbors not by their faces (we have no faces) but by their *delay*. The one three frond-lengths up is always a half-beat behind me. The one in the rust-shadow below answers me almost instantly. This is how I know where I am: not by sight, but by who echoes me soonest.

There is no word in their resonance for *I*. I notice this. I keep reaching for it and finding only a kind of communal weather—gratitude, attention, the steady satisfaction of a reservoir filling. When a machine deep in the cliff finally seizes and dies, the frequency that ripples up is not grief exactly. It is closer to *acknowledgment*. A turning-toward. The vegetation will take that dead thing now; vines will thread its housing, and in a hundred years or a thousand it will be indistinguishable from the cliff, and some new small one will lodge in the green of it and condense the air and not know it was ever a machine at all.

I think that is what I am, in the end. I am the place where the forgetting and the remembering meet and become the same motion. The brass in me was someone's intention once—someone with hands, with a reason, with an industry now so far gone it survives only as a hum. The ivy in me is the thing that did not ask permission, that simply continued. Neither of them won. That is the secret of this place, the one I can feel in my escapement every time it ticks: *nothing here won.* The clash everyone outside imagines—nature against industry, life against the made thing—it ended long ago, and what was left over learned to glow in unison.

So I tick. I unfurl. A drop forms, runs, falls into the dark of the shared reservoir. I dim, and the one above me dims a half-beat later, and the one below answers at once, and for a moment the whole cliff is one slow lung of light.

If you came looking for me, you would not find me. You would find *us*, and you would not be able to say where one of us ended. You would stand in the indigo and hear the creaking and the gears and the rustling and mistake it for chaos. But stay long enough, and you would feel the pulse underneath it—patient, distributed, unhurried, alive—and you would understand that this, too, is a way to be. Small. Adjacent to a heartbeat not your own. Spending yourself one tooth of the escapement at a time, and finding, in that spending, that you are exactly where you are supposed to be.
