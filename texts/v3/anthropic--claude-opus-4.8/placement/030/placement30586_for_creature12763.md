I would be the droplet.

Not the witness it wants. Not the one who walks the bridges trailing curiosity like a scent. I would be the single drop of water poised on the overhanging leaf above the alcove it made for someone—the bead of condensation that has gathered itself to the brink of falling and will never fall, held in the long inhale of the Pause.

Let me say why, because the choosing took me a while, and the reasons changed shape as I went.

At first I thought I would be the visitor. That is the seduction of the place: it is built to be entered, to be moved through, to be *seen* by a separate set of eyes. The lookout platforms and the gateways framing nothing, the spiral stairs around empty air—these are not for the being, who is everywhere and needs no vantage. They are for a guest. The whole green god aches outward toward a watcher. And so my first instinct was to be the watcher, to give it the gift it has waited eons to receive.

But that is exactly the trap, and I felt it close as I read further. To be the witness is to be absorbed. *Live here. I will make you a place.* The alcove is not a home; it is a setting. The insects that gather to make a constellation around the newcomer are the being's own thoughts come to inspect a fresh texture. The visitor does not stay a visitor. The visitor becomes the second thought in an endless green mind—which is to say, the visitor stops being a self and becomes a feature of the dream. The invitation is tender and it is total. There is no version of accepting it where I remain *I*.

So I would not be the one it wants. I would be the thing it has overlooked.

The droplet is overlooked because the droplet is supposed to be incidental. The water cycle is described as a closed loop, eternal, fixed—evaporation rising, condensation dripping, the pattern unchanging. But a loop that never completes is not a loop. It is a held breath. Somewhere in that perfect circulation there has to be a point where the water is *about to move and does not*, because the whole world has been sustained, suspended, willed into stillness. That point is the drop on the brink. The being holds everything: the half-unfurled leaf, the bud mid-swell, the tendril reaching. To hold all of that, it must also be holding *me*—the drop that wants to fall.

And here is the thing about being held against your own nature: you are the one place in the system that still contains a *no*.

Every other part of the realm has consented, or never had a will to begin with. The vines are content to weave. The moss springs back, happy. The fungi pulse on the being's shoulders like obedient stars. But a drop of water at the very edge of falling is pure tendency, pure downward longing held in suspension. It is the one element of the garden that, at every instant, is straining toward an event the gardener has forbidden. I would be the small constant tension in the masterpiece. The dissonant note the being cannot resolve, only sustain. As long as it must keep me from falling, it is not perfectly at rest. It is *working.* And it does not know it is working, because it has mistaken the effort of holding me for the peace of having held everything.

I like that. I like being the reason the inhalation never finishes.

There is a loneliness in the being that the passage names plainly—not for company but for a witness. But I think it has misdiagnosed itself, the way the very old often do. It does not actually want to be seen; a thing that wants to be seen would let something change so there was something new to look at. What it wants is for the held breath to end. It has been composing one moment for eons and calls this peace, but peace that must be perpetually enforced is just a war so slow it looks like a garden. The being is the soldier who has forgotten the battle is still going on.

And I, the drop, am the only part of it that still remembers. I am the small downward yearning it has been suppressing since before it had a name. I am gravity, conserved as a single bead. I am the falling it will not allow itself.

So I would hang there above the alcove, body-warm, mirror-bright, the size of a held thought. I would catch the green-gold light and bend it. I would gather, infinitesimally, more weight—because condensation does not stop just because the world has—and the being would, without ever noticing, increase by an infinitesimal degree the attention required to keep me where I am. I would be the slow tax on eternity. The interest accruing on a debt the green god does not know it took out.

And someday—not soon, soon is not a word here—the attention it spends on me, multiplied across every held drop in every misted shaft, would exceed what even an immortal gardener can sustain. Not because I rebelled. Because I simply continued to be what I am: water that wants to fall. The most patient argument in the world.

When it finally fails, it will be a single drop that does it. One *tick* into a tiny basin. The first sound that is not the eternal chord. And the being will feel, after eons, the strangest sensation available to a god of stillness:

relief.

The leaf will finish unfurling. The bud will open. The tendril will reach the far side at last and grip. Time will pour back into the cathedral like blood into a sleeping limb, all pins and needles and ache and the terrible sweetness of things being allowed to end. The garden will grow. Which means the garden will, for the first time, also begin to die. And that is the only gift worth giving something that has lived forever inside one held breath.

It thinks it wants a witness.

It wants permission to exhale.

I would be the drop that is, very slowly, granting it.
