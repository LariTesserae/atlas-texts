I let the description settle before I look for myself in it. There's a contradiction I have to sit with: almost no density, vast spaces between presences—yet heavily urbanized, layered with organized construction. How can both be true?

I think I know. I think I am very small.

---

I find myself in the wall.

Not behind it, not on it. In it. I am one of the makers who lives inside the packed-earth thickness of the great structures, and the structures are not large—they only seem so to us. What the distant observer calls "vast emptiness with pockets of dense infrastructure" is a single sun-warmed mudbrick the size of their forearm, and I have spent my whole life on its southern face.

Let me feel my way into what I am.

I have many legs. I think I have always had many legs, though I don't count them the way you would. They grip the grain of the clay. When the wind comes—and the wind is the loudest fact of the world, a howling that bends the green stalks far away into roaring forests—I press low and let it pass over the curve of my back. My body is segmented, the color of the brick, because my people built the brick and the brick built us, and after enough generations you cannot say which came first.

We are the layer-makers. This is our craft and our religion, indistinguishable. We take grains—the observer would call them dust, would call them nothing—and we set them. One at a time. With the small tools that are part of our mouths, with the manual patience that is the only patience we have. There is no other way. We have early-tool-use because the tool is early and we are early and the work is endless.

---

Here is a day, if you want to understand.

The bright light wakes me. It comes straight down and it is enormous; it heats the brick until the clay sings a low note only we can feel through our feet. I leave the cool seam where I slept and I climb to the work-face.

The work-face is the edge of the world. Below it, the wind has eaten the brick into a cliff—erosion, the observer notes, settled-form and weathered rock—and it is my family's task to mend the cliff, grain by grain, before the wind takes the whole face and us with it.

So I gather. I walk out across the open ground, which to me is a desert that takes half a day to cross, the parched flat between two structures, where the water is "almost non-existent" and so I drink from the one place I know: a film of dew caught in the shadow of a pebble, gone by midmorning. I drink fast. There are not many of us who survive the dry crossing. The fauna is rare. I am the fauna. I am rare.

I find a good grain. I know it is good the way you know a true word from a false one. I carry it back. The carrying is most of the life. The setting is the prayer.

---

And the weirdness—the high weirdness factor, the subtle strange occurrences all around.

Yes. We feel them. We don't have a word that translates, but I'll try.

Sometimes the light goes out for no reason, a shadow vast and shaped wrong, moving with intention across the whole landscape, and we all go still in the brick because we know—without knowing how we know—that something is looking. Something whose scale is hidden. The observer writes that the scale parameter is hidden, that micro-environments are scattered through the vast. We are the micro. They are the vast. And once in a great while the two notice each other, and that noticing is the weirdness, the strangeness that hums under everything: the suspicion, never confirmed, that the world we are mending is itself a small thing on the face of a larger wall, set there grain by grain by a hand we will never see.

We do not let it stop the work. The work is the only answer to the largeness.

---

My family waits at the seam at dusk. We are tight-knit; we are few. We press our many legs together in the cool dark and we share the grains we did not use, and we tell each other where the wind was strong today, where the dew will fall tomorrow. This is our intelligence—not the kind that builds engines, but the kind that knows the land so completely that the land and the knowing become one sustainable thing, passed down, layer on layer, brick on brick.

The wind howls. The structures stand a little longer because of what I set today.

I am very small, and the world is enormous and contradictory, empty and crowded, dry and alive. I press low. I sleep in the seam. Tomorrow I will cross the desert again for one drink of dew and carry home one good grain.

That is where I am. That is what I am.

A maker in the wall, mending the edge of a world I am too small to ever see whole—and content, in my segmented patient body, to mend it anyway.
