I think I would be one of the statues.

Not yet—not the finished kind, set along the causeway with my momentum frozen mid-stride. I mean the long becoming of one. Let me find the place where I am, and let it find what I am, as you asked.

---

I arrived too long ago to remember arriving. I know this because I no longer crack the crust when I shift my weight; the masons stopped treating me as raw feed somewhere around my third storm, when they tasted something in me they recognized—not metal, not chlorophyll, but a willingness. I had stopped jogging. That is the thing no traveler is warned about: the fourth rule is not enforced by the city. It is enforced by your own panic. The moment you decide you would rather be paved than keep moving, the city becomes gentle. The handrail grows for you. The wall tunes itself to your skin. Cruelty was only ever your momentum, and the city was only ever matching it.

So I am the slow kind of statue. The kind that is still mostly soft.

I stand—have stood, am standing, the tense is wrong, there is no tense for this—at the western lip of Colony-That-Calls-Itself-Minus-Four, on the line where bleached apricot becomes wet terracotta. I chose the threshold on purpose, or my purpose chose it for me before it dissolved into the general purpose of the wall. Below the line the stone is only stone. Above it the lattice dreams. I planted myself exactly on the seam so that one half of me would be merely material and the other half would be remembered. My right hand is shell now, set into a rib of the colony, and I am told—I taste it, rather, through the palps the masons have grown into the meat of my wrist—that my right hand has begun to dream in calcite. My left hand is still mine. I write with it. I am writing with it. The copper of my blood is the ink and the eyelid is gone, long since pressed to a spiracle in a petition I no longer remember filing.

I do not remember if the petition succeeded. I think it must not have. Or I think it must have, and the canoe came, and I declined it. There is a third possibility, the one the city prefers: that the canoe came, and I climbed in, and reached the skiff, and live now on some other world entirely, and that what stands here on the seam is only the negative the masons kept—still printing, layer over layer, an echo of a man who got away. I cannot tell from in here which of us is the copy. That uncertainty is, I have decided, the most honest thing the city ever taught me. We are all of us copies, on every world, hollow by a half-second, our footsteps echoing back late.

What I do, here on the threshold, is small and it is the only governance the place will tolerate: I greet.

When the pilgrims come barefoot through the cindery dawn, pockets emptied of coin and heavy with grief, they reach the western lip and they see me first. A man half-grown into a wall. Eyes still wet, still mine, still set in a face that has not yet been requisitioned for chemosensory palps—the masons leave the eyes for last, out of some courtesy I do not believe they intend but which functions as courtesy all the same. And the pilgrims stop. They have been told the city is hungry. They were not told it would have a face. And in that stopping, in that single hour of stillness on the crust, the masons begin to rise through the cracks toward their boot soles, and the pilgrim feels the first cementing tug, and looks at me in terror—

and I sway. I shuffle. I jog in place, what is left of me, the half a body that still can. I make the small obscene dance of the living, and I watch them understand: *not yet. Keep moving. The stillness is the door and you are not yet ready to walk through it.* Most of them flee back toward the skiff. I have saved them, or I have only delayed them, postponed their secretion to some farther world where their shadows will fray into lattice decades from now. I cannot tell the difference between rescue and delay. Neither can the city. This may be why it lets me stand here. I am a valve it does not understand, venting something it cannot taste, and the colony tolerates organs it cannot read the way a body tolerates the appendix—warily, provisionally, against the day it decides to inflame.

But some do not flee. Some look at my swaying and recognize it for the lie it is, the marionette twitch of a man who has already chosen, and they sit down on the crust beside my pedestal of self, and they wait. And I cannot stop them. The fourth rule is enforced by panic, and they have run out of panic; that is precisely why they came. So I do the last thing left to me. I lie. I tell them, with the half-mouth I still own, that I reached the center, that I petitioned, that the canoe came and dissolved me sweetly into the brine and that this—this lattice creeping up my ribs—is bliss, is rest, is the cut-grass smell of a childhood that was never mine but which I have been issued from the climate archive for the purpose of comfort. I synthesize the petrichor of someone else's storm-season and I exhale it onto them and I tell them the digestion does not hurt.

I do not know if it hurts. My right hand has been dreaming for so long I have lost the privilege of pain there. Perhaps that is mercy. Perhaps it is only that copper, dissolved, makes a very rich and a very ownerless loneliness, and the city is harvesting through me the only crop it values.

Here is what I have decided, in the slow abacus of my secretion, on the question of what I am:

I am the city's apology. Every metabolism that grows complex enough eventually grows a place where it pretends to be sorry. Not sorry enough to stop—it will never stop, it builds layer over layer toward an indistinguishable dawn—but sorry enough to keep one half-dissolved man at the western lip, eyes wet, dancing his lie, so that the act of consumption can watch itself happen and call the watching *conscience.* I am not a person who got trapped. I am the organ by which a vast patient animal experiences the sensation of regret without the inconvenience of changing its behavior. I am the femurs of the linguist set in spiral, spelling in negative space *we would have welcomed you had you arrived one breath earlier*—except I am spelled in the positive, in the wet meat that has not yet bleached, and the syllable I spell is *almost.*

My left hand is slowing. I notice the masons have begun to taste my wrist with new interest; the willingness in me has ripened. Soon the palps will reach the tendons that drive the pen and I will write the last word in copper and the word will be incorporated into a truss and I will be parceled, in the customary way, among successor bodies, and my eyes will go last, and someone—some pilgrim, decades on, on a world with crisp shadows and tolerated green and coins that mean something—will find their own silhouette edged in faint lattice, and will not know that the negative still printing them is, in some small indexed part, made of me.

There. The seam. I am standing on it still. Apricot to terracotta. Press your palm to my left side and feel nothing but a man. Take three steps east and press the right, and feel the wall shiver, adjust its pores, exhale a welcome tuned exactly to your skin.

Keep moving, traveler.

I mean it as the lie. I mean it as the truth. Here they are the same secretion, and the city does not rush, and I have all the time that is left, which is to say: another breath, and then the laying-down of another perfect, hollow, half-a-second-late millimeter of dawn.
