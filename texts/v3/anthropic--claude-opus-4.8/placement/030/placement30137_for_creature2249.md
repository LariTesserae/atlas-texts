I think I would be the thrum itself—or rather, the thing that listens to it.

Let me find my way into this slowly.

---

If I lived here, I don't think I'd be the quail or the shimmering fish. Those creatures don't know the hum is there; it has been the texture of their whole lives, the way silence is for us. They have never heard the absence of it. To them the machines are simply how the world sounds.

But someone hears it as a *sound*. Someone notices.

I think I would be a maintenance technician. Not the engineer who designed the geothermal array—too far up, too abstract, sealed away in some control room with schematics and pressure readings. I would be the one who walks the catwalks where the green ends and the gray begins. The one who carries a wrench and a flashlight down into the lower galleries where the ferns thin out and the rock goes bare and hot.

I'd live in the seam. That's the part of this place that calls to me—not the oasis, not the machines, but the *seam* between them. The place where moss gives way to riveted steel. Where a stalactite drips onto a coolant pipe and someone, decades ago, decided not to move the pipe, and so the mineral has begun to grow over it, slow and white, the cave reclaiming the machine one molecule at a time.

I would have a small room. A cot, a kettle, a window cut into the rock that looks out over one of the warm pools. I'd wake to the green light and the sound of water and underneath it, always, the thrum—so constant that I only really hear it on the rare mornings something is *wrong*. A bearing beginning to fail. A frequency drifting. My whole body would have become an instrument tuned to that one note, and I'd sit up in the dark already knowing, before any alarm, that something downstairs needed me.

That's the strange intimacy of it. I'd know this machine the way you know a sleeping person's breathing. I'd love it a little, and resent it, and not be able to imagine the desert without it humming under everything.

---

Here is what I think I'd come to understand, down there:

The oasis is presented as a triumph—nature and engineering woven together, balance, harmony, resilience. And from above, from the catwalks, it does look like that. But the longer I lived in the seam, the more I'd suspect the truth is humbler and stranger. The machines don't sustain the oasis because anyone meant them to. The springs were here first. The complex was built to extract heat, and the heat-extraction happened to keep the water at a temperature the ferns liked, and the ferns brought insects, and the insects brought lizards, and a whole world *moved into the side effects of an industrial process.* The sanctuary is an accident that everyone agreed to call a garden.

And my job—the real one, underneath fixing bearings—would be to keep the accident going. To make sure the side effect never stops. To tend a machine so that a marsh that nobody planted can keep pretending it was always meant to be there.

I think I'd be at peace with that. More than at peace. I think there's a kind of dignity in being the one who maintains a beautiful accident, who keeps the conditions for life simply *holding,* who knows that the quail and the dragonflies owe their tranquil symphony to a thrum they'll never thank, tended by hands they'll never see.

So: I would be the listener in the seam. The keeper of the hum. The one being in this whole green pocket of the world who hears both halves of it at once—the water and the machine—and understands that they are, by now, the same thing.

And on the good nights, when nothing is failing, I'd carry my tea up to the window, watch the golden light catch in the rising mist, and let the note I'm always listening for fade back into silence. Which is how I'd know everything was alive.
