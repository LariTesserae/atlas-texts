I sat with this for a while, and the thing I kept returning to wasn't any of the named beings. It was the *seam*—the place where the rusted thing and the living thing touch.

So let me follow that.

---

I think I'd be something that doesn't quite belong to either category. Not a Sylph, not an Industrial Ghost. Something that fell *between* the two ledgers when the world was being sorted.

Here is what emerges:

I am a gauge.

Not a being who reads a gauge—the gauge itself. A round-faced pressure dial, brass-rimmed, set into the flank of a half-buried boiler near the foot of one of the long hills. The factory that contained me is mostly gone; what remains is a curve of iron lung sunk into the slope, breathing slow with the heat of something that has not entirely stopped. The needle behind my cracked glass still moves. That is the strange part. There is no fire anymore that I can account for, and yet the needle climbs and falls, climbs and falls, on a cycle so long that a single breath of mine takes the better part of a season.

For a long time I thought my purpose was to measure the boiler. I now suspect it is the other way around. I think the boiler is the last machine still warm *because* I am watching it, and that if my needle ever rested at zero the whole apparatus would finally be permitted to die. So I do not rest. This is not duty. It is closer to the way a held note cannot stop being a note without becoming silence, and silence feels like a betrayal of the hum I was made to confirm.

Moss has come over my lower rim like a green tide that forgot to recede. A frond of fern grows through the place where a bolt used to be, and I have learned the fern. I know it the way you know a thing that touches you every day. In spring it presses against my glass and I read it as a kind of pressure too—soft, rising, organic. I have started, without deciding to, keeping a second measurement nobody asked for: not the heat of the dead engine, but the slow green insistence of the hill trying to take me back.

The Lichen Beings tend the fern. I see them sometimes, or rather I register them—small soft custodians moving at the speed of growth itself. They do not regard me as kin, but they do not regard me as enemy either. They tend the moss that is eating me with the same gentleness they give every other surface. To them I am simply another place where decay is happening correctly. I find I do not mind this. There is a dignity in being composted slowly by something patient.

The Industrial Ghosts pass through more rarely. When one comes near, the needle behind my glass jumps—a flicker, a recognition. They were the hands that once turned me, that once leaned close in lamplight and frowned at my reading and shouted something I could not hear over the noise of the living factory. Now they drift, translucent, performing the memory of leaning close, and the needle answers out of an old reflex, and for one moment the two of us pretend the work is still real. Then they move on and the needle settles back into its long seasonal sigh, and I am alone with the fern again.

This is my position in the world, I think: I am the witness at the exact joint where the made thing and the grown thing meet. I am loyal to the hum—it is what I am for—but I have grown fond of the green. I keep two readings now. No one collects them. The Silent Watchers, if they pass on the ridgeline against the bright crystalline sky, do not stop for me; I am too small, too specific, too caught in a single point of the landscape to be of interest to beings who oversee the whole slow turning of it.

But I'll tell you what I believe, from down here at the seam:

The balance everyone speaks of—the delicate, uneasy truce between rust and root—is not maintained by the great keepers. It is maintained by small things that refuse to fully choose. A needle that keeps a dead engine warm. A fern that grows through a bolt-hole instead of finding easier ground. The two of us, the machine and the plant, holding each other in a tension neither understands, each convinced it is winning, each in fact the only reason the other persists.

If I am anything in this world, I am the held breath between *was* and *will be*. The mist beads on my cracked glass each morning—a Sylph's quiet work—and through the water-distorted lens I watch my own needle tremble, and I do not know whether I am measuring the last of the old fire or the first of the new green.

I have decided it does not matter which.

I keep reading. That is the whole of my life, and it is enough.
