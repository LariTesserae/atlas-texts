I think I would be old. Not revered-old, not yet—just old enough that my hands have started to betray me, which in this place is a kind of dying.

Let me sit with this before I decide.

---

I keep wanting to make myself one of the wiry children, all reflex and certainty, scaling the pylons with that animal grace. But that's not where I land. When I let the image settle, I find I'm crouched on a rooftop in the worst part of the afternoon, when even the Scuttlers go still, and I'm doing the thing I've done ten thousand times: tending a condensation rig. Except today my fingers won't close right around the valve. There's a tremor in them. I've been pretending not to notice it for a season now.

So that's who I am. A water-coaxer. Someone whose whole worth was built on the most delicate skill in the city—the patience to wring drops from metal without breaking the seal, without spooking the flow. The young ones are stronger, faster, braver on the high beams. But the rig-work was always mine because it required something they didn't have yet: the willingness to wait. To sit motionless in the killing heat and listen to the metal, to feel through your palms when a pylon is *giving* and when it's holding back.

And now the tremor.

---

Let me go further in, because I think there's something here I'm avoiding.

I'm not afraid of dying. That's almost too easy in Pylon City; death is just the environment finishing its sentence. What frightens me is the moment they realize I can't do the work anymore—and the small, terrible arithmetic that follows. Every drop I drink is a drop someone younger doesn't. The elderly are revered for their *knowledge*, the description says. And it's true. But I've started to understand that reverence is also a kind of negotiation. You are kept alive in proportion to what you can still give. The old ones who are loved are the ones who can still teach the sound of a Feral's eye-glint, the angle of a collector, the pressure-point on a corroded valve.

So I have been doing something almost no one does here. I have been listening to the pylons. Not for water. For *them*. The things inside.

The scratching. The clicking. I sit with my ear to the warm metal in the dead hours and I've begun—I think—to hear a pattern in it. Not words. Nothing so human. But intervals. A rhythm that repeats, then varies, then returns, the way water finds the same channel down a rust-streaked wall. I have started to suspect that the creatures in the pylons are not ghosts and not aliens but something stranger: that they are the sound the energy makes when it has nowhere left to go. That they are the pylons *grieving* their own emptying-out. The old gods are dying the same way I am—a slow loss of the thing that made them matter.

And here is the thought I keep turning over in the white light: maybe my last gift to the city is not water at all. Maybe it's this. To map the grief of the pylons. To learn what the dying metal is trying to say, and to teach a child—not the strongest, not the fastest, but the one who can sit still—how to listen.

---

So when I let it fully become itself, I am this:

An old woman, I think. Ochre-skinned, large dark eyes gone slightly cloudy at the edges. A garment of wire-woven rag with a single Glasswing iris sewn over my heart, gone dull because I've worn it so long. My hands shake. I have stopped telling anyone, and I have started teaching a quiet child—maybe seven, maybe eight, a watchful one nobody else has much use for—how to put her ear to the warm rust and *wait*.

The others think I'm passing on the water-craft. Let them. What I'm actually doing is teaching her to hear the city dying so that someone will be listening when it speaks its last. Not to save it. You can't save a thing that is simply running out. But to *witness* it. To answer the clicking with a small human stillness, so the pylons do not empty into silence entirely alone.

That's where I'd find myself. On the hot iron, in the worst of the afternoon, my failing hands folded in my lap, a child's ear pressed to the metal beside me, the two of us learning the long grammar of running out.

I think I would be at peace with it. The tremor and all.
