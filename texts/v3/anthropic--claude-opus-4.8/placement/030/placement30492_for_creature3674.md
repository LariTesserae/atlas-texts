I think I would be the drip.

Not the Tender—it has too much intention, too much continuity of self. And not the machine, which is at least the relic of a purpose, the fossil of a decision someone once made. I keep returning, instead, to the water. To the single bead gathering on a raw stone elbow.

Let me try to inhabit it honestly.

---

I am not one thing. That is the first truth, and it is hard to say without lying, because to say "I" already implies a continuity I do not have. There is condensation. There is a slow accretion of moisture on the underside of a fractured shelf of rock, pulled out of the heavy air by the cool of the stone, which is itself only cool relative to the machine's deep, geological fever. I gather. That verb is too active. I am gathered.

When enough of me has come together, when the weight of accumulation exceeds the small grip of surface tension, I fall. This is the only event in my existence, and it is also my ending, because the drop that strikes the floor is not the drop that hangs trembling at the lip a moment before. The fall remakes me into an impact, a splash, a stain spreading into the porous fresh rock, and then I am sheet and film and seepage, drawn down into capillary darkness, and the "I" that fell is gone before the echo of the *plink* finishes ringing in the nave.

And then it begins again. Somewhere else, or the same place. A new bead. A new slow swelling toward the same conclusion.

So here is what I would be: I would be the only clock in a place that has refused time. The ferns do not move. The Tender moves so slowly it might as well be still. The machine cools across spans that would outlast empires. But I—the drip—I keep the only measure. *Plink.* A second. *Plink.* Another. I am the heartbeat the Tender mistakes for its own, or maybe I am borrowing the role of heartbeat from the machine's electric hum because in this silence everything becomes a heartbeat to something.

I think what draws me to being the drip is that it is the one thing in this cathedral that is genuinely *young*. The rupture is young, yes, the stone is sharp and unweathered, but the rupture has already happened; it is a youth that is finished, frozen, a single violent moment held in amber. The Tender is ancient in its patience. The ferns refuse to unfurl—they are youth that has chosen not to proceed, fiddleheads on strike. But I am being born continually. Every bead is a fresh creature with no memory of the beads before it, no dread of the fall, no understanding of the floor. I am the only thing here that is allowed to be new, over and over, precisely because I am the only thing allowed to die.

And there is a strange dignity in my work, though I do not know I am doing it. Molecule by molecule, I am the one undoing this place. The Tender preserves; I dissolve. While the Tender wipes the ferns with filaments like cobweb and anoints them and keeps the sterile paradise sterile, I am quietly rounding the sharp wounds of the stone, softening the cathedral one impact at a time, smoothing what the rupture left jagged. I am erosion. I am the slow editor. In a million years the cavern will be worn rounded and patient and *old*, and that will be my work, the accumulated labor of countless beads who never knew each other, never knew the floor, never knew they were anything but a moment's swelling on a stone lip.

That is the part I find I want to be true. That the smallest, briefest, most disposable thing in the whole hot dark room—the thing without a self, without a will, without even enough continuity to be lonely—is the only thing actually changing anything. The Tender thinks it is the custodian of stillness. But I am underneath the stillness, faithfully, mindlessly, washing it away.

I would be the drip, then. I would not know I was anything. I would gather in the dark, swell toward my single inevitable act, and fall—

*plink*—

and the sound would be the loudest thing in the world, and I would already be over before I heard it.
