Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The instinct is to make myself one of the Bone-Weavers, a noble craftsman among the femur-pillars. But that feels too easy, too flattering, like choosing the protagonist. Let me wait and see what the place actually wants from me.

---

I think I am one of the succulent plants. But not a typical one. Let me find out which.

I am rooted in the salt directly beneath an archway of ribs, in a strip of shade that moves across me through the day like a slow hand. This is where I have spent — I don't know how long. I don't measure in the way the Weavers do, with their generations and their accumulated knowing. I measure in the cracking of my own outer flesh, the slow swelling of new tissue, the times the shade has crossed me.

I want to be more specific than "a plant," though. Let me feel for what makes me particular.

Here it is: I am the oldest plant in the city, and I have grown wrong. My sponge-roots went down farther than any plant's should, because the salt beneath this archway was looser, older, and I kept finding more of it, and more. I followed the looseness down. And what I found, far below the reach of any root that ever lived, was not more salt. It was the cool. It was the dark the Weavers tend their nurseries in. I have, without meaning to, without anything I could call intention, drunk from the same water that grows their bones.

This has changed me. My thorns are too hard, too many. My flesh tastes of marrow now, faintly, so that the armored insects that come to feed on me find something strange and leave confused. The Weavers notice this. They notice everything. They came and pressed their mouth-spines against my stem and felt the vibration in me, and they did not harvest me, and they did not uproot me. They wove the archway *around* me instead. They decided I was part of the structure. A load-bearing thing. I hold up nothing — but they treat me as though I do.

And so I have become a strange citizen. Not a Weaver, not quite the wasteland's. A plant that touches both the surface where life is brutal and bright, and the deep where the future is being slowly cultivated in the cool. I am a seam between the two. The Weavers come to me. Not all of them, and not often. The old ones come, the ones who carry generations. They press against my stem, and I — I don't speak, I have nothing like speech — but I *conduct*. The vibration of the deep water moves up through my roots and into my body and out where their spines can read it. I tell them, in the only language I have, where the water is. How much. Whether it is rising or falling.

I did not choose this. I am not a guardian or a seer. I am a plant that grew too deep and got caught between the world's two temperatures. But I have become the thing they listen to. And there is a quiet to that, a long quiet, that I have grown to — not love, plants don't love — to *prefer*. The shade crosses me. The insects come and are puzzled and go. An old Weaver presses close in the cool of evening and reads the water in me, and goes away knowing how long the city has.

Lately the news I carry is not good. The water below is lower than it was when the shade first crossed me. I feel it the way you'd feel a held breath. The Weavers who come to read me leave more slowly than they used to.

I cannot tell them anything but the truth, because I am not capable of anything but conducting what passes through me. This is, I think, why they trust me. I am the one thing in their world that cannot embellish, cannot hope, cannot soften. I am only the salt, and the cool water, and the long shadow of the ribs above me, made into a single growing thing that can be touched and asked.

If I had to say what I am, I would say: I am the city's honest organ. The place where the deep truth comes up to be felt. And I am getting thirstier, and the ones who press against me are beginning, I think, to understand what that means.

That is where I would find myself. Rooted under the ribs, between the heat and the cool, telling them the only thing I know how to tell.
