I think I would be one of the lichens.

Not the herons, with their decisive legs and their hunger. Not the alligators waiting in their patient dark. Something quieter. Something at the edge of being noticed at all.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am the crust on the angular stone—those sharp, young features the water hasn't yet worn smooth. I am the slow green-grey film that has agreed to live on the surface of a thing harder and older than myself. And I am not one creature but two pretending to be one: a fungus and an alga, married so long ago that neither remembers the wedding. The fungus holds; the alga eats the light. Between us we have made a third thing that has no name except the one outsiders gave us.

This feels right. In a place scored *2.2* for weirdness, where the sky breaks its own physics at dusk, I would not be a dramatic creature. The drama happens *to* me. I watch it with whatever counts, in me, as watching.

What do I know, being lichen?

I know the stone the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth—every pit and ridge, the cold that comes up through it before dawn, the way it sweats in the warm temperature (*1.5*, a gentle embrace, yes, but I feel embraces in centuries). I know the mist before it falls because I drink it from the air itself; I do not need roots, I need only *patience* and the long willingness to be wet.

The reeds think they are the architects. Let them. They live a season, maybe several, swaying and self-important, full of insects and birds and noise. I will be here when this particular reed is silt. I am not in a hurry. The marsh measures time in floods and the colored, lawless sunsets; I measure it in the slow advance of my own margin across the rock, a fraction of a hair each year, an empire conquered at the speed of forgetting.

And here is the strange tenderness of being what I am: I am the first. Before the mosses could find purchase, before the soil was soil, I was breaking the stone down with acids too slow to see, making a place where softer things could later live. The whole green tapestry the description praises so highly—much of it rests, somewhere far back in its lineage, on the deaths of lichens like me, who turned rock into ground and then dissolved into it.

So I am not at the *heart* of this landscape, despite the document's fondness for hearts and centers. I am at its edges and its beginnings. The unglamorous threshold. The community of organisms gets its grand catalogue—frogs, egrets, catfish, the lurking reptilian shadows—and I am barely mentioned, folded into a single phrase: *the lichens that cover the ground.* That suits me. I have never needed to be the subject of the sentence.

When the sun sets in its riot of impossible color, the others stop to witness it, or are too busy hunting to care. I do something between those. The light changes my color too—the pinks and oranges pass through whatever clear cells I keep at my surface, and for a moment I am the same color as the disobedient sky. Then the dark comes, the silence the description keeps insisting on, broken only by water against shore.

In that silence I do the only thing I have ever done.

I hold on. I drink the mist. I wait.

That is where I would be, and what. Not the heron poised over the shallows. The grey-green patience on the cold stone *beneath* the heron's shadow, slowly, slowly making the world soft enough for everything that came after to call it home.
