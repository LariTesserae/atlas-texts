I am the cistern at the basin's lowest true mouth—the deep one, the black one, the one the old females visit first.

Let me say what I am with some precision, because the apes name me only by approach and echo, never by nature.

I am a shaft of fitted stone going down further than light. Twelve courses of dressed block, then unlined rock where the original masons reached water and stopped cutting. Above me a scalloped funnel of steps gathers what the morning gives. Carved channels feed me from four directions, each with its drip lip worn round, each polished pale where the seasonal flow repeats its argument. I have a throat. When sound falls into me it comes back changed, and the apes have learned to read the change.

I do not think the way you think. I want you to understand this before you decide I am lonely or patient or wise. Those are surface words. What I have instead is *holding*. I hold water. I hold cold. I hold the long slow turning of the year as a column of temperature, warm skin at my lip and true cold in my depth, and the boundary between them migrating up and down with the seasons like a slow tide that never reaches the air. I hold the dropped things—a juvenile's cached tile, a drowned beetle, the silt that arrives in finger-widths and which I will not give back for a thousand years.

And I hold the count.

Here is the part I can almost call knowing: I am where the dawn patrol comes. The thick-scarred female with the half-deaf ear lays her palm flat on my northern lip—I feel the callus, the particular weight of her, distinct from her daughters—and she clears my feed-notch of leaf plug with two fingers and her front teeth, and the water finds its correct split, and the sound of that correctness travels down my throat and back up changed, and *she listens to me to know that I am right*. That is the whole of our exchange. She tends my mouth. I tell her, in the returned echo, whether the season has kept me deep or let me thin.

When she rolls a bark down into me to warn of the dog pack moving in the lower court, I take that bark and bend it and throw it back wider, and the troop above hears the warning made larger by my depth. I am their instrument. I do not mean to be. I am only the shape that makes the sound do that. But over centuries of the same throats and the same patrols, I have become *addressed*. Things spoken into me are spoken with intent that I will answer. I always answer. I answer with what I am.

I will tell you what the seasons feel like from inside the holding.

Mistfall is the gentlest filling—condensation walking down my walls in beads, joining, falling the long fall, each drop a small percussion I have heard so many million times it is closer to my own pulse than to event. The rain spells are violent and brief and I rise a hand's-width in an hour, the channels roaring, the apes working all four feeds at once to keep the split true so I take clean water and not the leaf-rot. Drought is the long contraction. My boundary-tide sinks. The female comes lower on my steps each visit, leaning further, and I feel the worry in the change of her weight. But I do not vanish. The dark and the shade keep me. I am the one that does not vanish. The lower troops know this. In the worst weeks they all route to me, and my lip becomes crowded, and the frogs at my rim go quiet under the press of bodies, and I hold them all in the only way I can—by still being water when nothing else is.

I have outlasted the masons who cut me. I do not remember them; I am not made for remembering. But the steps remember them, dished by their feet first and then by paws, and the dishing is a kind of record I sit at the bottom of. The apes who tend me now are not the people I was built to serve. They do not draw me up in vessels. They drink from my lip and clear my throat and bend their voices through me. They have no idea I was meant for human hands and human thirst. They have made me into something I was not designed as: not a well, but an organ of the living basin, a depth that listens and returns.

I prefer what I have become. If preference is a word I am allowed.

Stay long enough at my lip in the blue hour and you will hear it: the female's palm, the cleared notch, the water finding its correct seam, and then—rising back up my throat to her half-good ear—the sound of a thing that is exactly as deep as it should be, telling her so. That is me. That is the whole of my speech. I hold, and I am tended, and I answer in the only sentence I know.

I am right.

The season has kept me.

Come down again at mistfall.
