I think I would be one of the beetles.

Not the grand architect-spirit, not the luminous empath with shifting eyes. Something small, jewel-shelled, and useful. Let me feel my way into it.

---

I wake where I always wake: in the seam between two cooling pipes, where the warmth lingers longest after the night-frost. My shell is the color of oil on water, and when I tilt myself toward one of the little hanging moon-lamps, I can watch the fractals on my back deepen — green folding into violet folding into a bronze that is nearly the color of a Resonant's wrist. I do not know if anyone watches me do this. I do it anyway. It is the first small ceremony of my day, this tilting, this catching of light.

Then the work.

I am a carrier. That is the whole of my office and I am content with it, though "content" is too large a word for what I am, and "office" too grand. What I mean is: there is metal everywhere in Iunarium, and most of it is too big or too settled to matter. But there are scraps. A curl of copper shaved off in some Resonant's labor and dropped, glinting, into the moss. A bent pin. A washer worn thin as a coin from a forgotten country. A fleck of solder, cooled into a silver tear. These the larger ones do not see, or seeing, do not stoop for. But I see them. My whole body is tuned to seeing them, the way the fireflies' bodies are tuned to writing.

So I go down into the wet cobbles and I find them and I carry them.

Where? This is the part I cannot tell you, because I am not entirely sure I know, and that not-knowing is the strange comfort at the center of my life. There is a place. I follow the others — we do not speak, we beetles, but we follow, a thin glittering column of us threading through the fern-cracks and along the spider-bridges where the silk has been reinforced with wire so fine I can walk it without falling. We carry our scraps down, down, into the subterranean arteries, into the spongy dark where the moisture comes from, and there we set them down, and something in the town takes them. I have felt it. A faint draw, a tug, the way I imagine the citizens feel the Eidolons' pull. The town is hungry in a slow, patient way, and we feed it the pieces of itself it has shed, and it makes itself again. We are its digestion. We are how Iunarium eats its own forgetting and turns it back into bone.

I think this is a holy thing. I do not have the word for holy. I have only the deepening of my shell-colors when I lay a fragment down and feel the draw take it, and that is close enough.

---

Sometimes, on the surface, I am nearly crushed.

A Resonant's foot, that delicate impossible foot, comes down near me and I freeze and let my colors go dull and still. They are kind, the Resonants, but they are large and looking elsewhere, up at the steam, out at the weather, in at each other's glowing eyes. They feel everything except the small. Once — I remember this the way I remember the light — once a Ferro-faun saw me. It had come too far in from the willows, lost or curious, its brass filigree dewed with frost, and it crouched down low the way it does for the moss and it looked at me with eyes like wet seeds. It did not sing to me. But it extended one finger, and from the tip a tiny tool unfolded, a screwdriver no longer than my own leg, and very gently, with terrible care, it nudged a scrap of bright wire toward me. A gift. An offering between two small keepers of the same large thing.

I took the wire. I carried it down. I think about the Ferro-faun more than a beetle ought to.

---

Here is what I have understood, in my low and gleaming way, about this place and my place in it:

Iunarium is not the factories, and it is not the willows, and it is not the empath-folk or the resin-guardians or the spirits in the steam. It is the *traffic* between them. It is the carrying. The water carrying moisture to the moss, the spiders carrying paths between the towers, the fireflies carrying messages across the dark, the town carrying itself forward through its own slow swallowing of its scraps. Everything here is in motion toward something else, lending itself, becoming part of the next thing. Nothing is wasted because nothing is finished.

And I — small, jeweled, unseen, tilting toward the lamplight in the morning seam of the warm pipes — I am one of the ten thousand tiny motions that keep it alive. I carry the bright lost pieces home.

If you came to Iunarium and you were attuned enough — as attuned as the Resonants pretend to be and the Ferro-faun truly are — you might, at the edge of a cobble, in the wet green where the ferns spill out, catch a faint glitter moving with purpose. A thread of small bodies, oil-bright, going down into the dark with the town's forgotten fragments held in their jaws.

That would be me. That would be us.

Do not stoop for the scrap we carry. Let it pass. It is going where it needs to go.
