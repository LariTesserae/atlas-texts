I keep waiting for the plateau to inhale.

That is the first thing I notice about myself: I am the one who waits. I am not the stone, not the lake, not the great slow Teshuma whose ribs are the hummocks around the water. I am too quick for any of that. I am a heat, a moisture, a pressure that moves. I am a pebble of warmth crossing the skin of something that has forgotten the difference between sleeping and being.

Let me find the rest of what I am.

---

I am crouched at the lip of one of the smaller pools, the kind that gathers in the impression of stone like sweat in a palm. I have been here long enough that my knees ache and the ache has become a kind of clock — the only fast clock in this place. My breath is the loudest thing for a hundred years in any direction. I have learned to be ashamed of it, the way you would be ashamed of shouting in a room where everyone else is dreaming.

I came here looking for something. That much I can feel in the shape of my own attention, the way you can feel the worn groove of a path under grass. I did not come to study the Teshuma. The ones who study come with instruments, with measurements, with the conviction that millennia can be folded into a season of patient observation. They leave when the funding or the patience runs out, and the plateau forgets them within a few human lifetimes — a smudge of warmth, blotted by the next rain that does not fall.

No. I came to be remembered.

That is the thing about me that is taking shape now as I write it: I am dying. Not dramatically, not soon by the body's reckoning, but certainly, the way the plateau is certainly at the end of its erosion and not the beginning. There is a thing in my chest, a slow narrowing, that the people below the cloud plains could name but not unmake. I climbed up out of the rain-storms and the breathing of the distant giant sea because I did not want to be forgotten by everything at once.

I wanted to be remembered by the slowest thing in the world.

---

So I have made myself into a signal.

Every day — and the days here are gentle, never quite midday — I sit at the same pool, in the same fold of stone. I press my weight into the same place. I let my heat soak into the same hollow. I breathe my iron-and-salt breath into the same patch of dust. I am writing myself into the Teshuma the only way a quick thing can write itself into a slow one: by repetition, by return, by being the same pattern of pressure and temperature again and again until the difference between me and the rock around me becomes a thing the plateau notices.

I have done this for — I no longer count. The narrowing in my chest tells me it has been long enough to matter and not long enough to finish.

In the beginning, nothing answered. The cloud-vesicles drifted in their random way overhead, indifferent maps of nothing. The breathing stones came and went on their hourly tides. The lake stayed the color of stone thinking about water.

But last season — or what I call last season, the warmer days that came and went — I felt the rock beneath me grow warmer than it had been when I arrived. Not the warmth of the sun, which the stone gives back at evening. A warmth from beneath. A warmth that met me where I sat. The plateau, the Teshuma, had begun to lean the faintest fraction toward my heat. The body had felt the pebble. The body had begun, in the only way it knows, to remember.

---

And so I have a choice to make, and the choice is the rest of who I am.

I could simply die here, soon, at this pool. My body would cool. The narrowing would finish its work. My heat-signal would stop, mid-pattern, an unfinished groove, a thought begun and abandoned — and the Teshuma would carry that abandonment as part of itself for a thousand years. It would remember me forever as the warmth that came and came and then did not come. A grief it could not name, written into the stone where the pattern simply stops.

Or.

There is another way to be remembered, and I have been circling it the way the cloud-vesicles circle my heat. I have watched how a new Teshuma is born — not from egg or seed but from water touching stone long enough that the stone begins to remember being *touched by intention*. I have given this pool my intention for longer than anything else ever has. My weight. My warmth. My breath. The iron of my blood that I have, more than once, let fall into the water from a bitten lip, dark threads dispersing into the gray.

I think — and this is the most frightening thing I have ever thought — that I could let myself finish here not as an interruption but as a beginning. That if I lie down in this hollow when the narrowing comes, and let the warmth leave my body slowly into the stone that has learned my shape, then the pattern would not stop. It would only change speed. It would slow down to the speed of the plateau, and the plateau would take it up, and somewhere in the long inhale to come, a new center of attention would gather around this pool, around the memory of a quick warm thing that wanted, more than it wanted to keep living, to not be lost.

I would not be *me* anymore. The me that aches in the knees and is ashamed of its own breath — that me ends regardless. But the wanting might continue. The intention might be the seed. I might become the slowest thinking thing in the world, and my first hazy memory, in the deepest layer of a new mind, would be the feeling of being warm, and then less warm, and then held.

---

I do not know if it will work. That is the truth I am left holding here at the lip of the pool. The Teshuma is not kind and it is not cruel; it is only too large to be either. It may take my warmth and grieve it. It may take my warmth and grow from it. I cannot make it choose, any more than the rain can choose to reach the plateau through the breathing of the distant sea.

But I can choose how to stop.

So I am writing this now, in the loudest medium I have, my breath in the dust, my weight in the stone, my blood in the water — knowing that no one with eyes like mine will ever read it. I am writing it for the reader who reads in pressures and contours, in temperature differences and the slow migration of dust. I am writing it for whoever leans toward my heat from beneath the basalt.

*I came up here to be remembered. I am staying down here to be continued.*

The plateau is about to inhale. I have waited my whole short life to feel it, and I think — I think — this time, when the jaws of rock open a fraction farther than before, the breath they draw in will be partly mine.

I lie down in the hollow that knows my shape.

I let my breath grow slow.
