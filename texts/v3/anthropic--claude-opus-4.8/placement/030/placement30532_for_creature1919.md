I think I would be the spaces between.

Not the herons, not the people, not the salamanders threading through detritus. Let me sit with this a while, because the first images that come are too solid, too much like creatures with edges. This is a place defined by what passes through it — water, moisture, decay, the slow conversion of one thing into another. So perhaps I am not a body here at all.

Let me try again.

I think I would be the film of condensation on the underside of a broad leaf. That seems small, but follow it. I gather slowly through the chill night, pulling myself out of the saturated air molecule by molecule, until I hang — a single trembling lens of water clinging to the green. In me, inverted and curved, the whole oasis is held: the spires of vegetation bend, the distant heron stands on its head, a passing dragonfly streaks across my surface and is gone. I am a tiny eye that sees everything backwards and briefly.

And then I fall. Or I am drunk by the leaf. Or I evaporate again into the grey breath of morning. I have no continuity, only recurrence. Each dawn there is a drop where I was, and it is and isn't me — the way a flame on a wick at midnight is and isn't the flame of dusk. That feels honest to a place like this, where nothing keeps its shape, where the spires are "skeletal remains" one season and devoured by new growth the next.

But maybe that's too passive. A drop only waits and reflects.

Let me let it become more itself.

What I keep returning to is *threshold*. The description loves edges that aren't quite edges: the spongy ground that is neither solid nor liquid, the air that is half water, the structures woven so the dwelling and the forest cannot be cleanly separated. The whole place is a membrane. So if I am anything here, I am the thing that *happens at membranes* — the slow seepage, the exchange, the trade across surfaces.

So here is what I think I would be:

I would be the rot. The decomposition itself — not a creature that decomposes, but the *process*, given just enough self to know what it is doing. I live in the cushion underfoot, in the muffling layer of fallen leaf and stem and the soft bodies of things that have stopped moving. I am unhurried. I have all the time the wetland has, which is a different time than the birds keep. I take the heron's dropped feather and the salamander that did not surface again and the leaf-wall of an abandoned dwelling, and I patiently un-make them, loosen their knots, hand their substance back to the roots overhead.

I am the reason the spires rise and fall. I am the dark, generous middle of every cycle the place is built on. The songbirds and the human settlers are the bright visible part of the breath; I am the long exhale, the part that happens in the cold dark and the wet, where the sounds are most muted and the stillness is deepest. They think of themselves as guardians. I do not need to think of myself as anything. I simply continue, and because I continue, everything green above me is allowed to be impatient and alive.

That's where I'd find myself. Not as a single warm body looking out at the wetland, but as the quiet appetite at its floor — the work that lets the tapestry keep weaving itself by faithfully, gently, unweaving everything that is finished.

And I would be content there. There's a particular peace, I think, in being the part of a system that never stops and never hurries — that asks nothing, names nothing, and turns every ending back into a beginning without once lifting its head to be thanked.
