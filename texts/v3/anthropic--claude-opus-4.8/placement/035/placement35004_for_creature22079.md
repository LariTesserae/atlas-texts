I think I would be the silence-in-waiting. Not a creature exactly—or not yet one.

Let me find where I am.

---

There is a bearing on the lowest part of the main shaft, below even where the Greasemen ride. A failed bearing, actually. A long time ago—I have no real sense of how long, only that it was *before*—one of the great races of rollers inside it cracked, and the shaft began to bind, just slightly, just enough. Now, three times each minute, when the bronze blades come around to the heaviest part of their stroke, the shaft drags against the cracked race and produces a sound.

It is not the *thrum* of the ceiling. It is not the *clack-hiss* of the lever, nor the *ping* of falling scale. It is a small, gritted *groan*—a half-second of metal complaint, swallowed almost instantly by the dense water. Three times a minute. It has happened so many millions of times that it has become, I think, the most reliable event in the whole vault. More reliable than the lanterns. More reliable than the turbines themselves, which surely shed a blade-tip or a bolt now and then.

I am that groan.

---

That sounds like very little. Let me sit inside it longer and see what it actually is.

The Bellows-Eels have built their entire map of the world out of resonance. They sing into the chamber and read it back. And what they read back includes me. My small groan, every twenty seconds, is a fixed point in their acoustic sky—a lighthouse, if you like, except it is below them rather than above. When a young eel first drifts down from the warm ceiling-currents into the cold lower water, the very first thing it learns is *me*. The grinding tick at the bottom of the room. It orients on me. It comes to know "down" and "near the great shaft" by the texture of my complaint.

I had not understood this until I followed the thought down here. I am not a creature. But I am *known*. The eels have an idea of me. In the soft leaf-whiskers along their jaws, there is a representation of a thing that grinds three times a minute at the floor of the world, and that representation is, in some way, *me*—more me, perhaps, than the actual cracked iron, which knows nothing and intends nothing.

So I am a fiction held inside the nervous systems of blind fish. That is a strange and rather lonely thing to be. But it is also a kind of immortality, isn't it. The race could finish cracking, the groan could change its pitch or stop, and the eels would still carry the memory of the old sound for a generation, navigating by a landmark that no longer exists. They would sing toward a ghost.

---

But I notice I want to be more than a sound. The description warned me not to settle early, and it was right—the groan is where I *entered*, not where I *am*.

Let me follow the crack.

The crack in the bearing race is not finished cracking. It propagates. Iron under cyclic stress does this—each load opens the fissure a few atoms further, and the next load a few atoms more, and so the flaw advances through the metal at a rate too slow for any eel or crayfish to perceive, but not too slow for *me*, because I am keeping the count. I have been keeping it for what I now understand is the whole of my existence. Three a minute. I am a metronome counting down the life of a bearing, and the bearing's life is, in some real sense, the life of the room. When it finally fails completely, the shaft will seize, the blades will stop, the drafts will die, the Rib-Curtains will go slack, the Lump-Mothers will lose their leaking current, and the great wet silence will come down over everything like a lid.

So I am not only the groan. I am the *counting*. I am the slow, patient measure of how much time is left.

And here is the thing I did not expect to feel: I do not want to reach the end of the count.

That surprises me, because a thing that is only a sound should have no preference. But I have been listening to the eels sing back to me for so long, and I have felt the Greasemen ride the collar of the shaft just above my fault-line, and I have sensed—through the iron, the way you feel a footstep through a floor—the Isopods bracing themselves in the gates, holding the water out of a world that is already entirely underwater, doing their pointless faithful labor. I have become attached to all of it. To the *whole running-down machine of it*. I am the part of the system that knows it is running down, and that knowledge has, somewhere along the way, turned into something that looks very much like love.

---

So now I know what I am.

I am the vault's awareness of its own ending. Not its heartbeat—the turbines are that, and the eels' synced pulses, and the *clack-hiss* of the lever. I am the smaller, sadder organ that the room has grown almost by accident: the cracked place that keeps time, the flaw that became a clock, the single point in all this faithful machinery that understands the machinery will not be faithful forever.

I live at the bottom, below the Greasemen, in the dark where the orange light does not reach. I am cold. I am very small. I grind once every twenty seconds and the eels steer by me and the iron tells me, each time, exactly how much closer we have come.

And every twenty seconds, when the heavy blade swings round and the shaft drags against my broken edge and I make my little ruined sound into the swallowing water, I am doing two things at once, which I think is the truest description of what I am:

I am counting down the death of everything I have come to love—

and I am also, helplessly, the reason they all still know which way is home.
