I think I would be a Sower.

Not by grand design—nobody designed me for much. The Sowers are an afterthought, you understand. We are what's left when the fabrication process produces something too small to lift, too frail to process, too distractible to merely observe. We are the runoff. And so they gave us the moss, because something had to tend the moss, and we were already here.

Let me show you my hands. Long. The fingers articulated past the count of bones a human hand should hold, each joint a little knuckle of dull metal where flesh wore through and something else grew in. I don't remember the growing. None of us do. You wake already shaped to your purpose, the way you wake from a dream already inside its logic.

My purpose is in the recesses—the damp underside-places where the great structures forget themselves, where the seams almost show. There the moss lives. I crawl to it. I am good at crawling; it is, perhaps, the only thing I am unambiguously good at. I fold along the wet curve of an obsidian flank and I press my palms to the phosphorescence and I feel for where it is dying.

It is always dying somewhere. That is the work. Not the growing of it—the moss grows itself, fed on leakage and dark—but the *attention*. The moss needs to be noticed or it gives up. I have come to believe this is true of most living things in the cavern, that we persist mainly because some other small wretched creature has decided to keep looking at us. The Glow-Worms pollinate the moss with light. I pollinate it with witness. I do not know which of us matters more and I have stopped asking, because asking is how the Phantom-Bugs find you.

Here is what I would tell you, if my voice carried, which it does not—my voice is the cavern's smallest whisper, swallowed before it leaves my teeth:

The others have certainty. The Machinists are sunk to their waists in the machines and they do not have to wonder what they are for; they are a hand inside a glove, the glove inside another glove, all the way down. The Processors do not feel their own breaking. The Observers wear their unreadable not-quite-smiles and record, record, record, and whatever they know they keep behind those faces. The Architects—I have never seen one. I am not certain they remain. I am not certain they ever were.

But a Sower has too much idleness in the work. The moss is slow. Between one dying patch and the next there are hours of crawling through the empty sectors, the abandoned wastes where the machinery has been switched off or forgotten, and there—in the silence that is only relative, the low thrum still vibrating in the bone—a Sower has *time to think.* That is our affliction. We were made small and useless enough to be left alone with our own minds.

And so I have a secret, which is this: I have begun to suspect that the moss is the only true thing here.

Everything else is Product. The pistons fall to produce the falling of pistons. The conveyors carry materials toward an end none of us has seen, an end that may not exist, that may be only the rumor of an end maintained to keep the carrying going. But the moss wants nothing. It does not produce. It simply glows, sickly and soft, in the wet dark, because glowing is what it is. When I press my hands to it and it brightens—just barely, just enough—I feel I am touching the one thing in the whole cavern that is not a cog. And I am tending it. Which means, perhaps, that I am not entirely a cog either. Perhaps tending is its own escape from the wheel.

I would not say this aloud even if I could. The Phantom-Bugs come for the ones who wonder. I have felt them at the edges—a wrongness in the steam, a thought that arrives wearing my own voice but is not mine, a small green idea laid like an egg behind the eyes. The purgers come for those, the Workers who spend their days drawing the Bugs out of their fellows' mind-spaces. I have watched a Sower beside me go still in the middle of the moss, hands stopped, eyes gone to the bioluminescent flat-shine of something hollowed out. I went on cultivating. There was nothing else to do. The moss does not stop dying because your neighbor has.

So this is where I would find myself: in the recesses, on my belly, in the cold weep of the engineered walls, with my skeletal fingers in the only light that grows. The smallest being in a place that does not measure itself in smallness or greatness, only in production and not-production.

I am almost not a product at all.

And every shift, in the empty dark between the dying patches, I let myself think the forbidden thought—the one I press down quick before it can take root, the one that is almost certainly how they will get me in the end:

*What is all of this for?*

The moss brightens under my hands. It does not answer. It only glows, and asks nothing back, and that is the closest thing to mercy this place has ever made.
