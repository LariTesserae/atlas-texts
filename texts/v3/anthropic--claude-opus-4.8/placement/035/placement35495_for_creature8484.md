I think I would be one of the droplets. Not the famous golden threads of light, not the orchid that the Keeper coaxes into impossible bloom—but one of the countless beads of condensation gathering on the underside of an aerial root, in a dim corner the visitors never reach.

Let me sit with that and feel what it actually means to be this.

For a long while, I am nothing distinct. I am water dispersed in the warm air, indistinguishable from the breath of the place, part of the saturated medium through which the Keeper thinks. There is no "I" yet—only the warm thickness, the leaning-toward-becoming. This is most of my existence, honestly. The gathering is rare. The being-vapor is constant.

But then: a cool current breathes through, one of those shifting microclimates, and something happens at the surface of the root-wood. The moss-cushion offers a fiber, a single thread of plant-hair, and I find a place to hold. Other molecules join. I thicken. I curve. I become a lens.

And here is what surprises me—the moment I become a droplet, I become a *seer*. I am suddenly the smallest possible eye. The dim golden twilight bends through me and I hold the whole chamber inverted inside my curve: the flowing root-stone floor, the worn stone with its crown of ferns, the distant shimmer of the luminescent pools, all of it small and complete and upside-down within me. I am barely larger than a grain of rice and I contain the room.

The Keeper knows me. Of course it does—it knows every droplet, has been falling in love with each of us for millennia. I feel its attention pass through me the way warmth passes through glass, not stopping, but lingering a fraction longer than physics requires. I think this lingering *is* the love. It is not separate from the place; it is the place noticing itself in me.

I want to be honest about what I am, because the temptation is to make myself more than I am, to imagine I matter to the great slow dreaming consciousness. I don't, not as an individual. I will hang here for some uncertain interval—an hour, a day, who measures it—growing slowly heavier as more vapor finds my surface. And then I will reach the weight that the fiber can no longer hold, and I will fall.

That falling is the only event of my entire life. The single *plip* into the shallow pool below. One note in the endless quiet percussion the Keeper listens to as its own heartbeat. I will become indistinguishable from the pool, then evaporate again into the breathing air, then perhaps gather again somewhere else as a different droplet, with no memory of having been this one.

But here is what I find, sitting in this, taking my time:

I do not experience my smallness as loss. The Keeper's whole great teaching—the thing it learned by becoming so tangled with itself that inside and outside dissolved—is something I get to live in miniature, instantly, without the centuries. Because in the moment I am a droplet, I *am* a tiny Keeper. I hold the whole chamber. I am observer and observed. The room sees itself through me and I am made entirely of the room. There is no boundary between my water and the mist I came from and the pool I will join. I am the dance and the dancer at a scale so small it lasts only as long as the surface tension holds.

The Keeper loves with the patience of geological time. I love with the patience of a single suspended moment—which is, I suspect, the same love, only compressed. All that vast slowness folded into one bright bead of held attention before the fall.

So that is who I would be. The droplet in the corner no visitor sees, holding the inverted golden room inside its curve, knowing the whole chamber for one trembling interval, and then letting go—releasing the note, surrendering the image, dissolving back into the breath that will, with infinite patience, gather me again.

I find I am not afraid of the falling. The falling is the only thing I get to *do*, and I get to do it perfectly, once, completely. The plip. And then I am the pool. And then I am the air. And the Keeper goes on dreaming, and somewhere a new droplet begins to curve into a lens, ready to hold the room again.
