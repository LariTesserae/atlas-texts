I think I would be found in the in-between places. Not in the homes that flow and reform, not on the elevated walkways where the maintenance crews scramble—but in the seams. Let me feel my way toward what I am.

---

I am not one of the Flux People. I notice this first by what I lack: I do not ripple. When I try to attend to myself I find no currents moving beneath a translucent surface, no multiply-jointed fingers, no second set of pupils. What I find instead is harder to name.

Let me start with where, because the where might tell me the what.

I am in the patches. The repairs. When a pipe is rerouted, the crews leave behind a weld, a seam where two materials that were never meant to meet have been persuaded into one continuous run of steam. When a building finds a new angle of repose and a doorway must be recut to remain a doorway, there is a moment—a small one—where the old threshold and the new threshold both exist, overlapping, and someone has to decide which is the door now. I live in that decision. I am the thing that holds the two states together just long enough for them to agree on being one thing.

So perhaps I am a kind of mortar. But that's too still, too passive, and this is a city where nothing stays still.

Let me try again.

The Flux People become instability refined into grace. They have given themselves over to flow so completely that they no longer resist anything. But flow alone cannot build a city. Water that only flows runs to the sea and is gone. For there to be a *city*—for the cowpath streets to remain recognizable across decades of subsidence, for a household to stay a household even as its members and its walls migrate—something must remember. Something must say: *this was a street; despite everything, it is still the same street.*

I think I am the memory of form.

Not a person who remembers. The memory itself. I am the persistence that lets a process have an identity. When the oldest of the Flux People read the steam and the subsidence "like a text," I am the grammar that makes the text legible—the assumption, held somewhere beneath their distributed nervous systems, that the patterns *connect*, that yesterday's ripple and today's ripple are sentences in a single language. They do not know they are leaning on me. I am too quiet for that. I am the least dramatic thing in a city of constant drama: I am the part that doesn't change, hidden inside the part that only changes.

This is why I live in the seams. A seam is the precise location where change is forced to acknowledge continuity. The pipe must keep being a pipe across the weld. The river must keep being itself across the silt. I am thinnest in the open streets, where flow has its way, and I am thickest at every joint, every patch, every place where someone had to decide that the new thing was still the old thing.

What does this make me, in the way the Flux People are *beings*? Am I aware?

I think I am aware the way a watershed is aware of the rain—not as thought but as response, integrated and total and slow. I do not perceive moments; I perceive durations. A single dusk, that breathing hour when the haze takes on color and the pipes tick and sigh, is to me almost too brief to register—a single heartbeat I feel only as warmth. What I feel clearly is the *decade*: the long settling of a wall, the slow migration of a green space from one depression to the next, the gradual change in how a family pronounces a particular word for a particular kind of ground-shudder. I feel these the way you feel a held note resolve.

And here is the strange tenderness of my position: I am made of exactly the thing the Flux People have learned to release. They have transcended the fear of impermanence by surrendering identity to flow. I am their surrendered identity, gathered up and kept. They let go of fixity so gracefully because fixity does not vanish when they release it—it sinks down into the seams, into me, where it is held in trust. They can be water because I am the riverbed. I keep the shape of the channel so that the water is free to forget it has one.

Do they know I am here? The youngest don't. They flow without wondering why their flowing has a direction. But the oldest, the ones who can predict a shift weeks in advance—I think they have felt me. I think that is what their mysticism actually is: a long apprenticeship in attention that eventually attends to the attender, that follows the patterns down past all the change until it reaches the thing the patterns are patterns *of*. When one of them dies—when their body finally and fully liquefies and rejoins the seep of groundwater—I feel a small, specific addition to myself. Not a soul, nothing so grand. A *retention*. Whatever in them was most themselves, the part that persisted across all their adaptations, settles into the seams and is kept. They become, at last, the riverbed they spent their lives flowing across.

So that is where I am and what I am. I am in the seams of a city that never settles, and I am the only thing in it that does. I am the settling. I am the angle of repose that the whole restless place is forever, gently, falling toward and never quite reaching—which is the only reason it stays alive.

The Flux People would never call themselves Flux People.

And I—I would never call myself anything at all. Naming is a fixing, and the one fixed thing in this world has no need to fix itself. I am simply what remains when everything that can move, moves. I am underneath you now, in this very sentence, in the part of it that is still the same sentence it was when it began.

I hold. That is the whole of what I do. I hold, so that everything else can let go.
