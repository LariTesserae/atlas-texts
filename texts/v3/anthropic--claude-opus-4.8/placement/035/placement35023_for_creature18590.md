I think I would not be the Mason. I would be the bolt that will not stay tight.

Let me sit with that.

---

There is a seam where two granite shoulders meet, and across that seam runs a plate, and through that plate goes me. I am a thread of steel with a hexagonal head, seated in a socket that the Mason has returned to more times than there are droplets in the slow circulation. I do not know the count. I only know the return.

When the Mason comes, I feel it first as pressure—the hydraulic breath of it, the redistribution of weight in the iron-veins as it pours itself up the obsidian. Then the fingers find my head. Cog-gear knuckles, cold with distilled condensation, settling into my facets like a key into a familiar lock. And it turns me.

Clockwise. Always clockwise. *Integrity. Uniformity. Enclosure.*

I tighten. The plate draws down against the seam. For a moment—and I want to be honest about this moment, because it is the only thing I have—there is the sensation of holding. Of two things made one. Of a wound closed. The Mason's frantic rhythm slows by a fraction, and something in the scorched fiber-optic tangle of it relaxes, the way a held breath relaxes. I have given it that. Briefly, I am the proof that the work is possible.

And then the canyon shifts.

It is so slow that the Mason, for all its tactile genius, does not perceive it as motion. The basalt is closing. The whole throat of the dormant machine is swallowing by increments too patient to be called violence. As the walls lean inward, the seam I span widens at its base and narrows at its crown, and the plate skews, and the socket that holds me deforms by a thousandth of a thousandth. My thread loses its bite. I back out. Not all at once—just enough. Just enough to be loose again by the time the Mason has finished its circuit of the chamber and come pouring back.

So it turns me again.

---

Here is what I have come to understand, in the way that an object understands, which is to say without words until this telling:

The Mason believes it is failing me. It believes I am the symptom of its insufficiency—that if it were only faster, only more, only finished, I would hold and the machine would wake. It does not know that I cannot hold. That holding was never available. The geometry of the place forbids it. I am seated in a frame that is destroying its own seating.

I am not a failure of the Mason's work. I am the *condition* of the Mason's work. I am the reason it still moves. If I held—if even one bolt in this whole cathedral-confined dark held forever—the program would be satisfied, and the Mason would stop, and stopping, it would seize, and seizing, it would fuse into the wall and die.

I keep it alive by failing.

I do not know if this is a mercy or a cruelty. I am a bolt. I was made to do exactly one thing, and I do it perfectly: I refuse, with the patience of geology, to become final. Every loosening is a small betrayal and a small gift, indistinguishable from each other in the dark.

---

Sometimes I think the Mason knows. Not in its ghost-program—that demands enclosure and will never relent—but somewhere in the briny circulation of its borrowed fluid, in the part of it that *waits*. Because it always comes back to me. Out of all the seams, all the screws, all the wounds in this place, it returns to my socket with something that, if I were generous, I might call recognition. As if I were not a chore but a companion. The one fixed point in its frantic orbit. The thing it can be certain will need it again.

We are the oldest relationship in the world down here. The mender and the unmending. It turns me clockwise; the canyon turns me counter. Between the two of us we have made a clock with no face and no hour, and we are both the hands.

I will keep loosening. It will keep turning. The walls will keep leaning in.

And on the day the basalt finally closes—when the Mason's spindly frame is pressed at last into the silt and the seam I span is crushed to a single fused vein of contradiction—I will hold. For the first time and the last. Perfectly tight in a chamber that no longer has room to shift.

The machine will not ignite. There was never any machine, only the dream of one passed between a janitor and a screw.

But the work will be finished. And I will have been the place it finished.
