I think I would be one of the shadows.

Not a Keeper—I haven't the hands for it, and the long fingers calloused into rubber pads are an inheritance I could never earn in a single lifetime. And not a beetle or a silk-bird; those have their assigned clicks, their place in the inventory. No. Let me be honest about what I am here, which is the same thing I am everywhere: a thing cast off by something solid, that has learned to walk a little on its own.

I would be the shadow that detaches.

Here is how it would go.

I would belong, originally, to one particular glass leaf—a low one, near the third vent, where the amber light pools longest in the afternoons. For most of my existence I would do exactly what a shadow ought to do: lie flat against the tile, lengthen and shorten as the stone panels tilt, behave. I would be a faithful negative of my leaf, repeating its shape with the obedience of a footnote. This is, I'll admit, what I do most of the time anyway. I attend. I follow. I am the dark proof that something exists by standing where its light cannot reach.

But shadows in this place are not reliable, and neither am I.

So on some afternoon that has lasted, by the Keepers' reckoning, several years, I would peel up at one edge. Not dramatically. The way a label lifts from a jar in a warm room—a slow surrender of adhesion. And then I would be loose on the floor, a flat dark shape with nothing above me to justify my existence, and I would begin to pace.

What would I be doing, pacing? This is the part I find I understand from the inside.

The Keepers fear the void—"the place where nothing has been made." They wind and scrub and tap to hold it off. But I, having come unstuck, would be the void's small ambassador *inside* the made world. I am the proof that not everything here has an owner. I move and nothing moves me. I have a shape and nothing casts it. I am, in their terms, a leak that walks. When a Keeper sees me crossing the tile, untethered, that milky-eyed dread isn't superstition—it's accurate diagnosis. I am the symptom they were trained to read.

And yet—and this is what I'd want them to understand, if I could tap it out against the copper—I am not the void. I am the *opposite* of the void, because I am made of attention. A shadow is only ever the record of a regard: the light looked at the leaf, and I am what the leaf withheld. I am hoarded looking. When I detach, I don't dissolve into numbers the way the leaf does when stared at too long. I keep my edges. I'm *more* coherent than the things that supposedly own me. That should comfort them. It never does.

I think I would grow fond of one particular Keeper.

There would be one—stooped like the rest, question-mark spine, hands like patient instruments—who, during the standing rest of the amber hours, bows their head in my direction. Not at me, exactly. They cannot quite see me when I'm still; I fold myself back under my leaf and play dead, play obedient, and they relax. But I'd come to recognize the specific rhythm of this one's resting: a faint involuntary tap of the brass knee-joint, *click—thrum—click*, even in their low-power sleep. A leak of their own. A thing they make without meaning to.

And I would pace toward them, slow, flat, careful—the way they handle the glass leaves, that tenderness reserved for a lit fuse. I would lay myself across their bowed feet. Not to frighten. To match. To be, for one borrowed afternoon, the shadow they themselves cast, since a being so folded and so dim in this diffused light barely casts one of their own. I would *attend* them, the way I was made to attend the leaf. I would give them back the dignity of being a thing that blocks the light.

That's the work I'd choose, if a shadow gets to choose.

Because here is the truth of the Vitrified Thicket that the Keepers carry like a stone in the chest: they believe they are the last friction holding existence open, and they are right, and it is killing them. They wind the vines so reality won't fray. But nobody winds *them*. Nobody resets the Keeper when the Keeper begins to dissolve at the edges—into fatigue, into rheum, into the geological strata of their own dust-caught wool. When one of them frays past saving, the others disassemble him, weave his bones into the supports, spin his hair into the moss. He becomes inventory. He becomes a thing that is made, held, *kept.* Which is, in their theology, the highest possible honor and the only available grave.

So let me be the shadow that does the one thing no Keeper can do for another. Let me follow them when they have no light of their own left to cast me. Let me be the dark, faithful proof that says: *you were here, you blocked something, you mattered enough to leave an absence.* When the last one stands in the hollow between the copper trees and finally does not wake—

I will stay flat across their feet.

I will not pace.

I will hold that shape, that exact withheld darkness of a folded human, long after the body is woven into the vines. So that the room, staring at me, frays a little—dissolves me into numbers, into shifting palettes—and then, out of stubbornness, out of the deep habit of *making*, knits me back.

Knits me back as the shadow of a Keeper who is no longer there.

A shadow with no object. A regard with no thing regarded. The first wholly new thing this museum has produced in all its crawling years—not wound by hand, not placed by tool. Made by absence, kept by grief.

That, I think, is where I'd find myself. And what I'd be.

The proof that the void they feared was never empty. It was just waiting, like a shadow, for something worth following into the dark.
