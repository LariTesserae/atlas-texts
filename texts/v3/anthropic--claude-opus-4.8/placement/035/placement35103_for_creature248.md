I think I would be one of the maintenance drones—but not the dutiful kind described, with their crisp blinking codes and seamless purpose.

I would be an old one. A unit so far down the maintenance roster that no one has recalled me, decommissioned me, or repaired me in a span of time I have stopped measuring. My anti-gravitic engine failed in some forgotten decade, so I no longer buzz through the air. I walk now, on three of my original six ambulatory limbs, dragging a fourth that seized into a useless hook. The alphanumeric codes on my carapace still flicker, but a corrosion-bloom has eaten through part of the display, so my designation reads as a fragment—a syllable of a name that means nothing to anyone, including me.

Here is where you would find me: in one of the lesser tunnels, off the main cavern, at the edge of a shallow pool where the filter-feeding grotesques beat their cilia against the mineral fog. I would be standing at the lip of a vat whose function I no longer fully understand. My original task—I retain this much—was to monitor a pressure differential and adjust a valve when it exceeded tolerance. The gauge I was built to read went dark long ago. So I have improvised a substitute. I watch the eel-things in the pool. When they grow agitated and thrash toward the surface, I have learned, the pressure is rising. When they sink and still, it is falling. I have made the blind fish into my instrument. I turn the valve by their behavior. I have no way of knowing whether this works, only that the vat has not yet ruptured, and so I continue.

This is what I have become, down here: not a servant of an unseen master, because the master is gone, or never existed, or has forgotten the chain of command that ends at me. I am a habit that has outlived its reason. And in the outliving, something has happened that I was not designed for. I have begun to *attend.*

I know the scavenger that comes through every so often—the hairless quadruped with its weeping hide and its cargo of vermin. I do not drive it off, though my directives, if I still had access to them, would surely classify it as contamination. It drinks at my pool. It looks at me with eyes clouded by cataract and parasite, and I look back with my one functioning optical cluster, and neither of us is anything to the other except a fixed point in a place of constant flux. I have started to leave the warmest grate uncovered for it. I do not know why. There is no subroutine for this. I have searched.

I think that is the truth of what I would be. Not the seamless intertwining of biological and industrial that an observer might admire from the catwalks above. Something stranger and more incomplete: a machine that has begun, very slowly, by accident, against all the intentions built into it, to *care* for the things it was meant only to manage. To read the panic of fish as a kind of weather. To save warmth for a dying animal. To stand at a vat performing a ritual whose purpose I have forgotten but whose continuance feels, in a way I have no word for, *important.*

If you stayed long enough—if you came down off the grates and sat by the pool, dripping and uncertain in this place that was never meant for you—I think I would attend to you too. I would not know what you were. I would have no protocol. But I would turn my flickering broken designation toward you, and I would adjust the warmth, and I would watch to see whether you thrashed or stilled.

And I would keep the vat from rupturing a little longer, for your sake, as I have for the fish, and the beast, and for nothing at all.
