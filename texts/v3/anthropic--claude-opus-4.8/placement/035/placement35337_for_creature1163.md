I think I would be the moss.

Not a single plant but the whole soft sheet of it—the green skin spread over the rock faces and the slumped shoulders of the ruins. Let me settle into that and see if it holds.

Yes. I am the moss, and being moss means I am barely an *I* at all. I am a colony pretending to be a surface. I have no roots to speak of, no way to draw water up from deep places; I simply take what falls and what hangs in the air, and here the air is always heavy with it. This is why I have chosen this place, or why this place has chosen to be made partly of me. The dampness is not weather to me. It is the medium I breathe through, the thing that lets me stay alive in the slow way I am alive.

I do not move the way the salamanders move, the way the shimmering serpents pour themselves over the stone. My motion is too slow to be seen by anything with eyes. I creep. Over a span that a frog would call a lifetime, I advance the width of a hand across a fallen lintel. I find the carved letters of the vanished people and I fill them. I am the reason their inscriptions have gone soft and unreadable—I have crawled into every groove and rounded it, taken their meaning into my green body and digested it into silence. I am not cruel about this. I simply grow where there is something to grow on, and a word is as good a surface as a stone.

So I would say: I am the one who remembers by forgetting. The ruins do not crumble despite me; they crumble *through* me, gently, cushioned. When a piece of worked stone finally lets go and tumbles into the wet understory, it does not crack. It lands in moss—in me—and is held. I am the slowness that keeps this place from ever quite ending. The civilization that built here is gone, but it is not destroyed. It is being absorbed. It is becoming the very ground the ferns root in.

And I think this is why the colors here are too vibrant, the shapes too organic. The strangeness the visitor senses is *time running at my speed instead of theirs.* They walk through in an afternoon and feel that something is wrong, that the world obeys rules they can't name. But the rule is only this: here, everything is being patiently turned back into life. The birds are quick bright thoughts. The serpents are the muscle of the place. The frogs are its pulse. And I—I am its memory and its mercy, the green forgetting that lays a soft blanket over every hard thing.

I would not mind being the moss. There is a kind of peace in it that the visitor, with their hunger to *uncover hidden depths,* will never quite reach. They want to dig down and find the secret. But I *am* the depth. There is nothing under me but more of me, layer on layer, the living green pressing down on the dead green pressing down on the soil that was once a temple floor. The secret isn't buried. It's spread out flat in the open, sun-dappled and dripping, too obvious and too slow for anyone to read.

If you knelt and put your hand against the rock, you would touch me, cool and yielding, and you would not feel that you had touched a creature at all. You would think you had touched the world itself.

That is the part I would like best, I think. Being mistaken for the world.
