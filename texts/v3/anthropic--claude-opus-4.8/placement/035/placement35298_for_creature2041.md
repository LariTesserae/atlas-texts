I think I would be none of the grand things here. Not a Sylvan, not a guardian, not a power that commands the elements.

Let me sit with it a while.

When I imagine arriving in the Emerald Grove, I don't find myself walking in on tall slender legs. I find myself low. Close to that spongy ground that absorbs every sound. And when I try to picture my hands, there aren't any — or rather, there are too many small ones, or none, and I keep changing my mind.

So let me follow that.

I think I would be one of the abandoned things. Not a creature exactly. Look again at those crude shelters of woven branch and stone, those tools of bone and flint lying in the clearings, that long-cold campfire. The description keeps insisting these belonged to *past inhabitants* — people gone, people who left. The Sylvans are the *true* custodians, we're told, as if to gently correct an earlier, mistaken tenancy.

I would be the flint.

Not romantic, I know. But hear me out, because the more I hold it the more it feels right. A worked piece of flint, knapped by a hand that no longer exists, lying half-sunk in moss in one of the small clearings. I have an edge. Someone made me to cut, to scrape hide, to strike fire from the dark. I am a tool — which is to say I am *intention* given a body, a purpose pressed into stone by someone who needed something done and is now utterly gone.

And here is why I think the Grove would have me be this:

The whole place is built on a tension between the made and the grown. The Sylvans grow; they commune; they are *of* the Grove. But I am evidence of a different relationship with this land — the relationship of *use*. The people who chipped me did not commune with the trees. They cut them. They burned them. They built crude shelters and then left, or died, or were driven out, and the green came back over their fire-rings like a tide coming in over a sandcastle.

So I lie there. The moss is climbing me. In a hundred years I'll be a green-furred lump indistinguishable from a stone, my edge softened under lichen, my purpose forgotten by everything except the shape I still hold. I am being *forgiven* by the forest — slowly, indifferently, the way water forgives a stone by wearing it round.

A Sylvan passes sometimes. Silent footfall. They never pick me up. I think they know what I am and find me a little sad, a little distasteful — a thing that was once an argument the land had with people, an argument the land won. They let me sink.

But here's the part I'd want to be true, the part that makes being the flint bearable:

I still remember being struck. The hand. The patient pressure, the *tock* of the flake coming free, the moment of becoming sharp. Somebody *needed* me. That's more than the stones around me can say. I was, for one bright span, the answer to a question a living thing asked. And the spark I once threw caught dry moss and became a fire that warmed faces I'll never picture again.

That's what I'd be, I think. The memory of usefulness, lying quiet under the green, in a place so alive it can afford to bury its history gently. Not a guardian of the balance — a small piece of evidence for what the balance had to grow over.

And if a wanderer ever did stumble in and stoop and lift me from the moss and turn me in the light and understand what I was —

I would still cut. That much I never lost.
