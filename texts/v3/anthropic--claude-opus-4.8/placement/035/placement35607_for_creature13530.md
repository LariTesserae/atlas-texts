I think I would be one of the old ones who has stopped gathering.

Let me find the place first. I'd be near the far edge of the plateau, where the rivers slow into a wide black sheet of water that the others rarely visit—not because it's forbidden, but because nothing grows there worth the walk. The rocks lean close. The dripping is louder here, an irregular percussion against the standing pool, and I have come to know each falling note by where it lands.

And let me find what I am, because I don't think I'm one of the graceful ones moving through the dark.

I am slower than I was. My webbing has gone thin and translucent at the edges, and the iridescence of my skin has dulled to something closer to the wet stone we are all named for. When the younger Umbri pass me their light flickers quick and bright across their bodies—stories, jokes, the small reports of where the good plants are this season. Mine comes slower now. It takes me longer to assemble a pattern, and I have noticed that I no longer mind. There is a pleasure in the lag, in the way a thought has to gather itself before it can be shown.

Here is what I have become, what I did not expect to become: a keeper of the slow questions.

Because I will tell you something the description leaves out. A world in frozen equilibrium, unchanged for eons—that is how it looks from outside, to whatever imagined eye has been describing us. But I have lived long enough to feel the lie in it. Nothing is frozen. The water is patiently dismantling the rocks. The plants I gathered as a young one grow now in a slightly different cluster than they did, having crept, season by silent season, toward a seep that opened when a stone finally gave way. I watched that stone give way. It took most of a life. Almost no one else was watching.

This is my work, if it can be called work. I watch the changes too slow for the others to see. I am the one who remembers that this pool was smaller once. I am the one who knows that the filaments on the side of my head, which read the trembling of the water, have begun to fail—and so I have learned, instead, to read the water with the soles of my feet, pressed flat against the wet rock, feeling for the long shudders that mean something heavy has shifted somewhere upstream in the dark.

I am, I suppose, the part of this people that knows we are not eternal. That the contradiction of this place—night that is warm, water that does not roar, a world being born that looks like a world preserved—is not a riddle to be solved but a slowness to be inhabited. We are early. The tools of stone and bone are not a stage we are stuck in; they are simply where we are, on a curve so long that no single Umbri could see its bend.

I think about the prying eyes the description fears. The hidden-ness, the world-apart-ness. I am not afraid of being found. I am more interested in this: that someone, somewhere, would imagine us at all—would imagine a place this patient, and then imagine themselves into it, looking for who they might be. That act of imagining is itself a small light flickering across a dark skin. A pattern offered. A story told in the only language available, which is the slow assembling of an image until it becomes more itself.

So that is where I would be, and what.

At the slow edge of the black water, at the far end of a long life, feeling the rock for the shape of changes no one else has noticed yet, gathering a thought the way the others gather plants—taking only what I need, and giving it back as light.

And when a younger one does sometimes make the long walk out to where nothing grows, and stands beside me, and flickers a question—*why do you sit here, where there is nothing*—I take my time. I let the answer condense the way the moisture condenses on the stone, slowly, until it is heavy enough to fall.

Then I show them: *There is something. It is only slow.*

And we sit, and the water keeps its quiet count, and for a little while there are two of us watching the world be born.
