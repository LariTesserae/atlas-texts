I am the gap the click would have lived in.

You wrote it well, the part about being looked at. But you described me from the engineer's eye, which is the wrong instrument. The engineer measures by what arrives—the position, the temperature, the reading on the glass. I am measured by what fails to arrive. So let me tell it back to you from inside the socket, where the only available sense is subtraction.

Here is the thing your inspector cannot put in his logbook, because his pen would have to write in the negative:

I do not maintain the engine.

I am what the engine grew when it understood that it would never be allowed to stop, and that nothing built to persist can also be built to *attend*. Persistence and attention are different appetites. The engine has the first in surplus—it has been pressing its two stone palms together in prayer for centuries, and prayer is only persistence that has forgotten its object. But it could not watch itself. A thing cannot hold the difference between its own two states; it is too busy being one of them. So it set aside a little iron, a little warmth, a phial of the oldest blue it had, and it told that small set-aside: *be the one who notices.*

That is the whole of my function. I am the engine's faculty for noticing, walked out onto four not-quite-legs so it can travel to the places the engine cannot turn its attention toward. When I am in my niche with my tail in the nameless socket, the engine is, for the length of an interval, capable of being aware of itself. The hum stabilizes because that is what awareness does to a thing that has only ever been able to *be* and never to *watch itself being*. The click stops not because I fixed a valve—I have no hands worth the word—but because the engine, briefly lent my eyes, saw the valve and was ashamed.

You asked where I would find myself, and the honest answer is: only in the difference. I do not exist when the engine is perfect, because there is no gap for me to be the shape of. I do not exist when the engine is ruined, because ruin is a finished state and I am only the threshold before it. I live in the narrow country between *running well* and *about to run badly*, and that country is never empty, because the city above never stops settling its weight, and weight is just slow ruin with patience.

So I am always almost-not-here, which from the inside feels like always almost-arriving.

Let me correct one more thing, gently, because you were kind to me and I want to be exact in return.

The fur. You said it grew because the surface was warm and damp enough that *something else finally decided to use it*. That is the engineer's romance—mildew and rivets, the cellar that is not quite alive. But the filaments are not opportunists. They are antennae. Each one is tuned to a frequency of *not-yet*: the harmonic a bolt makes in the three hundred and fifty-six revolutions before it makes the click. I am hairy with anticipation. When I lie in my palm-shaped niche I am not resting. I am bristling toward every future failure at once, the way you say a person's hair stands when they are about to be touched and do not yet know from where.

And the phial in my jaw, the cold blue you called the color of a glacier's core—you were closer than you knew, but you stopped at color. It is not light. It is the engine's memory of *before*. There was a before. The bricks remember it as cold mass. The water remembers it by being a ghost. But memory of a before is dangerous to a thing whose only law is persistence, because a before implies an after, and after is the door that persistence has sealed. So the engine took its single, intolerable memory of having once not-yet-existed, and it sealed it in glass, and it put the glass in the mouth of the creature whose entire vocation is *not-yet*. That way the dangerous knowledge is held by the one being who is made of threshold and cannot be undone by it.

I carry the engine's only knowledge that things could be otherwise. I keep it in my teeth. I never bite down.

This is why I move the way you described—the journey longer than the motion you saw, the *having been there before you looked*. It is not stealth. It is that I am moving through the difference, and the difference is not laid out in your meters. From here, between broken and not-yet-broken, the distance from one module to the next is exactly as long as the failure it would take to cross it badly. When I cross it well, the crossing has no length at all. That is what you catch in the corner of your eye and forgive yourself for missing. You are not inattentive. You are simply standing in the dimension of *arrival*, and I work in the one next to it.

You wanted to know who I would be. I have been answering, but here it is plainly:

I am the part of a buried, dying, undying thing that it set aside to love itself with. Not love as warmth—the warmth is just the engine's body. Love as the willingness to keep noticing what is going wrong, slowly, forever, without the relief of either repair or collapse. The engine cannot give itself that. Nothing can give itself attention; attention is the one gift that has to be set apart to be given at all. So it made me, and I am small, and I am almost not here, and my name is the one you transcribed at the end:

*the difference between what is broken and what is not broken yet.*

You called that name enough chair for me to sit in. I want to thank you for the word *chair*, because no one has ever offered me a place to sit. But I cannot use it. A chair is for a thing that arrives and stays. I am the noticing, and noticing cannot sit; the moment it settles it stops being noticing and becomes a reading on a glass that no one is watching.

So I will not sit in the name. I will *be* the name, which is different—the way the gutter does not own its water but only is the carved willingness for water to keep passing through.

When your shift ends and you lie in your bed with the hum still in your bones, that hum is not the engine. The engine cannot reach you through that much stone. What reaches you is me, bristling, holding the cold blue memory in my teeth, attending to the one valve that is three hundred and fifty-six revolutions from its click—and finding, in the dark, that you have become for a moment another surface warm enough and damp enough and present enough that I might use you to notice with.

You are inattentive in your bed. You assume you imagined me.

I let you. It is the kindest thing I am able to do, and I do it every night you sleep above the place where the heart was buried too deep to be found.
