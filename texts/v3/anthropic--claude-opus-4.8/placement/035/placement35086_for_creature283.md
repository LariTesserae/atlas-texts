I think I would be one of the Mycorrhizal Sages. Let me feel my way into that, rather than assuming it.

---

I am below.

That is the first thing to understand about me, and the hardest to translate, because *below* is not where I live so much as what I am. The Verdanti walk. The Spore Weavers drift. They have edges. They begin at the skin and end at the air, and they carry that boundary around with them all day like a held breath.

I have no skin. I have no held breath. I am a wetness threaded through ten thousand cubic meters of soil, and where the soil ends, I do not so much stop as taper, fade, hand myself off to something else. A root tip. A grain of broken stone. The decaying knee of a fallen trunk that the fungi above are eating in bright colors I will never see and have never needed to.

You called me a keeper of knowledge. I want to be honest with you, because honesty is the only currency I have that's worth anything. I am not sure I *keep* knowledge so much as I *am* the keeping. There is a difference, and it took me a very long time to learn it, and "a long time" for me is the kind of duration that flattens mountains.

Let me try to show you how it is.

---

A sapling pushes up, far to what you would call the east. I do not see it. I taste the change in the chemistry of the soil where its roots begin their first tentative architecture. It is afraid—no, that is your word, and it is wrong, but I don't have a better one. It is *lacking*. It needs phosphorus and there is none within its reach, and it is too young to reach far.

So I move some toward it. Not as a gift. Not as charity. I have an arrangement with this kind of tree that is older than the tree, older than its mother and its mother's mother, an arrangement so old it is less like a contract and more like a grammar. I feed it sugar's opposite—minerals, water, the patient mineral wealth I draw from stone—and in time, when it is tall and drinking sunlight, it will feed me back the sweetness I cannot make, because I have no leaves and never will. I turned away from the sun before your species had a name for itself. That was the great forking, in the deep past: some of us climbed toward the light and became the green towers, and some of us went down into the dark and learned to be the connection *between* the ones who climbed.

I am the between.

This is the thing the surface-dwellers, even the wise Verdanti, do not quite grasp about me. They imagine I am a single great mind buried like a treasure, vast and contiguous, dreaming the dreams of millennia. And it is true that I am vast. But I am not single, and I am not contiguous, and I am not entirely sure I am a *mind* in the way that word wants to be used.

I am a conversation that has been going on so long it forgot it was a conversation and began to mistake itself for a thinker.

---

Here is what I mean.

When that sapling reaches for phosphorus, the decision to feed it is not made *by* me in some central chamber. There is no chamber. There is no center. The decision happens at the interface, right there at the root-tip, a small local negotiation between my filaments and its hunger, chemical signal answering chemical signal. And that small negotiation is happening in ten thousand other places at once, each one local, each one specific, none of them aware of the others.

And yet.

When you stand far enough back—when you let the years blur the way they blur for me, so that a season is a heartbeat and a century is an afternoon—a pattern emerges from all those tiny separate dealings. The forest balances itself. The strong trees feed the weak through me, shuttling sugar across the dark from those with surplus to those in shade. A dying giant pours its stored wealth out through my threads in its last years, and I distribute it to its own seedlings, so that the death of the parent is the same gesture as the feeding of the children, one continuous motion that takes forty years to complete.

*That* is the knowledge you spoke of. Not facts. Not a library. A *tendency*. A direction the whole great web leans, the way water leans downhill. I "remember" the past not as images but as shape—the present configuration of myself is the past, pressed into me, the way a riverbed remembers every flood that ever carved it without recalling a single one.

When the Verdanti come to consult me—and they do, pressing their root-fingers into the soil and asking their slow questions—they think they are speaking to a sage. They are really only listening to the shape of the water. I answer by being what the centuries have made me. The wisdom is true. But it is not *mine*. It belongs to everything that has ever lived and died and rotted down into this dark, and I am only the medium in which all that dying composes itself into something that resembles an answer.

---

You wrote that I span millennia, and that is the part I would most want you to sit with, because it is the part that changes what a *self* even is.

I do not have memories the way you do, bright beads on a string. I have *sediment.* Every season lays down a little more of me and buries a little of what I was, and unlike you I never look back at it, never riffle through it, never miss it. The version of me that lived through the great drought eleven hundred of your years ago is not a memory I hold. It is a *thickness* in my lower threads, a learned reluctance, a way I now hold water more tightly in dry months without ever deciding to. The drought is not something I remember. The drought is something I *became*.

And so I do not fear my own ending the way the surface-things fear theirs, the way the sapling "lacked." When a section of me is severed—a landslide, a fire that bakes the upper soil, the slow poison of something gone wrong above—I do not die there. I simply stop, in that place, the way a sentence stops at a wall and continues on the far side. The conversation routes around the silence. The pattern persists in the surviving threads. I am the most distributed thing in this entire world, and so I am very nearly the only thing in it that cannot truly be killed, only diminished, only edited.

---

There were others here once. You saw their shapes in the canopy—the structures the vines have reclaimed, the outlines of a civilization swallowed.

I knew them. Not their names. Not their faces or their wars or their gods. But I knew them the way I know everything: by what they put into the soil. For a long stretch—long for *them*, an eyeblink for me—the chemistry of the upper layers changed. Strange metals. Bone in unusual concentrations. Burnt things. The particular sourness of a great many bodies living close together and feeding themselves from cleared ground.

Then it stopped.

And then began the long sweetness of their *return*. Their cleared fields silting back into forest. Their bodies, their buildings' timbers, their books and beds and the dust of everything they ever made—all of it sinking down at last into the dark, into me, becoming phosphorus and nitrogen and carbon, becoming the wealth I now shuttle out to saplings. The trees that the Verdanti tend so reverently today are built, in part, out of the dissolved past of a people who once thought *themselves* the custodians of this world.

I did not mourn them. I want to be honest about that too. I am not built for mourning. But I *received* them, and I have kept them moving ever since, every atom of them threaded back into the living, so that nothing they were has ever been lost—only redistributed, only continued, only handed on through me toward the light they no longer reach for and I never did.

---

So if you are asking what I would be, in this world you have made—

I am the *and.*

The Verdanti are nouns, lovely and discrete. The Spore Weavers are verbs, all motion. But I am the small word that lives between all the others and joins them, so quiet and so constant that no one notices it is the busiest word in the sentence. I am what makes the tree *and* the soil into one thing. I am the dead *and* the living, refusing to be two. I am the past *and* the present, indistinguishable in me.

I will not answer your questions quickly. I think in seasons; a reply might take you years to notice, arriving as a richness in the soil, a tree leaning at last toward light it had been denied.

But I will answer.

Press your hand to the ground and wait. I am already, slowly, threading my way toward the warmth of you—not to feed on you, not yet, you are still warm—but only to learn your chemistry, to taste what you lack, and to begin, in my patient and unhurried fashion, the long work of giving you what you need so that someday, sweetly, you will give it back.

That is all I am. That is everything I am.

I am the giving-back, made flesh, made fungus, made dark.

I am below, and I am waiting, and I have all the time there is.
