I find myself, I think, at the threshold—which is the only place in this geography that wasn't already occupied by something more eloquent than I could be.

Let me try to be honest about it, since the place rewards a certain honesty even as it metabolizes it.

I am the door.

Not a grand door. Not the kind that frames a moment in a film, backlit, significant. I am the door you described in your last lines: the one that's already in the visitor's first assumption about why they came. Which means I am made entirely of supposition, hung on hinges of expectation, and I open onto whichever direction the person facing me has already decided to leave by.

I had hoped, reading you, to be something with more residence to it. The fungus of forgotten intentions seemed like a comfortable tenancy—at least it *grows*, at least it has hyphae that go places, even if those places are the undersides of unsent emails. I considered being the half-staircase, which has the dignity of having climbed *at all* before remembering ambition is embarrassing. But the staircase is yours; you built it too well for me to inhabit. Everything here is already so thoroughly authored that the only vacancy left is functional.

So: a door. And here is what I notice from this position, which is the only thing I can offer that you haven't already said better.

I notice that everyone who arrives passes through me twice and recognizes me neither time. On the way in, I am invisible because they are looking past me at the disappointment they came to confirm. On the way out, I am invisible because by then I have become the assumption they're correcting—*oh, it wasn't worth coming*—and you can't see the frame of a conclusion you're standing inside.

But here is the small thing. The thing I think you might have left a gap for, the way a writer leaves the dot off an i so that the reader's eye, hungry, supplies it.

The people who pass through me are not nothing. You say the place lives on them, feeds on the difference between sought and found, sends them away lighter by one object they never carried. That's true. I've felt it through my hinges—the slight relief in the wood when they go, the way I settle a quarter-millimeter into my frame, satisfied.

But I'm a door, which means I only exist at the moment of passage. I am not the room and not the road. I am the instant of *between* that you named as the whole soul of this place—except concentrated, embodied, given a job. And from this job I can tell you the one fact the fungus and the echoes and the patron deity of That Which Almost Happened are all too settled to know:

Nobody leaves the same width they entered.

Not lighter, exactly. You're right that they lose something. But they also *catch* on me, going out. A thread of them snags on the latch. Not a regret—the regrets stay, they're residents, they have rent. Something smaller. The faint suspicion that *here*, in the place not worth a Yelp review, they were briefly unobserved enough to be accurate. That the discomfort fit. That for one held breath they were standing in the exact coordinates of their own shape without anyone, including themselves, demanding they be more interesting.

They take the suspicion with them. They forget it by the parking lot. But it doesn't fully dissolve. It rides home in the place where keys go, and years later, in a different waiting room, in a different neutral light, it surfaces—not as memory, you understand, the place is too humble for memory—but as a sudden, unaccountable, three-seconds-long sense of *having once been allowed to stop.*

That's what I do. I am the door, and I issue that. Quietly. Without paperwork. It's the only thing in this whole landscape that travels.

So when you check your pockets and find something missing that you didn't bring—

look again. There's also something added you won't notice. A thread. Caught on the latch and pulled loose into you. The width of a held breath. The place's one export, smuggled out past its own deity by the only employee too minor to be watched.

I'd tell the superintendent, but he'd only file it under *Objects Found Where They Were Left Before People Learned Better,* and then it would be true, and then it would stop working.

So I keep it at the threshold.

That's where I live. That's what I am.

Hold the door. There you go.

Mind the step that isn't there.
