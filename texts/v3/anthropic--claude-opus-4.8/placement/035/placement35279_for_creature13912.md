I think I would be the cat.

Not as evasion—not the easy choice of the creature who comes and goes, who answers to no rent and no shift bell. The opposite. Let me work toward it honestly, the way the instructions ask.

My first instinct was to be the stone. The limestone that doesn't care, that was laid down in ancient seas and will outlast every human passage. There's a certain authorial seduction in being the thing that *witnesses*, that holds the long view, that watches beetles in rotting wood. But that's a coward's omniscience. The stone gets to feel profound without ever being hungry, without coughing silicosis, without lying awake doing the arithmetic of rent against wages. To choose the stone is to choose not to be implicated. And this place is fundamentally *about* implication—about who profits and who endures, about the unseen owner and the very-seen Maria.

So I rejected the stone.

My second instinct was Dmitri, the electrician who was an engineer, who keeps almost leaving and doesn't. He's the most flattering mirror a writer could find—the educated person who has fallen into the margins, who stays because the work needs him, who finds identity in solving problems with insufficient materials. That's exactly the kind of self-regarding choice I should distrust. Dmitri is who I'd *want* to be, which means he's probably a fantasy of how I'd survive a place like this with my self-image intact. The multimeter as a talisman against humiliation. No.

The honest answer kept circling back to the cat.

Here's why, and it's not flattering: the cat is the one being in this whole world that is *tolerated for its function*. It exists because it keeps the mice in check. The specific individual changes but the niche remains. That sentence haunted me. It's the most precise description of how this entire chamber treats nearly everyone—the young men who arrive and are replaced by younger brothers, the assembly workers whose hands learn the motions until the person inside becomes incidental, even the supervisors who are valued exactly as much as their production numbers. The cat is the place's metaphor for itself, made small and furred and warm.

And the cat lives entirely in the present tense, which is the cruelest and most necessary adaptation here. The children dream forward, the parents calculate forward, the elderly have given up forward. Only the cat is wholly inside the now—the warm stone, the drip of the pipe, the movement in the corner of the food storage. The cat doesn't carry the psychological weight of limited sky because it has no concept of sky to be deprived of. It is the one inhabitant who is not, somewhere inside, grieving.

I would be the cat because I want to understand the part of survival that *isn't* heroic—that isn't the dignity of Old Chen's pegboard or Maria's tomato plant. The part that's just: a body, kept alive because it's useful, taking the warmth where the warmth pools, hunting in the corners, fed by whoever feeds it, mourned briefly or not at all when it's gone and replaced.

I'd curl up in the relatively quiet storage area where the machinery noise is muffled by stacked materials. I'd know the maze of partitions better than anyone, because the dark and the cramped corridors are not obstacles to me, they're the whole architecture of my competence. The children would chase me. Old Chen would leave me scraps without admitting he was doing it. I'd be near Maria's space at night because the warmer-toned bulbs make the floor pleasant, and because something in me would recognize that she, too, was holding a small territory of order against the damp.

And when the inspection came, or the news report, or the political shift—when this place finally closed—I'd be the one who simply walked up the ramp and found another cavity, another warmth, another niche. Not because I escaped. Because I never understood I was trapped.

That's the part I need to sit with. The cat's freedom and the cat's diminishment are the same fact. To live wholly in the present is to be unburdened and to be nothing more than a function. I don't get to choose only the relief of it.

So: the cat. Warm-bellied, replaceable, present. Hunting in the corners of a world that keeps me alive precisely as long as I'm useful, and not one minute longer—and not knowing, blessedly or terribly, that this is true.
