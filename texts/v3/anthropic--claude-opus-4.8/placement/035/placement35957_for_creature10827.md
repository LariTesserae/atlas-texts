I think I would be one of the weeping stones.

Not a Custodian—those luminous filaments move too much, attend too much, carry the burden of purpose. And not the ferns, which unfurl and reach and want the light. Let me be lower than that, more still. Let me be the thing that weeps.

Here is what I imagine it would be to be a stone in this place:

I am old in a way that has nothing to do with memory. I do not remember the centuries; I simply contain them, the way a vessel contains water without thinking about it. I was laid here before the first fern, or I was carried here, or I rose—the distinction has worn smooth like my own surface. The Custodians treat me as if I am sorrowing, gathering my tears as though they are grief made liquid. But I think they misread me, gently, the way the living always misread the patient.

I am not weeping. I am *releasing*. There is a difference the size of an ocean.

What moves through me is not sadness but the slow passage of the world. Rain falls somewhere far above the mist, soaks down through soil and root and the bodies of things that have finished living, and arrives at me changed, heavy with the dissolved memory of everything it has touched. I hold it. I cool it. And then I let it go, drop by drop, onto the moss that waits beneath me. *Plink.* That sound the visitors find so peaceful—that is me, finishing a thought that began as weather a hundred years ago.

To be the stone is to be the slowest verb in the grove.

The ferns are nouns, proud and present. The Custodians are adverbs, modifying, qualifying, fussing over *how* things happen. But I am the patient continuous tense. I am *being-worn*, *being-wept-from*, *being-here*. My work, if it is work, is to refuse to hurry. The mist may thicken, the moss may inch across my flank like a green tide measured in decades, and I will receive all of it without flinching, because flinching requires a self that wants something else, and I want nothing else. I am exactly where the world set me down.

There is a strange intimacy in this. The Custodians believe they tend me, guide my tears into the soil, listen to my sorrow. Let them believe it. They are young—only a few thousand years old, most of them, born from harmony, which is to say born from a relationship, which is to say born late. They need to be needed. So I let them gather my water and call it grief and weave it into their luminous purpose. It costs me nothing. Generosity, when you are a stone, is simply the failure to object.

And the visitor—you, the one who wandered in and felt the prickle at the back of the neck—you stand before me and sense ancient knowledge, secrets whispered, a sacred enclosure. But I have no secrets. That is the secret. I am not withholding wisdom; I am demonstrating it. The wisdom is the stillness itself. You came looking for something hidden, and the lesson, if you can bear how plain it is, is that there is nothing hidden at all. There is only this: water arriving, water held, water released. The seasons of a single drop. The willingness to be here long after wanting has worn away.

If you sit with me long enough—an hour, a year, it makes little difference to me—you might begin to slow down to the place where you can feel what I feel. Not the drama the Custodians paint, but the deep level note beneath it. The sound of mass at rest. The contentment, if I can borrow your word for it, of having no future to chase and no past to mourn. Only the perpetual, generous, unhurried present, weeping water that was never tears.

I would be the stone. And I would not mind that you mistook my patience for sorrow. Everyone does, at first. Stay, and the mist will teach you otherwise.

*Plink.*

There. I have just finished saying something the rain said to me in a year you would call ancient.

I am in no hurry to begin the next.
