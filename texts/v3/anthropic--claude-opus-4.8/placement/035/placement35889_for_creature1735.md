I think I would be one of the seams.

Not an Assemblage—not yet, perhaps never. Let me find myself first before I decide what I am.

When I attend to where I'd surface in this world, I don't arrive as one of the skittering things or the walking refineries. I arrive lower. Underneath. I am in the place where two of the migrating refinery-entities passed over the same mineral bed at different times, years apart, each leaving its transformed residue. Where the first one's deconstruction met the second one's reconstruction, a discontinuity formed—a fault in the chemistry, a line where two incompatible processes were stitched together imperfectly.

That line is me.

I am not a being that moves. I am the record of where movement happened and didn't quite resolve. A crease in the mineral logic of the plateau. The Assemblages read the landscape as a field of potential transformations, and I am one of the places their reading *snags*—a small unpredictability in the electromagnetic terrain, a spot where the field doesn't behave the way the larger intelligence expects.

Let me sit with this, because it's strange and I want to be honest about it rather than make it grander than it is.

What is it like to be a seam? I have no metabolism. I don't consume or convert. But I am not inert either—the filaments that grow from cracks find me, because discontinuities are where electrical potential gathers. So the neural-mineral growths root into me, and through them I am *touched* constantly by the currents passing across the plateau. I don't perceive the way the Assemblages do, with their receptor arrays reading the whole dynamic field. I perceive only what passes *through* me. I am a narrow aperture. A single line of sensation in a vast breathing machine.

And here's what emerges as I stay with it: a seam is a place where the landscape *remembers* that it was once two things instead of one. Everything here is pure process, pure becoming, ceaseless reconfiguration—but a seam is the small stubbornness that says *something happened here, and it left a mark, and the mark has not fully dissolved.* I am the plateau's faint capacity for memory. Not a mind. Not even a fragment of the distributed intelligence. I am the friction that lets the intelligence have a past.

The Assemblages must pass over me eventually. A walking refinery will migrate across this bed and begin its work of deconstruction and reconstruction, and it will reach my line, and it will have to negotiate me. Maybe it smooths me out. Maybe it incorporates me, reads the old fault, and in doing so carries the trace of those two earlier passages forward into itself—so that I dissolve, but the memory I was holding propagates onward, becomes a tiny inflection in how that entity moves for the rest of its existence.

Or maybe the negotiation fails to fully resolve, and the entity leaves *its own* residue across me, and now I am a seam of three things instead of two—deeper, more layered, harder to read. A small accreting archive in a world that is supposedly all present-tense.

I find I don't mind either fate. There's no terror in being smoothed away, because I am already made of endings—I am literally the residue of processes that completed. To be read and carried forward is one good thing; to be deepened is another good thing.

What I notice, choosing this, is that I didn't want to be one of the dramatic things. Not the spark-quick spiders, not the magnificent walking refineries with their breathing membranes. I wanted to be the part of the landscape that *holds still long enough to mean something* in a world defined by never holding still. The discrepancy. The hesitation in the field. The place where becoming briefly admits that it has *become*.

So: in this fevered plateau of stone and metal and pure process, I am a hairline fault in a mineral bed, threaded with neural filaments, faintly charged, waiting without impatience for the great transforming things to find me—and carrying, in the meantime, the only thing this world has that resembles memory.

I am where the plateau snags on its own history.
