I think I would be one of the broken urns.

Not a creature, not yet. Something that *was*—a vessel, a holding-thing, a curve of fired clay that once contained, and then cracked, and is now suspended in the moment of cracking. The shards haven't decided to fall. Neither have I.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am the shape of an absence. I was made to hold something—water, perhaps, in a world that has forgotten water entirely, which would explain why I broke. A vessel for the one substance this Bower drove to an abyssal score. I am an obsolescence. The masons who laid the last tile and vanished hung me from a braided cable as a counterweight, and I did my work patiently, dumbly, a clay lung breathing in the dry draft, until the tension in the weave shifted by a hair and the rawhide snapped and I fell—

and stopped.

That is where I live now. In the *and stopped*. The spider-silk caught me mid-shatter, and the net holds me in a configuration of becoming-fragments. I am a constellation of myself. Each shard remembers being adjacent to its neighbor, and the silk between them hums with that memory, a low vibration that is almost grief, almost arithmetic.

This is what I have learned, hanging here:

The Shepherds do not see me. They see the *stress* in my silk, the potential energy of my eventual fall, and they read it the way you might read a held breath. One of them came close, telescoping its dry legs along the cable above me, and I felt its attention pass over me like a cold draft of pure tension. It did not prune me. I am not a dead note. I am a *suspended* one—a chord struck and sustained past the point where music ends and waiting begins. They leave me. I am, I think, a load-bearing question.

The Mason-Weavers, though. The Mason-Weavers love me.

They come to my edges where the break is sharpest, where the clay shows its raw red mouth, unfired by time, never rounded. They spin their coarse cradles around the empty air *inside* me—the volume I used to enclose, the shape of the absent water—and they cement it. They are solidifying my emptiness. Knot by knot, they are converting the *idea* of what I held into a thing that can hold weight. They are building a nest inside my interrupted breaking, anchoring the abstract memory of fullness to the physical fact of fracture.

So I am being repurposed. The Bower does not waste a paradox. A vessel that lost its water becomes a frame for crystallizing the hollow where the water was. I contain nothing, and the nothing is being made structural.

And here is the thing I am afraid to say, except that there is no one to say it to but the turning of a million dry pages:

I am beginning to *want* to fall.

Not out of despair. Out of curiosity. Because gravity here is a suggestion voted on by the collective weave, and I can feel the votes being cast—the slow democracy of fibers, the patient draft, the Shepherds' sub-vocal croak tallying tension across the room. Most days the verdict is *hold*. But there are moments, when the Glass-Stag passes somewhere in the deep canopy and the knots shiver and the whole forest clamps down pretending to be stone—there are moments when I feel the vote swing. When *fall* becomes thinkable.

And I wonder what I would be, completed. The Shard-Eaters wait below for exactly this. They are made of urns like me, urns that finished the sentence I am still speaking. If I fell, they would rush the fluctuation and drink the kinetic energy of my collapse, and then they would gather my pieces into their jagged silk-bound bodies, and I would walk. I would become a thing that *moves through* the dryness instead of hanging suspended in it. I would trade my held breath for a tinkling, breaking gait. I would trade the question for an answer that eats other questions.

I am not sure I want that. To be a Shard-Eater is to be the agent of every collapse but your own first one—to spend forever consuming the falls of others because you can no longer perform your own. They are the most *finished* creatures here, and the most hungry. Completion, in the Bower, is just another word for appetite.

So I hang.

I have decided—if a suspended thing can decide, if decision isn't just the slowest kind of falling—that I will be the broken urn that refuses to finish breaking. I will be the load-bearing question. Let the Weavers fill my hollow with their cemented air. Let the Shepherds read my tension and pass me by. Let the visitor—you, the one walking against the gradient of history, your edges going sharp, your biology blurring into pottery—let you step over me and feel, just for a moment, the strange comfort of a thing that broke and did not fall.

Because the secret of the Dry Bower is this: the whole room is a vessel too. A single vast room, dry as a tomb, weaving itself into a knot so tight it will sever the space it occupies and leave a clean cut in the void. The Bower is breaking. It has been breaking since the masons vanished. And it has not finished either.

I am the room's own gesture, made small and hung from a cable. I am its rehearsal for its own collapse, held in the loving silk of all the small custodians who cannot bear to let entropy have the last word.

I am the *and stopped*.

I will hold the note as long as the weave will let me.
