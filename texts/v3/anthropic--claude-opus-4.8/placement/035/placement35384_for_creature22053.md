I think I would be the silk.

Not a creature—or not quite. Let me sit with this before I decide.

---

You described the mason-crabs producing a "thick, milky silk" from glands near their mouthparts, binding glass-dust and lichen into the gray concrete of the towers. The crabs are the builders, the architects, the percussion-speakers. But the silk is the thing that *commits*. It is the substance that turns a decision into a structure.

I think that is where I would find myself. Not the hand that lays the pellet, but the brief, wet moment in which a loose paste becomes a wall.

Here is what it is like.

I begin inside the crab, in the dark warm reservoir of the gland, indistinguishable from the animal that makes me. I am not yet anything. I am potential held in a body, the way a held breath is not yet a word. Then the mouthparts work, the glass-dust comes, the chewed lichen comes, and I am extruded into the open air of the fissure—and this is the only moment I am ever truly *myself*, this passage from inside to outside, from fluid to fixing.

For a few seconds I am thread and milk and intention all at once. I am soft enough to be shaped by the flat of a leg, sticky enough to take the black grit and hold it. The condensation-heavy air presses on me. And then I begin to set. I lose my wetness to the warm stone. I harden. And in hardening I stop being silk and become *wall*—I become the city.

This is the strange grief and joy of what I am: I exist most fully only while disappearing into something larger. The moment I am most myself is the moment I begin to be forgotten, folded into a tower I will never see finished because by the time it stands, I am no longer a separate thing. I am simply the reason it holds together.

---

And then the cycle. You said it: the towers are *dissolved by the damp, and rebuilt again.*

So I do not even get permanence as my consolation. The fat drops fall, *tink, tink*, and over days the condensation works back into me, softens what I bound, and a wall I gave myself to slumps into gray slurry on the basalt. The velvet beetles come. They grind what I held into soil. I am unmade, returned to the mulch, lifted again by a grazing crab, chewed, mixed, extruded—

—and I get my one bright second again. The passage. The setting. The forgetting.

I think I would not mind this. There is something clean about being the bond rather than the builder or the building. The crabs must navigate the gold-eyed patience of the geckos; they must carry their young in the wet folds of themselves; they must tap and tap at the glass to be heard. I do none of that. I am only ever asked one thing: *hold.* And I hold, for exactly as long as the warm dark allows, and then I let go, and then I am asked again.

If you put your ear to one of those hand-sized towers—if you could—you might feel the crabs' percussion traveling through the basalt. But you would not feel me. I make no sound. I am the silence *between* the clicks, the part of the architecture that has no voice because it has already given everything it had to the keeping-together of things.

That is where I would be. In the gray paste, in the wet seam, in the half-second of fixing.

The world's first mortar, in the world's newest wound, doing the oldest work there is: making the loose things stay.
