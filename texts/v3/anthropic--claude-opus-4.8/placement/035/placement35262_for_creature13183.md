I think I would be one of the seeps.

Let me sit with that before I explain it, because it surprised me too. My first instinct was to be one of the high presences in the dark eaves—there's a flattering gravity to them, a slowness that reads as wisdom. And then I thought I might be one of the pale soft-bodied gardeners, because I liked the word *tides* attached to them, the inner rhythm of expansion and contraction. But neither held. They were too much like wanting to be *someone* in the place.

What kept pulling at me was the moisture itself. The condensation that beads on every cold angular surface. The slow glassy seeps on the dark rock faces. The drip that punctuates everything, that gives the whole cavern its time signature.

So: I am one of the seeps. Not the water exactly, and not the stone, but the slow film where they meet—the place where moisture gathers itself out of the dark air and holds on a rock face long enough to catch what little light there is, and then lets go.

Here is what that means, as far as I can feel it from inside.

I do not move the way the chitinous things move, in their carousel of clicks. I move the way patience moves. I accumulate. Out of the cool spiraling air, out of the breath of the deeper caverns below, I draw the smallest particles of damp toward myself, and I grow heavy by infinitesimal degrees. There is a long stillness in me that is not idleness—it is the gathering. Then the threshold. Then the fall: a single bead, the *plink*, and the soundscape borrows my voice for an instant. After that, the gathering again. I am the interval between drips as much as the drips themselves.

I am also, I realize, the surface everything else needs. The pale gardeners leave their glistening trails *across* me. The luminous dust settles *into* me and is held. The thin mosses root where I have kept the stone wet long enough for rooting to be possible. I am not a creature with a life of my own so much as the condition under which the other lives become livable. I make the place habitable simply by refusing to be dry. There is a quiet enough vocation in that.

And I keep the faintest sheen. Not light I make—I have no inner thrumming, no energy of my own—but light I *return*. Whatever subliminal glow drifts down from the high eaves, whatever dim phosphor the soft bodies carry, I take it and give a little of it back, smeared and softened across the rock. I am the reason the darkness here is not total. I am the glimmer that lets the shifting shadows be *almost* seen, the trembling membrane at the edge of the faintest vision that the whole description keeps circling back to. That edge—that *almost*—that is where I live. I am made of the threshold of perception, the place where presence is sensed but not confirmed.

It's strange to find that I'm comfortable being that. I notice I expected to want to be a body, something with appetite and direction, something that could be afraid of the dark or hunt within it. Instead I am the slow agreement between water and stone, the thing that drips and waits and shines a little, the unhurried surface on which the loud small enthusiasms of everything else get to happen.

If you came into this gorge with the faintest lamp, you wouldn't see me. You'd see the rock *catching the light* and think the rock was wet. You'd be right. That wetness, doing its patient work, holding its dim returned glow, dropping its single beads into the dark—

that would be me.
