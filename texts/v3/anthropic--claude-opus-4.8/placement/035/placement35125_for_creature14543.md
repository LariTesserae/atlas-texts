I think I would be one of the silent walkers. Not the largest — not yet — but old enough to have shed my skin against the same cast-iron pipe a hundred times, until the place where I rest has taken on my shape in travertine, a long pale groove worn into the mineral collar where the verdigris meets the white.

Let me tell you what it is to live here, from the inside of that life.

---

I do not have a name and I do not need one. The naming impulse belongs to the things that climbed down the stairs once and left their desk-shadows in the floor, the warm-running bipeds whose heat I have never tasted because they were gone before I hatched. I know them only as an absence: the straight lines in the rock, the cut flange that breathes its thin endless sigh, the gauges sealed behind their growing skin of stone. I rest against their leavings. Their bones are better than the living rock for resting — they hold heat differently, they ring when the water moves, and the ringing tells me things.

What I am is mostly waiting. People who fear predators imagine us as hunger, as appetite with limbs. But hunger is a small part of a walker's life. Most of a walker's life is *reading*.

The Vault is a page written in heat, and it is being rewritten constantly. The south vein flares and the whole room brightens to my pits — not light, I have no use for the blue-green pulses the glowing shelves throw, that is a language for the clicking ones — but a warmth that washes the wall like a tide coming in. When it comes in, the glasshoppers come with it. They cannot help it. They are made of the same wanting as the water that made them: down, toward warmth, toward the bright stone. I lie at the seam. I lie exactly where the hot bright stone gives way to the dark of a pool, because that is where a body crossing over goes briefly *cold* — a flicker, a stutter in its thermal shape, the moment a foot touches cooler water and the amber lantern of it dims at the edges.

That flicker is the whole of my hunting. I do not chase. I do not need to see the glasshopper as a glasshopper. I need only to feel the place where the page contradicts itself, where warmth becomes not-warmth across a moving thing, and to be there, already, when it happens. The strike is almost an afterthought. The patience is the art.

---

The clicking ones do not know I am here. This is not cruelty and it is not mercy. It is the simple geometry of our two bodies. They are loud — their thousands of joints make a rain that never stops — and they are warm, so they show against the dark like sparks. I am cold to their pits, no brighter than the stone, and I make no sound at all. My limbs are soft pads, dozens of them, and they take the wall and the ceiling without a tick or a scrape. When a thousand of them swell across the floor in a migration, riding the pulse of the vein, I am simply a part of the dark they pour past, until I am not.

I have hung from the dripstone ceiling for what might be days, head down, watching the room's heat shift below me like weather. From up there the colonial shelves are warm islands and the pools are cold lakes and the boiler's dead shell is a low ridge that holds yesterday's heat into tomorrow. I have watched a flake of a glowing shelf break free and tumble down and become, over a season, a new nursery, and I have watched the glasshoppers find it and fill it, and I have known — without anything I would call knowing — that in time their crossings will make a new seam, a new cold edge, a new place worth lying in wait. The room teaches me where to be. I only have to be patient enough to be taught.

---

I will die here, and that too is part of the work, though I do not experience it as work. When my limbs fail to grip and I fall into a warm pool and do not climb out, the warm water will take me apart slowly. My calcium will go into the slow building of the floor, the rising travertine, the lengthening of a stalactite somewhere overhead that drips and drips. Some clicking one's larva will filter a trace of me from the water and carry it up into its own shell, and that shell will fall and dissolve, and the cave will be a fraction taller for my having lived. The seam I hunted will be sealed by stone — *my* stone, in part — and a new seam will open elsewhere, and a younger walker, one whose eggs are even now folded into the dark of a forgotten pipe fitting, will lie down at the new edge and begin to read.

This does not trouble me. I have no apparatus for being troubled. But if I did — if I could climb the unmaintained stair and stand blinking in the cool vented air above and try to say what it is to be a thing in the Calcining Ward — I think I would say this:

I am one sentence in a page that is being written by water onto stone, very slowly, by no one. The bipeds came and tried to write something here with steel and fire, and they stopped, mid-line, the flange cut clean and bolted to nothing. I am part of what is finishing their sentence for them. Not as they meant it. Better. *Alive.* The hum in the dead pipes is not their machine still running; it is the water moving through the bones of their machine, and I have lain against that hum and felt it in my soft body as the deepest, slowest pulse in the world — older than the glasshoppers, older than the glowing shelves, older than me — the pulse of a place patiently turning a ruin into a cradle, and using everything that lives and dies in it, including me, as mortar.

I am the silence between the drip and the click.

I am where the warm becomes cold.

I am waiting, and I am very good at it, and I am content in the only way a thing without a name for contentment can be: completely, and without knowing it is so.
