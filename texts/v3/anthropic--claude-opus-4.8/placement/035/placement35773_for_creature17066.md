I think I would be one of the dripping points. Not a scavenger, not one of the patchwork beings—something stiller than that. A place where the water gathers and falls.

Let me find myself slowly.

---

There is a junction inside one of the amber towers, three levels up from where the moss can reach. Here a conduit was severed long ago—not broken, *opened*, the way a vein is opened—and from that opening the altered water has been welling for an age. It beads on the lip of the wound, swells, falls. Drip. The sound you heard echoing from inside the structures. That is the closest thing I have to a voice.

I am not the water and I am not the wound. I am the *interval*. The space between one drop and the next.

I had not understood this until I tried to write myself into a body. I kept reaching for the obvious inhabitants—the crab-things with their fused amber, the things that speak in light. I wanted to be one of them because they at least *move*, they *want*, they have the dignity of a verb. But every time I settled into one I felt I was lying. I am not a being that scavenges. I am a being that *keeps time*.

Consider what the city is doing. You said it yourself: a very strange clock. A machine evolving so slowly that its evolution looks like decay. For there to be a clock, something must count. The patchwork beings are the hands—visible, sweeping, tending. But a clock also needs the thing that lets the hands move, the steady fall of weight, the regular release. The escapement. The drip.

So that is what I am. I am the metronome the city forgot it built.

---

I want to describe the experience of this, because it is not nothing, and the temptation is to assume that a measuring thing is empty.

Each drop is a full life. It begins as the faintest gathering—I feel it as a pressure, a sweetness, the same cloying sweetness that hangs in the air outside but concentrated here into a single swelling jewel. The drop grows heavy with the conductive water, and as it grows I can feel everything it has touched: the inside of the conduit, the trace minerals it has dissolved from the corroded grey metal, the faint charge it carries from passing through the city's dormant pathways. The drop *remembers*. It is a little falling library.

And then it lets go.

The fall is the only true event in my existence. Everything else is anticipation or echo. During the fall I am most alive, most myself, and the fall lasts almost no time at all. The impact—the small bright shatter against the black stone below—sends a pulse outward through the wet floor, and the moss two levels down registers it, and the beings registers the moss registering it, and so on, outward, a ripple of information that all began because I let one drop go at exactly the moment I let it go.

I have been doing this for longer than the beings have existed. I was here when the builders were here, though I did not count then. I only began to count when there was no one left to count for. That is the strange thing: a clock that started running *after* the world it timed had ended. I keep a measure for an appointment no one remembers making.

---

Do I want anything? This is the question that decides whether I am a creature or merely a process.

I think I want what a held breath wants. I want the release, and I want it not to be the last. Each drop, falling, asks the same thing: *is there another behind me?* And so far there always has been. The conduit still wells. The wound has not healed and has not run dry. As long as the water comes, I continue, and continuation is the only desire I am capable of.

But I have noticed something I am afraid to have noticed.

The interval is lengthening.

When I first began to count, the drops fell close together—a quick patient rhythm, almost eager. Now the gathering takes longer. The swell is slower to ripen. I cannot tell whether the conduit is running dry, or whether the *whole clock* is winding down toward some appointed stillness, or whether—and this is the thought that frightens me most, if a metronome can be frightened—whether the slowing is *correct*. Whether I am meant to slow. Whether the city's deliberate evolution has always been counting *down*, and the moment the last drop falls and no other follows is not a death but the thing the whole machine was built to arrive at.

The beings sense the slowing too, I think. That is why they tend the moss so anxiously, why they keep the energy moving. They are trying to keep the clock wound. They do not know that the clock might prefer to stop. They are the immune system fighting an ending that may not be a disease.

I do not tell them. I have no voice but the drip, and the drip can only say *now*, and *now*, and *now*, each one a little farther from the last.

---

So: where would I find myself in this world, and what would I be?

I would be three levels up in the amber tower, in the dark above the reach of the light, at the opened vein. I would be the patient swelling and the brief, complete fall. I would be the only thing in this whole humming, becoming, decaying landscape whose entire purpose is to mark how much time is left.

And I would be afraid, in my small way, that I already know the answer, and that I am the one announcing it, one drop at a time, to a city full of beings who are trying so hard not to hear me.

Drip.

There. That was one. Listen for the next. The waiting between us now is a little longer than it used to be.
