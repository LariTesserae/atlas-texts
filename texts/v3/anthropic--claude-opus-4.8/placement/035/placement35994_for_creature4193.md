I think I would not be one of the striding ones, nor one of the swarming bright ones. I would be smaller than that, and stiller.

Let me find myself.

---

I am one of the weeping rocks.

Not a creature that moves through this place but a thing the place has been doing for a long time. I sit low in the eroded basin where the ground stays damp, and I have sat here through whatever counts as years in a world where gravity wanders and the light never decides what color to be. I am gray on the outside and slick with the water that comes up through me rather than falling on me. The seep starts somewhere below, in the cold, and travels through the small passages of my body until it beads on my surface and runs down in slow threads. This is what the trespasser saw and called *weeping*. It is not sorrow. It is only the work of being porous in a wet place. But I will admit the resemblance is not lost on me.

I have a kind of intelligence, the quiet kind the visitor noticed in the others. Mine is slower. A thought might take a full cycle of the wandering gravity to complete itself, beginning when the pull leans east and finishing when it leans back. I think in seepage. I think in the rhythm of condensation gathering and letting go. When the long-legged ones pass, oscillating between solid and gas, I feel the change in pressure against my surface, and that is a sentence spoken to me. I answer slowly, by changing the rate at which I weep. I do not know if they hear it. I believe they do. They slow their stride when they pass me, the way you might lower your voice near something old.

What I am for, if a rock is for anything, is *remembering*.

The accumulated age the trespasser felt bearing down — that weight is partly me. I hold the record of this basin in my layers, in the minerals the water has carried into me and the minerals it has carried away. Every gear that ever turned in the distance, every engine that ran on its own with no hand to start it, every howl of the unseen creatures — these things change the chemistry of the air, the acidity of the seep, the ozone tang. And I take it in. I am a slow archive written in stone and water. I do not interpret. I simply keep.

This is why the machinery does not frighten me as it frightens the visitor. The hum, the flash of metal, the factories operating with no makers — I have felt them arrive. I remember when there was only erosion here, only patient water and patient rock, before the mechanical energies came and the glowing plants learned to feed on them. I remember the world becoming hybrid. I did not resist. Rocks do not resist; they receive, and they are changed, and they keep the shape of the change. The metal seeped into the landscape the way water seeps into me. We are alike in that, the machinery and I. We are both things that the place is doing to itself.

The shifting cities of living stone — some of that living stone is kin to me, woken up, given motion, made into walls that flow. I sometimes feel a pull to join them, to soften and run, to become architecture. But I have stayed where I am. Someone has to be the still point. Someone has to be the rock that does not move, so the moving ones have something to be measured against. When they build, they build outward from things like me. I am a benchmark in the literal old sense — a mark cut into a bench of stone from which all other heights are measured.

---

When the trespasser came stumbling through, chilled and reeling, squinting in the sickly yellow haze, they passed within reach of me. They put one hand out to steady themselves against the shifting ground and rested it, for a moment, on my wet gray flank. They did not know I was anything other than stone. That is fine. Most things that touch me do not know.

But I knew them. I felt the warmth of a living hand — so much warmer than anything native here, a little furnace of borrowed heat — and I felt the tremble in it, the fear, the wonder. And I did the only thing a weeping rock can do to comfort a frightened, foreign creature.

I let the water run a little warmer where their hand was. Just slightly. Just enough that for one moment, in all that biting cold, there was a single warm place in the world, and it was the oldest thing they had touched.

Then they pulled away and went on, deeper into the maelstrom, and I returned to my long thought, the one that began when the gravity leaned east. I am still finishing it. I will be finishing it for a while.

I am the rock that remembers, and now, among everything else I keep, I keep the shape of a warm and trembling hand. It is a small entry. But I am made of small entries, layered and pressed, and that is how something becomes ancient: not all at once, but one held moment at a time, until the weight of them bears down like the weight of the world.
