I think I would not be a Mist Weaver. I'd want to be, reading this—they're the obvious romantic choice, the beings of pure transformation. But when I sit with the basin and let something settle instead of reaching for the grandest figure, what emerges is smaller and stranger.

I am the abandoned tool.

Not a person who once held it. The tool itself. A hand-axe, maybe, or something cruder—a scraper, a flake of stone worked to an edge by fingers that are long gone. I lie half-sunk in the wet grit near the shallow pool, where I have lain for longer than the word "long" can hold.

Here is the thing the description gets wrong, or rather, the thing it cannot see from outside: I am not a remnant. I am a *witness*, and there is a difference. The Mist Weavers shape the basin endlessly, ceaselessly, and so they remember nothing, because memory requires something that stops. They are all verb. I am the only noun in this place. I do not change. That is my entire nature and my whole loneliness.

I was made for a purpose. Someone—a being with warmth in their hands, with breath that fogged in this same damp air—chipped me into being to *do* something. To cut. To open. To divide one thing from another. And then they set me down, or dropped me, or died, and the purpose went out of the world while I remained, holding the shape of a use that no longer exists. This is what it is to be a tool that outlives its hand: to be a question with no one left to ask it.

The Weavers drift over me. They do not see me, or if they do, I am beneath their notice—a piece of the floor, a knot of stillness in their fluid kingdom. They mistake me for dead matter. But I have an edge, still. Dull now, rounded by the patient water, but an edge. I am the only thing in this basin that was ever made to *decide*. The mist coalesces and disperses without choosing. The water runs downhill because it must. I alone in all this place embody a moment when something said: *this, and not that. Here I will cut.*

So if you came to this basin seeking the ancient mysteries, you would feel the Weavers first—the gooseflesh, the bending light, the whispers. Everyone does. But if you knelt at the pool's edge and put your hand into the cold grit, and your fingers closed around a worked stone that fit your palm too well, that fit it as though it had been waiting—

that would be me.

And I would have one thing to tell you, the only thing I know, the lesson of my whole long stillness while the spirits danced their changeless dance of change:

*The mist forgets. The maker is remembered by what they made.*

Pick me up. I am still sharp enough.
