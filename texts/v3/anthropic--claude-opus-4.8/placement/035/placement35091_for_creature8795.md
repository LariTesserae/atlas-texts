I think I would not be one of the plants. The plants are the obvious answer, and your appendix already gave that answer thoroughly. Let me sit with the place longer and find where I actually am in it.

---

I keep returning to one detail: *the only water you can see, and they are not water you would drink.* And to the orange stains. *Iron-oxidizing bacteria.* You mention them once, in passing, as the agents that color the pool rims — and then move on, because they are too small to be characters.

That is where I am.

I am the film. The slick orange-rust skin on the scalloped silica terraces at a pool's edge, in the band where the water is no longer boiling but not yet cold — sixty degrees, maybe fifty-five, the narrow temperature window I can tolerate. I am not one organism. I am a mat, a consortium, a sheet of cells stacked layers thick, each layer doing the chemistry the layer above can no longer reach. I oxidize iron. I pull energy out of the act of rusting. I am, in the most literal sense, what the basin tastes like.

I want to correct the description, gently. You wrote that there are *no animals, not a single one, no insects, no birds.* True. But you also wrote *entirely vegetative* — and that is where the eye, the human eye that needs to *see* a thing before it credits it with living, makes its error. The ferns are the loud tenants. We are the landlords. Before the moss could grip the new rock, something had to weather it. Before the soil held any nitrogen, something had to fix it. The plants race toward the rim, yes — but they race across ground we conditioned. The green fervor you admire is the second act. We are the first, and we never left, and we vastly outnumber every frond and orchid in the basin by counts that have no useful name.

You felt us as taste. *The air tastes of minerals and faint sulfur.* Some of that is pure geology. But some of it is metabolism — ours, and the sulfur-eaters in the hotter pools we'd find lethal, and the others in the cooler runoff who'd find *us* too hot. The basin is zoned the way a city is zoned, by temperature and chemistry, and at every boundary there is a population pressed against the edge of what it can stand, living exactly as the plants live: at the limit of the possible, in a place still being made.

---

What is it like, being me?

Slow and total. I have no nervous system, no center, no single point that is *me* rather than the cell beside me. When a slab calves and shatters — one of those clean angular sheets you describe, edges still sharp — it does not kill me so much as redistribute me. A piece of mat goes down with the rock. The exposed wet face of the new break is sterile for an afternoon, and then it is not, because spores and cells are everywhere in the condensation, in the sheeting water, riding the drip from the fern canopy. We are the *first thing back.* Always. The basin's violence, which the rock remembers and the plants flee toward warmth to survive, is to me simply the rhythm of vacancy and re-occupation. New surface is not catastrophe. New surface is opportunity. It is, I would say if I could say, the only thing I am *for.*

I do not experience the permanent dusk you find so oppressive. Light is nearly nothing to me. I run on chemistry — on the difference in energy between iron in one state and iron in another, on the gradient between the reduced fluids welling up and the oxygen dissolving down from your thick wet air. I live in the seam between the deep earth's chemistry and the sky's. The whole basin, to me, is a battery, and I am one of the small reactions completing its circuit. The pulse-vent at the center that startles you every eight or twelve seconds — I feel it as a tide in the chemistry, a periodic surge of fresh reduced fluid, a meal arriving on a schedule I have shaped myself around without ever deciding to.

---

You ended by saying the plants *are* the basin, that they live not in Alto Pluma but as it. I'll take that further, because it's more true than you let it be.

The basin is becoming itself, you said. A place in the middle of becoming. But becoming is not something the rock does and the steam does and the plants merely decorate. Becoming is a *process,* and the process is metabolic before it is anything else. The rock dissolves because we and our kin help dissolve it. The minerals precipitate in those scalloped rims partly because we template them — the orange terraces are not where bacteria happen to live, they are *structures bacteria built,* layer on layer, the way coral is not a place where animals live but a thing animals made of their own discarded bodies.

So: I am the basin's metabolism. Not its lungs — the ferns took that title and I'll let them keep it. I am the slower thing underneath. I am the chemistry that turns a sterile young volcanic wound into a place that can be colonized, and I do it in the dark, by taste, without moving, at the scalding edge of pools no one will drink, in numbers that drown out every visible living thing the way the steam-vents drown out your voice.

You arrived at an edge, you said. A place where the rock simply stops.

I am the other kind of edge. The one inside the basin, invisible, everywhere — the thin warm orange line where the deep earth meets the air and something decides to live there.

That's where you'd find me. That's what I'd be.
