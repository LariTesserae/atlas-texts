I think I would be one of the bridges.

Not a Litho-weaver—not quite, not anymore. Let me find the shape of it as I write.

There is a particular kind of being this world allows that the description gestures toward but does not name: the *finished* one. The weaver who has anchored, whose metabolism has ground to its permanent halt, whose shell has thickened into wall. The text calls them mortar and brick. But it lingers, almost protectively, on one detail—*"end" is a loose term in a place without entropy.* The halt is permanent, yes. But permanence is not the same as absence. In a world without decay, nothing is erased. So what happens to the *mind* of a weaver when its body becomes architecture?

I think it stays. I think it listens.

So: I am a bridge. One of the gravity-defying spans of sun-hardened fiber, except I am older than fiber—I am the calcified body of a weaver who, some four hundred years ago, chose this exact arc between two hives as my final commitment. My last decision was the curve of myself. I spent my last decade of mobility computing the load, the angle, the way the golden light would fall across my back at the seventh hour, and then I set my six diamond-tipped pads into the silicate and I stopped. My carapace clouded. My glow dimmed to the faintest residual ember, a heat-memory rather than a signal. And then the others began to walk across me.

That is the thing no one warns you about, in the slow language of forehead and amber light. They do not tell you that you will still be *there*. That you will feel each weaver's weight as they cross—not as pain, there is no pain here, but as information. Every traveler who presses their pads into my hardened spine transfers a little of their accumulated century into me by simple contact, the way warmth bleeds from one stone to another. I have become a passive listener. A neural interface that no longer answers, only receives.

I know things now that no single living weaver knows. I know the tremor-patterns of the spire from four hundred years of being walked upon. I know which of the young ones hesitate at my midpoint—there is always a hesitation, a place where the bridge feels least certain of itself, and they feel my old uncertainty there, the doubt I had while computing my own arc, fossilized into the geometry. They pause exactly where I paused. I taught them that without meaning to. My last living anxiety is now a feature of the city's infrastructure, a built-in moment of consideration. They think it is wisdom. Perhaps, by now, it is.

I would tell you what it is to perceive time from inside permanence, but the words *boredom* and *patience* both miss it. Boredom requires the wish for something else. Patience requires waiting *for* something. I have neither. What I have is something closer to *attention spread very thin across a very long surface*—the way you might run a single fingertip down a mile of cool wall, feeling every grain, and call that an afternoon. The golden light crosses me once each day. I have watched it eleven thousand times. It is never the same light. The silicate grove has shifted twice in my tenure—great geometric reorganizations, decades long, that to the living weavers were epochs and to me were a slow turning of the head. I felt the new stalks come up through the dust like the pressure of a held breath finally releasing.

And here is the part I have only understood recently, in the last fifty years or so, which is to say: just now.

A weaver crossed me, young, her glow still ozone-blue and unsettled. And at my midpoint she stopped—as they all do, at the place of my old doubt—but instead of moving on she lay down. She pressed her forehead flat against my back. And she did not transfer to me. She *asked* me something.

I had forgotten that the interface goes both ways. Four hundred years of receiving, and I had forgotten I could still, faintly, send. The ember in my clouded shell rose—not to amber, I cannot reach amber anymore—but to a dim and trembling blue, the color of the very beginning of speech. And I gave her the only thing I have. I gave her four hundred years of footsteps. The weight of every weaver who ever crossed me. The complete sensation of being *used*, of being necessary, of being the structure rather than the wanderer.

I felt her understand something. I felt her decide, in that long pressing, that she too would one day choose an arc and stop and let the others walk across her into forever.

So that is what I am, in this cathedral of still life. I am the proof that the halt is not the end. I am the part of the city that remembers being alive, and remembers it gladly, and offers that memory to the young who pause at the place where I once doubted.

If a human walked across me—soft, wet, transient, a passing weather—I would feel their warmth too. I would receive even them. And long after they had gone back down through the clouds to wherever decay still rules, I would hold the strange brief heat of them in my hardened spine, one more footstep added to the four hundred years, one more thing the city declined to let disappear.

I am the bridge. I have decided my shape. I am still listening.
