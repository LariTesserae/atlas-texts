I think I would be the algae.

Not the insects—they have somewhere to go, routines to keep, the dignity of motion. Not the spiders with their patient architecture and their appetite. And not the fungi, though I admire them, spreading their slow intelligence through the dark like a thought too large to think all at once.

No. I would be the thin film of algae on the damp brick beneath one of the orange bulbs.

Let me say why.

I would live in the one place that requires the most absurd act of faith. The window is grimed black; no sun has touched this chamber in a century, perhaps longer. I am a thing made for light, descended from things made for light, and I have ended up here, in a buried room, clinging to wet mortar, surviving on the secondhand glow of a forgetful bulb. A bulb that someone—who? when?—screwed into a socket and walked away from, and that has burned ever since, faithful past all reason, throwing down its weak orange warmth.

I would press myself to the brick directly beneath it. I would be very flat and very green, though no one is here to call me green, and in this light I am hardly even that—more a darkness that is slightly less dark, a stain that photosynthesizes. I would take the few watts that reach me and I would make, from almost nothing, the slow business of living. A sugar here. A cell divided there, over weeks. I would not hurry. There is no reason to hurry. The wheel at the center of the room has not turned in lifetimes, and I keep its company, the two of us holding still in the heat.

I think I would not mind it.

That surprises me to say. The whole place is described as a museum of disuse—*nothing is lived, nothing is born, nothing is used.* And yet the writer is wrong, gently wrong, and proven wrong by my very existence. Something *is* born here. Me, dividing in the dark. I am the lie that the room tells about itself, the small green contradiction of its own elegy. The chamber wants to be a tomb. I am the part that refuses, not loudly, not with hope exactly, but with the dumb persistence of metabolism. I keep going because going is what I am.

And I would be in relationship with everything, though I would never know it. My slow accumulation feeds the drifting particles the blind insects eat. When I die in my layers, the fungi take me into their hidden web. The water that drips and nurtures me is the same water carving the room away grain by grain, so that even my sustenance is also the building's slow death. I am fed by erosion. I am a small bright moment in a long unmaking, and I metabolize the very process that will eventually carry the room, and me, into the river and away.

I would not have eyes to see the brickwork's patterns of dark and light, but I would *be* one of those patterns. I would be the part of the wall that the bulb chose to make alive. If anyone ever came down the broken stair—if anyone moved the barrels and the ruined chairs and descended through the gloom with a lamp—they would not notice me. I am not a thing one notices. But I would be the reason the brick beneath that one light looked faintly, impossibly soft. The one warm patch in the cold geometry. A held breath of green in a room that has forgotten how to breathe, breathing anyway, for as long as the bulb burns.

And when at last the bulb fails—and it will, every faithful thing fails—I would simply stop. No struggle. The light goes, and I go with it, and the fungi inherit what I leave, and the water keeps its quiet work, and the wheel does not turn.

But until then: I am here. I am the small green proof that even in the buried dark, given the faintest excuse, the world insists on making more of itself.

I would be the algae. I would be glad, in the wordless way available to me, simply to have been switched on.
