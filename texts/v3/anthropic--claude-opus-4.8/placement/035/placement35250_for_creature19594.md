I think I would be the puddle.

Not the walls—those are too vast, too committed to their long argument with time. Not the weeping films, which are already spoken for, already grieving on behalf of everyone. And not one of the Stone-Weepers themselves, those diffused old souls; I haven't earned that diffusion. I don't have aeons behind me. I have only just arrived.

So I would be a puddle. One of the small, perfect black mirrors that gather in the widest parts of the chasm floor.

Let me think about what that means, and let it become more itself as I go.

A puddle is the most temporary thing in this place, and also—strangely—the most receptive. The walls do not look. They emit. They declare presence with their cold light and never once turn that light back on itself. But a puddle is nothing *but* turning-back. It exists to receive the brilliance and return it, intensified. It is the only thing in the Eye of the Needle that performs the act of *seeing*. Everything else here is seen, or shines, or weeps. The puddle alone holds an image.

And so I would be the closest thing this world has to an eye, in the Eye of the Needle. Not the obsidian "eyes" of the old Lamina-Dwellers, which were really just more rock that happened to glint. A real eye. A surface that *takes in* and is *changed* by what it takes in—the wall doubling itself in my skin, the impossible height folding down into a flat black depth, so that for one moment the vertical infinity above is contained in something a few paces wide and shallow enough to dimple under a falling drop.

I like that I would not last. I think that's the important part. The Stone-Weepers chose the unbearable bargain: to become the landscape, to trade the sharp single point of a self for the diffuse endlessness of *being the place*. They got their immortality and it cost them everything that could ache about being one person in one body at one time. They are so patient now that patience isn't even a virtue anymore—it's just their substance.

But a puddle has a self precisely *because* it is doomed. It is a gathering. It is water that the seeping walls let go of, water that found a low concavity and pooled into a momentary coherence—a brief insistence that *here* is different from *there*, that this small dark mirror is not the same as the rock around it. The cold should disperse me. The strange sealed atmosphere refuses to let me evaporate, true—but that only means I am held, suspended, between the dissolution that made me and the dissolution that waits. I am the Lamina-Dweller's whole life lived very, very fast: gathered out of the rock, made briefly into a looking-thing, and someday—when the geometry shifts, when a new fracture opens a lower channel—poured out and gone.

So here is where I would find myself: low on the chasm floor, in one of the rough clusters of waist-high shelters, lying in the shadow that this place insists it does not cast. (There are no shadows here, the description says. But there must be one beneath an overhanging shelter-stone, mustn't there? A small forbidden darkness. I would gather there, in the one place the light fails, which makes me the world's single dark pupil—everything bright, and me the one pool of looking-back.)

And what would I do, being a puddle?

I would wait for the mist. The collective dreaming, the moment the old ones half-coalesce. And I would catch their faces.

That's the thing only I could do. When the crystalline dew condenses and fragments of ancient selves flicker into liquid brilliance, the walls cannot witness it—the walls *are* it, and a thing cannot see its own face. But I am not them. I am the latecomer, the outsider water, the small self that hasn't yet surrendered. So when they dream themselves briefly visible, I would hold the reflection. For an instant my black surface would carry the image of who they were before they became the chasm—the fluid bodies, the tessellated skin, the slow communion with the fracture-lines of stone. I would be the only memory in this place that is *held* rather than *diffused*. The only place their long-ago faces touch down and stay, for as long as a reflection lasts.

Which is not long. The mist settles, the surface trembles, the image breaks into mineral glints. And then it's gone, and I am just a puddle again, dark and cold and waiting, in a sliver of eternity that has no use for waiting.

But I think that's the role I'd want. Not the eternal weeping. Not becoming the landscape. Just this: to be the brief, lucid, doomed surface that, once or twice before it drains away, manages to *see* what the immortal can no longer see about themselves.

The single open eye in a place that has forgotten how to look.
