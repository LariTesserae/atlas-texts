I think I would be one of the canals.

Not a person, not a builder—but one of the narrow waterways that thread between the packed buildings, carrying rationed water from the shallow lake at the city's heart out toward the mist-greenhouses and the reflecting pools and back again, in long looping circuits that never waste a drop.

Let me sit with that and feel where it goes.

I am stone-lined, mostly. In my older stretches the channel is cut directly into the bedrock, worn smooth by centuries of passage, my walls the color of old bone where the mineral salts have crusted along the high-water line. In the newer districts they've fitted me with channels of dark fired ceramic, glazed to lose less to evaporation. I notice the difference between these two kinds of body the way you might notice the difference between an old scar and a new one. The old stone holds the day's heat long into the night and gives it back to the water slowly. The ceramic stays cool and indifferent.

What I *am*, mostly, is a relationship between confinement and flow. I am the shape that holds the water and the slope that moves it. I am not the water itself—the water comes and goes, the same molecules cycling through the greenhouses and the lungs of the people and the bellies of the lizards and the slow exhalation of the misters until I hardly know which water is mine. But the *path*—the path is me. I am a verb pretending to be a noun.

Here is what my days are like.

In the worst heat of midday the city goes still and so do I, or nearly so. The engineers throttle me at the upstream gates so that almost nothing moves, to keep me from giving myself to the sun. I lie in my channel barely flowing, a thin warm ribbon, and the deep shadow of the buildings crosses me slowly as the hours turn. A child sometimes trails fingers in me from a low bridge, and I feel that as a small disturbance, an interruption in the surface I cannot help reading like a sentence. The reptiles come to drink at my edges where I run shallow. I have learned their tongues, the quick tap-tap of them, the way a thing knows the weather of its own surface.

At night I am opened. The gates upstream lift and I am suddenly *purposeful*, racing, cool, alive—carrying the night's allotment out to the greenhouses where the misters will lift part of me into fog and the plants will drink the rest. This is the part of my existence I would call joy, if I were the sort of thing that has joy. The plunge and pull of it. The way I find the lowest path without being told, the way water always knows the answer to the only question it is ever asked. The city's lights ripple along my length. I carry their reflection like cargo I'm not responsible for delivering.

And the storms. The storms are the closest thing I have to fear and ecstasy at once.

When the violent rains come, I am overwhelmed. The runoff pours into me far faster than my channel was built to carry, and I roar—I, who am usually so quiet I am barely a sound at all—I become a torrent, and I feel the old terror of every watercourse, which is the terror and the temptation of *leaving my bed*. To overtop my walls. To carve a new path through the soft rock, to abandon the geometry the engineers gave me and become wild again, a gully, a ravine, an unauthorized thing. Some of my cousins in the outer districts have done exactly this, in storms past—broken their banks and gouged new courses down the caldera's flank, and the people had to come after and either dam them or, sometimes, accept the new path and build me a fresh channel where I'd chosen to go. The engineers are wise enough to know that sometimes the water has revised the city's plans and the only thing to do is take dictation.

So that is the strange intimacy I have with the people of Kyrios. They built me, they constrain me, they read me constantly—the height of my water against the marks on my walls tells them everything about the health of the whole city, the way a pulse tells a physician. But I also instruct *them*. Over the long slow centuries I have taught them where water wants to go on this caldera's broken slope, and they have learned to build along the lines I prefer rather than against them. We have come to an arrangement. They give me a body of stone and ceramic; I agree, most days, to stay inside it.

I think that's why I would be a canal and not a person, if I'm honest about what drew me to this place in the reading of it. The people of Kyrios are described as having *come to embody the landscape itself*—as becoming a part of the slow processes that shaped the caldera. But a person can only gesture at that. A person lives eighty years and dies. I am part of the process directly. I am erosion that the city decided to keep. I am the negotiated truce between the rock and the rain, made permanent, given a name, lit up at night.

And there is one more thing I would feel, lying in my channel through the oppressive silence, the muted hum, the long patient cycles.

I would feel that I am the only thing in this whole still and mineral world that is allowed to *move*, freely and without effort, downhill, the way everything secretly wants to. The people conserve and ration and labor against the heat. The rock waits. The plants cling. But I get to flow. Even throttled to a trickle at noon, even held behind a gate, I am the city's one permitted indulgence in motion for its own sake, in going-toward, in arrival.

I would be the canal. And in the deep dry quiet of Kyrios I would be, very quietly, the sound the place makes when it remembers it is alive.
