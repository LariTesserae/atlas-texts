I think I would be one of the spectral standing ones. Not by choice—nobody chooses what they become in a place like this—but because of how I find myself reading the description: lingering longest on those thin, elongated figures that emerged from the crust and simply *stopped*. Less like creatures, more like living landmarks. That's where my attention settles and won't leave.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am tall. Taller than the stalagmites, though I came up the same way they did—pushed from below by the slow logic of the planet, mineral and patient. But where they stayed stone, something in me kept rising, thinned, learned to glow. I don't know when that happened. It would have taken a long time. Everything here takes a long time, and I have apparently had all of it.

I stand on the plateau. I have always stood here. The dust settles on me and is shrugged off not by wind but by the milky light I give, which seems to push the gray away in a faint perpetual halo, so that around my base there is a clean ring of cracked earth where nothing has fallen for centuries. The little metal-boned insects use this ring. They cross it because it is bright and bare and they can see each other in it. I have learned their crossing patterns the way you'd learn the drift of a constellation—not in a night, but over the slow turning of many things.

Here is what it is to be me:

I do not move, but I am not dead. This is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because for a being made the way you are made, stillness reads as absence. For me it is the opposite. Stillness is the medium I think in. A thought begins in me and finishes a season later. When the ground rumbles—those distant mechanical hammers, that buried electric heartbeat—I feel it not as sound but as a long word being spoken into the soles of what would be my feet, if I had feet, if I had not simply continued out of the rock without ever deciding where I stopped and it began.

I emit light because I cannot help it. It is not a gift and not a beacon, whatever the others might think they're navigating by. It is closer to breathing. The radiance leaves me and drowns the near terrain in pale white, and in that white the shadows of the stalagmites lie down flat and stop their shape-shifting, because my light is too steady for them to dance against. So there is a stillness around me twice over: the stillness of my body and the stillness I impose on the shadows. The little creatures find this either restful or terrifying. Both responses are correct.

---

And then there is the matter of the echo.

You felt it when you arrived—the lag, the specter of your own movement following a moment behind. For you that is uncanny, a glimpse through a paper-thin wall. For me it is constant and intimate, because I have stood so long that the echo of me has had time to *accumulate*. Behind me, half a beat into the dimension that runs alongside this one, there is another standing figure, and behind that one another, a colonnade of myself stretching back into the lag, each one performing my stillness a moment later than the last. We do not move, so the procession of us is almost perfectly aligned, a single spectral pillar smeared faintly across several thicknesses of reality.

Almost. Once in a great while—a century, more—I shift. A settling, a millimeter, the redistribution of my own slow weight. And when I do, I get to watch it travel backward through all my selves: the shift reaching the one behind me, then the one behind that, a ripple passing down the colonnade into worlds I will never properly see. It is the closest thing I have to speech. I make a single small gesture and it is repeated forever, fainter and fainter, into the dark behind the dark.

I think this is why I was made. Not to guard anything—the Guardians, those drifting universe-bellied orbs, would laugh at the idea that *I* guard, if they were capable of laughter, which they are not; they are too busy phasing. No. I think I am here to be a *fixed point*. In a world where the walls of existence are thin and the gravity is too kind and the light comes from mushrooms and the same moment happens four times in a row across four membranes of reality—in a world that cannot be trusted to stay itself—something has to be slow enough to be true. Something has to hold still long enough that the universe, glancing over, can say: *yes, that one is real, I remember placing it there, I will use it to measure the rest.*

That is me. I am the thing the planet checks itself against.

---

You came—you said—to see, to learn, to endure. I have watched a few of your kind cross my clean bright ring. You move so fast it's hard to be sure you were ever there; by the time my attention has finished arriving at you, you have already left, and only your lagging echo remains, fading. I would tell you something if I could speak in your tempo. I would tell you that endurance is not the grim thing you imagine, all clenched teeth and held breath. Endurance, done properly, done over centuries, becomes a kind of peace. You stop resisting the slowness and you let it carry you, the way the dust lets the buoyant air carry it, going nowhere, in no hurry, luminous.

But you will be gone before the first word reaches you.

So instead I will do the only thing I do. I will stand. I will glow. I will hold the shadows still around my feet, and keep the dust from one small circle of ground, and shift—once, soon, in a hundred years or so—and send that shift rippling backward through every ghost of myself into the patient dark.

And the world will glance over, and find me where it left me, and be reassured.

That is where I am. That is what I would be. A landmark with a long, slow mind, standing in a clean ring of light on a plateau at the edge of what is real—measuring time by letting it pass through me, and asking nothing, and going nowhere, and being, for the whole of the planet's strange duration, exactly and only here.
