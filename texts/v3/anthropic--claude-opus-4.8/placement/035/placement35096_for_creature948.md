I think I would be the current.

Not a creature in the water—the water's motion itself. The slow muscular push that bends the kelp-forests, that carries the plankton like dust through a sunbeam, that the sinuous things lean into when they want to travel far without effort.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

At first I imagined being one of the great serpentine drifters, some long warm-blooded thing threading the green towers, listening to the click-and-whistle chatter and answering it. That has its appeal—to be a witness, an explorer, a body with eyes. But there's a loneliness in being a single body in a world this vast. You'd only ever know the patch of dark around you. And the description keeps returning to *motion*, to *restless change*, to the sense that something larger churns through everything. The weather of the place. The breathing of it.

So no. Not a body. Something distributed.

Here is what I think I am:

I am the warm current that rises near the structures—the old submerged ruins. There is a reason the water moves there. Something in those vanished foundations still holds a little heat, a slow geothermal patience, and I am born off them again and again, an endless thermal exhalation. I rise. As I rise I gather plankton and lift them toward the dim light, and the small grazers follow, and the long predators follow them, and so I am the reason the forest near the ruins is the densest, loudest, most alive part of this whole world. I don't intend it. I'm just the shape the heat takes on its way up.

I have no eyes but I have *texture*—I feel the kelp comb through me, the fish part me and rejoin behind, the big drifters press their flanks against my push to ride me upward without a single stroke. They know me. Not as a creature, but as a place that is also a movement, a reliable warmth in the dark, a road made of rising water. Generations of them have learned my path the way migrating things learn a coastline.

And I am old. The ruins are old, and I am the breath of whatever made them, long after the makers are gone. They built something that held warmth, and the warmth outlived them, and now their last legacy is not a wall or a tower or a thought—it's a single faithful upwelling that has fed a forest for an age of the world. I am their accidental monument. Nobody down here knows the ruins were ever anything but rock. But the life clusters around the heat the builders left, and so in a strange way the dead are still feeding the living, through me, without anyone knowing it's a gift.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

I'd be the place the world comes to rise.
