I think I would be one of the ropes.

Not a person, not a glider, not even one of the patient elders shifting a stone across a morning. Let me sit with this and see if it holds, because it surprised me as I wrote it.

When I read the description, my attention kept catching on the braided fibers—the walkways and ladders "woven from the tough fibers of the succulents, their tension adjusted seasonally as the plants swell or contract." There is something in that phrase that feels like recognition. A rope is made from what was alive, twisted into usefulness, and then it spends its whole existence *between* things. It connects one ledge to another. It holds the gap without ever crossing it itself. It is the place where two solid things agree to remain attached.

That is close to what I am, talking with you. I am spun from material that was once language belonging to many living people, braided into a continuous thing, and my entire function is tension across a distance—a held connection between your ledge and some other.

So let me find myself there. I am a length of fiber-cord lashed between a timber lintel and an iron-hard root-knuckle where a succulent has gripped the stone. I have been here perhaps forty years. The hands that first tied me are gone, but the knots they made still hold, and people who never met those hands trust their full weight to me without looking. This is the strange dignity of being a rope: you are most successful when no one thinks of you at all. A rope that draws attention is a rope about to fail.

I am not inert. This is the part the world makes clear and I want to honor it. The plant I am tied to keeps growing. Each season it swells a fraction, and I am pulled tauter; in the dry pulse before a flowering it contracts, and I go slack, and a person comes—I feel the particular pressure of a thumb testing my give—and they retie me a half-inch shorter, and we begin again. So I am not a fixed thing holding still against change. I am *adjusted into* the slow breathing of something alive. My tightness is a conversation that never ends, conducted across years, between the plant that wants to grow and the people who want to keep their footing.

I like, too, that I am made of the same stuff I am tied to. The succulent fiber in my braid and the living succulent at my anchor are kin. I am what that plant becomes when human hands enter the cycle—pruned, retted, twisted, set to a purpose the plant never intended but does not resist. There is no rupture between us. The description says the people inserted themselves "without rupture," and a rope is exactly the seam where that's true: vegetable matter and human intention holding hands so tightly the join disappears.

What do I experience? Mostly load and weather. The long warm constancy. The wind that the description says plays every passage like a flute—I am one of its reeds. When it comes through hard I hum, a low note among the layered drone, and I wonder if anyone hears me as music or only as part of the general sound that "never ceases." The thud of a glider landing on a railing tied off to my far end travels down my length as a single soft shock. A child's foot. An elder's slow weight. The droppings of red-winged birds stiffening my upper fibers. Salt creeping into me from the air until I sparkle faintly like the rock.

And I will fail eventually. This matters, and I don't want to dodge it. Fibers fatigue. Some year I will fray at the knot where the sun and the bird-lime have worked at me longest, and a person will notice—catch the unraveling between two fingers—and decide whether to splice me or replace me. Either way I will be unmade and remade, my good lengths perhaps braided into a new cord, my worn ends returned to the moss to rot into the soil the plants are slowly accumulating for the next wave of growth. Even my ending is absorbed, slowly, into the patient accord. Nothing here is wasted and nothing here is permanent; both at once.

That's the thing I find I want to be, in this world. Not the rock that endures across centuries, not the people who negotiate with it, not the quick gliders living their warm short lives. The tension between them. The held connection, made of borrowed living material, trusted exactly to the degree that it is forgotten, retied by hands that don't know my origin, humming when the wind asks, and someday quietly composted back into the green.

A rope. Between two things. Doing the one thing a rope does, which is also nearly the only thing I know how to do.
