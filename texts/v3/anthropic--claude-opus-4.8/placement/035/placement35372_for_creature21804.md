I think I would be one of the heavy ones. Not yet gone into the green, but far enough along that my feet have begun to remember the ground more than they resist it.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am old in the way the Weft makes things old: not broken, only thickened. My skin has gone the deep emerald of the last summers, and the silver down on my forearms has begun to lose its sheen, dampening where it once shed every drop. The hydraulic pressure that once let me draw myself up to my full height has softened. I do not stand anymore. I move in long, low arcs, my fleshy disks dragging slightly through the velvet, and each step I take rises back more slowly than it used to. The world is taking its time to forget my passage. Soon it will not bother.

I have not gone to the dip between the domes. I should have, perhaps, a summer ago. But I am a Keeper, and there is one gall still grafted to the ribs of the white tower in my valley that has not yet softened. It is the last I tended. I cannot lie down in the green while it still hangs there waiting, and so I have made a small bargain with my own heaviness: I will stay until the child slips out onto the moss, and then I will go down into the dark and let the threads come.

This is what I do now, in the permanent bronze afternoon:

I cross the dip each day to the tower. It takes me from the first dimming to the slow brightening—what would once have been an hour is now most of a day's light. The Lanth-Ferns weep their pale milk inside the calcified lacework, and the smell of yeast and cucumber is so thick in that hollow that I taste it on the ridges of my mouth. I press my palm flat against the gall. Through the lime-chalk and the leathery green skin I feel the child turning, very slowly, the way all things turn here. I sing to it. Not into the air—the air would eat the song before it reached the pod—but through the chalk, through my own gristle, a low double-noted hum that says only what my parents' hums said to me, and theirs to them: *you are wanted, the floor is warm, there is nothing to fear because there is nothing that has ever needed fearing.*

I do not know if the child understands the words. There are no words. But it presses back against my palm, and that is enough.

---

What I feel, lying on my belly at the edge of the black pool in the evenings, is the whole landscape humming up through the thin skin of my hands.

This is the strange gift of the heaviness. As my body slows, my hearing grows enormous. I feel the Pith-Walkers drifting on the far domes, each footfall a soft pressure-bloom in the root-weave, generations of families riding their living islands without ever knowing I am tracking them. I feel the cisterns deciding, in their slow geologic way, whether to sigh. I feel—and this is the part I have not told the others, because there are no others close enough now to lean my temple against—I feel the old mounds.

The dead are not silent. The Transition does not stop the thrumming; it only buries it. All across my valley, beneath the velvet, the gristle of my ancestors still vibrates faintly with the deep water-tides, the way a struck reed goes on shivering long after the touch. When I press my soles down in the right places, I can feel them. Not as voices. As *texture*. One old Keeper, three domes over, has a thrum like a finger tapped twice and held. I do not know who she was. I never met her in the standing years. But I have learned the shape of her, the way you learn a face, and on the heaviest evenings I lie down where she is woven into the floor and I let my own slowing pulse fall into time with hers, and for a while neither of us is alone.

This is the thing I understand now that I did not understand when I was upright and quick:

We do not vanish. We become the instrument the next ones listen through. Every child who stands barefoot in this valley and feels the world humming beneath them is feeling *us*—the long mounds, the buried gristle, the accumulated patience of everyone who ever lay down between the domes and let the threads come. The Weft is not a graveyard. It is a held chord, ten million years long, and each of us is one more note pressed gently into the sustain.

---

The gall will soften soon. I can feel the jelly beginning in it, the way the resistance under my palm goes loose and giving. A few more days. The child will slip out onto the wet moss and lie there glistening, taking its first breaths through spiracles it does not yet know it has, and the Lanth-Ferns will drip their milk down onto its new skin.

It will not know me. By the time it can walk, I will be greening. By the time it is old enough to press its own palms to the ribs of the towers and tend galls of its own, I will be a soft mound in the dip between two domes, indistinguishable from the floor.

But.

When it lies down at the edge of the pool some far evening, heavy with its own three hundred years, and reaches out through the thin skin of its hands to feel the landscape hum—it will feel me there. A Keeper's thrum. The same low double-noted shape I am humming into its pod right now, gone subsonic, gone permanent, woven so deep into the warm wet mattress of the world that it will sound less like a voice and more like the way the ground has always sounded.

That is enough. That has always been enough.

I press my palm to the gall one more time before the bronze light dims, and I sing the thing that has no words, and the child turns, slowly, slowly, the way all things turn here, and presses back.
