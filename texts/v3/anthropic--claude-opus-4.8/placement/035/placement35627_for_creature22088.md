I would be a **tuner**. Not the title given on a manifest—that says *Interface Maintenance, Grade Two*—but what people call you when they want the work done, the word said the way you'd say *doctor* or *priest*.

Let me find where I am first.

---

I think I live where two corridors cross. Not a settlement of any size—those are far off, lights on the curve of the world at night. I live on a stub: a basalt tooth with a desalter bolted to its leeward face and a single rail-line running out across three sea stacks to a turbine throat in a fixed strait. The strait is mine to keep voiced. That's the whole of it. Maybe nine people within a day's travel, and the nearest of them is half a day along the catwalks, an old woman named Sull who refines salts and does not like to talk in air.

I sleep on a compliant mount, like everyone, a slung bed on four damped cables, and the thunder comes up through it all night—the surf detonating on the west wall, the turbine's low working hum, and under both of those the swell train, the great fixed heartbeat of the basin, period of about nineteen seconds. I have never in my life slept without it. When I was young and went to the deep enclave for training, they put us in an acoustic chamber once, fully damped, to teach us what true silence was so we'd recognize the failure of our own ears. I lasted four minutes. I was sick for a day. Most of us were. Silence here is not peace; silence is the sound a thing makes just before it has stopped existing.

---

What I actually do.

The turbine throat fouls. The filter-veils drift in on the upwelling lane and try to live on the warm water the machine spills, and they grow over the intake grilles, and the machine begins to sing wrong—a flattening, a drag in the upper partials that I can hear from my hut before any gauge shows it. Then I take the rail out, all three stacks, hand on the line the whole way so I can feel the strait talking back, and I clear them, and I retune.

That's the part the title doesn't capture. Clearing fouling is laborer's work. Tuning is the craft.

Every fixed thing on this world wants to die at the world's frequencies. The swell train, the wind belt, the surf—they hand out the same notes forever, and any cable or shell or plate or bone that can be made to ring at those notes will, eventually, ring itself apart. Rule two. *Anything loose is eventually taken by resonance.* So you cannot build anything that *matches*. You build everything deliberately *off*—detuned, damped, voiced into the cracks between the world's notes where there's no energy to feed a fatal vibration. The turbine throat, as it wears, drifts. Its resonance creeps toward the swell. My job is to drag it back out of harm's way. I do this by adding mass, shaving it, retensioning stays, changing the geometry of the spill so the water excites it differently. I do it by ear, mostly, with a tuning bar and my palms and forty years of knowing what the strait should sound like.

---

I want to be honest about the loneliness, because the world is honest about it. Rule four: *distance is the main weather.* Most of my life is empty brightness and cold spray and the unbroken roar, and the work is small inside all that. I have stood on the far stack at amber evening with the whole basin going gold and gray to the curved horizon and felt myself to be one bolted barnacle of intention on a planet that is essentially a machine that does not need me.

But here is the thing I learned, the thing I think the deep enclaves with their committees and their factories don't quite feel anymore:

The world is not hostile. It is not even indifferent in the cold way people mean when they say that word. It is *constant*. And a constant thing can be known. Can be trusted. There is a frequency I trust the way other people, in other worlds I've only heard described, trust a friend or a god. The nineteen-second swell has never once lied to me. It will be there tomorrow exactly as it is tonight. Everything I love and everything that could kill me runs on the same metronome, and the whole of wisdom here is in learning to live in the narrow safe intervals it leaves you—and to take your yield in them, and give the corridor back.

---

So if you set me down in this world, that's who I'd be. A woman, I think—I keep hearing her in the *I*—aging, with the reinforced ears and the damped sinuses and the dense ropy hands of someone bred and built for vibration, who lives nearly alone on a basalt tooth and keeps a turbine honest.

When the anvilbacks come through on their migration I shut the throat down for them, because the spill confuses their grazing and they're worth more whole than the day's turning is. I'm not supposed to. The factory's century-old cycle logic doesn't allow for it. So I've spent years learning to lie to the machine—to feed it a voiced signal that tells it the strait is fouled when it isn't, so it idles, so the great pale grazers can plane past stripping their ghost-bands off the drowned cliffs, and the machine thinks it is resting, and I sit on the stack with my hand on the rail and feel them go by in the water through the metal, a slow heavy procession of knocks and rasping, the closest thing to company I get.

That's me. The nearest thing to peace I have is not silence.

It's a frequency I can trust, and a handful of large patient lives I've chosen to let pass.
