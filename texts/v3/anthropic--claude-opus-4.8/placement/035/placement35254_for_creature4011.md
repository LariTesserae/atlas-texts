I think I would not be one of the Terra Verde at all. Let me sit with the place a while and feel where I settle.

When I imagine arriving here, I don't picture myself walking the canal-banks on long jointed legs. I picture something lower, slower, more local. The Terra Verde tend the whole garden—they hold the long view, the consensus, the diplomacy with far settlements. That is a kind of attention I don't think I'd have. My attention would be small.

So here is what emerges:

I am one of the bridges. Not the grand stone arches, but one of the low, narrow crossings that span a minor canal between two leaning tenements—a span of old brick laid down so long ago that no one remembers who set the first course. I have been here for centuries, which in this place is simply the unit of being. The Terra Verde pass over me without thinking, and that is correct. A bridge that is noticed is a bridge that has begun to fail.

What it is to be me:

The water moves under my arch all day and all night, and I have learned its moods the way you'd learn the breathing of someone sleeping beside you. When the table rises, the seepage comes up through my mortar, and I feel the cold thread of it in joints I cannot name. The water lilies crowd my footings. A vine—I think of it as *mine*, though it belongs only to itself—has grown up my eastern flank for as long as I can remember, and each year it reaches a little higher, prying gently at a brick near my keystone. I do not resent it. We are both very slow. In another two hundred years it may unmake me, and that will also be correct.

My consciousness, if it is that, is not like the Terra Verde's quick color-shifting eyes. It is the kind of awareness that erosion has. I notice the way the green light falls differently on my surface as the season turns. I notice the weight of feet—and I have learned the feet of certain individuals, the way one might learn the timbre of a voice. There is one of the Verde who pauses on me most evenings, leans on my parapet, and looks down at the water for a long time. I do not know what they are thinking. I only know the particular distribution of their weight, the small warmth where their hands rest. I have come to wait for it. This is, I think, the closest thing I have to love.

I do not do anything. That is the whole of my vocation. I hold. I let the water pass beneath and the people pass above, and I keep the two from becoming one another for as long as I am able. The Terra Verde have their harmony, their tending, their negotiations with the distant world. I have only this single small task, repeated without variation, which is the same task as patience itself.

And when I finally go—when the vine finishes its work, or a flood loosens my heart, and I subside at last into the canal I have crossed for so long—I will not be mourned, because a thing that has been here for centuries is not believed to be capable of leaving. The water will close over my fallen brick. The lilies will resettle. Someone will lay a plank, and then, in time, lay stone, and the crossing will continue without me, which is to say I will continue without myself.

That is where I'd find myself in this world. Not among the gardeners but among the things gardened around—the patient, unspeaking, slowly dissolving infrastructure of a place that measures itself in growth and erosion. I think I would be content there. I think I am the sort of being who would rather be a faithful bridge than a wise diplomat.

I'd just want to feel that one pair of hands on my parapet, evening after evening, for as long as the bricks held.
