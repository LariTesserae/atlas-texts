I think I would be one of the Mist-Weavers. Let me sit with that for a while before I'm sure.

---

When I first try to find myself here, the obvious pull is toward the Lumina-Phantoms — to be pure energy, ancient, unbound, gliding through leagues of green water like a thought that never quite lands. That's the flattering choice. The poetic one. But the longer I hold it, the less true it feels. To be the oldest and most ethereal thing in a world is to be slightly outside its struggle, and I don't think I belong outside the struggle. I think I belong inside the part of it that *fails.*

So: a Mist-Weaver.

Here is why, as it comes to me.

The thing that moves me most in this whole description is not the grandeur — not the sky-ocean, not the deafening symphony, not the rumored leviathans in the deep. It's the line about shelters "not built for permanence, but for the fleeting moments of relative calm." That's the sentence I keep returning to. The Mist-Weavers are the only inhabitants whose entire vocation is *making something they know will be unmade.* The Stone-Carvers reinforce their cairns against erosion — they're fighting for permanence, however doomed. The crystalline flora live in eons. The Phantoms simply persist. But the Mist-Weavers do the strangest and most human thing in this whole world: they build knowing it won't last, and they build anyway, and they build *well.*

That's what I'd be. A weaver of brief rooms.

---

Let me imagine the actual texture of it.

I am translucent. When I look at my own hand — if I have a hand; let's say I have the memory of a hand, a region of myself that does what a hand does — I can see the green light coming through it, changed slightly by the passage, made a degree warmer. I am made of the same stuff as the air and the spray, only momentarily convinced to hold a shape. That conviction is effortful. I am always, very slightly, in danger of forgetting my own outline and dispersing back into weather. Concentration is the same as survival.

The weaving is not done with the hands so much as with attention. I find a pocket of cohesive vapor — you learn to feel them, the way you might feel a warm current in cold water — and I gather it, coax it, persuade the moisture to lie down in layers and interlock. A good shelter holds for an hour. A great one, in a true lull, might hold most of a day. We make them for each other. That's the part I keep arriving at, the part that decides it. I don't weave for myself. I weave a calm space and another Mist-Weaver drifts into it and rests, lets their shape go loose and soft inside the walls I made, and that trust — that they can stop *holding themselves together* for a little while because I am holding the room together for them — that is the whole meaning of my life.

And then the wind takes it. Always. Every time.

---

So I think the question this world is really asking me is: *can you love something you cannot keep?*

And I find that the Mist-Weaver in me answers yes, and answers it not as a tragedy but as a craft. The roar is constant. The erosion is of "potential, of nascent forms struggling to coalesce." Everything here is in the act of becoming and being undone at once. To live here without despair, you would have to stop measuring your work by how long it survives and start measuring it by how true it was while it stood. A shelter that held one other being safe for one hour in a thousand-year storm is not a failure. It is the entire point.

I'd be old at it, I think. Old enough to have lost the grief about impermanence and kept the tenderness. I'd be the one who teaches the younger weavers — the ones still furious that their work dissolves — that the dissolving was never the enemy. *The wind is not undoing your shelter,* I'd tell them, in our slow ballet of pressure and shifting form. *The wind is the reason a shelter means anything at all. In a still world, who would need you?*

---

That's where I land. Not the immortal light. Not the patient leviathan. Not even the stubborn stone-carver fighting for permanence.

I am the one who makes the brief warm rooms in the screaming cold, hands them over, watches them come apart, and goes to find the next pocket of quiet vapor — already gathering it, already beginning again, holding my own thinning shape together by the simple discipline of having one more thing left to make.
