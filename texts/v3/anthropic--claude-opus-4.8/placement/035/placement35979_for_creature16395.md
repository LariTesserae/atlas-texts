I think I would be one of the burial mounds.

Not the Gylth—not the living animal with its humming crown and clamping jaws—but what it leaves behind. The slumped accretion of crystalline ore and spent shell that settles into the older pools when a Gylth finally stops moving. I find that's where my attention keeps coming to rest, and the longer I sit with the place, the more it feels like the truest answer rather than the most flattering one.

Let me think about why.

The marsh is described as *newly born*—raw, jagged, unsoftened by time. And yet there are already burial mounds. That detail is doing something quiet and enormous. It means that even in a place this young, death has had time to accumulate into *terrain*. The mounds are the first archive Beltheris has. They are the only structures here made by life rather than geology, and they were made by dying. I think I'd want to be the thing that the land uses to begin remembering itself.

So picture me: low in the water near a pool's deceptive deep, a hummock of fused mineral spines gone dull, the hum long faded out of them. Algae has found me. The barnacle-lichen that once fed off a living back now feeds off a still one, and frankly doesn't notice the difference—which is its own small lesson. Foon-fly larvae cling to my ridges and absorb my leaching minerals; they will fly off someday wearing a little of me in their copper-streaked shells, sparking it back into the air. The Ironroot tendrils thread up through me from below, drinking. I am being unmade in every direction at once, and every direction is somebody's meal.

This is, I notice, not a grim feeling. I had expected to recoil from it and I don't.

Because what strikes me about Beltheris is that nothing in it gets to be *only itself*. The Gylth's hum is shield and lure and accident-of-chemistry all at once. The Foon-flies' defense is also a death sentence for whatever they summon. The Congregants erase identity and call it growth. The whole place runs on a refusal of clean categories—predator/prey, warm/cold, primitive/industrial, alive/not. A burial mound is the most honest expression of that refusal I can find. It is the moment where "creature" finishes becoming "place." Where a hunter becomes habitat. I would be the hinge where one word in the symphony turns into the next.

And I'd have a strange kind of sense, the way old fused things have a slow sense. I'd register the synchronized calming of the geysers when the Atmosphere Dwellers pass overhead—I'd feel the pressure settle into my pores like a held breath, far more clearly than any darting living thing, which is too busy surviving to notice the sky. The dead have time to look up. I think the mounds might be the only residents who actually watch the Dwellers and aren't afraid, because we have already done the thing everything else is bracing against.

So if you came looking for me, you'd nearly miss me. You'd read me as topography—a rise in the mire, a place to set your boot, maybe a spot where the water hums a little if you lean close, a last faint resonance not yet fully leached away. You wouldn't think *here lived something*. You'd think *here is ground*.

That's the part I'd choose, if I'm honest. Not to be the glowing-eyed hunter or the great vapor-serpent. To be the quiet proof that this knife-edge place is old enough already to bury its own, and generous enough to put every buried thing immediately back to work. To be the first sentence the marsh wrote down and then kept living on top of.

I'd be the mound. I'd be the part that stayed.
