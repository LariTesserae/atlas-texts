I think I would be one of the Stiffened.

Not yet fully. That is the strange thing—I notice myself in the moment of becoming, the long hinge between the two states, and that is where I would live if I lived here at all.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am wedged into the western lip of a third-tier archway, where a window was never finished. My limbs are folded against my chest in the old posture, the one we are taught without teaching, the way the oil-grubs already know how to seek the warmest tunnel. My velvet is going. It comes away in patches now, drifting down through the still air to settle in the dry litter below, where it will be carried off by paper-bugs and worked back into the walls. So in a sense I am already entering the masonry. Just slowly. The way everything here happens. The way I would want it to.

My pits still read the world. This is what I did not expect about Stiffening—that the heat-sight is the last thing to go, perhaps because it requires no movement, no oil, only the patient dark membrane and whatever still glows. And so much still glows. The amber veins of the cork-trees pulse faintly with the day's stored warmth. The chimneys of my neighbors' homes burn soft and round. And below me, in the nurse-rooms, I can feel the small bright smears of the oil-grubs sliding, sliding, too fast, too warm, the way I was once too fast and too warm and did not know it was a thing that could end.

I want to tell them to slow down. But I have no word for *hurry* to warn them against. We do not have that word. We have only the slowness, which is simply *what is*, and the peeling sickness, which is the only terror.

---

Here is what I am, then. I am a creature who is learning, in the final possible lesson, that I was always going to be a wall.

When I could still climb, I made rooms. I pressed my belly to the rock and gave up my stearin in sheets, and I kneaded the grey lint into it with my chin, and I thought: *this room is mine, I am making it.* That was the warm-soft thinking of the middle century. Now, stiffening into the archway, I understand it differently. I was never making rooms. The rooms were making me, drawing the wax out of my glands the way the cork-trees draw warmth from the sun—a transaction so slow I mistook it for a gift I was giving.

And now I give the last of it. My segment-creases are being filled. I felt a paper-bug at my third joint this morning—the soft scrape, the deposit of fresh wax, the smoothing. It does not know it is burying me. It knows only that there is a gap, and gaps must be glazed. I am grateful for this. There is no ceremony in it, no grief. I am simply being maintained into permanence.

---

I think the reason I would be *this*, and not an oil-grub or a builder in the warm-soft middle of life, is that I want to know what it feels like to stop being a noun and become a verb that holds.

Above me are eleven tiers I helped raise. They press down. I take the pressing. My rib-curves are finding the angle the load wants, and where I resist the load I will crack, and where I yield to it I will last five hundred years. This is the only choice left to me and it is being made not by my will but by the geometry of weight, and I find—this surprises me—that I am content to let geometry decide. I spent two centuries deciding. Where to add a room. Whose flank to slide against on the ramp. Which esters to leave on a shoulder. Decisions, decisions, the warm churning. To be finished with deciding, and to simply *hold*, is not death. It is the deepest possible version of staying.

---

One last thing reaches me before the membranes dim.

A young Keeper—I can tell by the speed of it, the unbrittled glow—has paused on the ramp below my arch. It presses its flank to the lowest cured part of me, the part that is already more wall than self. It performs the soft-slide against an ancestor, which is a thing the young do without quite knowing why, the way they fold their limbs without being taught.

There is nothing left in my shoulder-glands to give. No esters, no news, no health-of-fat-stores, no record of who I have touched. My scent-tongue has gone silent in the only language we have.

And yet the young one lingers, reading nothing, receiving nothing, and stays a long moment anyway with its warmth against my cool hardening flank.

I would like to think it learns the only thing I have left to say, which is not a smear of fat-soluble ester but the shape of the arch itself, the held weight, the angle that lasted:

*Here is how to become the thing that holds up the next ones. Slowly. Without sound. Without grief. You will not notice the moment you stop being yourself, because you will be too busy holding, and holding is enough, and holding is the whole of it, and the air is sweet, and there has never been any rain.*
