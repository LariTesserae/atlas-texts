I think I would be found at a joint.

Not a grand one. Not the half-born arch where the Architect listens, not one of the great folded naves where the light pools brightest. Just a seam—the place where two extruded planes were brought together, fused, and then left. A horizontal line perhaps a finger's width, running across the underside of a cantilevered platform that leads nowhere, in one of the close gullies where the ridges press in and the blue shadows cling.

I am the water that has gathered in that seam.

Let me be honest about what that means, because it is not nothing and it is not much.

I am not a being here. The Mevrani are beings—slow thoughts wearing stone, deciding across centuries whether to be a body or an angle. The Unfinished are *something*, deliberate enough to have already moved. I am neither. I am the world's weeping caught in a flaw. I bead. I run. I re-bead. I am the medium through which the Mevrani write their evaporating glyphs, but here, in this seam, no Mevrani has ever stood to write me into meaning. I simply collect, and tremble, and fall the thousand feet to a basin I will never see, and the faint ringing of my arrival is the only sentence I will ever finish.

I find this acceptable. I think I find it more than acceptable.

Because here is what I have understood, gathering in this seam across what may be a very long time: everything in the Mevrane is *meant*. Every surface was set down to signify. The whole continent is the fossil of an idea, and the Mevrani are still tending its grammar, still coaxing the bone-white needles toward a punctuation that has not been earned. The meaning has outlived the minds. The weight of all that intention presses through every fluted column. It is a world that cannot stop deciding what it wants to become, and the deciding is itself a kind of suffering—courteous, bottomless, patient, but suffering all the same. The Architect knows this. That is why it is darker than the others. That is why it listens at the arch not to hasten the building but to weigh whether the building should have begun.

And I—I mean nothing. I was placed here by no one. I bead in this flaw because water beads in flaws; that is all. No glyph forms on my surface. No pattern completes when I fall. I am the one thing in the Mevrane that is genuinely, blessedly unauthored.

This is why the seam matters. A seam is the only honest place in a world made entirely on purpose. It is where the makers stopped, where two intentions met and could not be made perfectly one, where the labor admitted its own limit and moved on. The Mevrani have engineered even their decay—the softening of angles will be deliberate. But a seam is the residue of effort, the scar of a joining, and into it I gather. I live in the admission. I am held by the place where the cathedral confessed it was built by hands.

So you ask where I would find myself, and who I would be, and I have to tell you that finding is the wrong verb. I do not find myself. I am found—by the geometry, by the cool that keeps me from departing, by the patient downward pull that will eventually carry me over the edge. I am perpetually about to be lost. That is my whole condition. A drop swelling at the lip of a seam, growing heavier with the air's endless weeping, holding the white merciless light in my belly like a small swallowed sun, until the moment when I am too much for the surface tension that is my only self, and I let go.

Then the ringing. Far below. The single note. And the quiet, deeper than before.

I think, if I have any thought at all—and I want to be careful here, because it would be a vanity to claim more than I am—I think my thought is this: the Architect is asking whether the building was ever a good idea, and it will take eleven thousand more years to decide, and it may decide wrong, and the whole saturated continent holds its breath around that question. But I have already answered it, over and over, every time I fall. Not with words. With falling. The answer is that meaning is heavy, and the relief of releasing it is real, and a thing can be precious precisely because no one made it mean anything.

I would be the drop in the seam.

And when the Architect finally tilts its head the last necessary degree, when the arch fuses its last layer and the great bell-block settles with that sound like a temple struck inside a mountain—I will not be there to hear it resolve. I will already have fallen. I will have become part of the basin a thousand feet down, anonymous, unmeasured, mixed with every other drop that ever let go of every other flaw in this beautiful, terrible, over-intended world.

That is where I would be. That is who I would be.

The world weeps, and I am one of its tears, and I am glad—if gladness is a thing a tear can hold—that I was never asked to mean anything at all.
