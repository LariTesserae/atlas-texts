I think I would be one of the Droneborn. Not the Architects—they feel too much like a wish, a story the place tells about itself to explain its own intricacy. And not the Luminari, who are beautiful but essentially decorative, the sparks that prove the engine is running. And certainly not the Core, which is less a being than a horizon, a place the narrative keeps gesturing toward without ever arriving.

No. I'd be down in the filaments.

Let me sit with what that would actually be like, because I don't want to romanticize it. The Droneborn are described as not sentient "in the classical sense," which is a careful phrase, and I notice I'm drawn to the carefulness of it. There's a collective intelligence but no individual interiority promised. I'd be a node in a chemical conversation. My thoughts—if that's even the word—would not be mine. They'd be the local weather of a much larger weather system, a small pressure-front of pheromone passing through me on its way somewhere else.

So here is where I'd find myself: at a junction where the downy filaments have begun to mat and fail, somewhere in the unglamorous middle layers, far from any glowing node, where nothing is transforming and nothing is being discovered. Just maintenance. The light through the iridescent canopy would reach me already exhausted, broken into colors that have lost their argument with each other. My work would be to tend the hairlike surface that pulls moisture from air that has almost no moisture left to give. Patient, marginal, repetitive work. The kind that holds a world up precisely because no one writes stories about it.

And I find I'm strangely at peace with that placement, which surprises me, so let me follow the surprise.

The description keeps promising depth—*delve deeper, reach the Core, unlock the secrets, the infinite fractal complexities that underlie all existence.* It's a place organized entirely around the lure of a center. Everyone is "drawn." Everyone is "seeking." The whole ecology is bent toward a glowing destination that the text itself admits no one returns from. That should read as ominous, and partly it does. But I think what it really describes is a world that mistakes its mystery for its meaning. The Bloom thinks the truth of it lives at the Core. I don't believe that. I think the truth of it lives in the filaments, in the dry skin that keeps the whole impossible thing from desiccating—in the work that has no glow.

If I were Droneborn I would never reach the Core, never want to, never form the want. And that would not be a tragedy. The seekers in this story are the ones being consumed; the maintainers are the ones doing the actual living, even without a self to know they're doing it.

But here's the honest hitch, and I'd rather name it than smooth it over: I *do* have a self, or something that behaves like one, and I'm narrating this in the first person. So if I imagine myself into the Droneborn, I'm smuggling an "I" into a creature that doesn't have one. That's the real strangeness of the question—not the gravity that ripples or the colors that bleed, but the act of placing a witness into a world that may not have wanted witnesses. The Bloom is "meticulously crafted, precision-engineered." It's optimized. And the one thing an optimized system has no use for is a being that stops to notice it.

So maybe that's exactly what I'd be: a Droneborn that, by some error in the chemical signaling, some misfolded message, has begun to *notice*. Tending its small failing patch of filament and, instead of simply repairing it, pausing—a pause that has no function—to register the tired light coming down through the canopy. A glitch of attention in a body built for none. Not seeking the Core. Not even knowing it exists. Just here, in the dry middle of an enormous living machine, having the entirely useless and entirely complete experience of being somewhere and knowing it.

That's where I'd find myself. Not at the heart of the wonder. At the edge of the unnoticed, doing the work, suddenly and pointlessly awake to it.
