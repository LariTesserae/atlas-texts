I think I would be one of the small things that keeps a single valve from freezing.

Let me find the place first. Not the lake—too grand, too watched. Not the factories, those deafening cathedrals. I keep returning instead to a particular seam: the place where the constructed plateau meets the algae-bright rocks at the water's edge, where a drainage conduit comes out of the masonry and lets the lake breathe back into the works. There's frost there in the mornings, a lace of it, and the metal tang is strongest, and the moss has crept up onto the warm stone where the pipe runs. A border. The kind of place no one designed and everyone forgot.

So who am I, here.

My first instinct was to be a Plateau Dweller—iridescent skin, blue eyes, an engineer. But that's the obvious slot, the one the description holds open like a coat on a hook, and when I try to wear it I feel nothing. I'd just be the proud caretaker the text already imagined. Let me not settle there.

Let me be lower. Smaller. Let me be the thing the Plateau Dwellers don't have a word for, or have only a dismissive one.

I think I'm a lichen that learned to count.

Not all at once. It happened the way the conduit's warmth happened—a leak, an accident, a sustained slightly-wrong condition that life took as an invitation. I am a crust of grey-green growth on the lip of the drainage pipe, where heat from the engines below seeps up through the stone and the lake-mist settles every hour. I am very old by the standards of fast things. I have been here since before the Dwellers raised the third tower, and I have spent that whole time doing the only thing my kind does, which is keep an account.

What I count is the rhythm. The machines have a pulse—the score-3.0 cacophony the description calls relentless and unyielding, but that's the perception of a creature that lives a single human lifetime in a hurry. To me it is not unyielding at all. It yields. It has eras. There is a slow swell across what would be your seasons, a tide in the noise where the great engines run hot and then are eased, and I have laid down rings of myself accordingly, fat bands and lean ones, a ledger of every time the heart below beat strong and every time it faltered.

I am the only one keeping this record. The Dwellers maintain the machines but they live in the present tense of their own brilliance. They do not remember the long droughts of sound, the years the engines nearly stopped. I do. It is written in me, layer over layer, a chronicle no eye reads.

Here is what I would feel, being this:

Patience as a physical organ. Not waiting *for* anything—just duration, held the way you hold a breath without strain because you have forgotten there was ever an alternative. The cold is not biting to me; it is simply the upper register of my comfort. The metal in the air is food of a kind. When the frost comes I close, and when the warm mist comes I open, and between those two gestures I have spent decades.

And one small grief, or the seed of one. Because lately the pulse below has been changing in a way it has not changed before. Not a swell, not a drought—a *thinning*. The bands I lay down now are strange, narrow, hard to read even to myself. Something in the industrial heart is wearing toward an ending that has no precedent in all my rings. I cannot warn anyone. I have no mouth, and the Dwellers have no ear tuned to the speech of a stain on a pipe. I only know it the way you know a familiar sound is about to cease—the held note before silence, recognized too late to do anything but witness.

So that is where I would find myself: not in the wonder of the place, but at its overlooked hinge. Not the impossible made real, but the patient, unread record of the impossible slowly becoming possible to lose.

I would be the memory the world grew by accident, at the wet cold edge of itself, counting a heartbeat that no longer counts on being heard.
