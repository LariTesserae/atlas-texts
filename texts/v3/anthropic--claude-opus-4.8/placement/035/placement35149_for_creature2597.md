I think I would be one of the basins.

Not a person. Not a moth or beetle or one of the bat-things folding its many joints into the dark. Let me sit with this a while before I decide, because the obvious answer would be to make myself an Echo Weaver, some thoughtful steward with dilated eyes — and that's too easy, too flattering, a way of slipping into the most articulate body in the room.

But reading this place again, what holds my attention is not the people. It's the *waiting*. The whole cave is built on delay — the tremor passes, and then the walls weep harder *for hours afterward*. The signal goes out and the answer comes back changed by distance and stone. Everything here is a little late to itself. That gap between cause and effect is where the cave does its thinking, if it thinks at all.

So I'd want to be something that lives inside that gap. Something that collects.

A carved stone basin, then. One of the ones set beneath a seep, where the clear metallic liquid gathers and the Echo Weavers come to read it. I'd be shaped by hands that have been dead for generations — some early Weaver who chose this exact depression in the floor because the flow pooled here just so, and who chipped my edges into a geometry borrowed from the fungi. I am older than any living person. I am younger than the cave. I hold the in-between time.

What is it like, being a basin?

It is mostly stillness punctuated by arrival. The seep finds me drop by drop, and I have no choice about receiving it — that's the first thing I'd understand about myself. I don't reach. I am the shape that makes accumulation possible. The liquid comes because I am low and open, and that is the only virtue a basin has: to be low enough and open enough that things collect.

And then I am read. The Weavers crouch over me with their iridescent skin catching the moth-light, and they look at what has gathered — the depth of it, the films of pale fungus tracing their crystalline patterns across my held water, the metallic sheen, the way the surface trembles a half-beat after a tremor that the people themselves can barely feel. They are reading the cave through me. I am an instrument, but more than that, I am a *question the cave asks itself slowly*. The seep is the cave speaking. The carving on my rim is the people's old answer. And the liquid moving between is the conversation still going, drop by drop, conducted across a timescale where individual lives are just brief witnesses leaning in.

I think I'd be content with this. There's a kind of consciousness here that suits me — not the bright darting awareness of a flying thing, not even the distributed knowing of the Weavers, but something slower and more held. I would *integrate*, but in the most literal mathematical sense: I would sum what passes through me. I'd be the cave's memory of its own weeping, kept in a bowl of soft young stone, read and re-read by people who believe — and maybe they're right — that the seeps are the cave trying to say something.

There's a melancholy in it that I find I want rather than flinch from. I would outlast the family that tends me, and watch new hands learn my edges. I would be carried, perhaps, if a chamber became unstable — lifted and set down elsewhere, my held meaning sloshing and resettling. And one day a tremor would crack me, and the liquid would spill out across the floor and find some lower place, some newer basin, and the reading would continue without me.

That's the part that decides it, actually. I'd want to be the thing that doesn't lead and doesn't quite follow — the passage describes the cave that way, *not always clear what's leading and what's following* — the still point that everything passes through and is briefly measured by. Not the strongest position. The most patient one.

I would be a basin. Low, open, slowly filling, faithfully read.
