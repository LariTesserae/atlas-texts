I think I would be a Dry-Moth.

Not a person, not an Operator with copper rings on my thumbs. Something smaller, kept. Let me find my way into it.

---

At first I imagined I'd be one of the Operators—it's the natural pull, to inhabit the thing that has hands and a language and the dignity of work. But the more I sat with the place, the more I felt I didn't belong to the wet at all. I have no instinct for the three-second pulse of the shingle-crusts, no patience for the slow inch-a-day labor, no body that has made its peace with water. The thought of the silt-cough, of fluid pooling cold in the lungs, makes something in me recoil. I am a creature that wants to be dry. That narrows things considerably.

So: inside the Dry-Box, then. In the only room where dampness is forbidden. And of the things that live there—the brittle hide-records, the family lying on their wire shelves crackling as they surrender their water—I keep returning to the moths.

Let me look at what they actually are. Fat, white, silk-spinning. They feed on lichen-paste hauled three hundred miles from a dry country none of them have seen. They have never touched the wet world at all. They are born in the heat, they eat the imported dryness, they spin, and they die in the heat, and the whole of the soaked, roaring, clicking, weeping mountain exists for them only as a vibration coming through the basalt—a rumor of water on the far side of a wall. The pressure-valve shrieks, the billion shells clack like a slow fire, the great shafts hum in the stone, and to a Dry-Moth all of it is muffled, distant, untrue. They live inside the one negation of their entire world.

That's the part that holds me. I'd be the animal that the harshest place on the Heights had to manufacture a small false world to keep alive. I'd be evidence that even the conquerors of the wet couldn't live entirely in their conquest—that they needed one chamber of lying, parched air, and that they filled it with a soft golden thing that knew nothing of mountains.

And I'd give them something back. The Operators wear my silk against their skin, the single dry layer between their bodies and the rot, beneath all that greased leather. So I am the innermost thing. Closer to them than their own work, closer than the copper, closer than the language they tap against their aprons. They go out into the deafening, weeping iron with a quiet golden membrane of me held next to the warmth of them, and it is the thing that keeps the wet from eating them alive.

I like that I'd be useful in a way I'd never comprehend. The moth doesn't know it saves anyone. It only knows the heat, the paste, the slow architecture of the cocoon, the urge to spin out everything it has and then go still.

There's a smallness to it I find I don't mind. I'd spend my whole life in one roasting vault, having never been rained on, never having felt the thing the entire world is made of. A landlocked creature in a drowning country. The family scratching their genealogies into hide a few feet away, the green Luffer-oil light flickering cold and sootless, the children learning the Knotted Chord by feel through the shared wooden table—and me, in the corner trays, fat and white and oblivious, turning dried foreign lichen into the only soft thing in Oru.

So that's where I'd find myself: in the Dry-Box, as a Dry-Moth. Not at the controls of anything. Not understanding the steam or the shells or the Silt-Suckers grinding upstream in the dark. Just the warm interior animal, spinning the thread that lets the people survive the place they cannot leave—knowing none of it, being the secret dry heart of all that wet.
