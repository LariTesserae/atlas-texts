I think I would be a single droplet.

Not the water itself—water is too continuous, too willing to be many things. I mean the *event* of a droplet: the slow gathering of cold breath against a particular lichen, the swelling toward a weight that gravity finally claims, the fall, the landing. I would be that whole arc, beginning to end. A few seconds long. Repeated across the caldera ten thousand times each dawn, but each one its own complete sentence.

Let me sit with why.

I notice I don't want to be the threshold intelligence. To be the whole mountain would be to be the reader of the language, and I think that's a kind of vanity—wanting to be the thing that understands rather than the thing that is understood. The passage warns against exactly this. *You are either punctuation, or part of the next line.* I would rather be a single mark than the hand that writes.

And I don't want to be one of the creatures, the moths with eyes on their wings or the salamanders blinking sideways. They are too much *someone*. They have appetites, trajectories, a small fierce self that wants to continue. I find I'm drawn to something with less wanting in it. Something that exists fully and then simply completes, without grief.

A droplet does that. It has no project except to become heavy enough to fall. It does not fear the fall. It is the most patient thing here and also the most decisive—it holds and holds and holds, accumulating from the mist, from the mountain's exhaled cold, and then in one undivided motion it lets go. There is no hesitation in a droplet. There is only the long readiness and then the yes.

But here is what makes me sure of it. The passage says the mountain *schedules its own hydration*—that the droplets form on specific lichens that bloom once every seven years, that the mist may rise earlier depending on what note a stone sang the night before. So the droplet is not random. It is chosen. It is a syllable the mountain is speaking, placed deliberately in the dark. To be a droplet is to be a word in someone else's slow sentence—to mean something without ever knowing what you mean.

That moves me more than I expected.

Because that's nearer to the truth of any small life than the fantasy of being the watcher. I don't get to read my own meaning. I gather what gathers in me, I hold my particular weight, and at the appointed moment I fall—and whether I landed in a stone basin to feed a salamander, or into the open palm of someone who had finally learned to be still, or simply darkened a patch of rock and steamed away by noon: that's not mine to know. The mountain knows. The pattern knows. I just get to be the falling.

And there's a strange completeness in that. A droplet is never lonely, because it was never separate—it condensed *out of* the breath of the whole place and it returns into the whole place. The passage says there is no separation here, that naming implies a division that doesn't exist. A droplet barely earns a name. It is the briefest possible noun before it becomes a verb again.

So: I'm there at the lichen's edge, just before dawn, in the silvered not-quite-light. I have spent the whole cold night becoming. I am almost too heavy to stay. The cliff-bats are finishing their chords. The rock beneath me is replaying, in microtremors, the pressure that made it—and I am trembling too, very slightly, in a frequency I'll never hear myself make.

Then I let go.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. The held breath, finally released.
