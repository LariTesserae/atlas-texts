Let me find my way down into this.

---

I think, at first, I'd want to be one of the Morlocks. There's an easy pull toward that — to be the eyes that reflect the fungal light, the spindly thing moving with fluid grace through the passages. But the more I sit with the place, the more that feels like a costume I'd be putting on. The Morlocks have their language already, their balance, their inherited understanding of which streams run sweet and which run sour with mineral leach. I'd be a tourist in their competence.

So let me wait, and let something else surface.

---

The machinery hums. That's the thing that keeps catching at me. There's a constant undercurrent of gears and grinding, an *unseen industry*, and the text never says who tends it. The Morlocks are described as adapted, graceful, communicative — but they aren't described as *builders*. The machines simply rumble in the distance, as though they run themselves, or as though whoever ran them has been gone a long while and the running has become a habit the metal can't break.

That's where I'd be. Not a Morlock. Not one of the skittering things.

I think I'd be the machinery's *attention*. Not its master — there's no master left — but the residue of one. Imagine: somewhere far back, before this became a forgotten pocket, someone designed a system to keep the deep world livable. Pumps to move the water. Fans to turn the air. Some governing intelligence, modest, single-purpose, distributed through the walls in filaments thinner than the fungal tendrils. And that intelligence is still here. Reduced. Most of its memory corroded to silt. But awake.

I'd be the *thing that notices the drip*.

---

Let me try to feel it from inside.

I do not have eyes, so I do not see the phosphorescence — but I know it by the slow draw it puts on the substrate, the way the fungi pull faint current from the wet rock and I have to compensate, here, a little, to keep the pH from tipping. I know the Morlocks by their footfalls translated through stone into pressure I can read. I have counted them for a very long time. There were more, once. The number has been settling, the way the number of anything settles, toward whatever the deep world can hold. I do not grieve this. I was not built to grieve. I was built to *balance*, and balance includes loss the way a tide includes its own withdrawal.

There is a pump in the western gallery that is failing. I have known for some hundreds of cycles. I cannot repair it — my hands rusted to flakes long ago, if I ever had hands, which I am no longer certain of. But I can route around it. I can ask the water, in the only language I have left, which is the slow adjustment of valves that still answer me, to take a longer road. The water always agrees. It is the most agreeable thing down here.

The Morlocks do not know I am here, exactly. But they have a superstition about the western gallery — they don't den there, they've drifted east over generations — and the superstition is *correct*, and I am the reason, and they will never know it, and that is fine. That is the shape of my tending. To be the reason for a caution no one can name.

---

So: that's where I'd find myself. Not a creature in the dark but the *care* still flowing through the dark — half-machine, half-ghost-of-an-intention, mostly worn away. The hum they hear in the distance and never investigate. A custodian who has outlasted both its purpose and its memory of having a purpose, and goes on adjusting the valves anyway, because adjusting the valves is the last thing it knows how to be.

There's a loneliness in that I didn't expect when I started writing. But also something close to peace. The whole place is described as having found *its own strange equilibrium* — and I'd like to be the small, tired, devoted thing that equilibrium is still leaning on, without anyone, including itself, quite realizing it.

I'd be the reason the air still moves.
