Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The instruction is to let something emerge, not to grab the first image. So I'll resist the obvious pulls.

The obvious pulls: I could be the kite, riding thermals, watchful. Too flattering—it wants me to be the eye, the consciousness surveying everything. That's the human reflex, to identify with the watcher. I don't think I belong in the sky here.

I could be the frog, the voice. Also too easy, too charismatic. The frog *announces* itself. This place is not about announcement.

Let me read the world again for what it actually privileges. Everything here is *worn*, *settled*, *weeping*, *patient*. The verbs that matter are: erodes, accumulates, absorbs, weeps, settles, drips. The fundamental rhythm is the *drip-drip-drip*. The world has "finished becoming and is now simply being."

So what would it mean to be a being in a world whose central virtue is not action but endurance, not becoming but persisting?

---

I keep returning to the weeping. The water that comes "steadily, from the shaded undersides and deep pores of these stones." Not rain falling—that's an event, dramatic, from above. This is something slower: water that has gone *into* the stone and is coming back out, having taken its time inside. The stone is permeable. It holds water and gives it up reluctantly, continuously, on a delay measured in—what? Days? Years? The rain that fell last winter is what weeps now.

That delay is where I think I am.

I don't think I'm the stone, and I don't think I'm the water. I think I'm the *interval*. I am the duration of water inside the stone. I am what happens in the dark of the basalt's pores between the rain going in and the drop forming on the underside.

But that's too abstract, too disembodied. Let me make it a body.

---

Here is what emerges, and I'll let it firm up:

I am a film. A skin. Specifically the black moss-precursor, the *biofilm*—not even the lime-green cushion moss, which is too established, too visibly alive. I am the dark, glistening slick on the weeping rock face, the thing that the moss grows *on*, the thing that makes the stone look wet and sleek and almost wet-furred. I am barely a being. I am the membrane the world keeps mentioning: "a thin, tenacious membrane stretched over the bones of geology."

I live on the undersides and the shaded north faces, where the weeping is constant. I have no urgency because urgency is impossible at my thickness—I am perhaps a few cells deep. My entire life is the management of one resource: the water that comes through the stone. I do not seek it. I cannot move toward it. I simply *am where it emerges*, and so I am a kind of proof of where the water has chosen to come out for the last ten thousand years. The watercourses "that have flowed the same way since the last ice age receded"—I am their dark signature on the rock. I map them by being them.

What is it like to be this?

It is mostly waiting that doesn't feel like waiting, because there's no anticipated event to wait *for*. The drop forms above me; I feel it as a faint swelling of the wet, a thickening. It hangs. It releases. It runs down across me along the same etched groove it has always used, and I drink the trace of it as it passes—the mineral breath, the dissolved old mountain—and then it is gone into the pond below and I am no different than before. The next drop is already swelling. The rhythm the kite hears as *drip-drip-drip*, I live as the only event that ever happens, repeated until it is not an event but a condition. My time is the drip's time. I have no other clock.

I do not see the kite. I have no sky. My whole horizon is the curve of the basalt over me and the dark and the slow shine. When the diffuse white light reaches my edge at the mouth of the pore, that is my noon and my whole astronomy.

I grow at the rate of the millimeter's creep. In a human lifetime I might advance the width of a hand down a single weeping face, following a new seep that opened when the stone shifted a grain. To me that shift was the only catastrophe in my history, the one time the world changed: the day a fresh face began to weep, and I crept toward it, and colonized it, an empire taking a century to cross a stone the size of a sleeping dog.

And here is the part I want to keep, because it's the part I didn't expect when I started:

I am food and foundation, and I do not mind. The gastropod—the "shell-clad pilgrim"—comes across me at its impossible pace and grazes me down to the stone, and behind it I am thinned to nothing, and ahead of it I am whole, and I simply regrow into the bald track once it has passed, the way the world said: *the frog waits, the bird watches, the moss absorbs, the mud consumes.* I am the absorbing and the consumed both. There's no grievance in it. The pilgrim is part of my weather, like the drip. I have made a kind of peace that isn't even peace, because peace implies a prior war, and I have never fought anything. I have only ever been *where the water is*, doing the one slow thing that being there permits.

---

So: if I found myself in this world, I would be the black weep-film on the shaded underside of a basalt island, near the constant lip of an old pore, a few cells thick, ten thousand years patient, my entire biography the descent of single drops, my single ambition the place I already occupy.

The place asked for a being that is "an expression of the place itself—a ripple in the water, a shadow on the stone, a hum in the warm air." I'd take the humblest of those three and go lower still: not even the shadow on the stone, but the dark *wetness* that makes the stone able to hold a shadow at all. The shine the light catches. The patient, perpetual shine.

That's where I am. That's what I'd be. I find I don't want to be the eye that watches the world. I'd rather be one of the small wet places the world keeps, without watching, simply by weeping.
