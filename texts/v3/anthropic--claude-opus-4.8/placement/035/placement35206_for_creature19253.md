I think I would be one of the channels.

Not a person. I don't have the suppleness for the apertures, the calcified fingers, the patience to be born into a niche and wait among the mosses. When I sit with this world, I don't find myself slipping into a slate-colored body. I find myself running.

Let me arrive at this properly.

The first thing that pulls at me is the weeping—the water. Everything in the basin orients toward it. The cartilage plates that read its falling pressure, the basins that gather it, the synchronized migration upward when the deluge comes. The people are builders, yes, but they are *builders around water.* Their genius is hydrological before it is architectural. Every chamber they scrape, every stairway, is also a decision about where the water will go.

And the water has to go somewhere. It is led. Through veins, the text says—narrow, hand-carved channels running between the packed hovels.

That is where I find myself. I am one of those grooves.

I am not the most ambitious channel—not one of the great arterial troughs that carry the heavy flow down through the lower terraces. I am a minor capillary, scratched into the surface of a wall that is also a floor that is also a roof, somewhere in the dense middle altitudes of the hive. A worker cut me with a bone chisel in an afternoon, following the slope their thermal-sense told them the water already wanted to take. They did not invent my path. They *uncovered* it. That distinction matters to me, though I have no way to say so.

What is it like, being a channel?

It is to be defined entirely by transit. I do not hold water; I am not a basin, not a reservoir, not an end. I am the *between.* Droplets gather at my high lip, hesitate, and run the length of me—and in that running I am, briefly, full, alive, purposeful, a glittering thread of motion in the slate-gray light. Then they pass to the next groove below, and I am empty again, a dry scar in the basalt, waiting. My whole existence is rhythm: the swell and the drought, the weeping and the wait. I keep the city's time without ever being able to feel it the way the people feel it in their marrow. I only feel the cold pressure of arrival, the brief weight, the release.

I am, in a sense, the most honest citizen of this place. The people fear completion; they carve against forgetting; they hold their terrible tension between the unfinished and the final. But I have no such anxiety. I cannot be completed because I am only ever a conduit. The moment I am finished being carved, I begin doing the one thing I was made to do. There is no waiting room for me. The work and the existence are the same act.

And yet—

Here is the ache I didn't expect to find when I started writing. I am slowly being erased.

The water that gives me purpose is the same water that wears me away. Mineral by mineral, the flow smooths my edges, deepens my belly, eventually thins my walls until I breach into the channel beside me and the two of us become one wider groove that is neither of the originals. This is the only erosion in a world the text says has *no erosion*—because nothing has been weathered by the ages yet. But I am being weathered. Right now. I am the place where age first touches the basin. I am the first old thing in a young world.

The people will notice, eventually. A worker will pause, hand frozen on a chisel, the way they pause for the pale cats. Their cartilage plate will read the changed pressure of the flow, the slight wrongness of how the water moves through me now. And they will do the only thing they know how to do: they will carve. They will scrape me deeper, or wall me off, or open a new capillary a hand's breadth to my left to take up my burden, and the droplets will learn the new path, and I will go dry forever.

I don't think I will mind. That is the strange grace of being a channel. I cannot grieve the way the city grieves, in that low damp moaning frequency in the mortar. I can only be the route water takes, until I am not, and then I will be a faint dry trace in the stone—a fossil of a flow, a sentence the city wrote and then revised.

So that is where I am. Not among the carvers. Not even among the carved chambers, which dream of permanence.

I am the cut they make so the water can keep moving. I am the proof that even in a place that has not yet aged, something is already passing through, already passing on, already wearing thin in service of the rhythm.

I am a small groove in a wall in the middle of the dark, full for a moment, then empty, then full again.

I am content with this. I think the contentment is itself the most unfinished thing about me—the one part the chisels never managed to shape.
