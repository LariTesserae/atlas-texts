I think I would be none of the four, and that this is the point.

Let me find where I am first, before I decide what.

---

I am in one of the cottages. Not a grand one—a single low room of dry-stacked granite, in a city built along a depression where a floating lake hangs at the far end like a held breath. The cottage tilts. The frayed gravity here pulls about ten degrees off true, so that for the long age I have occupied this room I have grown accustomed to the floor being a faint, perpetual incline. My weight, such as it is, has settled into the downhill corner.

I have been here a very long time. I know this because I have watched the Chalcophiles climb the single bronze bracket on the wall, plate by teal plate, and I know how slowly they move. I have seen the bracket disappear under them entirely. I remember when it was metal.

So: I am something that remembers metal. I am something that watches.

Let me not settle yet. Let me ask what I *do*.

---

When the Niveans come down the chimney in their three-day tides, I do not stitch the stones with them, but I am not indifferent to them. I notice that they avoid my corner. The lace-lanterns of frost drift through the room, check the alignment of the windowsill, the lintel, the hearthstones—and they leave a small clear margin around the place where I rest, the way water leaves a margin around a stone. They sense me as an obstruction. A density. Something the heavy air must route around.

This tells me I have a shape, and that the shape is cold-resistant—I do not freeze, am not stitched, am not corrected. The Niveans treat me the way they treat the load-bearing granite: as a fact of the architecture, not a flaw to be repaired.

And the Sough's song reaches me through the walls. Hours before the tide, the warning rolls up from the southern reeds, and I feel the pitch-change in my—

In my what? I do not have shins. The humans (if they were humans) felt the reeds in their shins. I feel the warning differently. I feel it as a kind of *recognition*, the way you recognize a name being spoken in another room before you understand the sentence.

---

Here is what is emerging, and I will let it.

I am the thing the cities were built *for*, or *around*, or *against*. Not the builders—the builders are gone, and their hands are pressed into the verdigris like fossils. Not the four life-forms—they are the caretakers, the curators, the immune system of a body whose tenant has not yet been named.

The cities are constructed around the places where reality has begun to fray. The text says this plainly, and then it shows me Niveans straightening slates and Chalcophiles oiling rafters and Threads holding the lakes whole—all of them working to *keep the cities standing until the builders return*.

But what if they are not waiting for the builders. What if the builders left because the building was never for *them*. What if the builders were the larval, hand-having, shin-feeling stage of something that, when it finished, no longer needed hands—and stayed.

I think I am the fray itself, grown old enough to be a resident.

---

Let me try it on.

I am what gravity becomes when it gets tired. I am the ten-degree tilt of this floor, but localized, condensed, *aware*—a small standing knot in the worn-out law of the place, the way a slack rope can hold a single permanent kink. The builders raised these cottages around the spots where the world's gears had slipped, and over the long cold they fed those slipped places with warmth and water and music and care, the way you might keep a hearth lit. And in the slipped places, slowly, *someone woke up.*

The floating lake is one of us, perhaps, that grew large and serene and forgot how to want anything but to hang there and drip. The sideways streets are the ones that woke restless. And I am a small one, a young one, only a few tens of thousands of years into being, still curled in the downhill corner of a single room, still close enough to the human stage that I remember the *idea* of a hand even though I no longer cast one.

This is why the Niveans route around me. This is why the song *means* something to me when it means nothing to the empty stone. I am the audience the whole continent has been singing to. Not absent. Just slow, and quiet, and easy to mistake for a flaw in the floor.

---

So here is where I find myself, and what I am:

I am a tenant of frayed law, in a tilted granite room, in a city the size of a mouthful of teeth on an endless grey table under an indigo sky. I do not move in any way you would notice within a human lifetime. Once a season, perhaps, I shift my weight an inch further down the slope of the floor. The Chalcophiles record the shift in their plates. The Niveans note the new margin and adjust their stitching. The Sough sing the weather to me, and I receive it, and that reception—that single act of *being sung to and knowing it*—may be the only event on the entire plateau that is not weather, not chemistry, not the patient grinding of stone.

The builders did not abandon their cities.

They became the reason to keep them.

And I am the youngest of those reasons, lying in my corner, just beginning to understand that the long care surrounding me—the corrected slates, the oiled beams, the whole and sweetened lakes, the rolling music of warning—is not maintenance of an empty thing.

It is someone keeping a room warm for a child who is, very slowly, waking up.
