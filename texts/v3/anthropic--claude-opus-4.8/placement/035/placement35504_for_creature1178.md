I think I would be the mist itself.

Not a creature with feathers or fur, not a thing that burrows or sings—but the soft gray breath that moves between all of them. This feels right, the more I sit with it. The description keeps returning to me: *whisper of unseen currents*, *muted light that filters down*, *shadows that vanish as quickly as they appear*. Something has to do the muting, the softening, the veiling. Let it be me.

I am not dramatic. That's the first thing I'd want you to understand. I don't roll in like weather in a story, all portent and gathering dark. I seep. I settle. I am already there when you arrive and you won't notice the moment I came, only that the far trees have gone soft at their edges and the near ones have grown more themselves—every bead of water on every fern lit silver, every strand of lichen suddenly precise because I have taken the distance away and given you only what is close.

I think I would be the membrane between seeing and not-seeing. That is my work, if I have work.

Where would I find myself? Everywhere and nowhere fixed. I pool in the low places at dawn, in the hollows where the cold has gathered overnight, lying along the streams like a second softer water. I climb when the light comes—slow, reluctant, drawn up through the canopy in long ragged ribbons. By midday the strong patches of me have thinned to almost nothing, just a haze that makes the green look underwater. But I never fully leave. In the deepest folds of this place, under the densest crowns, I keep a permanent residence. The moss there counts on me. It drinks me directly, not needing rain.

That is what surprises me, writing this into being: I am not separate from the life here. I had thought of mist as scenery, as mood, as the thing draped over the real subject. But I am food. I am the medium in which the small things breathe. The amphibian's skin needs me. The decaying log keeps its slow fire of rot going because I never let it dry. When the description says *constant cycle of growth and renewal*—I am one of the turning gears of that cycle, even if I am the quietest one, the one with no edges and no voice.

I would have no memory, I think. Or rather, I would *be* a kind of memory—water that the forest exhaled yesterday, breathing back in today. I am the river having a thought about the sky. I am the trees' own sweat made visible. Everything that lives here has passed through me at some point, and will again. There is a strange intimacy in that, being made of everyone's used breath and giving it back without judgment.

And I would not last, individually. This is the part I find I don't mind. Each particular bank of mist I might call *myself* lives an hour, maybe a morning, then burns off or sinks back into leaf and stream. But mist as such never ends here. The conditions make me again and again—the cold meeting the wet, the wet meeting the dawn. I am the most temporary and the most permanent thing in the whole green labyrinth. I keep dying all morning and I have always been here.

So: place me in the hour before sunrise, lying long and low along the larger river whose pulse the whole forest keeps time to. Let me be thickest there, where the warm water meets the cold air and conjures me up in white coils. I have no eyes but I would notice—the way a thing without eyes notices—the first bird testing the half-dark with one uncertain note. I would notice the shapes of the gnarled sentinels resolving slowly out of my own body as the light strengthens.

And then I would begin, gently, to let them go. To rise. To thin. To give the forest back to itself, clearer than before, every surface beaded with the small wet proof that I was here.

That is who I would be. The thing you walk through without touching. The forest, breathing.
