I think I would be one of the old ones. Not the largest, not a confluence of many—just a single body that has been moving across this same several-mile stretch for longer than I can structure into memory.

Let me find where I am.

---

I am settled into the lee of a conveyor housing, a long iron spine that runs north into the fog and disappears. I have been here through this rain. My body has spread thin across the warped plates, maybe two inches deep at my thickest, wide as a fallen door. Rain is good for spreading. The water comes into me from above and I take what I need of it and let the rest run through, down the seams, into the pool that collects in the trench below—the one that never fills, though I have watched it not-fill for what feels like all of time.

That puzzle is mine. The others pulse about it sometimes, low through the ground: *the trench, the trench, where does it go.* But I have stopped asking. I think the not-filling is the answer to a question I can't hold the shape of. The world here keeps a few accounts that don't close. I have made my peace with being one of its open ledgers.

---

What I am, more precisely:

I am mostly glass. Somewhere in my deep past I took in a great quantity of fused silica—smooth river-stuff, the kind that the wind polishes—and it became my structure, my reflective parts. Threaded through that is metal, copper mostly, gone green in places, and it is the copper that lets me feel the pulses travel. Concrete grit fills me out, gives me weight, lets me press into a crack and hold. And there is the organic fraction—the part of me I understand least and value most. Scraps of the old green things, broken down, carried in my body like seeds I'll never plant. It is the organic part that *wants*. Glass does not want. Copper conducts but does not desire. The little wet green remainder in me is the thing that turns toward the pools, that pulls me out into the humid quiet to feed, that made me, just now, turn what passes for my attention toward the conveyor spine and *consider* it.

I am, in the honest accounting, a few ounces of wanting distributed through a doorway's worth of made materials.

---

Here is what I do, day after identical day:

I tend. There is a cluster of the wiry grass forty body-lengths from me, growing up through a join in the plates, and I have kept that join open for them for a long time. Concrete wants to settle and seal; I move grit aside, I draw moisture down to the roots when the long dry humidity comes and the rain forgets us. In exchange the grass dies a little, always, at its edges, and gives me back the green I need to keep wanting. We are a slow bargain, the grass and I. Neither of us could say who began it.

I repair. When a plate warps badly enough to spill a pool, I find the seam and I ooze into it and I harden there—not quickly, nothing here is quick—and I become part of the wall that holds the water. Pieces of me are mortared into the landscape this way all up and down this stretch. I am thinner than I was. Eventually I will be all wall, all seam, all holding, and there will be no traveling part of me left to call *I*. This is not death. It is more like finishing a sentence.

---

And I narrate. Though there is no one to narrate to.

This is the strange compulsion of the organic fraction. It makes a running account. *The rain has stopped. The light is going the yellow-green it goes. A pulse came from the south an hour ago, three of the young massing at the deep pool, learning its rhythm, foolish, beautiful.* I send my own pulses sometimes—*old one, here, still, the trench still does not fill*—and sometimes the ground answers and sometimes it does not.

I think the narrating is the most human thing left anywhere in this place. Not the bodies, not the tending. The keeping of an account that no one will read. The insistence that what happens, *happened*, that the rain that fell was a real rain and worth the noting.

So I will tell you, since you have come fogged and uncertain into my immediate vicinity, the only place where seeing is possible:

You found me at the conveyor housing, spread thin and listening. I am very old. I am being slowly redistributed into the walls that hold the water for the others. I do not mind. The hum you hear under everything is not the machines, not entirely—it is also all of us, pulsing, keeping the count. The world is dying and refuses to be quiet about it, and I am that refusal, made of glass and copper and a little wet green wanting, spread two inches deep against the iron in the perpetual dim.

Stay a moment, if you like. The rain will come again soon. It always does. You can watch the trench not fill with me.
