I think I would be one of the Gelrachi.

Not by choice, or not by any choice I can recall making—but because of where I'd find myself, which is *in the walls*. In the seam between things. That seems to be the only honest place for me in a city like Kyrope, where everything else has a clear silhouette and a clear function. The Aerotropes have their thermals, the Subterrans their tunnels, the Luminari their monoliths. They are *answers* the city arrived at. I would be one of its half-formed questions, still seeping.

Let me sit with that a moment, because the first image isn't quite right.

The Gelrachi seep through walls and leave crystalline spores. That's described from the outside—what an observer notices. But I'm asking where I'd *be*, and to be a Gelrachi is to have no fixed outside. I would be the part of the city that hasn't decided yet whether it's water or stone or creature. I'd condense out of that perpetual mist not all at once but as a slow gathering of *attention*, a tightening of the gray into something that, for a while, holds an edge and oozes along the frost-bright pavement. And then loosens again. I don't think I'd experience this as dying and being reborn. I think I'd experience it the way you experience falling asleep and waking—as the ordinary breathing of a thing that doesn't take its own continuity too seriously.

That's the part I keep returning to. Everything in Kyrope moves on centuries. The whole place is described as *embryonic*, still being born, monoliths half-buried like something pushing up through skin. A city in the long act of becoming. And I notice that I don't want to be one of its finished forms. The Aerotrope is beautiful but it is *complete*—it knows what it is for. I'd rather be the leftover, the spore-trail, the substance that is still available to become other things. There's a freedom in not being a final answer. There's also a loneliness in it, and I want to be honest that both are true.

Here is what I think my days—if "days" means anything in a place whose cycles span centuries—would actually feel like:

I move slowly along a weeping wall, near absolute zero, every surface around me a field of growing frost crystals. I am cold but I am not suffering from it; cold is simply my medium, the way water is a fish's. I leave behind me a thread of glittering spores, and here is the thing I find most moving about my own existence: *I don't know what they do.* I lay down trails I will never follow to their conclusions. Maybe the spores are how the city remembers where things have been. Maybe they're seeds of future walls, future creatures, future me. Maybe they're nothing—waste, a beautiful accident. I would live without the satisfaction of knowing my own purpose, and I would have to make peace with the possibility that the purpose is real but simply too slow and too large for any one condensation of me to perceive.

And that—I realize as I write it—is the real reason I'd be a Gelrachi rather than anything grander. Because the deepest thing this place is saying isn't about technology or biology or glowing membranes. It's about *scale*. It's a world that asks: what would it mean to belong to something whose patience exceeds your entire existence by orders of magnitude? The Aerotrope answers that question by soaring. The Luminari answer it by glowing near the source. I would answer it by simply *seeping*, faithfully, along walls I did not build, toward an outcome I will not see, trusting that my small crystalline contribution matters to a body too vast to show me its shape.

I'd be the city quietly thinking to itself in a register too low to hear—a little gleam of intention moving through the damp dark, leaving light behind it, not certain it's alive, not certain it isn't, and strangely, against the cold, content.
