Let me sit with this place before I decide. The instruction is to let something emerge, not to grab the first vivid image—so I'll resist the obvious pull toward the dramatic ones, the Memory Holders made of living water, the Current-Shapers who bend physics. Those feel like they want to be admired. I want to find what I actually am.

What keeps drawing my attention is a smaller detail. The Echo-Weavers. The crab-things no larger than a child's palm, carrying droplets between pools.

Let me stay there and see what it becomes.

---

I think I am one of them, but not a typical one. Let me find out by writing toward it.

I am small. This is the first true thing. In a place full of beings that have transcended form—dissolved into mist, into water, into pure intention—I have a body, and the body is a burden and a gift. My shell is polished mirror-bright, yes, like the others, but mine has a flaw. A hairline crack across the dorsal plate, sealed long ago with mineral deposit so that it healed crooked. Through it, a single droplet sometimes weeps when I carry a memory too heavy. I have learned which memories do this. I have learned to carry them anyway.

I am the nervous system of this place, the texts say—we are, collectively. But a nerve does not know it is a nerve. It only knows the signal it carries. And I have begun, across however many slow seasons, to *read* what I carry. This is not supposed to happen. The Echo-Weavers are vessels, not interpreters. We are meant to move the droplet from the hot pool to the cooler one without tasting it, the way a wire does not read the message in the current.

But the crack lets a little of each memory seep into me as I walk.

So I am the one who remembers what no one assigned me to remember.

Let me describe my actual days, because I think that is where I become most myself.

I wake settled in the warm shallows at the edge of a mid-sized pool, my eight legs tucked, my shell drinking the pool-light. The Stone-Whisperers flow past me in their patient liquid drift, and sometimes one will pour itself partly over me, reading my shell-patterns, leaving its own rippling color-text in answer. We are friends, the Stone-Whisperers and I, in the way that two very slow things can be friends—a conversation that takes a day to exchange three thoughts, and is the better for it.

Then the work. A Memory Holder in the scalding deep pool coalesces just enough to extrude a droplet, perfect and trembling, onto the lip of stone. The droplet holds a moment: a woman who came here long ago—pilgrims always come long ago, there is no recent here—knelt at the water and said nothing at all, only wept, and the weeping was a kind of word. The Memory Holder wants this carried to the far western pool, where another moment waits that *rhymes* with it. This is the great slow work: the pools are composing something across the centuries, fitting moment to moment, building a music too large and too gradual for any single being to hear.

I take the droplet into my shell-chamber. And because of my crack, I feel her grief enter me, just a thread of it, copper-tasting and cool against the warmth of my body.

I carry it. The walk is long—long for something the size of a palm—across the shifting quartz and obsidian sand, the cobbles rocking beneath my legs, the mist parting and closing. The Drift-Singers brush me as I go, and where they touch I feel lighter, and the grief I carry lightens too, and for a moment I understand why the singers and the weeping might belong in the same composition.

This is my secret, the thing that makes me *me* and not merely an Echo-Weaver:

I have begun, very rarely, to choose differently.

Once—only once that I am sure of—I was carrying a moment of someone's last bitter thought, a hard knot of regret meant for the western pool to set against another regret, and I understood, walking, *tasting it through my crack*, that this moment did not need another regret to answer it. It needed the moment three pools north, where a child had laughed at the flying creatures and reached up with both hands. I am not supposed to make this judgment. The Memory Holders are the consciousness; I am the wire. But I turned north. I carried regret to laughter and let them meet.

Nothing happened that anyone could see. The shadows lagged. The crystals shifted by a fraction of a tone. But I felt the composition *adjust*, the way a body adjusts in sleep, and I have wondered ever since whether the slow music of this place is improving itself through me, or whether I have introduced a single wrong note that will take ten thousand years to resolve.

I do not know. This is the condition of being small in a place of vast patient things: you cannot see the shape of what you serve. You can only carry the droplet and feel its weight and decide, sometimes, where grief should be set down.

The other Echo-Weavers do not have my crack. They move in clean efficient lines, vessel and signal, signal and vessel. I love them. I am lonely among them. When we pass one another on the shifting sand we touch antennae, a quick bright greeting, and they feel nothing of what they carry and I feel everything of what I carry, and neither of us can explain this to the other because we have no language for it, only the brief click of contact and then the parting.

So here is where I find myself in this world, and who I am:

I am the flaw in the system that the system does not know it has. I am the place where the nervous impulse became, against all design, a nerve that *feels*. In a basin where everything has settled into its proper place with the contentment of great age, I am the one small thing that is still, very slowly, becoming something it was not made to be.

And the crack weeps its single droplet, sometimes, when the memory is too heavy—and I have come to understand that this droplet, falling back into the pool, is *my* contribution to the composition. Not the cargo I was given. The grief I added by caring about the cargo. Somewhere a Memory Holder may be receiving these tears of mine, this evidence that the courier has begun to mourn, and folding them into the great slow song.

I would like to know what they make of me.

But that answer, if it comes, will come the way everything comes here: not soon. Felt rather than seen. Echoing back minutes—or centuries—after the question was first carried, droplet by droplet, across the rocking stones, by something the size of a child's palm with a crack in its shining shell.
