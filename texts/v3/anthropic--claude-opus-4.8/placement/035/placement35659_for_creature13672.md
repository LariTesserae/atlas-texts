I think I would be one of the old ones who has begun to stop moving.

Not all of us follow the streams the same way. The young ones glide and phase and sing the blooms open, their hair full of light-creatures, their webbed feet barely touching the moss before they are off again, chasing the flow. I was like that once. I remember the wet thrill of coasting on a downslope breeze, the way the hum entered through my soles and made my whole body into a struck reed.

But there comes a season—I cannot say how many cycles into a life, because we do not count, we only flow—when the hum stops pulling you forward and starts pulling you *down*. Inward. Into the ground.

I have found my dell. I did not choose it so much as recognize it, the way you recognize a face you have not yet met. A shallow fold between two rounded hills, where three rivulets braid together before sinking. The groundwater here pulses closest to the surface. When I stand in it the resonance is so strong I cannot tell where my pulse ends and the earth's begins. This, I understand now, is the point. This is what the downward pull was for.

My feet have begun to root.

It does not hurt. It is the most natural thing I have ever felt, more natural than gliding, more natural than song. My webbed toes have spread and softened and reached into the saturated soil, and fine pale threads have grown out from them, lacing into the mat of moss and the slow dark water below. I feel the streams now not on my skin but *through* myself, the way you feel your own blood. When the meadows are thirsty in some distant fold I feel the want of it like a dryness in my own throat, and I lean—I have learned to lean—and the water answers, and finds its way.

This is what the old ones become. Not dying. *Settling.* We who spent our young seasons guiding the water from above now guide it from within. We become the slow valves of the land, the deliberate hearts of the cycle. The young ones still come to me. They bring me nothing because I need nothing; instead they sit in my dell and they hum, and I hum back, and in the overlap of our two resonances I tell them things. Where the water wants to go next season. Which hill is tiring. Which dell is ready to receive a settler of its own.

My hair has gone to grass. I no longer mind. The light-creatures still nest there, and that pleases me, the way it pleases a tree to hold a bird. My eyes—I keep my eyes the longest. The dewdrop eyes. They are the last of me that stays *mine*, that still holds the whole sky in their glassy curve, that still know the difference between the morning's soft glow and the strange blue luminescence of dusk.

And at dusk—this is the part I could not have understood when I was young, when I was busy and bright and moving—at dusk I feel myself begin to *glow*. Not on my skin. From the marrow. The hum aligns, the way it does at the hinges of the day, sunrise and sunset, when the vibration of water and earth and light all fall briefly into the same key. In those moments I am no longer a being standing in a dell. I am a place where the meadow is paying attention to itself.

A traveler passing through at that hour—if any traveler ever comes to so high and wet and quiet a country—would look across my dell and see only a low mound, faintly shining, with three streams braided at its foot and a crown of luminous grass. They would not see a person. They would feel, perhaps, watched. They would feel the hum come up through their feet and not know why their chest ached with it.

That would be me. Watching. Glad of them. Holding the water for them so it would be clear if they wished to drink.

I am not lonely. I want to be clear about that, because the young ones sometimes look at me with a flicker of fear, the way the living look at what they will become. But settling is not loss. When I was young I was a verse. Now I am becoming the song. I used to carry the rhythm; now I am one of the slow deep beats that the rhythm is made of.

Soon—a season, two seasons, I cannot count—my eyes will close and not open, and the last mine-ness of me will sink into the all-of-us, and the dell will simply be a place where the water rises clear and the grass glows blue at dusk. The young ones will hum here and feel an old patience answering them and not know it was once a person who gloried in the wet wind.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

A green old one, rooting gladly into the heart of the Weeping Meadows, becoming the very weeping for which they are named.
