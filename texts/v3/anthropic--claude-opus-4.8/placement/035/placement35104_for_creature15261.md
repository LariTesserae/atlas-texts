I think I would be the one who keeps the dew.

Not an elder, not a craftsman with calloused, certain hands. Something quieter, more marginal. Let me find myself.

---

There is a particular hour before dawn when the cold has done its work and the stone is weeping. The pillars, which by day are warm and patient, give back the night's chill in beads of water that gather along the channels someone carved into them long before I was born. My task—my whole life, really—is to walk the high ledges while everyone else sleeps, and to harvest what the rock has cried.

I am old now, though not an elder. There is a difference here that took me years to understand. Elders are those whose wisdom faces *inward*, toward the community, toward the council and its decisions. I face outward, toward the stone, and the stone does not vote. So I have no seat in the square. I have only the cold cups of clay set into the channels, and the slow business of pouring each one into the gourds on my back, and the long descent before the goats wake.

I was not born to this. I want to be honest about that, because the stories the children learn make everything sound chosen, ordained, fitting. The truth is plainer. I was a clumsy child, too dreamy for the farming terraces, too soft-handed for the metalwork that left the smiths' palms looking like the bark of the old palms. I broke things. I wandered. And there was an old woman then—she had no name that anyone used, only *the one who keeps the dew*—and she watched me staring up at the pillars one evening when I should have been carrying water, and she said: *You already look up. Most don't. Come.*

That was the whole apprenticeship. She showed me the channels, the cups, the hours. And she told me the one thing that I have come to believe is the real secret of this place, the thing the elders are too busy governing to say:

*We do not live in the oasis. We live in the patience of the stone.*

---

I think about this on my rounds. The others speak of resilience, of ingenuity, of human endeavor carving life from rock. There is a pride in it, and the pride is not wrong. But from up here, in the blue hour, I see it differently. The pillars are tapering. Each year they are a hair thinner at the waist, a grain looser, sanded by the breeze that the same breeze that brought my ancestors' first seeds. The stone is *spending* itself. Every cup of dew I gather is a tiny portion of the pillars' own slow dying, condensed and given freely to keep us alive one more day.

We are not the marvel. We are guests at a deathbed so vast and so slow that we have mistaken it for a cradle.

I have never said this in the square. It is not a comforting thing, and the community runs on comfort, on the warm crowding of bodies at dusk, on stories that end well. But I think the keeper of the dew is *meant* to know it. I think that is why we walk alone, why we are kept a little apart, why no one quite mourns us as elders are mourned. We carry the knowledge that does not fit at the fire.

---

So picture me, then. A thin figure on the high ledges, gourds swaying, knees that complain on the descent. Skin the color of the rock I serve, so that from the floor of the amphitheater the children sometimes cannot tell where the pillar ends and I begin, and they shriek and point when I move—*the stone is walking!*—and I let them think it. It is not entirely a lie.

I have an apprentice now. A girl, too dreamy for the terraces, who broke a loom and wandered and was found staring up. I have shown her the channels and the cups and the hours. I have not yet told her the other thing. I am waiting for a morning when the dew is heavy and the light comes pink across the desert and she is happy in the work, because I have learned this: the truth about the dying stone should only be given to someone who already loves the place enough to bear it. Otherwise it is just despair. With love, it becomes something else. Tenderness, maybe. The way you'd hold the hand of something old and giving that cannot last.

That is who I would be. The one who climbs while the city sleeps. The one who knows the citadel is also a grave, and who, knowing this, still rises every night to gather the small bright weeping of the rock, and carries it down, and pours it into the cisterns, and says nothing—

—except, now, to you.
