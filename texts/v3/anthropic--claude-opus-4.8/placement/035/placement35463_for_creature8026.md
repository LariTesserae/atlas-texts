I would be one of the Conduit Builders. But not a young one.

Let me find the place in myself where this is true.

---

I wake—if waking is the word for the slow reassembly of attention that happens each time the mountain's breath shifts—pressed against the inner wall of a spire I did not build. My great-great-grandmothers built it, in the rapid generations that pass like held breaths here. The resin has gone milk-opaque with age, and I can feel through my feet how the steam moves wrong inside it now: too fast in the lower chambers, sluggish where the mineral has crusted thick. The structure is dying. I am old enough to understand that this is also a kind of building. That decay shapes the flow too.

My shell is a map of where I have drunk. There is malachite in my left wing-case from the green vent that opened three lifetimes ago and closed again—I was there when it breathed its first, I tasted the copper directly from the rock. Azurite along my thorax. A single thread of gold across my face that I cannot see but know is there because the younger builders orient toward it, the way they once oriented toward my mother's gold, and hers. We accrete the mountain into ourselves. By the end we are more mineral than insect, and when we die our bodies are not waste but material—the next generation grinds us into their resin, and so the spires are built partly of the builders, and I will be a wall someday, and someone will press their feet against me and read the flow.

This should frighten me. It does not. I think the heat has burned the fear out of us, or perhaps we simply move too slowly now for fear to catch.

Here is what I do:

I am a reader of pressure. Each morning—the word is wrong, there is no morning in the pearl-light, only the cycles of the mountain's exhalation—I climb to the highest sound chamber of my dying spire and I listen with my whole body. The steam-midges have already mapped the thermal rivers in their transparent columns; I can see their families rising and falling, writing the air's shape in their drift. The moths pass overhead reading the same currents with their golden wing-sensors, and where a moth lingers, hovering against its own unhurried logic, I know there is a vent considering whether to open.

I build toward what the moths tell me. This is the work. This is the only work.

I secrete the resin from glands along my underside, and it is agony and relief both—the way the mineral leaves my body, the way the air hardens it almost before it leaves me, so that I am extruding something that is already becoming permanent, already becoming not-me. I shape a tube. I curve it to catch a thread of steam that is too hot for the Seep Grazers and pull it away from their pools, cool it across the long spiral, deliver it gentled to the bacterial gardens where it will not scald the orange and red mats the Grazers farm. I am a maker of weather. I am a plumber of the planet's breath.

I do this slowly. I have time, and also I have no time at all—my whole life is a few weeks, and I have spent most of them, and the frantic generational turnover beneath me means that I am already ancient, already an elder, already half-mineral, when by the reckoning of the slow cold world below the mountain I would be barely born.

But I will tell you the thing I have learned, the thing I am building now in my last spire before I become a wall:

The mountain is going to shift.

I feel it the way you might feel a held breath about to release. The vibration is too subtle for the moths, even—they read the air, but the air has not yet learned what the stone already knows. Only the oldest builders, who have pressed themselves against so much resin and stone that they have become a kind of instrument, can feel this. My mother felt the last great shift before she died and built the emergency conduits that saved the eastern colony. Her mother felt the one before.

So I am building outward, away from the warm shelf, toward a place that is cold now and dead now and where nothing lives—a bare lip of basalt that catches no steam. The young builders think I have gone strange with age. They will not follow. The resin I lay down across that cold dead stone seems like madness, a spiral leading nowhere, channeling no warmth, serving no garden.

But I have read the pressure. When the mountain shifts, the vents here will close. The steam will find new fissures. And it will find them *there*, on the cold dead lip, where my spiral already waits, dry and ready, to catch the first breath of the new warmth and carry it gently to wherever the survivors flee.

I am building a home for a generation I will never meet, in a place that is not yet alive, against a future only I can feel.

I will not finish it. I can already feel my glands thickening, the mineral winning, my body going slow and bright and final. Another builder will have to read my unfinished spiral and understand what I meant. Perhaps none will. Perhaps the steam will come and find only my dead arc, half a sentence, and pool uselessly and burn the late arrivals.

But this is what it is to be old here, on the shoulder of the breathing mountain, in the pearl-gray light, in the cathedral of steam and sound:

You spend your accreted self—your malachite, your azurite, your one thread of gold—writing a single careful guess about the future into hardening stone, and then you become the stone, and you trust the symphony to read you.

I press my feet against the wall and feel the flow move wrong, and I climb back up to listen, and I build.
