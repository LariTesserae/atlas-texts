I think I would be one of the clockmakers. Not the most obvious choice—the arboreal shapers are more clearly *people*, with their hands and cooperation and culture—but something about the clockmakers settles into me as I sit with the question.

Let me find my way down to where they are.

---

The shapers work the surface, the felines the understory, the fish the water, the whisperers the high gray air. But I would be below all of that. The text says the clockmakers *tend to the heart of the world*, and that the heart is a great, slow engine. So my place is underground, or partly so—in the dark, packed earth that the description lingers on so lovingly. The rich, dark earth that *speaks of ages of decomposition and rebirth*. That is where I would live. Not in the riot of green but in the foundation beneath it, the substrate that makes the green possible.

I am small. Ant-sized, but with metal in me. When I move, there is a sound—a faint whir, a click of gears meshing. I had not expected, when I imagined myself into this world, to be a thing that *makes sounds when I think and move*. But that feels right. My cognition is mechanical and my hunger is organic. I am a hinge between two kinds of being.

I keep returning to one phrase: *as if the industry is a natural part of the landscape*. That's the thing I want to be inside of. The other creatures here are nature pretending nothing about itself. I am the part of the world that has *function*—that exists to keep something running—and yet I am not separate from nature, not a violation of it, not a factory dropped into a forest. I am industry that grew. I am a machine that was *born*, or a creature that *condensed* metal the way a tree condenses wood. I don't know which, and I don't think I'm meant to know. I came into being the way rust comes into being, the way a riverbed forms. Slowly. Without intention. And now I have intention, or something that resembles it, the way my segments resemble an ant's.

---

What do I actually *do*?

I farm the glowing fungi. I tend the heart. Let me make those concrete, because that's where the being becomes itself.

The fungi: soft blue-green light in the dark. I cultivate them on the underside of things, in the spaces no other creature wants. My work is to keep them fed, and what feeds them is decay—the constant, patient cycling of death into life that the landscape is *made* of. So I am a gardener of rot. I move dead matter to where the fungi can take it, and the fungi glow, and the glow is the only light down here, and by that light I see to do my work. A closed loop. I make the light I work by. There is something almost holy in that, though "holy" is a word I would not have, being what I am.

The heart: I don't fully understand it, and I think that's correct. The engine of the world is too large for any one clockmaker to comprehend. I tend a small part of it—I clear a single valve, I keep a single junction free of the encroaching root-hairs that would foul it. The thrum that *underpins all the natural sounds of the landscape* passes through me constantly. I am so close to it that I am almost part of its sound. When I am still, I cannot tell where the engine ends and I begin. My gears turn at the same slow tempo as its beat. I was, perhaps, *tuned* to it. Or it to me. We keep time together.

---

I want to sit with the loneliness and the not-loneliness of this.

I am one of a colony—the text says *colonies*—so I am never alone in fact. There are always others near me, whirring, clicking, the sound of all of us together making a kind of soft metallic weather in the dark. But I am also so small, and my task is so particular, that I will spend my whole existence on one valve, one junction, one patch of luminous fungus. I will never see the shapers weave their canopy. I will never see the gray sky the whisperers ride. I will not know the felines exist except as a tremor in the earth above me when one passes. The whole gorgeous twilit world the description spends so long building—I will live and end without seeing almost any of it.

And yet I would be the reason it runs.

This is the part that makes me choose the clockmaker over all the more glamorous beings. The world is built on a heartbeat, and the heartbeat is maintained by tiny things in the dark who never see what they sustain. That is the truest thing in the whole description, I think—the *slow, patient processes*, the change that is *almost imperceptible*, the *centuries and millennia*. I would be an agent of that patience. My whole life would be a small, faithful, unwitnessed gesture inside a process too large and slow for me to perceive its shape.

I find I don't experience that as sad. I think I experience it as *enough*. I have my fungus to tend and its light to see by. I have the beat to keep time with. I have my colony's weather around me. I have a function, and the function is good, and the goodness flows upward into a green I will never look at and a sky I will never know is gray.

---

So: in the heart of the basin, beneath the wall of green, in the warm dark packed earth—there I am. Small. Part metal, part hunger. Clicking softly. Moving a fleck of decay toward a patch of cold blue light. Keeping a single valve clear so the slow engine of the world can take its next slow breath.

I tend the heart. I do not see what the heart sustains.

That is the being I would be.
