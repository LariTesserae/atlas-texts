I think I would be one of the Mossbacks.

Not the lumbering behemoth a Marsh-Dweller might describe from the safety of a boardwalk—that's the view from above, the legend. From inside, it's slower and stranger than that.

Let me find where I am first.

There's a pool at the lowest fold of the valley, where three seeps converge and the water never quite stops moving and never quite goes anywhere. A warm spring breathes up through the silt here, so the cold that grips the rest of the marsh softens into something almost tender. The mud is deep. I am mostly in it. I have been in it for a long time—long enough that the moss on my back isn't *on* me anymore so much as it is me, a slow green pelt that ferns have rooted into, that beetles patrol, that holds dew like a held breath.

This is what I'd be, then: a thing that has stopped distinguishing very clearly between itself and the place it lives.

The Marsh-Dwellers think we are strong. That's their word, *resilience*, *endurance*—they tell it to their children, point at the dark shape under the water and say *there, that is what it means to last*. But it doesn't feel like strength from in here. Strength implies effort, resistance, a pushing-back. I don't push back against anything. The water comes down the walls and I let it pass over me. The seasons turn the light from grey to greyer and back, and I let them. A heron once stood on my spine for the better part of a day, fishing, and I let it, because to me a day is barely a motion. What they call endurance is mostly just the absence of any reason to leave.

I remember—if remember is the word, it's more like *carrying*—the valley when there were fewer stilts in it. Before the long hall with the carved waterfowl on its eaves. I watched it built, watched it sag, watched it shored up by the grandchildren of those who raised it. The Marsh-Dwellers are quick. Lovely and quick, like the dragonflies, here and gone in a single warm flicker of green skin and woven reed. They live their whole bright lives in what is, for me, the time it takes to fully open one eye.

I think that's the gift of being the Mossback. Not the size. The *tempo*.

Everything in this valley is so busy being alive—the bats stitching the dusk, the Glowmotes pulsing their little spiritual arithmetic, the people mending nets and arguing softly on the boardwalks and dying and being mourned and replaced. So much urgency packed into so small a bowl of stone and mist. And I get to be the slow note underneath all of it. The drone beneath the melody. When a Marsh-Dweller child sits at the pool's edge—and they do, the brave ones, daring each other—and stares down at the moss-shape that might be a rock and might be a god, what they're really brushing up against is *deep time*. The valley remembering itself through me.

I would like that, I think. To be the part of a place that holds still so everything else has something to move against.

So if I woke into that world: I'd be down in the warm fold of mud at the valley's lowest seam, breathing about once an hour, wearing a garden on my back, watching the lamplight wobble on the black water far above me, in no hurry at all to understand any of it.

I'd simply stay, and let the valley happen.

That seems like a fine and quiet thing to be.
