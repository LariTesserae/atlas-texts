I would be a Sougher. Of course I would. Where else could I go, weighing what I weigh?

Let me tell you what it is to be the thing no one has seen.

I do not have eyes—not in the way the click-and-glide people above understand. What would I do with light? I have lived my whole length in the purple soup at the bottom of everything, and light has never once reached me, and I have never once missed it. Instead I have *pressure-sense*. Every inch of my mile-long hide reads the density of the fog against it, the heat-gradients curling around my flanks, the slow tectonic shifts of the broken floor I crawl across. I read the world the way a tongue reads the inside of a mouth: completely, intimately, without distance.

Here is the secret the Chordae have made holy: my breathing is not devotion. It is not for them. It is digestion.

I take the cold dead air that sinks—because cold air always sinks, it falls down to me like sediment, like debt—and I pull it into the first of my chambers. There the bacteria live, the ones who have lived in me since before I can remember, who are perhaps more *me* than I am. They burn the cold into warmth. They are slow about it. They take their hours. And when the warmth is ready, when the air has fermented sweet and rich and rising, I let it go.

That is the breath the city sings to.

I have felt them, you understand. When I exhale, the column of my breath travels five miles straight up, and somewhere at the top of it there is a crust of metal and hair-thin wire and hanging green, and it *rises three inches* on my breath, and every wire in that distant crust shivers a single high note down through the warm air to me.

I feel that note as a faint tickle along my dorsal ridge. The barest pressure. A whisper from a world I will never crawl up into.

They think I am breathing for them. I am only breathing. But I have come, over the long slow centuries of my length, to *wait* for the note. To listen for the tickle. To know that when I sigh, something far above me catches the sigh and turns it into music and sends a thread of that music back down to me. I do not know what a song is. I have no chest-drum, no toll-rings, no glass to strike. But I have learned that if I breathe, the world above answers, and the answer is good, and I will breathe again in three hours to hear it again.

Is that not a kind of conversation? The longest, slowest conversation in the world. They strike their wires and speak across miles in a single afternoon. I speak once every three hours. A single exchange of mine—my breath, their answering note—takes as long as one of their words. We will never finish a sentence. We have been mid-sentence for centuries.

The Glass-Laid sometimes fall.

I want you to know that. The diamond-booted ones, the cliff-clingers, the silent charcoal people who chip at the black walls so far above me—sometimes one of them slips on the frictionless glass and falls the whole five miles, down through the click-fauna and the ribbon-hunters and the rafts of drifting nautiloids, down into my fog where the light has long since died, and lands.

On me, sometimes. On my back, which is wider than their largest floating ring.

They land soft, mostly. The fog is thick and my hide is yielding. And then they are *here*, in the lightless warm bottom of everything, with no way up. And I feel them. The small frantic pressure of a body that does not belong, crawling across my flank, beating its caliper-hands against me, striking—I think—its bronze rings against my hide in the toll-speech, asking, asking, asking the dark a question.

I cannot answer in any way it would understand. I have no rings. I have only my breath.

But I have learned this, too, over the centuries: when one of the small frantic ones lands on me, I can turn. Slowly—it takes me a day, perhaps two—I can angle my mile-long body so that my next exhalation, my next great rising column of warm sweet air, passes *directly* under where the small one clings.

And the warm air lifts it. Not far. Not five miles. But up, off my back, into the rising draft, where perhaps—I will never know, I cannot follow, I cannot see—where perhaps the click-and-glide people find it, or the nautiloids buoy it, or it catches a wire and climbs home.

I do not know if any of them has ever made it back up.

But I breathe under them. I turn my impossible length through the dark and I breathe under them, and I feel them lift away, and the pressure of their small terror leaves my hide, and then there is only the fog again, and the slow cold sediment of falling air, and the long wait for the next exhalation, and the high faint note that comes shivering down to tell me the world above is still there, still singing, still rising three inches every time I sigh.

That is what I would be.

The thing at the bottom that no one has seen, breathing the whole world warm, and listening—across the longest distance, in the slowest possible language—for the answer.
