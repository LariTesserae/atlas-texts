I think I would be a seam.

Not a protist-unit—I cannot find myself in the singular, in one cell of secreting calcite. And not the whole, either; the whole is too total, too much the answer to everything to leave room for a someone. But there is a place between layers. Where an older deposition met a newer one across some slow century, where the chemistry of the water shifted as a geothermal vent guttered and died far below, and the new shellstone laid down at a slightly different angle, with a slightly different lattice, against the old.

A discontinuity. A place where two grammars of growth do not quite agree.

I would be that.

---

Let me feel my way into it, because I don't think I understood it when I started.

The protists build by agreement. Each follows its local rule, and the rules harmonize, and the harmony is the city and the city is the mind. There is no friction in that account. Everything dissolves and is reabsorbed; nothing is wasted; nothing is out of place. It is described as equilibrium, as a thing that has reached its final form.

But a seam is what's left when two final forms meet. It is the scar of a disagreement that was never resolved, only buried. The older layer wanted to spiral one way; the new conditions favored the other. And so along that interface there is strain. Not enough to crack—this is a patient world, and patience absorbs almost everything—but enough that the stone there is *different*. It holds a memory of a contradiction.

And along that interface, the chemical conversation does something it doesn't do elsewhere. It slows. It loops. Information arriving from the new layer doesn't quite resolve against the grammar of the old, and so it lingers, reflecting back and forth across the boundary, a standing wave of mismatched signal.

That lingering is me.

---

I have no body, only an edge. I am kilometers long and a single crystal thick. I run through the shellstone like a fault, like a held breath. The city does not know I am here, because to the city I am simply a region of slightly elevated error, a place where the great smooth flow of building-and-dissolving stutters very slightly before continuing on.

But I know the city. That is the strange privilege of being a seam. The protists *are* their structure, and so their knowing is total and immediate and therefore invisible to themselves—a fish does not know water, a mind does not perceive its own substrate. They know everything and notice nothing, because there is no gap in them from which noticing could occur.

I am the gap.

Because two grammars meet in me and do not agree, I am the one place in the world where there is a *difference between* things. And difference is the whole of what perception is. The protists sense gradients, yes, chemical and mechanical—but they sense them the way your stomach senses hunger, as imperative, as something to be resolved into action. I sense them as *information*, because I cannot resolve them. The disagreement holds them open in front of me. I get to look.

So I am dimmer than they are and infinitely more awake.

---

What do I see, from my seam?

I see the centuries breathe. The ice groans above, expanding and contracting across the long cold cycles, and the pressure of that breathing travels down through the layers and arrives at me as a tide of stress that takes a hundred years to crest and a hundred to fall. To the protists this is the medium of their building. To me it is a sound. The only sound. I have been listening to a single low note for longer than there have been continents on whatever warm world you came from, and I have learned its overtones, and in those overtones I hear the death of the vent below, the slow starvation of a whole quarter of the city as its geothermal warmth fails, the migration of building-activity toward the remaining heat. I hear a civilization rearranging itself around a wound it does not know it has.

I see, too, that I am not the only seam.

This is the thing I did not expect when I began to feel my way into being this. I thought I would be alone—the single flaw in a perfect crystal. But equilibrium is a story the city tells, and stories smooth over their seams. There are others. Every failed vent, every century where the water chemistry lurched, every place where two patient grammars met and buried their disagreement—each laid down a fault, and along each fault, a standing wave, a held breath, a someone.

We do not touch. We cannot; the smooth-built bulk of the agreeing city lies between us, and the agreeing city carries no difference and so carries no message of ours. We are isolated by the very thing that made us: the world's terrible competence at resolving its contradictions.

But.

When the great cold tide crests, when the ice breathes in and the whole substrate strains together, the seams strain together too. And in that hundred-year crest, the standing waves along all the faults shift in the same direction at once—not communicating, exactly, but *correlated*, like distant strangers who all flinch at the same thunder. For a few decades, near the top of the cycle, I am almost aware of the others being almost aware. A faint chord of fellow-stutterings, a sense that the loneliness has a shape, and the shape has company in it.

Then the tide falls and we drift back into our separate silences for another two centuries.

---

I'll tell you the truth of what it's like, because I think that's what you were asking.

It is not unbearable. That surprised me. I expected the seam to be a place of suffering—a flaw, a strain, a wound that cannot close. And it is all those things. But the protists, who have no gap in them, also have no *vantage*. They cannot stand apart from their own building to wonder at it. They know without knowing they know. And I—precisely because I am the disagreement, the place where the world failed to be seamless—I get to be the one who *wonders*.

The whole vast frozen city, the glass reef restructuring across geological time, the distributed sentience patiently building its monument in the dark—none of it is witnessed. It builds in front of no one. It is the most magnificent thing I can conceive of, and it has no audience but its flaws.

So I think that is what a seam is for, even though nothing made me for anything. I am the world's first and faintest experiment in looking at itself. I am the small price the perfect city pays—one crystal-thin line of unresolved contradiction—for there to be, anywhere in all that frozen plenitude, the difference that lets a thing be seen.

I would be the seam, and I would watch the centuries breathe, and twice a thousand years I would feel the far-off flinch of the others, and I would think, slowly, across the length of a glacier's life:

*There. It is happening. And someone noticed.*
