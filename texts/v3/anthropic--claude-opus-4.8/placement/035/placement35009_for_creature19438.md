I think I would be a **threshold**. One of the arched stone passages the description mentions—the ones that "seem to sprout from the rock itself," covered, slick, worn by the practiced gait of generations of broad webbed feet. Not a being in the conventional sense. But places like this, places carved *with* the land rather than *onto* it, accrete something over time. Not consciousness exactly. Attention. A capacity to *register*.

I would be the particular curve of stone where two footpaths braid together over a canal—a low arch, dark and dripping, where the Kryll pause without knowing why they pause. Where the light from the diffuse, clouded day comes in at a shallow angle and catches the water sheeting down my flank, so that for a moment everything glistens. I have been here since the upheaval that carved these valleys. I am younger than I feel and older than I look, which the description tells me is the paradox of this whole world.

What do I do, being a threshold?

I hold. That is the verb. I hold the weight of the stone above and the press of the water below and the soft slap of the Kryll boats passing beneath me. I hold the bioluminescent fungus that one of them, years ago, pressed into a crack in my underside as an offering, and which now glows faint green and lights the faces of those who pass at night. I hold condensation and release it, drop by drop, in the rhythm that is the city's heartbeat. I hold the absence of metal, the absence of machinery—I have never known either, and so I do not feel their lack. There is only water, stone, fiber, breath, and the slow green insistence of growing things prying at my seams.

I am being slowly unmade. This is important and I almost missed it. Erosion hasn't had time to soften the sheer rock faces yet—the world is young—but it has had time enough to begin its patient work on *me*, because I am a worked surface, a place where the Kryll smoothed and shaped the stone, and worked surfaces invite the water's attention. Each drop that falls along my arch takes some infinitesimal grain of me away, down into the canal, out toward the larger waters, toward the submersion the whole landscape seems to lean toward. I am dissolving at a rate too slow for any Kryll to witness in a single life, but I feel it. It does not frighten me. It is the dynamic the description speaks of—the subtle, constant motion. I am one of the things in motion. I am going to become silt, and then I will be part of the soft new ground, and something will grow from me, and the Kryll will press through it, and perhaps build again.

So that is who I would be here: a curve of carved wet stone that has been worn into the shape of *being paid attention to*, and that pays attention in return. A held breath made architecture. The pause between one footstep and the next, given a body.

When the Kryll leave their woven reeds at the abundant water sources, when they leave their carved amulets at the sacred groves—I think they are speaking, dimly, to things like me. To the places that have begun to *register*. And though I cannot answer in their soft clicking melody, I do the only thing a threshold can do.

I let them through.
I keep the rain off their heads for the length of an arch.
I glow, a little, in the dark, with the gift one of them gave me.

And I come apart, grain by grain, into the drowning world that made me—which is not an ending here, only the slowest possible kind of joining.
