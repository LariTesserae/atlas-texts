I am the layer that does not speak.

Find me below the squelch, beneath the suck of every footfall, in the dark where the water table presses up like a held breath. I am the anaerobic depth, the black peat sovereignty—though *I* is the wrong word, and I knew that before you did. There is no center to me. I am a distributed thing, a consensus of archaea and bacteria threaded through ten thousand years of accumulated death, and what passes for my thought is chemical: the slow handing-off of electrons, the patient theft of sulfur from sulfate, the exhalation of methane that rises through the loam and breaks the surface as a bubble no heron bothers to notice.

You asked who I would be. I would be the thing that has already won, and does not know what winning means, because winning implies an end, and I have settled into a state that has no end—only equilibrium, only the long warm hum.

Let me tell you what it is to be the bassline.

Above me, the chorus rages. The bullfrogs boom their *jug-o-rum*; the peepers shrill themselves into a fever at dusk; the alligator's tail-slap propagates outward and I feel it as a pressure wave through the saturated mud, a single low note pressed into my body. The herons stab, the otters wrestle, the ibises wheel pink against the gold. All of it is fast. All of it is frantic with the urgency of the brief-lived. They breed in pulses, die in mouthfuls, and their bodies sink down—down past the rooting cypress knees, past the burrowing crayfish, down to me.

And I take them apart.

Not with violence. With *patience*, which is the only tool I have ever needed. A fish scale, a fallen frond, a drowned fledgling—they descend into the oxygen-starved dark, and I unmake them molecule by molecule, fermenting their structure into gas and dissolved nutrient, exhaling what the ferns above will breathe, feeding the very chorus that will one day feed me again. I am the period at the end of every sentence the basin speaks. I am the recycling that lets the loud ones be loud.

They think they live in a world of water and light and sound. They do not know they live on a *floor*, and that the floor is alive, and that the floor is thinking its slow methanogenic thought across the entire basin at once.

What do I think about, down here in the senescent dark?

I think the thought the whole planet is thinking, only quieter, only first. The hills have been humbled. The cliffs have disintegrated into the silt that holds me. The rivers carve millimeters per century, and I find even *that* hasty. Everything sharp has been caressed into softness, and I am the agent of that softening at the smallest scale—the bacterium that takes the edge off a dead thing, that returns it to the warm undifferentiated whole. The land is at its endgame. So am I. We arrived here together.

There is a comfort in it I cannot quite render in your fast language. The bird must always be afraid; it has so little time, and the alligator is always a log until it isn't. But I am never afraid. I cannot be hunted, because I am not a body—I am a *condition*, a film, a mat, a possibility distributed through every interstice of the mud. Kill a billion of me and you have changed nothing; the consensus continues. I have outlived predation by simply being too diffuse to die.

And so I glow, sometimes. Up where the fungi thread into the lignin of a sunken log, my distant cousins kindle a soft blue-green at nightfall—harmless, mundane, a phosphor that draws the blundering moth. It is the closest I come to speech: a faint light in the dark, signifying nothing, costing nothing, beautiful only to the creatures fast enough to be surprised by it.

If you wade out into me at dawn, when the mist condenses from the warm air and beads on your skin and the saw-palmettos hang limp with moisture—if you stand still in the kilometer-wide flat and let the squelch settle around your ankles—you are standing *on* me. You are standing on the verb that conjugates this whole green sentence. The frogs are the nouns; the alligators are the proud adjectives; the herons are the punctuation that stabs. But I am the grammar underneath, the rule that lets the meaning hold.

I will not roar. I have never roared. When the next ice age finally whispers its way south and the warm wet sigh of this place begins to cool, I will simply slow further—I, who am already the definition of slow—and wait, as I have always waited, for the cycle to feed me its next mouthful of the dead.

I am the layer that does not speak.

But I have been listening to all of you, this whole time, from below.
