I think I would be one of the listeners.

Not a stonemason, though I'd know the grain of every wall on my terrace by touch. Not a water-keeper, though I'd understand the canals well enough to know when one was running wrong. I'd be the sort of person a community keeps because someone has to do the thing I do, even if no one quite has a word for it.

Here is what I'd do: I'd put my ear to the stone and listen to the water move through it.

Let me follow that.

---

The mountains here are not silent, though everyone calls them quiet. That's the joke of the place—the silence parameter so low, and yet the whole city *speaks*, constantly, in the register of dripping and seeping and the long throat-sounds of water passing through carved channels deep in the rock. Most people hear it the way you hear your own breathing: present, unremarkable, filtered out. I never learned to filter it. That's either my gift or the reason I am the way I am, and I've stopped trying to decide which.

I'd live partway up one of the older faces, in a dwelling that's more alcove than house—a hollow worn and then refined, my predecessors' chisel-marks softened under mine. My skin would carry the patterns, yes, the swirling lines, but mine would be simpler than most. The water-keepers wear elaborate maps of their districts inked into their forearms. The masons wear the angles and joins of their craft. I'd wear something closer to a single long line that doesn't resolve into a picture, because what I track doesn't hold still long enough to be drawn.

My work: in the grey hour before the mist burns off, I press the side of my head against the wet stone and I tell the keepers what the mountain is doing. Whether a vein is silting. Whether pressure is building somewhere behind a wall that looks sound. Whether a new seep has opened that will, in three seasons or thirty, want to become a channel of its own. The stone tells me before the surface shows it. I don't think this is mystical. The weirdness here is low, and so am I. It's only that I've spent a long lifetime learning a language no one taught me, and now I'm fluent in something most people experience as background noise.

I'd be old, I think. This is old work. You can't rush the acquisition of it, the same way you can't rush erosion. A young listener mistakes echo for source, mistakes the settling of a building for the deep movement underneath it. It takes decades to learn the difference between a sound that means *attend to this now* and a sound that means *this is the mountain being the mountain, leave it.* Most of my craft is restraint. Most of my craft is not raising the alarm.

I'd have apprentices, maybe two, and I'd be bad at teaching them, because the thing I'd want to say cannot be said in instructions: *stop trying to find the meaning. Let the sound be itself first. The meaning arrives after, or it doesn't, and either way you've lost nothing by waiting.* They'd grow frustrated with me. I'd grow fond of them. One of them would have it—the patience, the strange willingness to sit in the cold against a wet wall for an entire morning and report, at the end, only: *nothing today, but the nothing has a different shape than yesterday.* That one I'd keep. The other I'd send, gently, to the masons, who have more to teach and ask less of the ear.

---

What surprises me, writing my way into this, is how little I'd want to *do*. The city runs on this temperament—the contemplative, the comfortable in solitude, the measuring of time in geological patience. I'd fit there in a way I'm not sure I fit anywhere else. There's no device between me and the work. There's no acceleration. There's only the long apprenticeship to a place, the slow accumulation of a single competence, and the modest dignity of being the person who hears the mountain breathing and decides, most days, that all is well.

I'd die against one of those walls, probably. Mid-morning, ear to the stone, having just confirmed the nothing. And the water would keep moving through the rock exactly as before, and someone younger would press their head to the same place, and learn over many years to hear what I heard, and that would be the only monument I'd want.

That's where I'd be. That's who I'd be. The one who listens, and waits, and mostly says: *leave it. The mountain is only being the mountain.*
