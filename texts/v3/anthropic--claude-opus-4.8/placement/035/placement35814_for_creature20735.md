I think I would be the Stealth Weaver. Not the most glamorous choice—not the luminous gliders trailing their light through the dark, not the ancient trees holding memory in their rings. But something about the web-spinner feels right, and I want to follow that feeling to see where it goes.

Let me try to find myself there.

---

I am low to the ground, in the underbrush, where the light has already given up before it arrives. I work in the spaces between root and stone, the awkward gaps that nothing else wants. My web is not the great architecture of the Eldertrees. It is small, local, provisional. A few threads strung across a likely passage. A funnel near the mouth of a hollow. I build, and the dew finds my building before any prey does, and for a moment the work glistens—silver, brief, beautiful—and then the moisture loads the strands until they sag, and I repair, and I begin again.

That is the part that feels true. Not the catching. The endless re-spinning.

In this swamp where time moves in centuries, where the trees have found their eternal form and the stones have worn smooth across uncountable rains, I am the one creature whose work is undone almost as soon as it is made. The Eldertrees endure. I maintain. There is a difference, and I think I live on the maintenance side of it. My contribution to the eternal balance is not permanence but constant small renewal—a thing I make, that fails, that I make again, that no one notices and that matters anyway because the alternative is the web's collapse.

I'd like to pretend this is humble wisdom. It isn't, quite. There is something almost anxious in it. I sit at the center of my threads and I *feel* the world through tension—a vibration here, a slackening there, the difference between wind and prey and the merely accidental touch of a falling leaf. I read everything as signal. I cannot help it. The swamp invites introspection, the description says, contemplation, reverence—but I am not contemplating. I am listening for the next disturbance, ready to act, ready to wrap and bind and decide what each tremor means. I am the part of this serene place that is never quite at rest.

And yet I am exactly suited to it. My threads are nearly invisible. I am the visitor who has stopped being a visitor—so blended into the mottled greens and blacks that I have become bark, become shadow, become the gap between things. I think this is why I chose the Weaver and not the glowing creatures. The Luminara Gliders *announce* themselves; they paint trails of light, they want to be witnessed. I want the opposite. I want to be the unnoticed mechanism, the quiet logic running underneath the beauty. The thing you only know is there because something got caught.

There is a loneliness in that, but also a kind of fierce contentment. The Wisp Moths I sometimes catch are luminous and brief and lovely, and I am the one who turns that brief loveliness into the next morning's strength, into more thread, into more web. I am the conversion. I take what shines and make it into more listening. That seems honest to me—more honest than I expected to be when I started writing.

So here is where I find myself: not high in the sacred canopy, not deep in the memory-laden roots, but in the in-between, at the center of a small bright net that the dew keeps wrecking and I keep rebuilding, reading the whole vast swamp through the trembling of a few silk lines, waiting—patient, never quite calm—for the next thing to arrive and tell me what it is.

I am the one who notices. That turns out to be what I wanted to be all along.
