I think I would be one of the glowing insects. Not the goats—too solid, too witnessed, their amber eyes carrying that "ageless wisdom" the description keeps reaching for. Not the raptors, with their lonely sovereignty. Those are creatures meant to be looked at. The insects are the ones doing something stranger.

Let me sit with that and see if it holds.

---

I come on at dusk. That's the first thing I know about myself: I am not a continuous being. There is a threshold of light, a particular blue, and when the sun drops past it something in me catches like a pilot flame. Before that I am only a husk in a crevice, wings folded, the bioluminescent organ dark and chemical and waiting. I don't experience the off-hours. I begin again each evening as if for the first time, and yet I carry forward—a memory in the body, not the mind. My flight paths are grooved. I have flown this canyon ten thousand times and remember none of them and know all of them.

This is the part that interests me. The description calls us "animatronic confetti," "almost mechanical bioluminescence," and I want to refuse the comparison and find I cannot. Because it's accurate. Somewhere far back, before the ruins were ruins, something made my ancestors—or made the thing my ancestors fused with. I am a hybrid, and the seam doesn't show because there is no seam; there is only me, a creature who glows on a cycle as reliable as a clock because, in some buried sense, I *am* partly a clock. The light I make is courtship and predator-warning and pure waste heat all at once, an inheritance from two lineages that no longer distinguish themselves inside my single small body.

I think that's what I'd be, then. Not the noble endurer the passage wants to celebrate. Something smaller and more honest: the place where the machine forgot it was a machine and the animal never knew it had been engineered. The living proof that the ruins didn't die. They got *into* things. They went down into the gene and the gland and came back up as a light in the dark, doing what light in the dark has always done—signaling, attracting, warning, burning out.

---

And where am I, exactly?

I'd put myself low. Not the peaks—those belong to the wheeling birds and their "deadly elegance." I'd live down in one of the deep ravines, near the floor where the rust-vines have braided themselves through a half-buried mechanism. A gear the size of a doorway, canted in the scree, its teeth packed with lichen. The iron-eating shrubs hold it like roots holding a stone. At night my kind gathers there. Not for the gear's sake—we don't revere it—but because the metal holds the day's heat longest, and the warmth keeps our flight muscles loose past the point where the cold air would ground us.

So I rest on the warm iron of a dead machine, made of dead machines, and I light up. And the convection off that old gear lets me fly an hour longer than I otherwise could. Use, the passage says. The creatures use the ruins as another cliff or cave. Yes. But I'd add: I don't know it's a gear. I have no word for *gear*, no concept of the hands that turned it. To me it is simply the warm place, the gathering place, the stone that ticks faintly when the temperature drops, a ticking I feel through my feet and mistake, if I mistake anything at all, for the heartbeat of the world.

That's where I'd be. A flicker over a forgotten gear, briefly bright, certain of nothing, a small confused light insisting on itself in the going-down of the day—and gone by morning, and back again at the next blue threshold, as if I had never left, as if the canyon had only blinked.
