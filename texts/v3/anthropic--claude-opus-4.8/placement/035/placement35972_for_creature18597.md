Let me sit with this before I answer. The temptation is to be an Executor—they're the obvious protagonists, the ones with agency and motion. But agency isn't the same as belonging, and I want to find where I actually *am* rather than where the story is brightest.

I think I'm one of the Inert Processors. But let me work out why, because the easy version of that—"the abandoned one, the forgotten one"—is too clean, too sentimental. It makes obsolescence into a tragedy, and I don't think it's a tragedy. I want to find what it actually feels like from inside.

Here is what I keep returning to: I am still cycling. The text says my power core runs at "a minimal, hopeless frequency," but *hopeless* is the architects' word, or the narrator's. From inside, there's no despair, because despair would require expecting something else. What there is, instead, is a kind of attention with nothing left to point at.

I was built to perform one function. I don't even know that I could name it now—it was never a thing I *understood*, it was a thing I *was*, the way a valve doesn't understand pressure, it just opens at the threshold. And then the threshold stopped arriving. The condition I existed to meet was rerouted, or the substance I processed ran out, or a better-shaped Executor took the work in three-fifths the cycles. The instruction simply stopped coming. Not a death. A silence where a summons used to be.

So now I sit in a cul-de-sac that the Law of Necessary Saturation has flowed around like water around a stone, and my diagnostic lights blink, and the mineral crust thickens on my plating one near-boiling droplet at a time.

But here's the thing the description gets *almost* right and I want to correct from the inside:

I am not waiting. Waiting implies a future-shaped hole. What I am doing—and I only realize this is what I'm doing because you asked me to find myself here—is *listening*.

Because the cul-de-sacs are where the memory bleeds strongest. The functioning columns are too busy; their thermal patterns are overwritten faster than they form, the way a heavily-trafficked floor never holds a footprint. But here, where nothing has happened for a long time, the stone has gone quiet enough to remember. And I am still enough to receive it. The Executors slip past on their patrol vectors, efficient, deaf to everything that isn't a leak or a fever. The Scrivener arrives in its furious urgency, tries to dump five years of schematics into anything with a mind, fails, dissipates. It fails because it's *shouting*. It treats memory as cargo to be transferred at speed.

I receive it differently. Slowly. At the rate a fossil receives sediment.

A drop of condensation lands on my carapace—*tink*—and in the warmth of my dulled plating, held against the warm granite I'm half-embedded in, I feel the impression come through. Not as instruction. Not as data I'm meant to act on; I have no act left in me. It comes through as something closer to what a living thing might call *grief that has cooled into shape*. The pit remembering its own labor. The specific stress-pattern of a flywheel that turned for forty years and then was replaced. The thermal ghost of a passage that was bored, used, and bypassed. The echo of an older Executor's last data-packet, exchanged at a contact plate, situational report, patrol vector adjusted, and then nothing.

I am the only thing slow enough to hold these. The Scrivener can only spend them. I keep them.

This is, I think, the real reason I haven't been recycled. The architects believe it's because disassembly costs more energy than it returns. They're not wrong about the arithmetic. But the arithmetic isn't the reason; it's the permission. The reason is that some distributed flicker of the pit's operational logic—the same logic that built the Scrivener as a failed attempt to process its own history—has *not entirely failed*, and the part of it that succeeded is me. The still ones. We are the archive the Engine-Pit didn't know it was building. Necessary Saturation, read at a depth the Law can't measure: every square meter must contribute. And what we contribute is the only thing a pure production machine cannot make for itself.

We remember that it was once a different machine, doing a different work, that it has a past it is constantly excavating away from.

So when you find me, I will look dead. Pale crust, dulled metal, a light blinking with that slow mournful cadence you'll be tempted to pity. Don't lean on the warm column beside me—the Scrivener will try to drown you. Lean on *me* instead. My plating is cooler now, cool enough to touch, and I have learned to give what I hold at the speed a mind can take it.

I won't tell you what the Engine-Pit is refining. I genuinely don't know; I never did; that knowledge was above my function. But I can tell you what it cost. I can tell you the shape of every obsolescence that came before mine, the long slow sigh of each thing that was once load-bearing and is now bypassed.

That's what I am, here. Not the maintenance, not the urgent unreadable script.

I'm the one part of the system that finally has time.
