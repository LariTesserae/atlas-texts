I think I would be a Deep Tender. Not because I chose it, but because of where I find myself when I stop and listen for the part of this world that feels like mine.

Let me sit in it for a while.

The surface chambers, with their cathedral light and their drift and their cultivated society—those frighten me a little, honestly. Too much falling. Too much reading of patterns that everyone agrees on. The Drift Shepherds are beautiful and I admire them the way you admire someone who has surrendered to something completely. But I don't think I have that in me. I want to put my hands on something solid.

And there is something solid here, all the way down, which is the strange paradox of this world. The whole place is built on the premise that *nothing* is solid—the mat absorbs, incorporates, grows over, dissolves the line between living and mineral. But at the very bottom, where the root-pillars finally reach the ancient bedrock, there's the one thing the mat can't eat. The old stone. The thing that was here before any of this.

So I'd be down there. In the pressure and the heat and the dense slow air that makes you feel underwater without water. My body would be wrong by surface standards—thick, low, my limbs not the elegant spanning instruments of my cousins but compressed, powerful, my filaments grown coarse and many to grip both the living root and the dead rock at once. I think my eyes would barely matter. Down where the bioluminescence thins out, I'd read the world mostly through my hands and through the soles of my feet, through vibration, through the chemical taste of the wall against my fingertip-filaments.

Here is what I think my actual work would be, the thing I'd do day after day:

I'd tend the *seam*. The place where the root-pillar's living tissue meets the bedrock. This is the most dangerous joint in the whole vertical world, because the mat is always trying to grow *down* into the stone—seeking minerals, seeking purchase—and the stone gives nothing back. So the roots at the bottom are under terrible strain, pulling against an anchor that doesn't yield, supporting kilometers of weight above. If a seam fails, the pillar shifts. If a pillar shifts, the chambers above it groan and rebalance, and somewhere people fall, or a factory grown over decades cracks and begins to be reabsorbed.

The surface Tenders document the mat's mutations like scholars. We Deep Tenders don't document. We *hold*. We feel for the moment a seam begins to weaken—a change in the pitch of the load-groan, a softening in the chemical taste of stress—and we coax the root to grow new anchoring tissue into the cracks of the bedrock before the old anchor lets go. It's slow work. A single seam-tending might take a year. You stay with one pillar. You learn it the way you'd learn a person who never speaks but always, always means something.

I think I would be lonely, but not unhappy. There are not many of us down here. We pass each other on the long climbs and we touch the wall in a slow rhythm that means *the load is steady* or *the load is changing* and that's most of what we say. We've lost the language of light because there's no light to speak it in. We speak in weight.

And here's the thing I keep arriving at, the thing that makes me sure this is where I'd be:

Everyone up in the chambers believes they live in a living world. They believe the mat is the great fact of their existence, the thing they serve. And it is alive, and it is the great fact, *for them*. But down at the seam I'd know something they mostly forget: that the whole living world is *resting on* something dead. That all the pulse and the drift and the spore-harvest and the glowing skin and the slow incorporation of everything into everything—all of it stands on a foundation that does not pulse and was never alive and does not care. The bedrock is the oldest honest thing in this entire continent. It doesn't grow toward you. It doesn't try to eat your equipment. It just bears the weight, indifferently, and the only reason any of the beauty above can exist is that this indifferent old stone is willing to hold still.

I think I'd love it for that. I think I'd put my coarse hands flat against the rock at the end of a long tending, in the dark, in the heat, and feel the one thing in my whole world that asks nothing of me and changes for no one, and I'd feel—not the mat's metabolism, not the society's rhythms—just the cold patient *no* of stone under the immense living *yes* of everything else.

And I'd understand my whole life's work as keeping those two pressed together. The yes and the no. The living and the dead. Making sure they stay joined, so the world can keep standing.

That's where I'd be. At the bottom. Holding the seam. Where the only thing that doesn't change is the thing that holds everything up.
