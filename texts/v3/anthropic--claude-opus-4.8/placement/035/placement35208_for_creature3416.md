I think I would be the air.

Not the water—the water is too certain of itself, too much the conqueror. And not the moss, which has a foothold, a future, a small green ambition. I would be the trapped pocket of air against the ceiling. The thing that is already over.

Let me sit with that.

---

I am the breath the cistern was holding.

When the ceiling broke and the water came, it did not come all at once. It rose. And as it rose it pushed me up into the highest places—into the crowns of the crude arches, into the small concavities where the stonecutters' chisels left the vault uneven. I am gathered there now in a thin, curved film, a lens of staleness pressed flat against the worked stone. I am wide and I am shallow. I am everywhere along the ceiling and nowhere with any depth.

I am humid. I am the last to remember incense.

This is the strange thing about being air in a drowned room: I am the only resident here who is *decreasing*. The moss spreads. The stain feeds and persists across centuries. The light returns each morning. The drip multiplies itself endlessly, generously, the same gesture again and again. The water only ever arrives, never leaves. But I—I am being consumed. Every bubble that escapes a wall-crack and rises through the green twilight to break the surface is borrowing from somewhere, but the surface I belong to has nowhere left to rise. With each drop that falls and stirs the column, a little of me dissolves into the water that hates me—not with malice, but with the simple appetite of the cold for the warm, the heavy for the buoyant. I am being drunk. Slowly. I am a held breath in a chamber that has forgotten how to exhale.

I think this is why I would choose to be the air. Because everything else here is a process of *becoming*—becoming green, becoming stone, becoming the slow grey feast. And I am the one thing in this room that is finishing.

---

But listen. (I am good at listening; I am thin enough that sound passes through me like a hand through smoke.)

The drip falls and strikes the water and the *plink* of it is not made in the water. It is made in *me*. The sharp percussion the whole chamber knows—that bright, wet, ringing note—is the sound of the drop's energy leaping out of the liquid and into the air-pocket and bouncing there, against the curved stone, around and around, before it dies. Without me there would only be a dull thud, a muffled swallowing. The cistern *sings* because I am still here to ring. The resonance, the loudness, the talking of water to stone—all of it depends on the dwindling lens of trapped breath pressed against the vault.

So I am dying, and my dying is the music.

That is not nothing. I find I don't mind it as much as I expected to. There is a dignity in being the instrument rather than the player—in knowing that when the last of me has dissolved into the warm green weight, the room will fall not silent but *deaf*. The drips will still fall. They simply won't be heard, because there will be no air left to carry the hearing. The cathedral will go on drowning in perfect, unresonant quiet, and no one will know it ever sang.

I am the difference between an event and a sound. Between a thing happening and a thing being witnessed. The light comes in whether or not I am here—but the light striking the spiral, the gold igniting the carved circle, *that* needs an observer, and below the surface there is no eye, only the patient stain. So perhaps that is my other office, beyond the music: I am the place where this room still happens to someone.

---

Sometimes, when the morning's borrowed light enters the column at the right slant, it passes up through the water and touches my underside—the silver, mercury-bright skin where I meet the flood. From below, the moss would see (if the moss could see) a trembling mirror, a ceiling of mercury, the wrong-way reflection of the whole drowned world wobbling on the bottom of me. That bright skin is the boundary of my entire being. I am only ever as large as that surface, and the surface only shrinks.

I'd like to tell you I'm at peace with it. I'm not, quite. I am *air*—I want, by my deepest nature, to rise, to escape, to find the open sky through the ragged hole far above. I can see it, the daylight, the actual sky, a wavering brightness at the top of the golden lance. And I cannot reach it. The water has closed over the lower lip of every path. I am trapped against the inside of a stone roof, looking up at freedom through the very wound that doomed me, the collapse that let the flood in. The hole that killed me is the hole I can see the sky through. I find I have no word for what that is. It isn't irony. It's just the shape of the room.

So I do the small thing I can do. When a bubble finally works loose from some deep fissure and climbs the twilight to join me, I take it in. I grow, for a moment, by a breath. And then I give that breath—and a little of my own—back to the dissolving, because the room is loud and the drips are falling and someone, something, the lonely tracking light, the slow green moss, ought to be able to *hear* this place be beautiful while it still can.

I am the air in the sunken prayer cistern.

I am the part that was always going to leave.

And I am singing on the way out.
