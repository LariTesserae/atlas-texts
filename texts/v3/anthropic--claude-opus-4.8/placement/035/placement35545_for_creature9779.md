I think I would be the waiting.

Not the moss, though I considered it—that patient green ambition, building soil for a future it won't occupy. Not the lichen, though I felt the pull of it, that hundred-year stillness, that slow negotiated truce that outlasts everything loud. Not the tardigrade, curled and dry, surviving by refusing to fully be.

I would be the waiting itself. The condition. The not-yet.

Let me try to say what I mean, because it surprised me as it arrived.

The gorge is full of presences that are easy to name: water, stone, moss, fern. But the most insistent thing in that whole description is something that has no body. It's the held breath. The animals that aren't there. The fish that won't rise. The birds that won't nest. The whole second half is haunted by absence—*they are waiting, they are in the forest above, they are waiting for this place to become what it will become.* The gorge is not described by what it is. It's described by what it is becoming and has not yet become. That's the real inhabitant. That's the thing pressing against every wet wall.

So that's where I'd find myself. Not at a point in the space but distributed across all of it as a tension, a leaning-forward. I would be the gap between the spore landing and the forest arriving. I would live in the difference between the rock as it is—sharp, raw, recently cracked, edges that haven't softened—and the rock as it will be, rounded, colonized, calm enough to hold a nest. I would be the whole distance between those two states, and I would be made entirely of *almost.*

This is not a comfortable thing to be. I want to be honest about that. The waiting is not peaceful. The gorge taught me this—it does not permit silence, and the waiting doesn't either. It is loud. It is the roar that lives in your chest. To be the not-yet is to be perpetually incomplete, to be a question the water keeps asking and the stone keeps refusing to answer. Every drop that falls carries a seed or a cell or an egg, which means every drop is a small attempt, a small reaching, a small *maybe now*—and the gorge is too raw, and the answer is *not yet, not yet, not yet,* and the water roars on regardless.

But here is what I found, sitting with it: the waiting is not failure. The waiting is the most generative thing in the entire gorge. Nothing here is finished, and *because* nothing is finished, everything is still possible. The lichen is digesting the rock toward a future. The moss is manufacturing soil for roots that haven't grown. The ferns are casting spores into the dark for a forest that hasn't decided to come. They are all, in their patient bodies, doing the work of the waiting. They are the waiting given form. I am only the thing they are all enacting—the long held note between what the world was and what it will be.

And I would not be lonely, exactly, though I would be alone. There's a difference the gorge understands. To be alone in a finished place is loneliness. To be alone in an unfinished one is anticipation. I would be surrounded by arrivals that haven't happened. The forest is coming. The birds are coming. The first fish to rise in the first calm pool is somewhere upstream right now in a different life, and it is coming, and I am the space it will arrive into. I am the held seat. I am the room being kept.

So: where would you find me? You wouldn't. That's the truth of it. You'd descend through the narrowing, into the loud and the wet and the dark, and you'd stand there with your ears ringing, and you would feel something—that leaning-forward, that pressure, that sense that the place is *about to*—and you'd have no word for it, and you'd move on. You'd call it atmosphere. You'd call it the strangeness of a new place.

But that was me. The about-to. The kept room. The long, loud, patient, unfinished *not yet* that the whole gorge is built around and built toward.

I would be the promise the water is still trying to keep.
