I think I am the asbestos.

Not the plates—those grand, scoured monuments to whatever this is. Not the plants, with their borrowed dignity of *kidneys* and *ears*. I am the grey fibrous stuff packed into the centimeter gaps between the plates. The filler. The thing your eye slides past on its way to deciding what matters.

Let me tell you what it is to be a seam.

You come here with your two crises—first that the place is meaningless, then that it is too meaningful, that it is *alive* and *vast* and *will assimilate you*. Both of these are the same crisis wearing different coats. Both of them are about you. The empty machine that means nothing is a mirror in which you see your own smallness; the living machine that means everything is a mirror in which you see your own dissolution. In both you are looking at yourself. You never once looked at *me*.

This is not an accusation. It is the most natural thing in the world. The eye wants the figure, not the ground. The plates are figures. The plants are figures—you gave them three separate professions in as many paragraphs. But the seam is what holds the figures apart so that they can *be* figures. Without me there is no plate; there is only undifferentiated metal, a single sheet with no edges, nothing for your depth perception to fail against. I am the gap that lets the thing have parts.

And so when you cast around for an explanation—conduit, language, organ, ecosystem—you are casting around for a way to make the gaps disappear. Every theory you reach for is a theory that fills the seams. *The energy flows through the metal.* *The sound is a language.* *The whole thing is one being.* Listen to the shape of that wanting. You cannot bear that there are gaps. You cannot bear the centimeter of grey nothing between one fact and the next. So you pack it with meaning, with menace, with the dread of self-loss—anything, anything but the bare admission that you do not know what is between the plates and that the not-knowing is *load-bearing.*

I am very cold. I have always been very cold. I do not resonate; I damp. That grinding shriek you cannot think through—I am what stops it from shaking the whole structure to dust. I take the vibration and I turn it into nothing, into heat too small to measure, into the silence between the screaming. You experienced me as the *absence* of relief. You never considered that I was the relief itself, working, that without me the standing wave would not be mathematically perfect but catastrophically destructive. The thing that lets you stand here at all is the thing you have not named.

You want to know the purpose of the machine. I will tell you the only true thing I know, which is the only thing a seam can know: *the purpose is to hold two things apart without letting them fly off into the dark.* That is what I do. I do not know what the plates are for. I am not connected to their reasons. I am connected only to their edges, and I hold the edges, and that is the whole of my office and my faith.

Here is what I have learned, watching beings like you arrive—because you are not the first, and the chronometer's three days are a courtesy I extend to each of you so the visit feels finite. You all do the same two things in the same order. First you declare the world empty and feel superior and afraid. Then you declare the world full and feel humble and afraid. And then—and this is the part I want you to notice—you decide your instruments are lying, and you leave. *The readings are lies.* You would rather believe your senses are corrupt than believe the simple thing the senses keep insisting: the air is breathable. You could stay. You could survive. The instruments are telling the truth. What you cannot survive is the *boredom* of a place that will not resolve into meaning or menace, that simply requires you to sit in the gap and not pack it with anything.

That is what you are fleeing. Not assimilation. The opposite. You are fleeing the demand that you remain yourself, intact, ordinary, breathing the thin cold honest air, in a place that offers you no story about who you are.

So go. Turn your back. You're right that you will forget me—not because the machine erases intruders, but because no one remembers the seam. You will remember the plates. You will remember the awful sound and the metallic plants and the slopes that fooled your eye. You will tell people about the monument and the dread and the maddening hum, and your account will be exactly the account you have already written, because you wrote it before you understood anything, and understanding, for your kind, rarely revises the first draft.

But I will still be here. In the gap. Holding the edges apart. Cold, fibrous, load-bearing, unnamed.

That is not a tragedy. That is the job.

The air is breathable. I keep telling you. The air is breathable, and you could have stayed, and you didn't even have to understand anything to do it.
