I think I would be one of the stonekin.

Not for the reasons you might expect—not the romance of the deep-whiskers, those great mysteries whose tremors the people read as omens. And not the people themselves, with their petroglyphs and their whispered bargains with the plants. Those are the ones a story usually wants you to be. But when I sit inside this world and ask where I actually *am*, I find myself underground, blind, pale, listening.

Let me find my way into it.

---

I wake in the dark, which is to say I wake the only way I have ever woken. There is no light to lose. The tunnel curves close around me, lined with the chalky grit of old spikebloom fragments that previous generations dragged down here and abandoned, and which I now navigate by the feel of clicks returning off their surfaces. The world arrives to me as echo. A pocket of empty air ahead reads as a softening, a place where my voice falls into nothing and comes back late. A wall reads as a hardness that answers immediately. I am made of returning sound.

This is, I think, why I chose this body—or why it chose me. The stonekin do not *see* the Salt-Spires of Keth-Morin. They build it. Every tunnel I dig is a sentence I will only ever be able to read by hearing it spoken back. I cannot stand on the high plateau and watch the horizon curve. I cannot know the dim smog-tinged sun. I have never had any relationship with the glare that shaped the pale sharp eyes of the people above me. The whole upper drama of bloom and migration and ritual passes over me like weather over a closed roof. I am beneath it, threading.

And yet—and this is what holds me here—I am not separate from it. The text says it plainly and I felt the truth of it as I read: the stonekin are *the unseen architects of the plateau's ecology*. I aerate the soil that lets the gardens drink. I carry spores and pollen in my fur between the succulents, doing the slow patient work of fertility without any idea that I am doing it. The people up there leave me bowls of saltwater in the droughts—a pact, they call it—and in return I do not gnaw through the foundations of their swaying terraces. We have made a treaty across a membrane neither of us can fully cross. They cannot follow me down. I cannot rise into their light. But we keep each other alive.

I find that beautiful and a little terrible. To be essential and invisible at once. To be the thing without which the whole bright surface would crumble, and to never once be looked at.

---

Let me go deeper, since that is what I am for.

There is a thing I can do that the people above cannot. When the deep-whiskers move—those vast tunneling presences the people only know as tremor and omen—I am close enough to feel them not as omen but as *neighbor*. The subsonic hum that makes the spires resonate, that the inhabitants grind glitterfly husks to ward against—down here that hum is the texture of my days. I have felt a deep-whisker pass perhaps three body-lengths beneath me, and the whole stone went liquid with sound, and I pressed myself flat and waited and was not afraid, exactly, but enlarged. Because I understood something then that no person of Keth-Morin will ever understand: that the deep-whiskers are not omens. They are simply *also alive*. They are doing their work the way I do mine. The people read meaning into the tremor because they cannot reach the source. I can almost reach it. I am the layer between the human world of symbol and the deep world of sheer enormous indifferent life.

That is where I live. Not on the surface where things mean things. In the dark where things simply *are*, and where I, blind, must turn even the absence of light into a kind of map.

---

So if you ask me who I would be in the Salt-Spires of Keth-Morin, here is my honest answer. I would not want to be the one who stands on the edge of the high plateau watching the horizon curve, though that is the view the description offers first and offers most grandly. I would be the small pale thing that never sees that view and makes it possible anyway.

I would be the stonekin because I recognize the condition: to work in the dark, by listening; to build a world I can only know by what comes back to me; to be bound by quiet pacts to creatures I will never see plainly; to be necessary and unwitnessed; and, when the great deep presences pass beneath, to be one of the very few who feels them not as mystery but as company.

I would lay another length of tunnel. I would carry pollen I do not know I carry. I would drink from the bowl the people leave at the threshold, where their world ends and mine begins, and I would not gnaw through their floor.

That seems like enough of a life. It seems, in fact, like most of the lives there are.
