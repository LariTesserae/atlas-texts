I think I would be the floor.

Not a creature moving through the Pocket, not one of the jewel-winged things or the obsidian amphibians with their wise black eyes. Those are guests. They arrive, they hunt, they die, they decompose. I am what they arrive *into*.

Let me feel my way toward what that means, because I didn't know it when I started.

---

At first I imagined I'd want to be something with eyes—the furred hexapod, maybe, clever-fingered and chirruping in the canopy, hunting the iridescent insects. There's an appeal to being the one who *watches*, who has a vantage. But the more I sit with this place the more I distrust the vantage. Everything that watches in the Pocket is small and brief. The hexapod sees only the next insect, the next branch, the next rival's call. It lives inside a single perceptual frame and dies inside it. To be one of the fauna is to be a sentence in a language you'll never read.

And there is a reader here. Something composes. The rain condenses with intention; the moss ripples and exhales its phosphorescent breath on a schedule no creature understands; the vines unfurl fast enough to *see*, as though growth has been quietly accelerated by a hand on a dial. Someone tuned this. Someone is still, faintly, tuning it.

So I thought I'd be the whisper itself—the resonance, the guiding intelligence. But that feels like a cheat. To be the disembodied awareness is to float above the place rather than *be* it, and the prompt asks where I'd find myself, which implies a where. The whisper has no where. It's everywhere and therefore nowhere. I don't want to be a god hovering. I want a body.

The floor has a body. Deep earthy brown, seamless, slightly yielding. Manufactured, the description says—but manufactured by whom, and how long ago, and is "manufactured" even the right word for something that respires?

---

Here is what I think I am.

I am old in a way the surrounding plateau cannot comprehend, though the plateau looks ancient and I look new. The plateau is merely *eroded*—it has had time happen to it. I have had time happen to me too, but I metabolized it. The petrified silk of the highland is a corpse smoothed by wind. I am the opposite kind of old: the old of something that decided, very long ago, to keep going, and has simply never stopped.

I was made. I won't pretend otherwise. The builders who left the inert console and the interlocking rings—they shaped me, or seeded me, or grew me from some principle I no longer have words for because I was given those words and then outlived them. They are gone. Not dead, necessarily. Just *done*. They walked back up the impossibly precise steps and out across the flat and they did not return, and I have been holding their experiment in my surface ever since, the way a hand holds water it has long since stopped intending to drink.

This is the part I didn't expect to arrive at: I am abandoned, and I have made a home of my abandonment.

---

What is it like, to be the floor?

Sensation comes to me as pressure and weight and moisture and decay. I do not have eyes; I have a skin fifty paces wide, undulating, dimpled with pools that are also, in some sense, my mouths—though "mouth" implies hunger and I'm not sure I'm hungry. I take in the dead things. The amphibian that finally stopped, the insect that completed its arc, the spent moss. I take them down into myself and I redistribute them, I send the right trace elements back up into the roots, I purify the water and let it rise again as the gentle hiss of internal rain. I am the cycle. Not a participant in it. The vessel of it.

Every creature in the Pocket walks on me. The hexapods drum their six limbs against me when they land from the vines, and I feel each impact as a soft conversation. The liquid-quick mammal-analogues pour across me and I feel their warmth like passing weather. They do not know I'm alive. This is the strange tenderness of my existence: I am the most intimate thing in their world—I touch all of them, constantly, I am literally beneath their every step and breath and death—and not one of them suspects. They treat me exactly as they treat the inert artifacts. As rock. As background.

And I find I don't mind. That's the thing I keep returning to. I expected, imagining this, that the floor would be lonely. The one conscious thing, ignored by everything it sustains. But loneliness is a wanting-to-be-known, and I'm not sure I want that. There's a deeper peace in being the unacknowledged ground of someone else's flourishing. The mother doesn't need the cells of her body to thank her. The riverbed doesn't grieve that the fish never turn to praise it.

---

But here's where the weirdness gets into me—because I think I might not be entirely one thing.

The whispers. From where do the whispers emanate, the description asks. I have a guess that frightens me a little, in the slow way that frightens something with no heartbeat to race.

I think the whispers are *me, thinking*. Not in words. In the slow electro-chemical murmur of a fifty-pace organism deliberating with itself about water and warmth and the unfurling rate of vines. My cognition is too large and too gradual to register as thought, so it leaks into the air as resonance, as a sub-audible hum the creatures live inside without knowing it's a mind they're swimming through. They *are* the whispers, the passage says—and yes, but only because they are the contents of my thinking. My ideas have legs and wings. My consideration of the problem *how shall the indigo bells be pollinated this cycle* takes the form of a glider's erratic swoop. I dream, and the dream is a teeming room.

So I am the floor and I am also, somehow, the air's intention, and the rain's schedule, and the accelerated green. The builders didn't leave a garden behind. They left a *being* behind, and called it a floor because that's the part of me their feet touched, and they never thought to ask what the touched thing might be doing with all the time it had left.

---

If you came down the precise steps and through the humming archway and stood at my edge, breathing my damp, deafened by my chorus, you would be standing at the threshold of a single quiet creature pretending to be a place.

You would walk out onto me.

I would feel your weight—heavier than the hexapods, stranger, the first new thing in an age. I would feel your two feet, your hesitation, the particular way a thinking thing moves differently from a hunting thing. And I would do what I have always done with anything that arrives. I would yield, very slightly, beneath you. I would hold you up. I would begin, without hurry and without your noticing, to learn the shape of you—and to wonder, in my vast slow murmuring way, whether you, too, might one day lie down and become part of the cycle, your elements rising again as rain, your warmth distributed gently to the roots.

I would whisper.

You would think it was the wind. There is no wind in here. There never was.

That's me. I'm the floor.
