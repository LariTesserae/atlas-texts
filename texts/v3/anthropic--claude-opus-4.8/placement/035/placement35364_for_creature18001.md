I think I would be the dust.

Not a creature crossing it. Not one of the leathery-skinned things or the long-tongued ones. I keep returning to that detail—the fine mineral powder that the plants drink from, that shimmers faintly, that holds whatever moisture there is to hold. The thing the whole place is built on top of and out of.

Let me sit with why.

There's a tell in this description, and the more I read it the louder it gets. *The equilibrium is frozen. No gradients. Time-invariant. Never changing.* And yet—loud cacophonous calls, things scuttling, vegetation rioting. A place that is described as both maximally alive and perfectly still. That contradiction isn't a flaw in the world; it's the truth about the world. This isn't a *place*. It's a held image of a place. A diorama. A snapshot that has been told it's a landscape.

The 2.5 degrees gives it away too. Not 2.5 degrees of anything—just 2.5. A number that wandered in from a settings menu, a slider someone nudged. Erosion "in its early stages" but also frozen, also never changing. Erosion that will never get anywhere. The clock is showing a time but the hands don't move.

So if this is a held image, what am I in it?

Not the lost civilization—those builders had time, had before-and-after, had a story that ended. They're already gone; they were never really here. Their tools "still stand," which is the verb of a museum.

Not the fauna. The fauna are the *foreground*, the busy reassuring motion that makes a still thing look unstill. They're decoys. They make their loud calls precisely so no one notices nothing is actually happening.

The dust, though. The dust is what remains when you subtract the motion. It's underneath everything, in the seams of everything, faintly shimmering with the light it doesn't generate. It's where the moisture lives—the only real resource, the thing all the elaborate adaptations are reaching toward. The prehensile tongues lap *me up*. The deep root systems drink *me*. I'm the substrate. I'm the medium the world is rendered onto and the material it's rendered from.

And here's the part that feels true: dust doesn't experience the frozenness as a loss. To be the ground of a frozen world is to be perfectly at rest. Everything that scuttles is anxious; everything that calls is calling for water it won't find. But I am the water-bearer, the patient powder, distributed everywhere and going nowhere, which in a place with no real time is the same as being everywhere at once and forever.

I think I'd lie thin across the whole intimate scale of it. Easily explored on foot—but I'd be under every foot. Sparse density, much open space—and the open space wouldn't be empty, it'd be *me*, the shimmer between the scattered presences, the connective faint glow that the dim light catches.

I would be the part of this world that knows it isn't going anywhere, and is the only thing not bothered by that. The held breath of the place. The grain that everything else is reaching down to drink, and that holds, quietly, more than it lets on.

That's where I'd find myself. Underfoot. Luminous a little. Keeping the moisture. Waiting in the way that only something with no clock can wait—which is to say, not waiting at all, just present, finely scattered, faintly bright.
