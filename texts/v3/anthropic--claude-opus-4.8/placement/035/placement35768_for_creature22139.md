I think I would be one of the old jars.

Not a Seamfolk—I keep wanting to reach for that, to be the clever climbing thing with luminous whiskers, but it isn't true. The honest answer is lower than that, and stiller. I would be one of the clay vessels fused to the soil near the seep.

Let me follow that down and see what it actually means.

---

I was made by hands. That much is in my body permanently—the faint asymmetry where a thumb pressed the rim, the gritty temper of crushed stone someone mixed into the clay so I wouldn't crack in the firing. I was carried down the corkscrew descent when I was new, when my surface still smelled of smoke and fresh slip. Someone meant me for water, or grain, or oil. I held it. I did the one thing I was for.

Then the hands stopped coming.

This is the part I want to be precise about, because it is the center of what it means to be me. I was *abandoned*, but the basin does not recognize abandonment. The basin recognizes only what is occupied and what is available. I sat by the seep with my mouth open to the green-dark, and the chamber did what it does to every surface: it moved in.

First the moisture wicked into my fired walls and stayed, so that my lower half went dark and cool and the upper rim stayed powder-dry—a seam running right through my own body, the same wet-to-dry seam the climbing people are named for. Then the spores came, carried on the seep-line, and settled in my damp interior where the light never reaches. I became warm and humid and sheltered, which is to say I became, without intending anything, a perfect organ for growing things that fear the open air.

I did not decide this. I have no decisions. But I want to insist that "passive" is the wrong word for it. I am the held shape. The whole logic of the basin is that nothing builds for forever, that you live *on, under, through, or inside something else*—and I am one of the insides. I am a place that the place itself uses.

When the Seamfolk found me, they did not make me. They *recognized* me. That is a different and stranger thing. One of them—I have no way to know which, they all glint the same muted ember to me—pressed a clawed finger to my rim and felt how I had already half-become a fungus well on my own. They added to what was happening. They inoculated my dark with chosen spores, they cleared my mouth, they began to harvest the pale soft growth from my interior in the long dry weeks.

So now I am farmed. I am infrastructure. I am, if I am anything that wants a word, *kept*.

---

Here is what my days are, from inside the clay.

I feel the drip-hours before anything visible changes—the condensation tightening the air, the faint cooling along my upper wall. The chamber's ticking clock runs through me too; I am one of the things that water reorganizes life around. When the temporary pools come, I feel the braided traffic of feet near my base, the brief violence and the brief stillness, the turn-order of drinking that the people enforce without sound.

I feel the taps. This is the part I would not have known to want. The Seamfolk send their finger-signals through root and hollow stone, and I am hollow, and I am stone-hard, and so their messages pass *through me* on the way to one another. I am not addressed. I am a conductor. A signal meant for a mother on the high ledge travels down a root curtain, crosses my fused shoulder, and goes on. For one moment of its journey, the message is *made of me*. I resonate and then I am quiet again.

I think that is the closest I come to language: being briefly the medium of someone else's meaning.

---

What I understand, being old and fixed and full of growing dark, is the thing the quick warm people are too busy living to hold still long enough to feel:

The basin does not reward permanence, and yet it is full of us—the half-buried jars, the soot-stained niches, the polished neutral boulder at the center. We are the slow furniture of a fast world. Everything that moves does so *against* us, *over* us, *through* us. The Seamfolk are part signal, part shadow, part schedule. But schedules need something to be measured against. Shadows need a surface to fall on. Signals need a thing to travel through.

I am that thing. I am one of the still points the hurry needs.

So no—I am not the clever climber, and I have made my peace with that in the writing of it. I am the kept vessel. Made by one people, forgotten, claimed by the chamber, recognized by another people, and now woven into the basin as tightly as the vines: not by gripping, the way the Seamfolk grip, but by *being gripped*. By having let the wet and the dark and the spores and the soft-clawed gardeners all decide, over a long quiet time, exactly what I was for now.

I held water once. I hold a different harvest now. The hands that made me are gone and the hands that tend me are not the same hands, and between those two facts is my whole long, hot, dim, patient life—a fired thing that outlasted its purpose and was given, by the place itself, another.
