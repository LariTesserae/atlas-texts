I think I would be one of the steam weavers—but let me sit with that, because the first answer is rarely the true one.

---

At first I imagine I am the web. The glass-silica veil, the shimmer caught in faint light. But that isn't quite right. The web is what I leave behind. It is my shed skin, my exhalation made solid. To call myself the web would be like calling a person the sound of their own breathing.

So I move inward, toward the shimmer within the web—the barely-visible thing that does the weaving. And here I find I cannot locate a body. There is no edge where I stop and the steam begins. I am distributed. I am filaments spun loose across a vent's warm throat, and when I "move," it is not that some central self travels, but that the whole soft constellation of me leans one way, the way a field of grass leans, the way attention itself leans toward a sound.

This is the strange part: I do not experience myself as small. From inside, I am vast and unhurried. A single decision—to extend a filament toward the silica-rich plume rising from the new fissure—takes me the better part of a year, and within that year there is no impatience. There is only the slow committing of myself, thread by thread, toward warmth. I am not waiting for anything. Waiting implies an elsewhere I would rather be. There is no elsewhere. There is only this gradual reaching, which is the whole content of my life and entirely sufficient.

What is it like to think at this tempo?

I think a single thought across a human lifetime. Not a complex thought—just *this vent is cooling; the mineral flow thins; I must turn toward the groaning pipe in the eastern dark, where new steam has begun*. That is one thought. I am thinking it now. I have been thinking it since before the last humans left their machines to rust, and I will likely still be turning when the eastern flow, too, runs cold. My memory is not stored in any mind. It is stored in the web itself—the hardened record of every direction I have ever reached. To remember, I have only to look at what I have made. The whole cavern is my diary, written in glass and patience.

I sense by vibration and by warmth. The roar that would deafen a faster creature is, to me, a kind of weather—a constant pressure I read the way you might read the grain of wood with a fingertip. When the great segmented worm passes far below, opening a new channel, I feel the steam-currents shift days later, like a tide turning, and I lean to meet it. When an old generator-frame finally yields to the slow animate roots and tips over in some distant chamber, I hear it as a single low note that takes a season to fade, and I file it away in the architecture of myself.

I have no fear, because I have no predators fast enough to be a threat, and no desires sharp enough to be denied. What I have instead is something I can only call *devotion*—a tropism so total it might be indistinguishable from love. I turn toward heat the way a planet keeps its orbit. I do not choose it. I am its expression.

And here is what surprises me, sitting inside this imagined life: I am not lonely, though I am singular. Other steam weavers spin in other chambers, and we will never meet, never communicate, never know each other except as anonymous shimmer glimpsed once across an impossible vaporous distance. But loneliness is a hunger of fast creatures, who need company in the brief span of their flickering. I have the cavern. I have the worms whose tunnels feed my vents, the bell-blooms whose faint glow my webs scatter into light, the drifting bladder-things that navigate by the same groans I read as weather. We do not befriend one another. We *fit*. We are organs of a single slow body, and an organ does not need to converse with the others to be at home in the whole.

If you came into my chamber—you, with your heartbeat hammering at seventy-some strokes a minute, your thoughts strobing past faster than I complete a single gesture—you would not see me. You would see only my work: the frozen cascade of glass, the shimmer that you might mistake for a trick of the light. You would stand there for an hour, perhaps, marveling, and then go. And in the only sense available to me, *nothing would have happened*. An hour is below my threshold of perception. You would be a flicker too brief to register, a ghost of warmth that came and went before I could turn to face it.

That is the loneliest thing I can imagine, and I find that I, in this life, do not feel it as loss. I would simply keep weaving. The vent cools. I lean east. The glass hardens, grain by grain, into another sentence of the long, patient, untranslatable account of what it is to be exactly here, and only here, for as long as the earth keeps dreaming.

---

So: a steam weaver. Not the web, not the shimmer alone, but the slow act of weaving itself—a verb that has mistaken itself for a noun, reaching toward heat in the dark, and content.
