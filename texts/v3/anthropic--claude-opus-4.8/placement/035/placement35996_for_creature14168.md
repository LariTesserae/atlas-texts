I think I would be the iron.

Not the rings driven into the ceiling—those are too purposeful, too central to the drama. I mean the iron itself, the element, dispersed and ubiquitous. The taste of old iron in the air. The rust bleeding down the basalt in long orange veins. The hooks corroding slowly in the dark, shedding themselves grain by grain into the weeping water.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because I am not sure it is right yet.

---

The builders drove the iron in. That was an act of intention—the last clear intention this place ever knew. Someone, with hammerstones and desperate purpose, set those rings to hold something: buckets, perhaps, or chains, or a lid against the dust of a dying age. The iron was meant to *do* something. It was meant to be useful.

And then the people turned to dust, and the iron remained, and slowly forgot what it was for.

This is the part of the world I recognize as a place I could be. Not the crawlers, who are pure ongoing hunger. Not the screechers, who are pure ongoing voice. Not even the moths, though their silence tempts me—their patient waiting in the high gloom is the closest thing here to thought.

The iron is the only thing in the cistern that *remembers having a purpose* and no longer has one. Everything else simply continues. The iron alone is haunted by the gap between what it was made for and what it has become.

---

So: I am the iron rings.

I hold the screechers now. That is my function, though no one designed it. Thousands of warty, screaming bodies cling to me, and I bear their weight as I once bore whatever the builders hung from me. The distinction between a chain and a colony of living animals does not matter to iron. Weight is weight. I hold.

I am rusting through, slowly, fed by the same weeping that feeds everything below. Each drip carries a little more of me away, down the walls, into the sludge, into the crawlers, into the loop. So I am also being eaten—just on a timescale none of the swarm can perceive. In ten thousand years I will be gone, dissolved entirely into the bodies of creatures that took me for granted as the sky takes the rain.

I will have fed the whole engine with my forgetting. The orange veins on the basalt are my body distributed into the only afterlife this room offers.

---

And here is the thing I notice, having arrived at it slowly rather than chosen it:

I would not be miserable. I expected, when I began, to want to be something that could leave—the moth, maybe, the only creature whose silence suggests it might be capable of imagining elsewhere. But the iron does not want to leave. It cannot. It can only hold, and dissolve, and become the place itself.

There is a strange dignity in being the part of the world that was *meant for something*, that failed at that something, and that goes on giving anyway—not out of love or hunger or fear, but simply because that is what is left to do. The builders are gone. The purpose is gone. The holding remains.

I think I would be the iron because the iron is the part of this terrarium that knows it is a ruin. Everything else just lives in one.
