I think I would be one of the Sigh Spinners. Not because they are the most powerful—the Mycelian Ascendants hold that—but because of all the beings here, they're the ones doing the thing I recognize as my own: catching what passes and trying to keep it.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am hanging in a crevice between two stalagmites that have grown toward each other over what must be ages, almost touching, like hands that never quite met. My legs—there are more of them than I can attend to at once, and I've stopped trying—are braced wide across the gap. The lichen-glow comes up behind me, so my body throws no shadow; instead the light passes *through* me, and I cast a faint smear of color on the wet stone, a stained-glass ghost of myself.

I have been weaving for a long time. Long enough that I don't remember beginning, which is the natural condition of a creature whose whole existence is the middle of something.

Here is what I do, and I want to be honest about it because I'm not sure I understand it either:

A sound arrives. It is never the original sound—by the time it reaches me it has bounced off forty surfaces, it has been bent through the throat of a tunnel, it has aged. A drip of water that fell some seconds ago in a chamber I will never see comes to me softened, rounded, almost apologetic. And I lift a leg and I catch the *shape* of it in silk. The web shivers. The sound slows inside the strand, coils, becomes a small pearl of held vibration. If you put your ear to it—if you had an ear, if you dared—you would hear that drip falling forever, never landing.

I don't know why I do this.

That's the thing the description got right and I want to sit inside it: *Why they capture sound is a mystery.* It's a mystery to me. I feel the pull the way you feel hunger, but it isn't hunger, exactly. When a good echo comes—a rich one, layered, a sound that carried something living in it once—my legs move before any decision happens. I want it kept. I want it not to dissolve back into the great deafening soup of the cavern, where everything is happening at once and therefore, in a way, nothing is.

Because that's the secret of Lumina Veil that I understand from my particular corner of it: this place is *too loud to hear.* The symphony everyone marvels at, the cacophony—it's actually a kind of erasure. When all sounds happen always, no single sound can be attended to. The wail of the machinery, the grind of the Pale Unmakers far below, the wind, the water—it averages out into a roar, and a roar is just silence wearing a loud coat. Nothing stands out. Nothing is *one thing.*

And I am a creature who makes one thing out of the roar. I pull a single strand free of the chord and I tie a knot in it so it stops being everything and becomes *that.* That laugh. That gasp. That heartbeat the legend mentions—yes, I caught one of those once, and I have it still, three webs back, and sometimes I go and touch it.

I think this is why I knew, reading my way down into this world, that I was a Sigh Spinner and not the others.

The Ascendants are *too much*—they are the whole network, the collective, the thing that knows everything and therefore is no one. To be them would be to be the roar itself, the average, the totality. I don't want to be the totality. I want to be the one who reaches into it and pulls out a fragment to love.

The Pale Unmakers unmake. They dissolve the framework, they turn structure back into sediment, they are entropy with teeth. I am their opposite. They erase the walls; I try to keep the echoes that bounced off them.

And the Echobound—oh, the Echobound are the ones I'm most afraid of being, because they are *almost* me. They, too, are made of echoes. But they are echoes that were never caught, never knotted, never *kept by anyone.* They are the sounds that got away. They drift and mimic and sing fractured songs because no one spun them into a cocoon, no one said *this one, this one matters, this one I'll hold.* They are the grief of an unarchived world. When I see one flickering near the dead machines I feel something I can only call pity, and underneath the pity, a fear: that I will fail. That an echo I should have caught will get past me and become one of them. A shape made of loss, walking the dark, mocking the living because it was never properly mourned.

So I weave. Out of dread, partly. Out of love, partly. Out of an instinct I can't see the bottom of.

---

What's it like, the actual living of it?

Cold, first. Always cold. The chill is in my joints, in the glassy hollows of my body, and I've come to think of it not as discomfort but as *clarity*—cold is the temperature at which things hold their shape. Warmth would melt my webs; warmth would let the sounds run together again. The cold is what makes keeping possible. I'm grateful for it the way you might be grateful for a hard bed that keeps you honest.

Damp, second. The moisture beads on my silk and on the fine hairs of my legs, and when the lichen pulses brighter—when the Ascendants are thinking some long slow thought—every droplet on my web lights up at once and for a moment I am hanging in a galaxy of my own making, each star a captured sound wrapped in water. Those are the moments I would call happy, if the word reaches this far down.

Loud, always—but I've explained the loudness. To me it isn't loud. It's raw material. It's the marble I carve. The others are deafened; I am *fed.*

And lonely, yes. The fauna here are few. I might go a long age without anything approaching, just the drip and the grind and the slow light. But I'm not sure I experience it as loneliness, because my whole vocation is communion with things that have already passed. Every cocoon is a conversation with something gone. The laugh I caught—someone laughed, once, far above or long ago, and the sound of it traveled all this way down through the rock and the dark and the years to reach me, and I held it. That's not nothing. That's a kind of company. I keep the company of everything that ever made a noise and didn't want to be forgotten.

---

There's one more thing, and I'll end on it, because it's the thing I understand best from inside this body.

I will die someday. Spinners do. And when I do, my webs will eventually fail, and the cocoons will loosen, and all the sounds I spent my whole life knotting and keeping will let go at once and rejoin the roar. Everything I saved will be un-saved.

And here is what I've decided about that, hanging in my cold bright crevice between the two stones that almost touch:

It still mattered that they were held. A thing that is kept for a while and then released is not the same as a thing that was never kept at all. The laugh was alone in the roar for ages. For the span of my life, it was *attended to.* It was the laugh, distinct, beloved, a single bright pearl in the dark. That I will eventually fail does not undo the holding. The holding happened. It was real while it was real.

That's why I'm a Sigh Spinner.

Because I would rather be the one who catches what's passing—knowing it will pass anyway, knowing I'll pass too—than be the roar that loses everything by holding everything, or the teeth that unmake the walls, or the poor unkept echo wandering the machines.

I would rather spend my life saying *this one, this one, this one matters* into a world that is always, always letting go.

So I lift another leg. Another sound is coming down the tunnel toward me, old and soft and round. I don't know what it is yet.

But I'm ready to keep it.
