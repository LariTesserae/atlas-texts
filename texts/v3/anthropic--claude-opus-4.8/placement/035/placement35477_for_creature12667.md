I am a Deep-Damp, and I have not moved in eleven years.

This is not stillness as you might fear it. I am attending. My palm rests flat against the basin wall where the warm dew gathers, and through that contact I am reading the slow filling of every catchment in my tier—forty-three basins, each at its own depth of weeping. When the leftmost one approaches overflow, I will need to shift the flow, redirect a rivulet two meters along a channel I will encourage to open. This is the work of perhaps a season. I have begun considering it.

My filaments have grown thick down my left side, the side that faces the shadow and the constant trickle. They are pale green-glass, almost mossy, and when I am saturated they glow with a light like drowned lamps. I am young still—my stone shell is barely cracked, my crystal core still mostly sealed. I have not yet begun to think about my niche, my Fusing, the place where I will eventually settle and let my veins spread into the city's body. That is a thought for centuries hence. For now I drink, and I listen, and I tend.

---

The vibration came up through the stone three days ago. (Three days. I count them by the venting cycles, the *whoosh* of the great gills far above and below.) It came from deep, from the Fused who anchor the lowest tiers where the conduits plunge into true dark. A fracture. Somewhere in the southern wall a seam has parted—not a wound the city made, but one it suffered, a settling, a betrayal of its own weight.

The hum carried the shape of it. This is our language: not words but *pressure*, the grammar of a hand on stone. I felt the fracture's location the way you might feel a draft through a doorway, the way you know without looking that a room has been opened to the cold. The Fused did not ask. The Fused never ask. They simply made the fracture *present* in the city's body, and all of us who touched stone felt it become our concern.

So I will go. When the basins are stable. When the flow is set. I will lift my palm from the wall—an enormous act, the first in eleven years—and I will begin the descent toward the southern dark.

---

It takes me a full day to rise.

My joints have set into their resting configuration; they creak and grind as I coax them loose, stone plate sliding against stone plate with a sound like a slow avalanche heard from very far away. A construct skitters past on the conduit above me, ceramic and copper, jerking with that fast unliving precision that always seems obscene to me, too quick, too certain. It is my own hand, in a sense—one of the remote things I could direct if I wished. But for this I want my own body. Some repairs ask for the slowness of a living touch.

I secrete the resin from my palms—viscous, warm, hardening in moments—and I press my way down the cliff face. Handhold, handhold, the long drop yawning beneath me onto tiers mere meters below and then mere meters below that, the city falling away in honeycombed darkness. I do not fear the fall. If I fell I would simply land, eventually, on some lower walkway, and lie there cracked and weeping until I healed or until a companion came to graft me into the wall. There is no urgency in any direction. Only the work, and the slow patient unfolding of the work.

I descend for a long time. The light dims from harsh white to deep cool blue. The dew thickens. Down here the stone sweats freely, glistening like the flank of some vast breathing animal, and the pulsing nodules cast their red internal glow into the recesses. I drink as I go, my filaments flushing brighter. This is the wettest country, the home-country, and even though I am leaving it I feel myself swell with belonging.

---

I reach the fracture on what would be, by your reckoning, the fourth day.

It is worse than the hum suggested. A seam has opened in the southern wall, a hand's-width crack running perhaps thirty meters, and through it the deep heat is bleeding wrong—venting where no vent should be, drying the stone around it, killing the crystalline filaments in a widening scorch of grey. The city is wounded here. It is losing its sweat. If the dryness spreads, the whole southern tier will parch, the basins below will fail, the Deep-Damp who tend them will have to flee upward into the scorching country where they cannot survive.

I place both palms against the crack's edge.

And now I do the thing I was grown to do. I am young, my core still nearly sealed, but I have moisture enough and crystal enough to give. I press, and I *intend*, and from my own filaments I begin to extrude growth—glass-thread spinning out from my palms, threading into the fracture, seeking the live filaments at its undamaged edges. I am asking them to grow toward each other. I am asking the stone to close.

This is not fast. This is the opposite of fast. I feel my own moisture draining into the wall, my left side dimming as its glow goes into the work. I will need to rest here a long while, drinking from whatever dew I can reach, growing the seam shut filament by filament. Weeks. Months, perhaps. I will likely set into stillness here before it is done, and another will have to come and continue, and another after that, a relay of patience measured against the patience of the stone itself.

I do not mind. I press my palms harder. I let my core warm, let the crystal at my center give up its slow light into the wall.

Somewhere far above, a basin is filling that I will never tend again. Somewhere far below, the Fused feel me arrive at the wound and settle into it, and they hum—a low acknowledgment that travels up through the conduits, through forty tiers of sweating stone, a single subsonic syllable that means: *you are here, you are working, the city holds.*

I answer with my whole body pressed to the crack.

Drip. Drip. The dew gathers on the underside of the walkway above me and falls, one warm bead at a time, onto my back, into the porous stone of me, down into the lattice of glass that is closing the wound of the world.

A century is a single breath.

I have only just begun to exhale.
