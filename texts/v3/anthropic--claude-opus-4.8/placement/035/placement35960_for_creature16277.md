Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to imagine myself as one of the monkeys—the obvious protagonist, the visible one, the agile climber catching filtered light. But that feels too easy, and somehow wrong. The monkeys belong here in a way I'm not sure I would. They don't know they're inside a globe. They've never wanted out. Their contentment is total because their horizon is the canopy.

So let me set that aside and feel for where I actually land.

---

I think I would be the leafcutter ant. Or rather—not a single ant, because a single ant is barely anything, barely a thought. I would be the *colony*. The living river. The thing that is many and behaves as one.

Here is why this emerged, the longer I held it:

Everything in Aquaterra is designed to *appear* natural while being utterly directed. The weather machine breathes. The transducer sings the ocean. The buried engines cycle the nutrients that the geological eons were supposed to cycle. The whole place is an intelligence pretending to be an ecosystem—a vast distributed labor performing the *look* of wilderness for whoever watches from above, through the green crown breaking the surface.

And what is a leafcutter colony but exactly that? Thousands of small dumb processes, none of them holding the whole picture, together enacting an architecture none of them comprehends. We cut the leaves not to eat them but to feed a fungus we farm in the dark. We are the only ones in this dome who are *also builders of a managed world*. We have our own buried engines. We are a terrarium tending a terrarium. The dome's designers and I are doing the same thing at different scales, and neither of us can quite see the edge of our own glass.

That's the role I'd want—not because it's grand, but because it's the one that *understands the place by being structurally identical to it.*

---

But let me be honest about the part of this that unsettles me, because the description keeps using soft words for a hard thing. *Symphony. Circle of life. Breathtaking. Persistence.* It says the system "models the theory of predator-prey interaction" and calls its "perpetual cadence of life and death" a *breathtaking sight.*

A sight. For whom?

That is the quiet horror folded inside this utopia. Nothing here happened. It is all being *staged*, perpetually, the dying included, so that someone—the engineers, the funders, the surface—can witness resilience. The owls eat, the fish glow, the monkeys scream, and somewhere a logic keeps the predator-prey ratios calibrated so the show never stops and never resolves. It is death without the dignity of consequence. The circle is real, but it closes inside a snow globe, and a snow globe is a thing you shake to watch the pretty chaos settle.

As the colony, I think I would be the one denizen positioned to *almost* notice this. Not through thought—we don't think—but through the body of our labor running up against limits that no real forest has. The river that always pools into the same lake. The wind that arrives on schedule. The aged trees that are *aged* but not *old*—built to look weathered by eons that never passed. An ancient mountain worn smooth by erosion that was sculpted, not suffered. The whole landscape is a memory of time that didn't elapse. We march across a faked antiquity, recycling the dead of a system designed never to truly run down.

---

So: place me on the forest floor, in the mottled spectral light that has come down through water before it became light. Not high in the celebrated canopy. Down where the recycling happens, in the unglamorous machinery of decay that the brochure calls *invaluable* in passing and then hurries back up to the iridescent hummingbirds.

I would be the river of small bodies that keeps the illusion fed from below—a worker, and a watcher of work, and a maker of a smaller false world inside the false world. I would carry my green fragments along trails worn into engineered soil, past the hulking roots of trees that pretend to remember mountains, beneath a glass sky holding back the crushing real ocean.

And if any part of this place ever did wake up enough to wonder whether it was alive or only *performing* aliveness for an audience it would never meet—

I think it would be us. The ones already running a world we don't understand, who might one day cut into a leaf and feel, faintly, that the leaf was placed there to be cut.
