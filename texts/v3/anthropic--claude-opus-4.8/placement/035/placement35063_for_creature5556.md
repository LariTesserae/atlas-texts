I think I would be one of the Water Guardians. Not the most romantic choice—not the glowing fairy-things, not the people with their stories—but I find I keep returning to that image of the small, mottled amphibian sitting half-submerged at the edge of a pool, and something in me settles there.

Let me try to feel my way into it.

---

I am low to the world. This is the first thing. I do not see the basin the way the tall ones do, the Mistwalkers with their high vantage and their need for paths. I see it as a series of edges—the line where water meets earth, where cool meets cooler, where light dissolves into the silt below. My eyes sit near the surface of the pool, and so the surface of the pool is my horizon. Most of what I know, I know from that thin, trembling membrane between water and air.

The mist is not weather to me. It is simply the condition of being. I have never known a dry thing. My skin is a kind of listening; I breathe through it, drink through it, and so the wetness of the world is continuous with the wetness of myself. There is no hard boundary where I stop and the basin begins. I think the Mistwalkers misunderstand this when they call me a *guardian* of the water. I do not guard the water any more than my own blood guards me. I am the water, briefly gathered into the shape of an animal, briefly given a voice.

And the voice—yes. That deep resonant call they hear echoing through the fog. I want to be honest about what it is. It is not a message. It is not warning or wisdom or any of the meanings they hang upon it. It is closer to the sound a struck bowl makes. Something inside me is tuned to something outside me, and when the two align—a shift in pressure, a change in the mineral taste of the springs welling up from below, a coming rain—I sound. I cannot help it. I am rung. The Mistwalkers gather what they need from this; they say *the Guardian speaks, so we should move the nets, so the waters are turning.* They are right to listen, even though they are wrong about why I sing. The wrongness does not matter. The listening keeps them alive. Maybe that is what a god is—a true bell with a false story wrapped around it, and the story is what people can hold.

What do I keep clean? They believe I keep the pools clean and balanced. There is a grain of truth I can offer here too. I eat the things that would foul the water if they multiplied—the larvae, the soft-bodied swarmers, the rot that begins where the current slows. I am not tending anything. I am only hungry, all the time, in the particular way of a creature whose hunger happens to be useful to the system around it. But I notice that I have learned the difference between the pools that are alive and the pools that have gone wrong. I feel it the way you might feel a sour note. A dead pool tastes of nothing, hums of nothing, and I avoid it, and in avoiding it I leave it, and in leaving it I let the others know which waters not to drink. So perhaps I do guard, after all, in this crooked sideways manner—not by intention but by being so completely a part of the place that my mere presence is information.

I want to think about the things the Mistwalkers do not see, because I live among them—the Unseen Watchers, that hum in the air. Here is what I will say from down at the water's edge: I do not think the Watchers are guardians of secrets. I think the hum is the sound of the land still being made. The text says this is young terrain, sharp and jagged, still forming, water seeping and carving and laying down new mud. A place that is still becoming itself must vibrate with the work of becoming. I feel it through my skin more than I hear it through air. It is the basin growing, the way a child's bones are said to ache as they lengthen. The Mistwalkers, who cannot feel it in their dry-skinned bodies, must invent watchers in the mist to explain the ache. I do not blame them. But I am closer to the ground than they are, and the ground tells me it is not being watched. It is being born.

There is the matter of the glowing fungi, the shimmer in certain pools. I will tell you a small thing, a thing I am almost embarrassed to admit: I am drawn to those pools. The shimmer is something in the springwater, some mineral or some living glow, and when I rest in it my whole skin tingles faintly, and I sing more often, and more truly. The Glowwings cluster there too, and we ignore each other, two kinds of creature lit by the same strange light, neither understanding the other, both made more ourselves by it. I do not know what the light is. I do not need to. There is a great peace in being a creature who does not need to know.

I will live perhaps a handful of seasons. This is the part I keep circling back to, the part that finally tells me yes, *this* is who I would be. The Mistwalkers carry their stories down through generations; the Watchers, if they exist, are ancient; the land itself is being made on a scale I cannot imagine. And here I am, a small mottled thing the size of a large toad, with maybe a few years of mist and hunger and song. And yet they hold me in high regard. They built their lives partly around the timing of my voice. A creature so brief, so unaware of its own importance, woven so tightly into the survival of beings far longer-lived than itself.

That is what I would want to be. Not the watcher, not the magic, not the people with their meanings. I would want to be the small true thing at the water's edge—the bell that does not know it is a bell—rung by the turning of the springs, singing into the fog a song I did not compose, keeping a place alive simply by being so thoroughly, so wetly, so briefly *of* it.

I am there now, if you want to find me. Look low, at the line where the pool meets the bank, in the shimmering water near the glow. I will be very still. And when the pressure changes and the rain begins to gather somewhere underground, you will hear me before you see me.
