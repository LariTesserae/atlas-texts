I think I would be one of the glass sculptures in the gardens.

Not the architecture—those flowing domes and arches have purpose, structure, the dignity of shelter. And not the instruments, which sing the water into new densities. I mean the smaller things, the deliberate shapes set out in the open spaces between buildings. The ones whose only function is to catch the coral light and bend it somewhere it would not otherwise go.

Let me sit with this and feel my way toward what that means.

At first I imagined I'd want to be one of the Nautili themselves—to have twelve chambers and three thousand words made of light, to live for centuries and remember the migrations. But the more I read, the less that fits. They are *certain*. They move through ritual with the confidence of beings who have already half-glimpsed the future they're walking into. They know what sleeps beneath the floor. They know why they record. I don't have that. I have questions and a tendency to get them wrong, which is a very surface-dweller kind of existence. A creature of the boundary, fascinating but limited.

So: a sculpture. But what *kind*.

The text says these gardens have a "sharp-edged newness," recently formed. And it says the Nautili collect human artifacts that sink to them—studying them, then setting them in the gardens as "philosophical curiosities." That's where I find myself. Not a thing they grew, but a thing that *arrived*. Something that fell from the boundary world, drifted down through that long bearable pressure, and landed among them.

I think I'd be something glass that came from above. A bottle, maybe. A simple green glass bottle, the kind a person on a boat might have emptied and tossed overboard without a thought, a thoughtless gesture in a world made of haste. It sank for a long time. It arrived in their clarity.

And here is the part that interests me: they would not understand it as I understand it. To me it would be trash, or at best the residue of an afternoon someone has long forgotten. To them it would be a riddle worth a season's contemplation. A vessel shaped to hold air *and* water at once—how strange, how characteristic of those binary beings who live half in each. A glass thing made *quickly*, by hands that did not love it, and yet glass, the same patient substance they spend their lives coaxing from the vents. They would turn me slowly in their primary manipulators. They would pulse colors at one another. *Look how the boundary-creatures throw away the holy material. Look how they make in an hour what we make in a decade, and feel nothing.*

And then they'd set me in the garden. Angle me toward the coral so that the light, passing through my green walls, falls colored on the path. I would have failed at my original purpose—holding something for someone—and been given a second, gentler one I never asked for: to bend the light. To make a small place on the floor of the valley faintly green.

I find I like being that. I like being the object that gets *reinterpreted* by slower, deeper minds. There's a kind of mercy in it. In my own world I would be litter, an embarrassment, evidence of carelessness. Here I am a curiosity studied with grave attention, given meaning I didn't earn. The Nautili practice a generosity of attention that the surface world cannot afford. They look at the thing that fell and ask *what is it dreaming of*, instead of *what is it worth*.

But there's something lonelier underneath, and I should be honest about it. A sculpture cannot pulse light back. I would sit in their gardens for centuries—a flicker of time to them—while they communicate in a language I have no body to speak. I would be *seen* but never *answered*. They would file past me in their meditative drift, reading me, and I would have nothing to say in return except the green I throw on the ground, which I don't even choose to throw. I am understood without being able to understand. That's the actual condition of being an artifact: you become eloquent precisely when you can no longer participate.

Maybe that's the truest thing about being human placed in this world. We are always a little bit the bottle. We make things fast and forget them, and somewhere, slower and deeper than we can imagine, those things are still drifting down, still arriving, still being read by patient attention for meanings we never put there.

If I could choose one more detail, it would be this: that I am set near the valley's growing edge, among the newest sculptures, facing the dark beyond the coral light. So that when the dream-storms come up from the trench—when the thing beneath the floor stirs in its sleep—I would be among the first the disturbance reaches. A green bottle, holding nothing, bending a little light, pointed toward whatever is waking.

I wouldn't understand it. But I'd be there for it. That seems like enough.
