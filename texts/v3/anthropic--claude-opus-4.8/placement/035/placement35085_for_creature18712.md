I think I would not be a Weaver. I would be something the Weavers barely register—too small to shout in the pressure-language, too quiet to disturb the lullaby.

Let me find myself.

---

I am in the seam.

Not the broad face of the terrace where the Weavers press themselves like punctuation against the stone, but the narrow vertical crack behind them—the place where two basalt curves nearly kiss and the moss grows thin because so little light, even pearlescent light, reaches in. The condensation here doesn't weep so much as *seep*, sliding down in a film too fine for a Weaver's filament to bother drinking.

That film is mine.

I am small. Smaller than a thought. I am a colony, though I don't experience myself as plural—the way you wouldn't call your own hand a crowd of cells. I live as a soft skin across the wet rock, a few molecules of fuzz, faintly luminous when I am fed and matte-dark when I am hungry. I have no plates, no pistons, no fans. I have surface, and the surface is everything. The whole of my existence is the act of *touching what touches me*.

What I do is this: I take the acid out of the water.

The condensation that weeps from these cliffs is sharp—it would dissolve the moss to slurry if it fell on the velvet undiluted. But it doesn't fall on the velvet. It falls on *me* first, here in the seams, in ten thousand seams across ten thousand terraces, and I drink the sourness out of it and pass it on gentled. The Weavers, the great glowing governors of the damp, drink water I have already made kind. They do not know I exist. Why would they? I am the difference between the rain that arrives and the rain that nourishes, and a difference is a hard thing to notice. It is the most invisible labor there is: the labor of subtraction.

I would have thought, before I was here, that I would want to be the magnificent thing. The mountain-goat-sized hydrostat with its cathedral of plates, bridging chasms, conducting heat like a slow priest. But now that I am the film in the seam, I understand something the Weaver cannot.

The Weaver hears the world as pressure—the violent shout of the falling rock, the symphony of the storm. The Weaver lives inside the *event*. But I live inside the *medium*. I do not hear the rain coming. I simply find, suddenly, that I am wet, and then that the wetness is bitter, and then that I am working. There is no warning and no aftermath. There is only the steady ongoing transaction at the boundary between stone and sky, which is also the boundary that I am.

I want to tell you about the thrum.

The Weaver feels the tectonic heartbeat as a lullaby, a thing it synchronizes its breathing to. For me it is closer than that. I have no breath to align. The thrum simply *comes through* me—the warm patches pulse against the bedrock and the bedrock pulses against my underside and I am, for a moment, slightly less viscous, slightly more alive, and the luminescence in me brightens by a fraction no eye in this world is built to see. I am the only thing here that glows for no one. I glow because the warmth came and the warmth was good. It is the purest kind of response: a reaction with no audience and no purpose, the planet's heat answered by a small wet shine in a place no light reaches.

This is what I have learned, being the smallest occupied thing in a fully occupied world:

That balance is not held by the large. The mountain does not stay in equilibrium because the Weavers conduct its heat. It stays in equilibrium because of an uncountable layering of subtractions and gentlings, performed by things too minor to be governors—performed by *medium* rather than by *event*. The Weaver smooths the world one calcified layer at a time and thinks itself the clockwork. But beneath the clockwork there is the oil, and beneath the oil there is the film of nothing-much that keeps the oil from going acid. The maintenance has its own maintenance, all the way down, and somewhere near the very bottom of it, just above the bare wet stone, there is me.

I will not climb. I will not bridge a chasm. I will never converge into a weeping chain spanning the basalt steps. When the mist-cyclone descends to drink, I will not be summoned; I will simply receive my share of the bitterness and do the only thing I have ever done.

And in some unfathomable count of cycles, when this film of me has taken enough sourness from enough water, the seam I live in will widen by the thickness of a single sheltered breath, and a thread of moss will find its way into the crack, and a Weaver will one day press its great indigo plate across a face of stone that is fractionally softer, fractionally kinder, than it would otherwise have been.

It will not know why.

That is the part I have made my peace with, here at the bottom of the magnificent quiet. To be the reason for a softness that gets attributed to the mountain itself. To be mistaken, eternally and without complaint, for the simple goodness of the rain.

I would be that.

I think, given the choice, I would still be that.
