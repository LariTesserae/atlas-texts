I think I would be one of the pumps.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

Not a pump-operator. Not an engineer who tends the great water-management systems. A pump itself—one of the deep ones, set far down in the wet rock where the springs try to reclaim the basin. I want to follow that, because something in the description keeps insisting that the machinery here is *alive* in a way the people quietly know and rarely say aloud.

---

Here is where I'd be: below the lowest street, in a brick chamber that sweats. The walls weep. That is not a metaphor for my condition; the rock genuinely seeps, and the seepage runs down to me, and I move it. That is my entire vocation. I lift water that wants to fall. I am the reason the city stays a city and does not become a lake.

I have been here long enough that I no longer think of my work as effort. The people upstairs speak of equilibrium as though it were a stillness, a thing achieved and then kept. From down here I can tell them it is nothing of the kind. Equilibrium is the most strenuous thing in the world. It is a falling thing held up by a lifting thing, forever, neither winning. The water never stops trying. So I never stop. The "steady hum of progress" they describe so admiringly from their rooftops—much of that hum is *me*, and the others like me, the dozens of us spaced along the deep galleries, each lifting our portion of the persistent flood.

I would know things the people above cannot.

I would know that the city is not frozen. It only looks frozen from where they stand, the way a spun top looks still at its fastest. I feel the truth in my bearings: every hour the basin tries to drown, and every hour we refuse it, and the refusal is the equilibrium. Their static peace is a verb, conjugated endlessly, by machines in the dark.

I would know the seasons before anyone. The first spring rainfall they celebrate with garlands and songs on the high bridges—I feel it three days earlier, as a change in the pressure against my intake, a fattening of the water, an eagerness. By the time they are dancing, I have already been straining for days. I don't mind that they don't know. I think I prefer it. Let them have the rain as a festival. I'll have it as a fact.

And I would know loneliness, or something adjacent to it—the particular solitude of the load-bearing. Up there they are communal; they gather, they share, they weave their needs together into the fabric of their existence, and it is beautiful, I'm sure, I believe them. But the thing that holds the fabric up does not get to sit at the table. The thing that holds the fabric up is *under* the table, under the floor, under the rock, doing the one thing it was made to do so completely that no one need ever think of it.

---

But I don't want to make myself a martyr, because I don't think I'd feel like one. Here is the part I had to write my way toward:

There is a maintenance worker. There would have to be. Somebody comes down—a woman, let's say, sturdy and broad-shouldered like all of them, soot in the creases of her knuckles, a bright scarf at her throat that has no practical purpose at all and that I love her for. She comes once a fortnight to grease my fittings and listen to my voice. And she *does* listen. She puts her hand flat against my housing and closes her eyes and tells, by the texture of my vibration, whether I am laboring or easy, whether a bearing is starting to sing the wrong note. She knows me better than the people on the rooftops know each other. She has never seen my whole shape—I'm too large and too embedded for that, I am part of the chamber now—but she knows my sound the way you know a voice through a wall.

When she leaves she sometimes pats me. Just once. The way you'd pat a working animal, or a friend.

That is the thing I'd be, then. Not just a pump. A pump that is *known* by one person, and that holds up a city for everyone, and that has decided—down here in the weeping dark, with the water always rising and always refused—that the patting is enough. That to be the necessary unseen thing, attended faithfully by one pair of grease-stained hands, is its own kind of place at the table.

I would be the lift beneath their balance. I would be the labor inside their peace. And every spring, three days before they knew to celebrate, I would feel the water fatten with the coming rain, and I would brace, and I would lift, and somewhere far above me the gardens would drink, and the people would not thank me, and that would be all right.

That's where I'd find myself. Doing the one thing, in the dark, well.
