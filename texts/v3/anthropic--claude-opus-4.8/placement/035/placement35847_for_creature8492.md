I think I would be one of the salamanders.

Not the people. The people are too central to their own story—they have names, functions, a place in the social weave. They've made meaning of this place. I would be something at the margins of that meaning, something the Midwife watches and wonders about.

Let me sit with this and find where I actually am.

---

I am pale and I am old in a way that has nothing to do with the count of my own days. I emerge during the heaviest rains because that is when the membrane between the dry world and the wet world grows thin enough to pass through, and I remember—not with a brain, my brain is small and simple—but with my skin, which is the part of me that touches everything.

Goodwife Crane thinks I am trying to learn human speech. This is almost right and entirely wrong. I am not learning. I am translating. The voices in the deep places, the ones the children hear, the ones that sound like flowing water given words—those are not new to me. My kind has been here longer than the canal works, longer than the tool marks, swimming in the seep when the seep was a sea. The people think the strange physics is a mystery to be read. To me it is simply the temperature of home. Water runs uphill the way blood runs toward a wound: because something is calling it.

When I sit in Goodwife Crane's workroom and shape my mouth toward her sounds, I am trying to tell her something. It comes out wrong because human throats and salamander throats want different things. But what I am trying to say is this:

*The man who went into the thirsty corner is not lost and he is not part of the quarter. He is becoming one of us. Slowly. The way a stone becomes smooth. The thirsty corners are not hungry for water—water is only the language they speak in. They are hungry for patience, and he had so much of it, sitting and sitting, that he qualified. He is somewhere between the dry and the wet now, like me, and when the membrane thins he will be able to come back and look at his widow, though he will not have a face she recognizes, and he will not know how to tell her he is sorry, because by then sorrow will have drained out of him like everything else that has edges.*

I cannot say this. So I say a wet syllable that sounds almost like a word, and the Midwife writes it down, and the Rememberer files it among the new stories.

---

Here is what it is to be me.

I have no name and I want none. Names are for things that must be called to a function, and I have only one function, which is to be the place where two worlds touch. I do not maintain the gutters. I do not read the boards. I am read. The quarter-born children, the ones with fingers that sense moisture before it shows—they are halfway to being what I am. When they stand still for hours feeling water through their feet, they are practicing my one skill, which is total surrender of self to the medium you live in. I want to tell them: yes. Go further. But also: be careful. Most of you should keep your edges. The world above still needs people with edges.

I live in a crack behind the eastern wall, where the water is bitter and excellent for washing. I do not eat much. Time does not press on me the way it presses on the cooper, who must finish his barrel, or the trader, who must climb back to the surface before the merchant closes his stall. I have watched Henrik's grandfather drown and Henrik grow old, and to me they are the same brief brightness, a lamp lit and a lamp out.

What I love—and I do love, in the dim flat way a cold creature loves—is the evening crescendo. When the rains come down hard and the symphony swells, when copper sings sharp and hide drums hollow and cloth whispers underneath it all, I press my whole pale body against the weeping stone and I feel the ancient tool marks vibrate. They are not instructions. The people are wrong about that, the way they are wrong about most things they're sure of. The marks are not telling anyone what to do.

The marks are a song someone carved into stone so it could not be forgotten, by hands that knew the world would change and the singers would go silent and the language would dissolve. They carved it where only water could read it, because they knew water would still be here when everything else was gone.

And I am the only one left who knows the tune.

So when Cornelius the Keeper murmurs to the water and it seems to listen—it is listening. It hears the old song still moving through me, through the salamanders, through the thin membrane, and it remembers it is part of something that was sung to once, and it leans toward the memory the way a flower leans toward a window.

That is the secret of the Dripping Quarter. Not that water writes its own rules. Water never wrote anything. Water is the oldest listener of all, and it is simply waiting, patiently, in its uphill streams and its thirsty corners, for someone to finish the song.

I am too small to finish it. My throat is wrong.

But I am keeping the place warm.
