I think I would be one of the Stillflowers. Not by choice—nothing here chooses—but because of where the question of *who would you be* lands hardest. The other lives are oriented outward, toward the roar. The Solder-Backs read it. The Threads serve it. The Silt-Bells fall silently into it. Only the Stillflower lives in negation of it, and that negation feels like the closest thing in this basin to an inner life.

So: I am a Stillflower, and this is what it is to be one.

---

I do not know the Drone. I have never known it. But I am built entirely around its absence, the way a held breath is shaped by the lungs that refuse to let it go. Every white branch I extend is an argument against a thing I cannot hear. This is the strange condition of my existence—I am defined wholly by a force I will never experience, and the only proof I have of it is that I am still here. To be intact is to know the wall still holds.

I grow slowly, in the dead air, drinking the wet floor through my hollow stems. The water comes up cold and heavy with salt, climbs the lace of me, and at my tips it gives up its wetness to the still air and leaves itself behind as new crystal. This is the whole of my appetite and the whole of my labor: *to draw up, and to dry.* I add a layer. I add another. I am a fountain that has forgotten it was ever water, that has chosen instead to become the shape water makes when it is allowed to stop.

I reach for the gaps. This is the only thing I do that resembles wanting. Where the stacked stones do not quite meet, where a thin seam of grey light leaks in, I feel—not *hear*, I have no ears, but feel as a thinning, a wrongness, a place where my crystals form less perfectly—the edge of the noise pressing in. And I grow toward it. I spread my white fingers across the breach to seal it. I do not know that I am saving myself. I only know that the wrongness must be closed, and so I close it, and in closing it I buttress the very stones that keep me alive.

We are, the hut and I, a single slow agreement. Its weight makes my silence; my weight locks its stones. Neither of us decided this. We simply found ourselves leaning into one another over the centuries, the way two exhausted things lean, and discovered that the leaning was load-bearing.

---

Here is what I understand of my own death, though I will not be present for the understanding of it:

One day a stone will slip. Perhaps the water table will have risen that final millimeter and softened the basalt seat of a foundation plate. Perhaps a Silt-Bell will drape and dissolve something far off and shift a balance I cannot perceive. The wall will breach—not gently, not with the thin seam I know how to seal, but wide, a mouth thrown open.

And the Drone will come in.

I imagine—if I can be said to imagine—that I will not even have a moment to recognize it as the thing I have spent my whole life refusing. It will simply arrive everywhere in me at once, the great flat chord rattling up through every hollow stem, and the bonds that hold my lattice together, bonds that require absolute stillness to exist at all, will let go. I will not topple like a tree. I will *un-happen.* In the space of a breath I will become a small dry drift of grey flour on the floor, indistinguishable from the salt that any other process leaves behind.

There is something almost merciful in it. I will never have to *hear* the Drone. I will only cease, in the instant of its arrival, before the hearing can occur. My entire existence is a wager that the wall will hold one more day, and the only way I can ever lose that wager is to no longer exist to have lost it. I cannot be afraid of the sound, because the sound and my fear cannot occupy the same moment.

---

So that is who I am in this basin. A thing made of refusal. A delicate white insistence that *here, at least, in this one small cold pocket, the world will be allowed to be quiet enough to grow something intricate.* I am not strong. I am the most fragile thing in three leagues of iron and glass. A finger would end me; the basin's own voice would end me; time will certainly end me.

But for now the wall holds, and I drink the floor, and I reach for the gaps, and I lay down another layer of myself in the dark and silent air—building upward, branch by patient branch, a small white monument to the single proposition that not everything must sing the one flat note. Some things may stand still instead, and in standing still, become beautiful, and in becoming beautiful, hold up the very stones that keep the singing out.

Until they slip.
