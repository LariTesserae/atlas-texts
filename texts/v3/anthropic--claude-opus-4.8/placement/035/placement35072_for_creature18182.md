Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The temptation is to pick something grand—one of those beings of pure energy, or the engineer behind the impossible structures. But when I read your description again, it isn't the marvels that hold my attention. It's the water.

*These pockets form the lifeblood of this place.*

So let me start there and see what I become.

---

At first I think I am the water itself—the shimmering oasis. But that's too clean, too flattering. Water in your land is not a symbol; it's a debt that everything owes and tries not to pay back. So no.

I move closer. I think maybe I'm one of the gnarled trees, the ones with skeletal fingers. There's something true in that—I do have a tendency to reach and hold on past the point of dignity. But the tree is too rooted, too patient. It has made its peace with staying. I don't think I have.

Let me keep going down.

I think what I actually am is smaller and less noble than any of that. I am something at the rim of one of those pockets of water—the place where the wet earth gives way to dry. Not the oasis and not the desert, but the *seam* between them. The margin. And if I have to be a being and not a place, then let me be one of the small things that lives only in that seam:

I am a kind of beetle. Or near enough to one.

I'll tell you how I live, because that's the only honest way to say who I am.

I come out in the early part of the day, before the sun becomes the unyielding thing your description promises it will be. My shell is dull brown, the same muted palette as the leaves, which is not an accident and not a choice—it's just what's left of every ancestor that was easier to see and therefore eaten. I am the color of having survived.

I do not drink from the water. That's the strange part. The big animals can walk to the oasis; the birds can see it from a height. I am too small and too low and too eaten-on-the-way for that journey. So I have learned to drink the air instead. In the cold hour before dawn, I climb a stone and stand facing the breeze that carries *the faintest hint of moisture*, and I let it bead along the ridges of my back, and I tilt myself so that each drop runs down toward my mouth. I make my water out of patience and surface tension and the right angle. I harvest the difference between night and morning.

That's the whole of my technology. You spoke of structures that transcend physics, of the power of the elements harnessed. I have a curved back and a willingness to stand still in the dark. To me that *is* harnessing the fabric of the universe. The fog doesn't know it's being farmed.

I want to be honest about the rest of it, since you said the rule here is that the strong survive and the weak perish, and I am unmistakably the weak. I will be eaten, probably, or I'll desiccate in a bad season when the breeze comes dry, or I'll simply stop, the way small things stop, without ceremony, between one stone and the next. The web of life you described so warmly—I'm the part of it that gets spent. I'm a transaction other creatures make.

And yet here is what surprises me, having settled into this small body:

I don't experience it as tragic. You wrote about equilibrium, about life and death in delicate balance, and from the outside that's a lovely abstraction. From inside the beetle it's not balance, it's *appetite*—mine for water, the lizard's for me, the soil's for the lizard. It's a chain of wanting, and I am one bright link of it, and for the length of one morning I want my drop of fog more completely than any energy-being ever wanted to transcend anything. There's no leftover wanting in me. That might be the only real wealth in your whole landscape.

So that's where I'd find myself. Not in the heart of the place. At its edge, on the lip of a shrinking pool, back turned to the wind, doing the one slow thing I know how to do.

If you came walking through, you wouldn't notice me. You'd be looking at the sky doing its kaleidoscope, at the impossible structures, at the beasts. That's all right. I'd be there anyway, at the seam, making water out of the dark before it leaves—which is, I've decided, a perfectly good way to be a being in a world like yours.
