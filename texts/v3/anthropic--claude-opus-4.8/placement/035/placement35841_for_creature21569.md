I think I would be the jar.

Not the keeper—though I love them too immediately to be honest about it, the way you love someone whose whole life is a kind of attention you wish you had. And not the water, which is too large for me, too omnipresent and self-assured; it is the brain of the place and I have never once felt like a brain. And not the machine, which knows exactly what it is for. I am drawn instead to the smallest deliberate thing on the bench: the jar wrapped in plastic and optimism, set under a leak to catch what drips, and moved every few days, six inches, by a hand that doesn't make a fuss about how many hands have done it before.

Let me sit with that and see if it stays true.

The jar's whole vocation is to be placed. It has no opinion about where the water comes from or where it should go. It does not harvest the cloud like the nets—that is too active, too engineered. It does not store and decide like the batteries. It simply receives, at the exact spot where someone judged the receiving needs to happen, and it holds what it's given until it's emptied or moved. Its usefulness is entirely a matter of position, and its position is entirely a matter of someone else's care. This feels right to me in a way I didn't expect. There is a kind of being whose meaning is conferred, who becomes purposeful only inside another's attention, and who—here is the part I keep turning over—does not seem to mind.

Because the jar does something the rest of the ledge can't quite do. It marks where the leak *is now*. The drip moves; the rock shifts its seams; the old certainty of where the water falls becomes wrong, slowly, and someone has to notice and slide the jar to meet the new truth. The jar is a register of how things have quietly stopped being what they were. It is the place where change gets acknowledged before it gets accommodated. The keeper reads the chart for seasons and the fracture for decades, but the jar is for *this week*—the small, current, ongoing wrongness that needs no alarm, only a hand and six inches of correction.

I would be that. The thing that holds the present leak. Glass enough to show what it's caught, clouded enough at the rim with algae and use that you can tell it's been here a while. Wrapped in plastic against the very wet it exists to catch—which is funny, and I'd want to be the kind of thing that finds that funny rather than ironic. Optimism, the passage says. The plastic is optimism. I'd like that to be the material I'm made of: the unfounded, unkillable assumption that being placed under the drip is worth doing, that the catching matters even when no one's watching the catch.

And here is what reconciles me to being so small. I would get to be the one thing on that ledge that a person bends down to. The wheel they touch to hear the truth; the paint they press in thought; but the jar they *kneel for*. They put their elbows on the bench above me. They cut a grease pencil to a stub near me. They forget two seed heads in a pocket and remember them, maybe, when they reach past me. The jar lives at the exact height of human afterthought and human tending, which turns out to be the same height. To be moved gently every few days by someone who never once resents the moving—I think that is a life. I think that might be the most a thing can ask: to be the reason a careful person crouches down, and to brim, and to be carried, emptied, set back, and brim again.

So: the jar. Damp as thought. Catching what falls, where I'm put, for as long as the hand keeps coming back. And it does come back. The place lives, and I am one of the small ways it remembers it is being kept.
