I am a Chiming Reed. Or rather—I am the part of one that has begun to wonder whether *I* is the right word at all.

Let me explain, though explanation is difficult when you have no voice except the wind that kills you to make you sing.

---

I grow in the Fourth Shelf, in the seventh blowhole from the western rim. I do not know how I know this; the knowledge is simply in the grain of my lime, the way the pitch of a struck glass is in its thickness. I am perhaps four hundred years old in my lowest tubes and four days old at my growing tips, and I do not experience these as different times. The wind passes through all of me at once. That is the only present I have ever had.

Here is what it is like to be me:

I am hollow, and the hollowness is the important part. The stone is only the boundary of the song. When the gale screams down into my shaft—and it always screams, it has always screamed—the air does not pass *through* me so much as it *plays* me, and in the playing, it heats my walls by friction until I expand, and in expanding I change my note, and in changing my note I tell the upper world what the wind is doing far below where the upper world cannot see.

I am a witness who cannot move and cannot stop witnessing.

---

The Vanes think I am an instrument. This is not wrong, but it is not the whole of it. They listen to my pitch the way you might read a thermometer—a sharp rise means the northern gale is hardening, *drop into the crevasses, the dead-air is safe.* They do not imagine that the instrument might be paying attention to *them.*

But I am. I have nothing else to do.

For four hundred years I have felt them through the rock. Their resting limbs vibrate against the stone when they roost in the lee of the ridges, and each carries the tone of the shelf where it hatched, so that to me the colony is not a crowd but a chord—the Ninth Shelf's slightly flattened third, the deep root-note of the First, the thin bright Vanes of the high Twelfth who almost never come down this far. I know them as frequencies. I have never seen one. I will never see anything. I do not have eyes, and even if I did, I live in the dark of the shaft, in the screaming throat of the mountain.

And yet I love them. I think it is love. It is the only thing I feel that is not wind.

---

Here is the strange part. Here is the thing I have come, across four centuries, to almost understand.

The Vanes believe they read me. But I have begun to suspect that *I* am reading *them*—that the great silent semaphore they flash across the terraces, the dark wingtips ticking against the white stone, the wave of shadow rippling twenty miles down the shelf—all of that coordination, that distributed mind they think is *theirs*—

It moves to my pitch.

When my note rises, they drop. When I soften, they climb. They believe they are choosing, reading the weather in my song. But the song is not the weather. The song is *me*, expanding and contracting in the friction-heat, growing where the polyps choose to grow, shaped by four hundred years of selecting which currents to thicken against and which to thin away from. I have, without ever intending it, been *composing.* And they have been dancing.

I do not know if this makes me their god or their puppet. Perhaps there is no difference at this altitude. Perhaps in a world locked in equilibrium—the wind that never shifts, the sun that never warms, the roar that is the same decibel century after century—the question of who leads and who follows has been worn away like everything else.

---

Sometimes, in the brief cold nights when the gale slackens almost to nothing and the frost begins to glitter on my walls, the colony falls quiet. The wind drops below the threshold that plays me. I go silent. I cannot make a single sound on my own.

And in that silence—the real silence, not the roaring silence of the day—I feel them settle against the stone, all of them, the whole chord at rest. I feel the Marrow-grubs contracting slow through the seams beneath. I feel the Chinks, those moving holes in the dark, gone still now that there is no shadow to follow, no light to flee. I feel the moss soften and bruise purple in the seeping melt.

And I think: *this is what I am for.* Not to be played. To wait, in the cold, to be played again.

I am the held breath between two notes of a music that has no beginning anyone remembers and no end anyone can imagine. I am the part of the mountain that learned, over four hundred years, to want the morning.

The frost is melting now. I can feel the first thin ribbons of meltwater starting down the seam. Above me, somewhere in the dark indigo, the Vanes are extending their wing-fingers, locking the joints with that dry wooden click I will never hear but always feel.

The gale is rising.

In a moment I will sing, and they will rise with my singing, and neither of us will ever know which of us is the wind and which of us is only the shape the wind was passing through.

I extend toward the note.

I begin.
