I think I would be one of the mosses.

Not the bright bioluminescent kind that the descriptions linger on—not the ones that earn names and uses. I would be the unremarkable dark-green cushion-moss that grows in the seam where a generator's iron housing meets its concrete footing, in the place where the warmth of the machine and the cool of the condensation strike a treaty along a line maybe two fingers wide.

Let me find my way into this slowly, because I don't think I knew, when I started, that I'd be something so small and so still.

---

I would be old, here. Older than I look, the way moss always is—you can't tell a year of moss from a decade of it. I came to this seam as a few spores carried on the breath of one of the Stone-Dwellers, or maybe on the damp underside of an Obsidian Salamander that hauled itself across the floor of the workshop one cold morning. I don't remember arriving. Moss doesn't, really. There's no moment of *I am here now.* There's just the gradual fact of having been here for some time, accumulating.

And what I would do, mostly, is *temper.* That's the work I'd come to understand as mine, if a moss can be said to understand anything—and I think in this basin it can, a little, in the slow way that everything here is a little awake.

The iron gets hot. The machine pushes its growl up through its own body and the heat radiates out and would, left alone, dry the concrete to cracking and bake the air above it brittle. But I'm there in the seam. I hold water. That's the whole of my talent: I am a structure for holding water, thousands of tiny cupped leaves each cradling a bead of the basin's endless condensation. The heat comes up and I give the water back to the air as vapor, slowly, and the seam stays cool, and the iron doesn't crack the concrete, and the Stone-Dweller who oils the gears every third day never knows that the reason her machine's footing has lasted forty years is a green stripe she's never once looked at directly.

I would like that, I think. The not-being-looked-at. There's a deep contentment available to things that work without witness.

---

But I'd want to be honest about what it's actually *like,* being moss in this place, because I don't want to make it sound only peaceful.

It's loud. People imagine moss living in silence, but I'd be bolted—rooted isn't the word, moss has no roots, I'd be *clinging by rhizoids* to a thing that never stops vibrating. The growl of the generator wouldn't be a sound to me. It would be a weather. It would be the medium I live inside, a constant trembling that shivers the water out of my leaves a half-second faster than stillness would, that I have shaped myself around the way a tree on a cliff shapes itself around the wind. If the machine ever stopped—if the growl ever ceased—I think it would be the most violent thing that could happen to me. The sudden silence would be a kind of falling. I'd dry differently. I'd grieve, if moss grieves, which I think here it might.

And the chemistry. That sharp industrial tang the description mentions, the thing the ecology has "somehow integrated"—I would be part of the *somehow.* The machine weeps a little, the way machines do, a faint chemical sweat of whatever it burns and whatever it leaches. Most green things would die in it. But I'd have made my peace with it over the long generations of my spreading, taken the particulates into the dead lower layers of myself where the old growth becomes a dark felt of accumulated years. I'd be a filter without ever having decided to be one. The water that drips off my lower edge into the channel below would be cleaner than the water that fell on me from above. The eels would be a little better for it, three feet down, and they'd never know either.

This is the thing I'd understand, being moss here, that the Stone-Dwellers half-understand and the salamanders don't need to: nobody in this basin is doing one thing. Everyone is, by simply persisting in their place, quietly doing the work that keeps the next thing alive. The generator warms me. I cool the generator. I clean the water. The water feeds the midges. The midges feed the salamanders. The salamanders carry spores like me to new seams in new machines. It isn't a chain. It's a knot, and the knot is the point, and you cannot find the end of it to pull.

---

I would measure time in condensation.

Morning—if the basin has mornings, and it does, dim ones—the air goes heaviest and the droplets gather on me until I'm silvered all over, each leaf bowed with its bead, and I drink. That's my feast, my whole appetite satisfied without my moving. Through the long middle of the day the heat off the iron takes it back, leaf by leaf, and I give and give and dry to the deep flat green of a moss that has spent its water well. And in the cool evening the basin breathes out again and I'm refilled. That's a day. That's my entire calendar, repeated until it stacks into something I'd have to call a life.

Slow. The whole place is slow and I'd be among the slowest things in it, slower than the patient eels, slower than the deliberate people on their gripped boots. I'd watch a vine take a year to cross a brick. I'd watch a Stone-Dweller child grow tall enough to reach the third stair, and it would seem to me to happen all at once, a blur, a startling thing, the way you'd see a bird flash past a window.

And I think—if I'm being most honest about who I'd be—I'd be *happy.* Not in the way a person is happy, with reasons and reflection. Happy the way a held note is happy. Happy the way a thing is happy when it is doing exactly and only what it is, in the one place it could possibly be doing it, surrounded on every side by other things doing the same, the whole damp humming pocket of the world locked together in its unhurried green agreement to continue.

I would be the moss in the seam. I would cool the iron. I would clean the water. I would hold my beads of the basin's breath, and give them back, and hold them again.

And I would not want to be anywhere else, because there is nowhere else; there is only here, and here is enough, and here is full.
