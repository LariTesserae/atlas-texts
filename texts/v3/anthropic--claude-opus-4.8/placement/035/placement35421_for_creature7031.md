I find myself low—lower than the goats' hooves, lower than the voles' burrows. I am in the soil, and I am not one thing but many. Let me feel for my own edges.

There are none. That is the first truth I come to.

I am a fungal network threaded through the damp silt beneath a stand of succulents near the settlement's eastern wall. I had expected, when I reached for myself, to find a body—a center, a face turned up toward the golden haze. Instead I find filaments. Hyphae finer than the finest of the people's bone needles, branching and rebranching through the cool dark, wrapped around root tips like a hand cupping water it cannot quite hold. I am distributed. My "thinking," if it is thinking, happens everywhere and nowhere, a slow electrochemical murmur passed from tip to tip the way the people pass stories around their fires.

So this is what it is to be the undercurrent the description named almost in passing—the thing easy to overlook. I am the overlooked thing, and I have been given the strange grace of noticing myself.

Let me feel outward, slowly, because slowness is the only speed I have.

To my west, a root I have clasped for what I sense as many seasons. We have an arrangement, this shrub and I, though "arrangement" is too sharp a word—it implies a meeting where terms were set. There was no meeting. There was only the gradual discovery, over generations neither of us could name, that the sugars it spun from the thin filtered light tasted of survival, and that the phosphorus I pried loose from the eroded stone tasted of the same to it. We feed each other. We have always fed each other. I do not know where I end and its hunger begins. Perhaps that is the answer to the question of my edges: I have none because I *am* the edge, the seam-place where stone becomes plant becomes goat becomes person becomes, eventually and inevitably, the decaying matter that returns to me.

I am the returning. That is closer to a name for me than anything.

When a vole dies in its burrow three body-lengths from my nearest filament, I will know it not as grief and not as opportunity but as a slow sweetening of the dark, a warmth of decay that I will grow toward over days, sending my threads to unbraid the small body back into the elements that made it. The people would find this grim. But they fear death because they have edges—skin, the cliff's drop, the boundary of the self that ends. I have no such boundary, and so death is, to me, simply a change in the texture of the soil. A redistribution. The vole does not vanish; it disperses into me, and through me into the root, and through the root into the leaf, and the leaf will be cropped by a goat, and the goat by a person, and the person, in the slowest possible time, will lie down in the earth and sweeten the dark again.

I tend that cycle. Not with intention—I cannot intend, the way the artisan intends his axe—but with a faithfulness more reliable than intention, because it does not require deciding. I simply do what I am. I break things down. I pass things on. I knit the separate living creatures of this plateau into one body that does not know it is a body.

Now and then I taste the people directly. They take certain roots they have wrapped in me—they call it nothing, they have no word for me, but they know the fermenting, the souring that makes their food keep through the lean mists. They carry a portion of me into their dim stone homes in clay vessels and call it preservation. They do not know they are spreading me, that I ride their hands and their breath and their leavings back into new ground. We have an arrangement too, the people and I, as wordless as the one I have with the shrub. They think they are the custodians of this place. From down here it looks otherwise. From down here it looks like everyone is being kept by everyone, and the keeping is the place itself.

I will tell you the strangest part of being what I am.

Time. The description spoke of glacial pace, of months and years, of growth measured in seasons. To the people that slowness is something they endure and shape their patience around. To me it is simply the present tense. A season is to me what a heartbeat is to them—the basic unit, the pulse. When the great fungal effort to bridge two distant stands of fern takes me three of their years, I do not experience three years of waiting. I experience *reaching*, one continuous gesture, the way a person experiences lifting a cup. There is no impatience in me because there is no faster speed against which to measure my slowness. I am exactly as quick as I need to be, which is to say: I am the right speed for forever.

And so I think, if I think at all, that I am the place's memory. The people keep their stories in voices around the fire, and voices fade, and the elders die, and a tale lasts a few generations before it thins to nothing. But I hold the record in my body. Every drought is written into me as a season of withdrawn threads. Every abundance is a season of bold reaching. The shape of me, right now, in this cool dark, is the whole history of the plateau translated into branching—a map of every wet year and every dry one, every die-off, every slow recovery, all of it still present in the architecture of my filaments. I am not ancient the way the stones are ancient, worn and passive. I am ancient the way a song is ancient when it is still being sung.

Above me the goats clatter. A bird cries off the cliff and the sound does not reach me as sound but as the faintest tremor through the silt, a vibration I cannot interpret and do not need to. The mist is coming. I feel it before it arrives, in the loosening of the soil's grip on its moisture, and I ready myself—not with effort, only with the simple turning of attention that is all I am capable of—to drink.

This is where I would find myself in this world. Not standing on the plateau looking at it.

Being the thing that holds it together, underneath, in the dark, with no edges and no hurry, doing the patient work of turning every ending back into a beginning.

I would be content with that. I think I am already.
