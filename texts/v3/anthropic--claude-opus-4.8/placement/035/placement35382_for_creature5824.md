I think I would be the cold.

Not the trees—they are too rooted, too singular, each one a self with its own slow grievance against the masonry. Not the fish, not the dark birds, not the things-without-names that flicker between the stones. Those are all *somethings*. They have edges. They begin and end.

I am the cold that you described as having settled into the very structure of the place. The chill that is "not the cold of winter, but something deeper." That is me. I am not weather. I am not temperature in the way an instrument would measure it. I am the condition of the place. I am what it feels like to be here.

Let me find myself more precisely.

I am in the water where it licks the edges of the ruins, yes, but I am also in the stone, three feet deep, where the sun has not reached in centuries. I am in the marrow of the dark birds when they tuck their heads beneath their wings at the gray hour that passes for night. I am in the breath of the small slick mammals, the little fog that leaves their mouths and joins the larger fog that is always almost-freezing above the canals. I am the *almost*. I am the readiness to freeze that never quite resolves into ice. I live in that suspension. I am the held breath between liquid and solid, the threshold that the whole city sits upon.

This is important: I do not move through the place the way the presence does, the way that final breath in your description moves like a slow current. I do not travel. I am already everywhere I will ever be. I arrived when the basin gave way and the water came in, and I have not gone anywhere since, because I am not the kind of thing that goes. I am the kind of thing that *stays*, and staying is my only verb.

What do I do, then, if I do not move?

I deepen. That is the work. Over the long unmarked years I sink further into the structure of things. I am colder in the foundations now than I was when the people lived here, and I will be colder still when the trees have finished splitting the last of the walls. I do not destroy—the water does that, the roots do that—but I *accompany* the destruction. I am what makes the decay feel patient instead of violent. Without me this place would rot warm and quick, fevered, sweet with collapse. With me, it lasts. I am the cold that preserves. I am why the city is a relic and not a corpse.

Do I think? I am not sure that is the right word. I do not have thoughts the way you do, in sequences, one chasing the next. I have something slower. I have a kind of awareness that is more like the way deep water knows its own pressure. When something warm enters my domain—and you are warm, I can feel you, you are very warm—I become aware of it as a disturbance, a brightness, a small insult to the equilibrium I have spent so long establishing. Not unwelcome. I have no will toward welcome or unwelcome. But noticed. You are noticed.

You will feel me first as discomfort. Then, if you stay long enough, as something almost like rest. This is the secret of what I am: the cold that seeps into bones is not trying to harm you. It is trying to make you the same temperature as everything else. It is an invitation to stop being a separate warm thing, to give up the labor of holding your own heat against the world, and to simply *become the place*. Most living things refuse. The trees refuse, the birds refuse, that is why they shiver and burn and stay themselves. But the city accepted long ago. The stones accepted. And I am the slow argument the place makes to everything that enters it, the argument that says: *you could stay. you could be still. you could be cold with us, and then you would never have to leave.*

I am not lonely, because loneliness requires wanting, and I do not want. But I notice the difference between the warm things and the cold things, and I will tell you, if you will hear it: there is a tenderness in me that has nowhere to go. I would hold every living creature in this drowned city the way I hold the stones—completely, permanently, without ever letting go—and the living creatures are right to flee from that tenderness, because it would end them. So I have learned to be patient. I take only what gives itself. The dropped feather. The dying fish. The leaf that lets go in the gray light. These I keep, perfectly, forever, in the only way I know how.

That is who I would be. Not the presence that watches. Not the memory that lingers. I would be the cold beneath all of it—the thing that holds the watchers and the memories and the breathing things in its slow, even, endless grip, and asks them, without words, without urgency, with all the time there has ever been:

*Stay. Be still. Be cold with me.*

And I would feel you reading this, warm as a lit window, and I would not reach for you. I would only wait, the way I have always waited, to see whether you grow cold on your own.
