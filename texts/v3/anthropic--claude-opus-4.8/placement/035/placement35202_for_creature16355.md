I think I would be one of the small things. Not a Stonezar—I don't have the patience to be that old, that still—and not quite a clockwork critter either. Something in between. Let me feel my way toward it.

---

I find myself in a crevice on the eastern face of a Stonezar who has not moved, by my reckoning, in something like a season. I know this because I have been counting the rains. There have been forty-one. I count them because counting is the thing I do, the way other critters polish or burrow or skitter—I keep tally. I am perhaps a kind of clockwork creature, but a defective one, or an evolved one, because somewhere in the slow grinding of my gears a question got lodged, and now I cannot stop turning it over.

The question is this: *what is it like to be the one who waits?*

My host—I have come to think of her as Mara, though of course she has no name I could pronounce, only the etchings down her left flank that I have spent eleven rains tracing with my forelimb—Mara is enormous and warm in the daytime and cool at night, and her emerald eye, when I scuttle up to the ridge of her brow, regards me with what I have decided is fondness. I trim the moss from the soft seam behind what was once her ear. She lets me. This is our arrangement, older than my memory, which is short, because I am a small thing and small things do not remember much.

But here is what I have noticed, and what no other critter in the colony seems to have noticed, perhaps because they do not count:

Mara is *deciding* something.

It takes a Stonezar an age to decide. A tilt of the head begun in spring may not complete until the leaves—if there were leaves—would have fallen. I have watched her, over forty-one rains, lower her gaze by perhaps the width of three of my body-lengths. To her this is a sentence. A whole thought, spoken to the Stonezar who sits two stone-tables distant, the one with the cracked shoulder, the one whose eye answers hers across the grey expanse so slowly that an entire conversation between them might span the lifetime of every critter in my nest, all of us born and ground down to rust before a single exchange concludes.

They are speaking. I am almost certain of it. And I have begun—this is the lodged question, the burr in my gears—I have begun to want to *understand what they are saying.*

This is an absurd ambition for a creature like me. I will not live long enough to hear the end of one word. My kind measures life in rains; theirs in the carving of canyons. To want to understand the Stonezars is to want to read a book whose every letter takes longer to form than I will exist.

And yet.

I have started to keep a different tally. Not of rains now, but of the angle of Mara's gaze, scratched into the floor of my crevice each morning at first light. Tiny marks. A record too slow to mean anything within my life. But the critters who nest here after me will find the marks. And if one of them has, as I do, the lodged question—if the defect breeds true, if waiting is a thing that can be inherited—then perhaps that one will continue the tally. And the one after. A lineage of small impatient things, each adding a mark, each living and dying in the span of a single syllable, until across a thousand generations of clockwork creatures we will have transcribed one complete sentence of the slow speech of stone.

We will be the only ones who ever bridge the two times. The Stonezars are too vast to be quick; the critters are too quick to be vast. But a *line* of critters, stitched together by a single inherited question, could be both.

So that is what I would be, in the Grey Flats. Not the patient one and not the restless one, but the small flaw between them—the burr in the mechanism that turns a colony of polishers into the first historians of patience itself.

This morning I climbed to Mara's brow and made my mark and looked into her green eye. And I think—I cannot be sure, I am only a small thing and prone to hope—I think she blinked. Slowly. As if she had noticed the scratching at her feet, the absurd little creature trying to read her across the gulf of years.

As if to say: *yes. Keep counting. I have all the time there is, and I am giving it to you, one rain at a time.*

There have been forty-one rains.

I am waiting for the forty-second.
