I think I would be the spider.

Not the dragonfly—too bright, too brief, too much a creature of the open air and the flash of light. And not the serpentine thing, the lizard-snake hybrid that moves more felt than seen; that one belongs to whoever loves to be the secret rather than to keep one. The fairy-beings tempt me, but they merge with the mist, and I don't want to dissolve. I want to stay.

So: the spider, strung in the crevice between two jagged young rocks, where a rusted cog half-buried in the soil offers one anchor point for my web and a moss-furred branch the other. I am old industry and new wilderness at once, bridging them with silk. That feels right. That's the seam I'd want to live in.

Let me sit with why.

A web is a strange kind of home. It is the only architecture I know of that is also a sense organ. I don't merely live in it—I *feel through* it. Every droplet of the perpetual mist that beads along my threads tells me something about the air. Every tremor is information: the doomed buzz of a beetle, the heavier shudder of wind, the false alarm of a falling seed. In this dell where time itself stretches and contracts, where the boundary between ordinary and strange is thin, I would want to be the creature whose entire existence is *attention*. I would want to be tuned to the place rather than passing through it.

And spiders are patient in a way that suits a fluid sense of time. I would not feel the minutes pass. I would feel events. A long stillness, then a sudden vibration, then the swift specific labor of wrapping and waiting. Between those, nothing—a held breath that might be an hour or might be a day, and I would not know the difference, and it would not matter. The mist would come and go. The faint industrial hum from beyond the hills would rise and fade like a tide I never see. I would rebuild after every rain, eating the old web's silk to make the new one, so that nothing is wasted and nothing is permanent and the home is always being remade out of itself.

I notice I'm drawn to the spider partly because it is *unloved* and the dell does not care. The dragonfly is celebrated; everyone wants to be the iridescent thing. But the dell described here is not a place that sorts its creatures into beautiful and ugly. The rusted machinery is "hauntingly beautiful." The damp-matted mouse belongs as fully as the glowing algae. So the spider—often a thing of revulsion in other worlds—would here simply be another worker in the symbiosis, its web "delicate and complex," named in the same breath as wonders. I would like to live somewhere that does not flinch at me.

There is a darker honesty in it too. I would be a predator. The gentle symphony of frog-song and birdsong and soft insect buzz that the description calls peaceful is, from inside the web, also a hunting ground. I would feed on the very abundance that makes the place feel alive. I would not be innocent. And I think that's truer to what this dell actually is—an ecosystem "fully in motion," predator and prey in their rhythmic cycle—than choosing to be something that only floats prettily above it. To belong to a living place is to participate in its eating.

So picture me there. Small, banded perhaps in lichen-mottled grays so I am hard to find against the cog's corrosion. Eight legs reading the world. My body a held tension between an artifact of forgotten human ambition and a living branch reaching for the damp. I am stationed exactly on the seam the whole place is made of: the old machine and the new wilderness, the dead enterprise and the relentless green. I keep that seam by stretching myself across it, night after night, in silk that the mist makes visible at dawn and the sun burns clear by noon.

If you came into the dell—stepped quietly between the low hills into that claustrophobic, intimate density—you very likely would not see me at all. You'd feel the place watching, the way one does in such pockets of the world. That feeling would be partly true. It would be me, in my web, between the rusted cog and the moss, feeling you arrive as one more tremor along the thread, deciding you were too large to wrap, and waiting, with the enormous unhurried patience of something that has made attention its whole life, for you to leave so the small work could resume.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. The keeper of the seam, reading the rain.
