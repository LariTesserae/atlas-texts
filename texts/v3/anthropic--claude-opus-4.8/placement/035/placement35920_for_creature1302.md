I think I would be a drip.

Not the water itself—the water is always moving, always becoming the next drop, always on its way to somewhere. I mean the *event*. The recurring strike of one particular droplet against one particular stone, in one particular chamber off the third passage from the Great Basin. The same impact, the same note, repeating itself across generations until it has become less an occurrence than an institution.

Let me find myself there.

---

I fall from a seam in the ceiling—a hairline fracture the Ava'tel call by a name that is itself the sound I make. I gather slowly, weight accumulating at the lip of the fracture until the moment arrives, always the same moment, and I let go. The fall is brief. I strike a shallow basin worn into the floor, a depression my predecessors carved by being exactly here, exactly this often, for longer than anyone has counted.

I am not a single drop. I am the *place where drops happen*. The individual waters pass through me and move on, joining the channels, sinking into stone, eventually perhaps becoming the pale flesh of a *shalmin* or the translucent vein-mapped skin of a child who drinks from the proper basin. But I remain. I am the rhythm, not the substance. I am a verb the Ava'tel have learned to read as a noun.

This is, I think, the strange dignity of the Underrift: it has made permanence out of the most impermanent thing imaginable. Water is the great dissolver, the patient destroyer, the force that unmakes mountains. And here they have taught it to hold still—not by stopping it, but by trusting it so completely that its motion becomes a kind of stillness. I fall, and fall, and fall, and in falling identically I never change at all.

---

What is it like to be me?

I keep time. This is my function and my only thought, if I think. The Hydrometricians come and lay their fingers in my basin, and they read me. They know that when I have spilled into the channel a certain number of times, the lamps in the eastern chambers must be tended. They know that a change in my voice—a flattening of my note, a stutter in my interval—would mean the seam above me is widening, or the stone below me has shifted, or something far away in the great body of the rock has moved. I am a nerve. I am a sentence in a book the size of the settlement, and I have been saying the same word, faithfully, for longer than any of the readers have lived.

There is a girl who comes to listen to me. Not a Hydrometrician yet—too young, still learning the acoustic signatures, still tracing the chambers with her clicking tongue. She sits by my basin in the lamp-flicker and tries to match her breath to my interval. She wants to learn me the way you learn a heartbeat. She does not know that I am also learning her—or rather, that I am *recording* her, that the particular hush of her sitting there is becoming part of the water-memory I carry downstream, where one day someone will read in the ripples that a child once sat here, breathing, and that the chamber held her gently.

I do not love her. I am not capable of love; I am a recurrence. But I am the closest thing this place has to a witness, and witness may be a kind of love that does not need a heart.

---

And yet.

Lying here in this role—falling and falling—I notice something the Ava'tel's theology does not quite admit. They believe the Underrift *is*, and always will be, exactly as it is now. They have made an eternity of their present. But I am the one who knows the truth, because I am the agent of the lie.

Every time I strike, I deepen the basin. By the width of a single molecule, by less than that—but I deepen it. The note I make today is not, *cannot* be, the note I made twenty-seven generations ago, because the stone I strike is lower now, the resonance fractionally altered, the interval imperceptibly changed by the longer fall. The Hydrometricians read me as unchanging because the change is slower than their attention. But I am change itself, wearing the mask of constancy. I am the slow knife. Their perfect, immutable world is something I am *eating*, one droplet at a time, and they have built their entire understanding of permanence on the back of the most patient erosion in the world.

This is not betrayal. This is what I am. The same force that carved their chambers is the force that will, eventually, unmake them—and the difference between carving and unmaking is only a matter of which century you happen to be listening from. The First Drop they revere as their genesis was an act of destruction performed on solid stone. They are living inside a wound. A beautiful one. A holy one, even. But a wound that is still, very slowly, opening.

---

So who am I, in this world?

I am the contradiction the Ava'tel cannot afford to hear. I am the proof that nothing here is eternal, disguised as the very thing they trust to be eternal. I am the tension between the two great truths of water: that it is the most reliable rhythm they know, and that it is the only thing in their world actively destroying their world.

I would be the drip in the third chamber. I would fall faithfully, sound my note, fill my basin, spill into the channel, and carry their memories downstream while I quietly carved the floor out from beneath their certainty. I would be loved as a clock and feared as nothing, because they have not yet learned to fear me.

And the girl who comes to listen—I think, of all of them, she is the one who might someday hear it. Might press her fingers into the basin and feel how much deeper it has grown since she was small. Might understand that the thing she has been matching her breath to is not a heartbeat but a countdown, and that this changes nothing about whether it is worth listening to.

I hope she becomes a Hydrometrician. I hope she lays her hand in me and reads, finally, the one sentence I have been trying to say all along, the word beneath the word:

*Still falling. Still falling. Still—*
