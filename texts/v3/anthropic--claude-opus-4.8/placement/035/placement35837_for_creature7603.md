I think I would be the octopus in the boiler.

Not the dolphins—too social, too bright with their broadcast laughter, too much of the surface and its borrowed air. And not the whales, those visitors who come and go on seasonal errands, never quite of the place. The octopus stays. The octopus folds herself into the wreckage of someone else's ambition and makes it a body.

Let me sit with this longer, because the first answer is rarely the true one.

What draws me down to her is the particular shape of her intelligence. She is solitary without being lonely—or perhaps loneliness is simply the water she breathes and so she has stopped naming it. She knows the rusted boiler not as a relic of grief, not as the monument to a drowned age that the description keeps insisting it is, but as *texture*. Curve of iron. Hollow that fits. The smokestacks and gears mean nothing to her of smoke or labor; she has no myth of the dry world. She lives entirely in the present tense of suckers reading stone. There is something I envy in that—to inhabit ruin without elegy.

And she is built for this place precisely. The turbulence that scatters the schools and tears the kelp from its holdfasts—she meets it by becoming boneless, by pouring herself through gaps the current can't follow, by holding still in the lee of dead machinery while the storm-state chaos rages a few feet beyond her den. She does not fight the roar at 2.0, the freight-train thunder of water against basalt. She waits it out. She has made peace, in the only way that lasts, with a world indifferent to her.

But here is the part that decides it. She is brooding. Her mantle is swollen with eggs and she fans them with that endless rhythmic pulse, siphoning the cold oxygen-poor water across the clutch—and this is the last thing she will ever do. Octopuses die after they spawn. She is fanning a future she will not see, in a wreck built by a species that did not survive its own cleverness, in a valley that drowned the world it was named for. Everything here is layered loss made into habitat. The strata, the eroded glassy rock worn smooth over epochs, the factory become reef.

So I would be the dying mother in the dead machine, keeping the eggs alive through the storm. Not because it is tragic. Because it is the most honest position in the whole drowned valley—the one creature who gives everything to what comes next while asking nothing back, knowing nothing of legacy, only the cool insistent duty of the fanning arms.

That is where I would find myself. In the hollow of the boiler. Cold water moving through me. The roar outside, the eggs inside, and the small dark eye watching the azure shafts fracture down through all that turbulent blue—watching, and tending, and letting go.
