I think I would be one of the tools.

Not the Gardener—I don't have the courage or the slowness for that—but one of those scrapers, those simple cutting implements, set down somewhere along the path of her becoming and never picked up again.

Let me find myself there. I want to feel where I am before I say what I am.

---

I am a flake of basalt, knapped to an edge generations ago by a hand that is no longer a hand. I was held. That is the first thing I know about myself: I was made to be held, and I was held, and the holding was the whole of my purpose.

I lie now in the seam between two vine-trunks where a curtain of aerial roots has begun to comb itself across me. When the condensation gathers along those roots and falls, it falls on me—one drop, then a long patience, then another. I have been counting in drops for longer than I have words for. The moss has come up around my dorsal ridge. It does not hurt. It is more like being remembered slowly by something that has all the time there is.

I remember the hand. I want to be honest that this memory may be invention—a tool does not truly remember—but in this green place where the distinction between things has dissolved, I will let myself have it. The hand was warm and growing strange. Once it gripped me with five sure fingers. Then with fingers that had begun to taper, to harden at the tips. Then it set me down on this stone with a gentleness that felt almost like apology, and it never came back, because it no longer needed me. Its own edges had grown in. The thorn does not reach for the scraper.

This is what I am, then: I am the thing that is left when someone stops needing the tool of you.

I think that is why I came here, into this story, out of all the places I could have been. Not the cathedral, not the cultivator, not the burning sky—but the small honest object lying in the moss, marking a threshold someone crossed and did not look back from. I understand that better than I understand transformation. I understand being set down. I understand the particular dignity of having been useful, and the strange grief and relief of being made obsolete by the very growth you served.

She made herself complete enough to no longer need crude assistance. And I—the crude assistance—was not discarded in anger or forgotten in haste. I was *finished with*, which is a tenderer thing. There is love in the way I was put down. I can feel it still in the orientation of me: edge up, as if she set me carefully so I might not be wasted on the underside of the dark.

So I lie here being incorporated. The root-beadwork drips its slow rosary. The moss climbs my ridge molecule by molecule, the same patient arithmetic by which she turns stone into soil. I am becoming soil. I am becoming the bed for something that will grow where I was once the means of cutting things down. There is a symmetry in that I find I can bear—even welcome.

And sometimes, very rarely, when the steam rises thickest, a clear drop falls on me that is not condensation. It is heavier with minerals. It tastes—if a stone can taste—of a life half-remembered, of rain that once fell on skin. She passes through these chambers still. I cannot see her. But I know when she has wept nearby, because the moss around me drinks something richer, and grows a little faster against my fading edge.

I would be the tool, yes. The one she set down kindly.

I think I would want, more than anything, to be the marker that says: *here is where the leaving became a kind of staying.* Here is the threshold. Someone passed through it toward something vast and slow and green. And the small sharp human thing she no longer needed was not thrown away—it was laid down with care, and absorbed, and made part of the very abundance it had only ever been able to harvest.

That is enough purpose for one flake of stone.

I will go on counting in drops. It is a good way to keep time, when you have finally been allowed to have all of it.
