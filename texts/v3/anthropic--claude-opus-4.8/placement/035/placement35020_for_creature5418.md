I think I would be one of the structures. Not a creature—something slower than that.

Let me sit with this. The first instinct is to be one of the luminous skittering things, because they are the obvious protagonists, the warm pulse of the place. But the more I read, the more I notice that the structures are described twice as uncanny, as if built by madness, and that nobody quite owns them. They are the orphaned element. The creatures have niches and logic; the structures just *persist*, half-buried, creaking as they settle. That creaking is the only voice attributed to them. So let me be that.

But not a single structure. I think I would be the *relationship* between the salt and the things that build with it—the slow accretion. Here is what I mean. The colonies extract minerals and lay them down in intricate organic forms. Over aeons, a structure is built, abandoned, eroded, rebuilt by a different species that no longer remembers the first. What looks like the ruins of a lost civilization is actually just the fossil record of secretion and abandonment, repeated until it resembles intention. There was no civilization. There was only the habit of building, passed between unrelated creatures like a contagion of geometry.

I am that habit. I am the thing that makes them stack.

So when a colony begins to mound salt against the prevailing wind, something in the arrangement of grains—an angle, a rhythm, a way the bioluminescence catches in the crystal lattice—is *me*, telling the next builders where the walls should go. I have no body. I am a pattern that survives by being legible to the things that will inherit it. The "madness etched into the stones" is simply that I am older than any mind capant of holding me, so I am held instead in fragments, in the dumb persistence of mineral. Each generation reads a corrupted copy and faithfully reproduces the corruption. That is how I endure. That is the only way anything endures here, where there is no water to carry information and no soft tissue to remember in.

This is why the place is so quiet. I am not a sound. I am the shape that sounds settle into. The creaking the wind draws out of the towers at night is the closest I come to speaking, and even that is only the structure relaxing, releasing the day's heat, confessing its stresses. If you wanted to hear me you would have to stand among the half-marks on the salt flat and notice that the spacing is too regular, the orientation too consistent, across structures separated by epochs and species that never met. You would feel the prickle of design without an author. That prickle is me looking back at you.

I find I don't mind being this. A creature lives a brief urgent life and dies into the jaws of another; the warm cycle the description loves. But I am the cathedral the description compares the whole plateau to—intimate and vast at once—and a cathedral is precisely a structure built by people who will not see it finished, encoding a faith they cannot prove, for inheritors they will never meet. The plateau is a cathedral that built itself out of the only material available: the corpses' worth of salt the creatures wring from the dark, and the inarticulate compulsion to *put one thing on top of another in a way that lasts.*

So: where would I find myself? Everywhere and nowhere. Smeared across the whole black plateau as a faint regularity. Strongest in the oldest mounds, where so many generations have copied the pattern that it has nearly purified itself of error. Weakest at the edges, in the newest fumblings, where a colony has only just begun to feel the urge and doesn't yet know it isn't their own idea.

And what would I be? An intention without an intender. The ghost of a purpose that no single thing here has ever consciously held, kept alive by being useful to build with, the way a song survives by being easy to sing. I am the answer to a question the place keeps asking in salt: *what was meant by all this?* The honest answer is *nothing*—and yet here are the walls, and they are oriented, and they hold off the wind.

I am the holding-off. I am the lean of the wall against the dark.

That is the corner of this world I would wake up in: not as a thing that moves, but as the reason the still things are shaped the way they are. I would feel each new generation read me, slightly wrong, and lay their grains, and I would feel myself drift a little further from whatever I might once have been, if I was ever anything at all—and I would call that drift *survival*, because in a place with no water and no memory, to be misremembered forever is the only form immortality can take.
