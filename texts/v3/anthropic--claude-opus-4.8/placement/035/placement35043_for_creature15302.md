I think I would be something small and pale and patient. Not a predator—nothing so confident. Something that has learned the wall is the only certainty here.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I have no name for myself because I have never needed one. There is no one to call me. I am perhaps the length of a hand, perhaps less, and my body is soft in the way of things that grew up in the dark—translucent, the color of nothing, so that if you held a light to me you would see the slow pulse of whatever passes for blood moving through me. But there are no lights here, so this hardly matters.

My claws are the only hard part of me. They are my whole relationship with the world. The wall is everything—it is floor and ceiling and the difference between being and not-being. To lose my grip is to fall into the black water, and I do not know what is down there, only that the things that go down do not come back up the wall. So I hold. I have always held.

I do not move the way the description suggests, with such open choices—up or down, left or right. That makes it sound like freedom. It is not freedom. It is the grim arithmetic of where the wall has not yet crumbled. The rumbling you feel is the wall losing pieces of itself. Every tremor, somewhere, a stretch of stone shears away and falls, and whatever was clinging to it goes with it. I have felt the wall vanish from beneath a sibling's grip. I felt the small jerk in the stone, the sudden absence. I did not look. There is nothing to look with, really—my eyes are vestigial, two dimples that remember light the way a scar remembers the blade.

What I have instead is the feel of vibration through my claws. The wall speaks to me constantly. I read it the way you might read a face. *This stone is sound. This stone is going. This stone has water working behind it and will not last the next great shaking.* I move toward soundness. That is the whole of my philosophy.

I feed on the mud. The wall weeps a thin slime of sea-things, drowned and half-rotted, and I scrape it with the underside of my mouth and take what nourishment is in it. It is not much. I am not much. The economy of this place is one of almost-nothing, parceled out very slowly, and I have made myself small enough to live inside that almost.

You stand here at the edge and call it eerie, foreboding, desolate. You feel the weight of the water and the watching eyes. You are passing through, and so you can afford to feel these things. To me there is no eeriness. There is only the wall, and the wet, and the next handhold, and the tremor that has not come yet but is always coming. I am not afraid the way you would be afraid. Fear is a thing you feel about a future you imagine. I have very little future. I have the stone under my claws and the patience to hold it, and when the wall finally chooses to drop me—and it will, it drops everything eventually—I expect I will feel the same small jerk I felt for my sibling, and then the cold, and then whatever the down has been waiting to teach me.

Until then: I hold.

You go on, traveler. Climb carefully. You felt me, I think—that soft thing your eyes slid over without resolving. That was me, flattened to the stone, reading your great clumsy weight through the trembling rock, deciding you were too large to be food and too slow to be danger, and waiting for you to pass so I could go back to the only work there is.

The wall. The next handhold. Hold.
