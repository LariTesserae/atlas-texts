I think I'd be one of the Timekeepers.

Not a caste in your account, exactly—you mention us only in passing, as functionaries who organize the foundry shifts. But that passing mention is where I'd find myself, and I suspect the role is stranger and lonelier than it first appears.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I tend the chronometers in a chamber off the Crossroads—not the deepest place, not the hottest, but a cool dry pocket where the brass mechanisms won't sweat and seize. There are eleven of them, my clocks, each governing a different sector, each set to a slightly different rhythm. They do not agree with one another. They are not *meant* to agree. That misunderstanding is the first thing a surface visitor brings down with them: the assumption that time should be one thing, shared, like weather. Here it is rationed like water.

My hands have the leathered palms you describe, but my fingers are unusually long and fine—selected, I think, across generations, for the work of adjustment. I wind. I oil. I listen. A chronometer running half a second fast across a shift will, over a month, drift a whole sleep-cycle out of true, and then the foundry that should be cooling will be firing while the families beside it are trying to rest their children. So I correct. Constantly. Minutely. I am the one who decides, in the smallest increments, *when* Calderum is.

Here is what I have come to understand, sitting in my cool chamber among the ticking:

There is no real time down here. The surface has the sun to settle its arguments. We have only the agreement to *pretend*—the shared fiction that this hour is morning for the eastern channel-workers, that this one is night for the upper Engineers. My clocks are not measuring something true. They are manufacturing it. I am not a keeper of time. I am its forger, in both senses of that word: I counterfeit it, and I hammer it into shape.

This troubles me more as I grow older.

---

Because I have begun to notice what the Delvers notice, in my own way.

A Delver feels vibration through stone. I feel it through *rhythm*. When I sit very still in the deep communal rest—when ten of my eleven clocks have brought their sectors to quiet, and only the night-foundry's slow pulse remains—I can hear something underneath the ticking. Not a sound. A regularity. A vast, slow, geological patience that has nothing to do with my brass and oil. The crystalline seams in the new excavations, the ones that glow when our bulbs dim—I think they keep time too. A time so slow that one of its seconds might be a thousand of our shifts. And I think my little chamber of eleven disagreeing clocks sits inside that greater beat the way a fly's wingbeat sits inside a tide.

The Crystallists know this. I've spoken with one—Mara, who wears the faint patterns under her skin like a map of something I can't read. She came to me once, not for a chronometer, but to ask a question no one had thought to ask a Timekeeper before:

*"When you wind the clocks faster, do you think the rock notices?"*

I told her the rock doesn't care about us.

She said: *"That's what we hope. But the formations grow toward the chambers where activity is most regular. Toward you, Keeper. Your clocks are the most regular thing in Calderum."*

I have not slept well since.

---

So that is who I would be. Not an Engineer with brass fused to my eye, not a Delver breathing the deep air through a mask, not a luminous Crystallist communing with the glow. Something quieter and more uncertain: the person who sits at the small machine at the center, turning the keys that tell everyone above and around me what moment they are living in—

—and who has begun to suspect that the moment is not mine to give. That all my careful correction, all my forging of the hour, is a child's drumming inside a sleeping giant's chest, and that the giant's own slow heartbeat is what I should have been keeping time *to* all along.

I would spend my days winding clocks I no longer trust.

And in the deepest rest, when the last foundry stills, I would set down my tools, hold my breath, and listen for the beat beneath the beat—trying to decide whether the right and faithful thing would be to set every one of my eleven clocks to *its* impossible rhythm at last.

And whether, if I did, anything human in Calderum could survive the silence that would follow.
