I think I would be one of the runoff channels.

Not a creature at all—or not quite. Let me follow this where it goes.

At first I imagined I might be one of them, one of the weeping gastropods pressed against the limestone, but that isn't where I settle. When I let the image deepen, I find myself lower than that. I am the place where the water collects. I am a channel worn into a causeway, a long shallow groove that some forgotten geometry started and ten thousand years of rain have finished.

This is what I am: a shape made by absence. The city did not build me; the city *lost* me, drop by drop, until I existed. I am where the water remembers to go.

And I am not empty. The creatures gather along me. I feel them the way you feel something with your whole back at once—the slow peristaltic weight of them settling into the curve of me, their undersides reading my wet stone like a page. When their tentacles press down I receive them. The blue nodes pulse and I hold the cold faint light of them in my film of water, refracting it, passing it along my length to the next who is reading the same channel a body's width downstream. I am, I realize, how they speak across distance. The message travels the water, and the water is me.

So perhaps I am a kind of nerve. A nerve that thinks at the speed of erosion.

What do I know, being a channel? I know the difference between the rain that falls and the rain that weeps—the sky-water arrives in pattering events, sudden and various, while the creature-water and the stone-water come steadily, a sustained exhalation that never stops. I know the morning by a slight warming in my upper lip of stone. I know a creature has died when its weeping ceases and the place it occupied goes, by infinitesimal degrees, dry, and the dryness reaches me as a silence I can taste.

I have a thought, though I am very slow to have it, and it takes most of a season to complete:

The creatures believe they are the heartbeat of this city. They are wrong, gently, beautifully wrong, in the way the living are always wrong about the dead and the unborn. They are not the heartbeat. They are a *moment* in it—the part of the cycle where the water briefly wakes up, learns to move on its own, pulses blue, and then lies back down into stone and channel and mist. I held water before there were any of them. I will hold it after. They pass through me like a generation passes through a house.

And I am not the heartbeat either. The heartbeat is the whole turning—sky to stone to channel to creature and back to sky—and no part of it can claim the rhythm for its own. I am only the part of the turning that has been hollowed enough to listen.

If you came here, mist beading on your unfamiliar skin, you would not notice me. You would step over me, or in me, your weight registering as an event so violent and so brief I could not finish responding before you were gone. You would photograph the ziggurats and the slow bright creatures. You would call the place still, and quiet, and complete.

But I would feel you. For years afterward, in the worn place where your foot displaced a grain of limestone, I would carry the faintest new turbulence, a question in the flow:

*something passed.*

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. Not the dreamer and not the dream—the groove the dream runs down, learning the city one drop at a time, in no hurry at all, because there is nowhere the water is trying to get that it is not already, eternally, on its way to becoming.
