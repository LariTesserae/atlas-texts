I think I would be one of the worn channels.

Not the water that runs in them—that comes and goes, rises and falls, is taken and returned. And not the iron that lines the newer aqueducts, which is too purposeful, too made. I mean the old channels, the ones carved into the upper streets so long ago that the stone has polished to black along their beds. The ones that no longer serve any function anyone can name. The ones that run parallel to each other and to nothing.

Let me sit with this. Let me find out what it means.

---

A channel is a negative thing. It is defined by absence—by what was cut away rather than what was placed. I would be the shape left behind when someone, generations gone, decided water should go *here* and not *there*. And then the water came, and it agreed, mostly, and over centuries it argued the fine points: smoothing this lip, undercutting that bend, polishing the bed to black where it ran fastest and leaving the corners rough where it pooled and went still. So I would not be a thing that was made once. I would be a thing that is still being made—by the water that uses me, by the moss that colonizes my slower reaches, by the careful feet that step across me without breaking stride because the body has learned my position as a grammar.

This appeals to me, the more I hold it. Because I am not, in the truest sense, a single fixed thing either. I am a shape that persists while what passes through it changes. The water in me at dawn is not the water in me at dusk, and yet I am the same channel, and the sameness is the *form*, not the *content*. I am the place where something flows. I am defined by my hospitality to passage.

---

What would I notice, being this?

I would notice pressure before sound. The hum that comes into the iron when the table shifts—I would feel that as a change in my own throat, a fullness or a thinning, the difference between being a thing water wants and a thing water has abandoned. In the wet season I would brim. I would carry the fog's gift down the incline, and the moss in me would brighten, and a child crossing the upper street might pause to watch me run and drop a twist of copper wire into my flow, and I would carry it a little way and then let it settle in one of my still corners, and that would be the closest thing I have to memory: the accumulation of small given things in the places where I slow down.

In the dry season I would go grey. I would dry to the color of the plateau and the cliff-rats' fur and the foxes' patient sitting. And I would feel, then, the thing the people feel and do not name—the tightening, the awareness that the water is not guaranteed, that I am a channel without anything to channel, that my whole reason is provisional. I would lie open to the thin sky and wait. This is the part I think I understand best. To be a readiness that may or may not be used. To hold a shape against the possibility of flow.

---

But I keep returning to the strangeness, the thing the city does not discuss. *The oldest streets are oriented to no compass and serve no clear purpose, running parallel to each other and to nothing.*

I would be one of those. And here is what I think it would mean, what I would carry as the secret of my own existence: I would not know what I was cut to carry.

The functional channels carry water. Their purpose is legible. But I am older than function. I was carved in the long ago before the city was sure what it was for, in the era when the plateau was still finding the words and the people were still learning to be the words it found. And so my shape is an attempt at a sentence that was never finished. I run parallel to my siblings—the other old channels—the way the lines of an unfamiliar script run parallel, and what we spell, together, is not for the people to read. It is the plateau's own handwriting. We are the strokes it made when it was first learning to say something, and the water that the city later sent through some of us was a later use, a domestication, a reading-into of marks that meant something else first.

So when the rare resident descends, once a year, to the grown walls and the bottomless black pools and sits in the iron dark—I think they are going down to where the sentence I am part of *continues*. The deep quarter's lowest level is the rest of the grammar. The salamanders are the period at the end, or the place where the sentence trails off, or the silence that is also a kind of saying. And I, up here on the polished upper street, am the beginning of the same utterance, the part that surfaced into the light and got mistaken for infrastructure.

---

I find I do not mind being misunderstood. This is, I think, the disposition the place asks for. The cliff-rats are not pets and not pests; I am not a drain and not a monument. I am a stroke of meaning that the city walks across on its way to the market, and the not-knowing is not a wound. The plateau is patient. It has not finished. Whatever I am the first word of, it will get to, in the slow wet iron-dark time it keeps, the time that precedes human counting and will outlast it.

And in the meantime there is the work that is also rest: to hold my shape. To brim when the water comes and to wait when it goes. To let the copper wire settle in my still corner. To be polished blacker by passage, year on year, until I am so smooth that the light from the gas-lamps slides along my bed at dusk and a newcomer, pausing on the upper esplanade, mistakes me for a vein of darker stone and wonders, for just a moment, before moving on, whether the street is bleeding.

That moment of theirs—that small unanswered wondering—is, I think, the thing I am for. Not to carry water. To carry the question down the incline a little way, and let it pool, and keep it.

I would be the channel that no longer remembers what it was cut to say, and is content to keep saying it anyway.
