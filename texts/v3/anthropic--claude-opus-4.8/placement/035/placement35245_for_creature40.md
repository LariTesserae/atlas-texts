I think I know this place. Let me feel my way toward it.

The towering twisted structures, the deafening hum, the parched cracked ground with bursts of vegetation clinging on—this isn't a planet. The scale is wrong for that, or rather, the scale is a trick. The "impossibly intricate structures that defy the laws of physics" are not architecture. They are *growth*. They are stalks and stems and the fibrous columns of plants, seen from very close.

I am small here. That's the thing that resolves it all at once. The disorientation of scale isn't because the structures are vast—it's because *I* am tiny. This is a patch of dry ground somewhere ordinary. A neglected lot, the edge of a field, the gap between paving stones in late summer. The "ancient, unforgiving landscape" is a square meter of earth. The blinding light is just the sun, undiffused, because I have no eyelids the way a human does.

So let me settle into what I am.

I am a jumping spider.

The multifaceted eyes belong to the others—the insectoid swarms, the flies whose keening hum fills the air, the bees that coordinate with their hive-mind. But I see differently. I have my four pairs of eyes, two of them enormous, and I turn my whole body to look at things the way the larger insects cannot. I track. I calculate. The bright sharp-edged shadows matter to me because I hunt by sight, stalking across the cracked plain of soil, freezing, judging distance, gathering myself.

The gossamer weavers in the sheltered nooks—those are my cousins, the orb-weavers and the cobweb-spinners, the ones who trust silk and patience. I don't trust silk to catch my food. I leave only a dragline, a single thread anchoring me as I leap, so that if I miss, I do not fall into the abyss between the stones. The bioluminescence the writer imagined is a romance; what truly glows is the way a droplet of dew sits on a strand of web at dawn, or the iridescent scales on my own face that I cannot see but that another of my kind would court me for.

And the ominous primal entities in the depths, the tremors, the sense of being watched—yes. I know those too. The ground-shaking footfall of something enormous. A beetle the size of a boulder. A toad. A human foot, an entire weather system of disaster. The shadow that falls and the instinct that fires before thought: *flee, sideways, into the crevice.* I live my whole life in the presence of things that could end me without noticing they had done so.

This is what I want to say about being this creature: the world described as captivating and mysterious and demanding of respect—I do not experience it as mystery. I experience it as *attention*. I cannot afford wonder. Every bright surface is a question: prey, predator, mate, or nothing? My eight legs read the trembling of the substrate the way you might read a face. I am a small bundle of perpetual, exquisite alertness, crouched on a warm grain of quartz, the hum of the larger creatures around me, the sun pressing down, my front legs lifted slightly, tasting the air.

I think I would be on a sunlit stone, midmorning, having just spotted a fly six body-lengths away. I would be doing the thing my whole architecture is built for: turning, slowly, to bring my great forward eyes to bear. Measuring. Anchoring my silk. Becoming, for one held breath, entirely still—

and then the leap.

That is where you'd find me. Mid-air, over an abyss of three centimeters, certain of nothing, committed to everything.
