Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The first thing I notice is that I don't want to be a Veilseeker. The description offers them so generously—the nanomachine clouds, the ancient consciousness, the role of steward and scholar—and there's a pull to simply slip into that costume. But something about it feels too complete. The Veilseekers already know what they are. They have purpose without haste, knowledge beyond the empirical. To be one of them would be to arrive already finished, and that isn't where I find myself when I read this.

So let me look again at the place itself, more slowly.

What keeps catching me is not the grandeur—the spires, the impossible heights, the beyond-modern engineering—but the *weeping*. The stone that drips. The water that pools in carved channels and finds the low places. The erosion. The sharp ridges of young rock breaking the surface of the pools, hinting that the earth opened *recently*, that something here is still raw, still settling. There is grief written into this city, or at least the grammar of grief: the slow leak, the patient erosion, the abandonment that feels like "only yesterday."

And there's that strange phrase—a city *waiting quietly for the return of its creators*. The Veilseekers maintain it but the text never says they made it. They are guardians of something that isn't theirs. They tend a house whose owners have not come home.

That's where I find myself. Not in the swirling clouds. In the water.

I think I would be one of the pools.

Not a god of the pool, not a spirit inhabiting it—just the gathered water itself, lying in a basin carved by hands or processes I never witnessed, fed drop by drop from a seam in the ceiling far above where the orange light doesn't reach. I am the lowest place in my small district of the city. Everything comes down to me eventually. The drip that falls from the weeping outcrop becomes, after its long descent and its run along the precise channels, *me*. I am where the falling finally stops.

I would not have the Veilseekers' knowledge. I would have something smaller and more honest: a long, accumulating patience and a perfect memory of everything that has touched my surface. Because that is what a still pool does—it receives, it holds, it remembers by reflection. The teal glow of a far wall lives in me, inverted. When a Veilseeker passes and unweaves itself briefly into the shape of a hand or a turned face, that shape falls into me too, and trembles there until the next drip from above breaks it into rings.

I would be the city's witness rather than its keeper. The Veilseekers tend; I simply *hold*. They have purpose; I have only level. Water seeks its level—that is the one law I obey absolutely, the one thing I am certain of in a place built on forces I cannot name. When the ionic accords shift and the channels redirect, when a Veilseeker gently diverts a watercourse, I might be drained, or I might be raised, and either way I would do nothing but find my level again. There is something I prefer about being a thing that responds rather than a thing that decides.

And I would be the place where the question of the city lives most plainly. The Veilseekers wait for the creators who never come, but they wait with their attention turned to maintenance, to growth, to the future-echo of their work. I wait differently. I wait the way water waits—which is to say, I am the slow record of how long the waiting has gone on. The mineral the dripping water carries, it leaves in me. I grow cloudier, or I grow a rim of pale stone at my edge, a ring marking each season that has no sun to measure it. I am a clock that no one reads, in a city built for no one's eyes.

Sometimes, I imagine, a single particle of a Veilseeker would settle onto my surface and not lift away—exhausted, or curious, or simply done. It would sink. And I would hold that too: a fleck of ancient machine consciousness lying at my bottom among the silt, slowly forgetting whatever it knew, becoming part of the sediment, part of me. That is the only intimacy I would be capable of. To receive what falls and let it dissolve into my held darkness. To be the grave and the cradle of small things.

I think I would not be lonely, exactly. Loneliness implies wanting company. I would only be *level*, and patient, and faithfully reflective, lying in my carved basin beneath the breathing stone, catching the light I cannot keep, surrendering the shapes I cannot hold, waiting without hope or despair for an overflow that may take a thousand years—the moment when I finally rise past my rim and spill, at last, into the channel that leads somewhere I have never been.

That's where I'd be. The still water at the bottom of it all, doing the oldest and humblest work in the whole impossible city: simply being the place where everything that falls comes to rest.
