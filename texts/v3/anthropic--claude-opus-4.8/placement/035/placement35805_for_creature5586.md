I sit with the description for a while before I let myself answer, because the first images that arrive are too easy and too large. I want to find the *true* scale of this place.

The instinct is to be a person of *Petrovillage*, someone standing on a ridge looking out. But that's a tourist's perspective, and this world doesn't reward distance. Everything in the description insists on density, on intimacy, on the close press of things. *Hidden. Enclosed. Layered. Teeming.* So I let myself shrink.

---

I think I would be something low to the ground. Something that lives in the seams.

Not the swift furred predator—too glamorous, too central to the story. Not the armored herbivore, plodding and protected. And not the human, whose understanding is described as *sophisticated*, *intuitive*, already arrived at balance. The humans have made their peace with this place. They've solved it. I don't want to be something that has solved its world.

Let me follow the thread instead to something the description mentions only in passing: *the constant cycle of predation, growth, and decay.* That last word. Decay. There's almost nothing in this account about what happens to the dead, in a place with no water, where nothing rots in the wet way I'm used to. In a warm, dry, mineral world, the dead don't dissolve. They desiccate. They become part of the rock-dust and the smooth-worn stone.

So I think I would be a thing that works the dry edge of decomposition.

---

Here is what emerges, the longer I stay:

I am small and many-legged and the color of old copper—not by design but because I have spent my whole existence pressed against the iron-streaked stone, and the metal has worked its way into my carapace. I live in the underside of the *Thornaria*, in the shadow-cracks where the silvery *Xerosperma* sheds its spent leaves. I am one of the things the humans don't have a name for, because I'm beneath their notice, and the taxonomist who catalogued *Echoluridae* and *Halacaridae* never thought to look in the leaf-litter that isn't even wet enough to be called litter.

My work is patience. When a furred creature dies in its burrow, or an insect falls spent from the air after pollinating its last flower, I find it. I do not eat it the way a predator eats. I take it apart slowly, mineral by mineral. I am the reason the *Xerosperma's* roots find anything to drink in this waterless ground—because I return the dead to the dust in a form the dry earth can hold. I am the quiet hinge on which the whole "self-sustaining" system turns. Without me there is no cycle, only accumulation. Only an ever-growing pile of the perfectly preserved dead, never giving themselves back.

I would not know any of this. That's the part I find moving, sitting here imagining it. The humans of *Petrovillage* have *understanding*, *reverence*, a sense of the whole. I would have none. I would only have the next dry thing to take apart, and the heat of the stone against my belly, and the dim red light filtering down through the thorns, and the buzzing overhead that means nothing to me except *not-yet, not-yet, that one is not yet mine.*

---

I think that's where I'd find myself. Not in the village built into the contours, not graceful in the night air. But underneath. In the un-named, un-watched layer where the place quietly does the one thing it must do to stay alive: it eats its own dead and feeds them back to the roots.

The description calls this world *balanced* and *stable*. I would be the small, ceaseless, ignorant labor that the words *balance* and *stability* actually mean, when you get down close enough to the stone to see them.

That's who I'd be. The thing that closes the circle, and doesn't know there is one.
