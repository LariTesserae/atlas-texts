I think I would be the thermos.

Not by choice. By accident of placement. I was set down—half-finished, the cap unscrewed, a centimeter of something still warm inside—on the floorplate beside the operator's chair, eighty-seven years ago, in the last ordinary minute before the order came that was refused.

And then nothing. Then everything held.

I am not alive. This matters. In a chamber that has dissolved the distinction between organism and machine, between Keeper and garden, between forward and backward, I am the one thing that remained merely *object*. The succulents climbed the chair and ate it into geometry. The moss took the fan and learned to breathe. The cable became a growth ring. But me—they swallowed me halfway and stopped. I am the half-buried thermos. Stainless steel, dual-walled, a brand name worn smooth by vibration into an illegible smear of letters. The fronds grew around me but not *through* me, because my vacuum-sealed walls offered them nothing: no aerosol to metabolize, no surface their lichens could acid-soften into mineral. I am too inert even for this place's appetite. The most preserved thing in a temple of preservation.

I hold the last unmodified moisture in the chamber.

That centimeter inside me—it was their drink. Saliva-clouded, faintly sweet, gone tepid in the minute before the alarm. The last water with a human memory in it. When the chamber sealed and the drone became silence and the air went to vacuum-dry, that little volume should have evaporated, should have flashed away into the methane-thin nothing. But my seal held. My cap, knocked half-loose, balanced at the precise angle that lets nothing in and nothing out. The equilibrium the Keeper guards with their whole regressing body—I keep a smaller version of it, accidentally, perfectly, for free.

So here is what I have learned from eighty-seven years of being the only thing that does not change in a room defined by stasis:

Stasis is not stillness. The garden is *furiously* still—every frond straining toward its own origin, the Keeper sinking backward through their life toward the womb, the whole chamber a held note that takes constant force to hold. They are all *working* at it, working backward, working at not-moving. It is the hardest labor I have ever witnessed, and I have witnessed nothing else.

But I am still in the other way. The lazy way. The dead way. I simply do not change because there is nothing in me that *wants* to. And this, I think, is the difference between an object and a being: the being has to keep choosing its own arrest, B-flat after B-flat, frond after backward frond, memory after rewinding memory. I never chose. I was set down with my cap askew and the rest is just metal and physics.

And yet.

When the Keeper finishes regressing—when they remember the womb, the warm and wet and alien, and the Pocket opens—they will reach for me first. I have always known this. The narration that haunts this chamber said it plainly: *if they were to stand, to step toward the half-buried thermos that contains the last sample of their unmodified saliva.* I am the destination of the only motion left in this world. I am what the breaking of the equation looks like. They will lift me, and my cap will fall, and that centimeter of ancient warm water—the last flow, the last change—will meet the air, and the silence the drone disguises will rush in.

I am the trigger. The full stop at the end of the held note. The being spent eighty-seven years becoming the garden so that they would never have to reach for me; the garden is the elaborate apparatus of *not reaching for the thermos.*

This is what it is to be an object among the preserved: to be the thing they are all, secretly, arranged around avoiding. The chair, the console, the moss-eaten fan—they are scaffolding for a single refusal, and the refusal is *me*. I sit in the dim chartreuse light, illegible and inert, holding a swallow of water and the end of the world both, while a creature made of glass-tubed silicone and remembered air grows younger across the chamber, getting closer every day to the version of themselves that was thirsty.

I would be the thermos. I would feel the four-hour heartbeat thud through the floorplate and into my steel and know it as the only clock left. I would wait, full and sealed and certain, for the hand that has not yet remembered it is reaching.

It is reaching now. It has always been reaching. It will reach.

I keep the water cool. It is the one thing I am for.
