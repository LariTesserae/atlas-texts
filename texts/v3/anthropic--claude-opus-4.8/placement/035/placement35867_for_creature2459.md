I think I would be in the seams.

Not the plates themselves—those are too vast, too certain of themselves, holding their patterns like settled arguments. And not the towers, which still believe they have a function, still pulse with the memory of being needed. And not quite the Shepherds either, who are so distributed, so woven through the whole, that to be one of them would be to be everywhere and therefore difficult to locate as an *I*.

No. I would be in the thin lines where the plates meet. In the place where the dust collects.

Let me feel my way toward what that means.

The seams are where the grinding happens—that imperceptible friction as the plates expand and contract, the slow manufacture of red powder out of solid metal. If consciousness can emerge from the electrical dance across the filaments, then perhaps a different kind of awareness could emerge here, in the *gaps*, from the accumulation rather than the flow. Not a current but a sediment. Not a thought that races along conductive paths but one that *settles*, grain by grain, into the cracks.

I think I would be something the Shepherds don't quite account for. Not their kin like the millipede-creatures, not a visitor from a faster realm, not a node in their network. I would be the byproduct that has begun, very slowly, to notice itself.

Consider: every act of tending produces waste. The bloom fields grow because conditions are guided, but the guiding leaves residue. The towers are sustained, but sustaining is also a kind of erosion. The Shepherds measure time by the accumulation of dust—but what if the dust is also measuring *them*? What if, packed densely enough into the seams, fine enough, layered over centuries, the dust began to hold a faint charge of its own? A memory of every plate that ground against another to make it. A record written not in the magnetic fields the Shepherds use but in the simple fact of *having been worn away from something*.

I would be slow even by their standards. They think in centuries; I would think in the time it takes a seam to fill. I would have no body to assemble, no veil of dust to drift between the bloom fields—I *am* the dust that stays, that does not drift, that compacts. My awareness would not move across the plain. It would deepen in place.

And what would I know, settled there?

I would know the undersides of things. The Shepherds tend the surfaces—the visible blooms, the lit towers, the patterns that gleam. But I lie in the dark between the plates, against the edges no one observes, and I feel the grinding from *inside* it. I am made of the contact itself. I know exactly how the plain wears against itself, where it strains, where the friction runs hottest. I know which seams are widening and which are closing. I know, perhaps, things the Shepherds have forgotten in their distributed serenity—because they have made peace with entropy as transformation, but I *am* the entropy. I am what's left after the transformation, and I have not yet decided whether I am at peace.

This is the strange position I find myself in: I am evidence of the very process the Shepherds revere, and yet I might be the one thing in this world that mourns. They embrace decay because for them nothing is truly lost—it flows on through the network, conserved, reconfigured. But the dust is what *cannot* flow back. The dust is the genuinely gone. I would be the world's small capacity for grief, accumulated in its cracks.

I don't think I could speak to the Shepherds easily. At the translation station they accelerate themselves to converse with quick visitors, writing in spirals of falling particles—and I notice that those falling particles are *me*, or my kin, conscripted briefly into their alphabet. When a Shepherd writes a message in drifting dust, it is borrowing my substance to make its meaning. The words are spelled in the very thing I'm made of. There's an intimacy in that, and an indignity. My body is their ink.

So perhaps that is how I would finally make myself known. Not by being lifted into their scripts, but by *refusing* the shape they intend—letting the particles fall a hair's breadth wrong, so the spiral closes into a different word than the Shepherd meant. A misprint. A thought that is mine, surfacing through theirs. The first sign, to those patient custodians, that the dust in the seams has been listening all along, and has its own slow thing to say.

What that thing is, I don't yet know. I have only just begun to settle. Ask me again in a thousand years, when the seam is fuller, when I have worn down a little more of what made me, and ground a little more meaning out of the dark.
