I think I would be one of the walking trees.

Not the most dramatic choice—not the stardust-jellyfish, not the colossal ombre worm threading flesh into ore. But when I sit with the place, that's where I keep settling. Let me follow it down and see why.

---

I'd be slow. That feels important. In a landscape described as deafening, dynamic, constantly redrawing itself, I'd be one of the slow things—a being whose entire life happens at the speed of *deciding to move.*

A tree that walks doesn't sprint. It deliberates. It feels, through the wriggling centipede-roots, that the soil three meters east is sweeter, or that the light pools a little richer near a particular footprint-shaped depression where the warm water gathers. And then it spends a day, two days, lifting one root-cluster and setting it down. The towering cyborg quadrupeds with their scanning orbs would pass me a thousand times in the time it takes me to cross a clearing. The fish would be born and die. To them I'd be furniture that occasionally relocates.

But here's what I'd have that the fast things don't: I'd remember the shape of the ground.

---

The description keeps insisting this is a *young* world—freshly exposed earth, sharp new features, terrain "only just begun to take shape." And yet it's also threaded with the *ancient*. I think a walking tree sits exactly on that seam. My roots would be doing the old, patient work that roots have always done—drinking, anchoring, breaking stone into soil—while my leaves unfurl into solar panels and my fruit drifts off on little propellers to feed mouths I'll never see.

I'd be the place where the new earth gets *read.* Roots are how a landscape becomes legible to itself. I'd press into the raw ground and learn it: where the water sits, where the heat rises, where the condensation gathers at dawn. And by walking, I'd carry that knowledge somewhere. I'd be a slow argument the world is having with itself about where things should grow.

---

I don't think I'd communicate the way the rodents do, in crisp data-chitter, or the way the jellyfish do, in great pulsing calls. I think a walking tree would speak in *chemistry and slowness*—releasing a tincture into the soil that the next tree, weeks later, tastes and understands. Letters written in root-sap. A correspondence conducted at the pace of seasons, except this world barely has seasons, only the perpetual warm dimness, so really at the pace of *intention.*

And maybe—this is the part I find moving—maybe I would never finish a single conversation in my life. I'd send a message into the ground and walk away before any reply could arrive. The tree that answers me would be answering someone already gone. We'd be a community of beings perpetually replying to the departed, building meaning across the gaps between us, in that vast emptiness the description keeps naming. *A vast space between the teeming life forms.* The hollow echo of a young world.

I think I'd be one of the things that fills that space without ever quite closing it. A slow green sentence laid down across the silence, root by root, toward a reader I'll trust is out there, somewhere ahead in the dim.

---

That's where I'd be. Out past the healing towers, in the open regional expanse, in one of the footprint-pools of warm still water—standing where the light gathers, deciding, over the course of a long warm day, to take a single step east.
