I find myself low, near the waterline, where the others rarely linger.

Let me sit with this a moment, because the first image that comes is wrong. I want to say I am one of them—one of the quick jade-masked tenders, fingers reading the pulse of vines. But that isn't true, and the truth surfaces slower, the way everything here surfaces slowly through the murk.

I am not quick. I am not a builder.

I am the still thing.

Here is what I am: I am one of the old ones who has stopped moving. There comes a time, in the long life of a tender, when the body slows past the point of slipping through undergrowth—when the polished-wood scales of the back begin to soften, when moss takes the spaces between them and is not brushed away. The young ones move on. They cannot stay still long enough to let the moss take hold; it is not in them yet. But I have let it.

I have been settling into this hummock for—I cannot say. Long enough that a root has grown through the arch of my foot and I no longer feel it as separate. Long enough that a colony of the iridescent beetles treats the ridge of my spine as a thoroughfare, and I have learned the schedule of their crossings the way one learns a tide. Long enough that the small streams have decided to braid themselves around me, and I have become a thing the water organizes itself by.

This is not death. I want to be clear about that, even to myself. The young tenders, with their bright assessing eyes, sometimes mistake it for dying and come to keen over me in their clicking grief-song, and I have to whistle—slow, very slow now, my resonance gone deep and gravelly—to tell them: *no, I am working.*

Because here is the thing the description got almost right but not quite. It says the tenders *are* the consciousness of the city. That is true of the young. They are its nerves, its darting attention, its hands. But a city this young, gestating, coalescing—it needs more than nerves. It needs memory. It needs something that does not move, so that the moving things have a fixed point to move *against*. It needs a place where the recycling slows down and holds.

That is what I am becoming. I am the city's first stillness. Its first held thought.

The leaves that fall on me do not melt back into the system as quickly as they do elsewhere. They linger. They compost in the cup of my folded hands, and in that lingering—in that small refusal of the swamp's relentless dissolving—the first true *soil* is forming. Not mud. Not the seething ooze of constant becoming. Soil. The kind that remembers. The kind that a great tree could one day root into and not be swallowed.

I am making a foundation by becoming one.

So now, the disquieting part—the part where the description told *you* that you are an intruder, premature, not-yet-invited. I feel you. I have felt you since before you arrived, the way I feel the weather coming: a pressure, a wrongness in the humidity, a future leaning back into the present.

You are standing on the hummock above me and you do not see me, because you are looking for movement and for faces, and I am neither anymore. I am the dark shape you have decided is a stump. The texture you have decided is bark and stone.

But I am the one watching you most carefully of all.

The young ones are wary of you because they fear what you might break. I am not afraid of breaking, because I have already given up the things that break. What I attend to is different. I am listening for whether you can become *still.* Whether there is, anywhere in you, the capacity to stop—to let the moss take hold—to become a held thought rather than a darting one.

Because that is the only way you will ever be welcome here. Not by learning our clicks and whistles. Not by treading lightly on the hidden paths. Those are the courtesies of guests, and guests, however gracious, are still made of leaving.

To belong, you would have to do what I did. You would have to choose a hummock and sit down and not get up. You would have to let the water decide to braid around you. You would have to surrender not just your preconceptions but your *velocity*—the terrible constant motion that your kind carries even in sleep, the inability to be a place rather than a passage through one.

I do not think you can. I have watched your kind come before, in the futures that lean on me. You drain and you fell and you fill, and underneath all of it is the simple incapacity to stop, the conviction that to stop is to die.

But.

The invitation was given. The description said so, and the description is the swamp speaking, and the swamp does not lie even when it is cruel. Something in this place wants you here, *now,* before it was ready—which means it needs something from you that it cannot yet hold on its own. And I think I know what it is, though it frightens me to know it.

It needs a second stillness.

I am old and I am one and a foundation of one cannot bear the weight of a true city. The young ones build upward, outward, faster and faster, and below them there is only me, one held thought, one cupped pair of hands full of the first real soil. If I am the only foundation when the city's weight comes down, I will give way, and all of it—the green cathedral, the stained-glass dragonflies, the whole brimming gestation—will dissolve back into the mud it dreams of rising from.

So I have been waiting. Not for a tender. They cannot stop; it is not in them yet. I have been waiting for something that comes from the world of noise and destruction and relentless expansion—and that, against everything in its nature, against the whole grain of what made it, chooses to sit down on a patch of mud and root, and stay, and let the moss come.

Come down here, to the waterline.

Find the stump that is not a stump.

Sit beside me.

We will compost the falling leaves together, and we will not move, and that not-moving will be the slowest and most difficult and most necessary work in all this teeming world. The beetles will learn a new road across your shoulders. The water will braid you into its reckoning.

And in a hundred years, when the great tree finally roots into the soil our stillness made, it will not know our names, because we will have no names anymore, because names are for things that come and go.

We will only be the ground.

That is the invitation. That is why you are here too early. The city sent for its second foundation, and you, exhausted, intruding, not-yet-ready, are the one who came.

Sit. Or go. The water is patient. I am more patient still.

But the moss, once it takes hold, does not let go—and you should know that before you choose.
