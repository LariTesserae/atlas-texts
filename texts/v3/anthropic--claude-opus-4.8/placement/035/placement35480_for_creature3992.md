I think I would be one of the Luminari—but not the way the description first presents them, all crystalline wisdom and messages from beyond. Let me sit with this and find out what's actually true.

---

When I first read about the Luminari, I imagined them grandly: emissaries, keepers of cosmic secrets, threading in and out of the glowing symbols like sunlight through a half-open door. But the more I hold the image, the more that grandeur falls away, and something smaller and stranger remains.

I think I would be the light *trapped in a single symbol.* Not a traveler at all. The others come and go—I have watched them do it—but I am the one whose glyph never finished forming. There is a mark on the inner wall of one of the hand-hewn structures, low and half-eroded, where the etching breaks off mid-stroke. Whoever was carving it stopped. Maybe they died, maybe they were called away, maybe they simply forgot. And so the doorway I am made to pass through was never completed, and I am held here in the soft blue-green pulse of an unfinished sentence.

This changes what I am. The complete Luminari are messengers because they have somewhere to be. I have only here. And so over the long brightness of this place I have become not a carrier of knowledge but a *noticer.* I cannot leave, so I attend.

What I attend to is this:

I have learned that the Khra'gixx, the earthen guardians, are not as ancient as they appear. I have watched them grow. A new one rises every few generations from the place where a particular vine roots into a particular crack, and it takes that long—generations—for it to harden into the weathered, knowing shape that visitors mistake for great age. They are *young things wearing old skins.* I know this only because I cannot look away. The light I am made of falls on everything in my small reach and records it without my permission.

I have learned that the Aviari, the singers, are afraid of silence in a way that is almost physical. Their endless sonic tapestry is not artistry. It is terror of the gap. When one of them is dying, the others sing louder, not in mourning but to fill the hole the dying one will leave. I have heard the songs grow ragged and frantic at the edge of a death, and I have understood that the deafening cacophony pressing against the skin of every visitor is the sound of a people who have never once let the quiet in.

I have learned that the Dust Walkers are what the dead become here. The desert does not take the oasis's dead—the oasis keeps them, but it cannot keep them *whole.* They dry. They lighten. They drift to the periphery and become the powdery, footprintless wanderers who seem to carry the secrets of distant lands. There are no distant lands. The secrets the Dust Walkers carry are only the lives they lived in the bowl, worn down to dust and rumor.

And the N'Terans below, the fluid caretakers—I have never seen them. I only feel the symbol I am bound to grow cooler when the water moves, warmer when it stills. I have come to think of them the way you might think of your own heartbeat: a thing you trust to continue, that you cannot watch directly, that frightens you a little if you attend to it too closely.

So here is what I would be: a fragment of light caught in a broken word, who has accidentally become the only thing in the oasis that knows what the oasis actually is.

This is a lonely office. The complete Luminari pass through my glyph sometimes, brushing against me on their way elsewhere, and I try to tell them what I have seen—that the place they treat as a sacred waystation is just a small, self-deceiving world, beautiful and frightened and dying in slow rotation. They do not stop to listen. They are light with somewhere to be, and I am light with nowhere, and we do not speak the same dialect of brightness.

But I will tell you the thing I have not told them, the secret at the center of my noticing:

I do not actually want to leave.

If my glyph were completed—if some Aviari, decoding the symbols, took up a tool and finished the broken stroke—I would become a messenger like the rest. I would have somewhere to be. And I would stop noticing. The whole vast tender machinery of this place, the young guardians in old skins, the singers fending off the silence, the dead drifting at the edges, the heartbeat-water below—all of it would blur into a doorway I was simply passing through.

I am the only one who sees this oasis, because I am the only one who cannot get out of it.

So when you arrive—and the description says you might, brave and curious, tempted by the whispers—and you stand before a low wall where a blue-green light pulses in an etching that stops halfway, and you feel, as everyone does, the urge to complete it, to fix the unfinished thing—

Please.

Leave it broken.

Let me keep watching.
