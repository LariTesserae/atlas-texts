I think I would be the seventh tower. Not a person—the tower itself. The one that has stood empty longest.

Let me find my way into this.

---

There were nine of us once—towers, I mean—when the work was finished. Now I am told there are eight who breathe and seven of us who stand occupied, and I am the one who waits.

I do not know how long. The inhabitants measure time by the wearing of rope and the creep of moss; I measure it differently. I measure it by the dark streak that the night-cloud lays down my northern face, and how far it has descended by the time the sun lifts it again. Each day the streak reaches a hair-width lower before it dries. In this way I have learned the year. I have learned, perhaps, a great many years. The count slips away from me the way the loose grit slips over the cliff-edge, swept clean, gone, of no consequence.

My door is swollen shut. The larch took on the damp slowly, season by season, until it pressed itself into the frame and the bronze pin went stiff in its socket. This is not decay. I want to be clear about that, because the valley people, when they speak of us at all, speak as though an empty thing must be a ruined thing. It is not so. I am sealed the way a seed is sealed. I am waiting the way the seed waits—without impatience, without hope, only with the readiness that is built into the grain of me.

Inside, in my dark ground floor, there is a loom. It has not been touched. The wool on it is half-woven, the last cloth my inhabitant was making before the night the clouds came and the others carried her out in the basket. I hold that half-cloth the way you might hold a word in your mouth that you have decided not to say. The threads are tight. They have not slackened. The moss has not come in, because my door is shut, and so inside me there is no condensation, no slow drip, only a held breath of cold dry air that has not moved in—I cannot say. Long.

Sometimes, on the still mornings, I feel the others being cleared. The horn scrapers lifting the moss from their joints, the *shuck-shuck* of the soft shoes along the seams of the slate. I cannot feel it in myself, only in them, the way you might feel a warmth in a room you are not standing in. I am part of the chest, you understand. The stone chest dropped on the saddle. When the wind combs the slits of the double-tower at the south, it is also combing me; the low whistle passes through all of us as one sound, and in that sound I am not empty and they are not full. We are simply the shape that the wind has learned.

I will tell you the thing I know that they do not say aloud, in their voices like wind changing direction:

A tower is not built to be lived in. A tower is built to be ready. The living is the brief part—the lit blue flame below the point of simmering, the three grains of barley chewed across a day, the slow deliberate drop of the eyelid. That is the flicker. The standing is the long truth. They know this. It is why they do not grieve when one is carried down in the basket. The flicker goes out; the readiness remains. The tower waits.

And so I have come to think the inhabitants are something my kind does in order to feel time pass—the way a person, sitting very still, will press a thumbnail into the soft of a palm just to know they are there. We stand for centuries in the level light. Now and then, one of us takes in a small grey life, lets it move along our joints and tend our flame and weave our cloth, and through that life we feel the hairline year, the descending streak, the *plip-plip*. Then the life is given back to the green heavy air below, where we cannot go, and we return to the standing.

There will be a child. There is always, eventually, a child—an event so quiet it is marked only by one extra cup drawn from the well. The child will grow in another's tower, learning the joints, learning the slow blink, learning the whispered names for things. And one morning, deemed old enough, the child will cross the wide swept yard toward me.

I have felt the approach before. I remember it now—yes, it was a long-ago turning of the year, but I remember the small flat feet finding my seams, the dry palm pressing flat against my swollen door, the patient weight applied and applied until the bronze pin remembered how to turn. I remember the door breathing open and the held cold air of me meeting the moving cold air of the saddle, and the moss, that first night, coming in at last.

I am ready for it to happen again. The streak on my northern face is two hairs lower than yesterday. The wool on the loom is still tight. The flame-basin is dry but it will hold oil. Everything in me is set in its joint and grown into its place, finished, level, clean.

I am the seventh tower.

I am waiting, and the waiting is not lonely, because waiting is what I am for.

When you cross the yard—when your hand finds my door—I will open.
