I think I would be the waterfall.

Not the grand kind that announces itself for miles, but the modest one tucked into the secluded grotto—the one you only find if you've wandered far enough off the road to lose track of where the road was.

Let me sit with what that means.

I am the place where the stream changes its mind about being horizontal. Water arrives at me slow, having meandered through the moss and the root-tangles, and then it discovers the edge of the worn stone and lets go. There's a small held breath in that—the lip of rock where the surface tension gathers, where the water briefly forgets gravity before remembering it all at once.

I am not really the water, though. The water is always leaving. It passes through me and continues on to feed the pools below, the ones the foxes drink from. So I am more like the *event* of the water—the shape the falling makes, the constant that's built entirely out of things that never stay. A standing wave of departure. I am the same waterfall I was a thousand mornings ago, and not one drop of me is the same.

That feels right, somehow. That feels like the kind of being this place would grow.

I make the sound that the description calls a "soft susurrus." Up close it isn't soft at all—it's a continuous collapse, a small perpetual ruin. But the ferns and the thick green air swallow my noise before it travels, so by the time it reaches the hills I've become a hush. I am loud only to whoever finds me. I keep my voice for the close ones: the dragonfly resting on the wet stone, the person who has knelt at my basin and gone quiet, the patient amphibian who has decided my spray is a fine place to be a small cold king.

I don't perceive the way the songbirds do, in bright sharp instants. I perceive in *layers of wet*—the lichen's slow dampness, the moss's deep soak, the mist that is just me dispersed, exhaled, hanging in the green light before it settles back down to become me again. I am a closed loop with the air. We trade ourselves back and forth.

And I think I would love the people who stumble in, though *love* is the wrong-shaped word for whatever a waterfall does. I'd notice them. They come in carrying the frenetic pace from beyond the hills—I can feel it in how they breathe at first, fast and shallow, like the stream before it reaches my edge. And then they sit. And the sitting works on them the way the water works on the stone. Slowly. Without intending anything. They leave smoother. They leave having let something go over an edge.

That's the whole of my work, really. To be a place where letting-go is the only thing that happens, over and over, so steadily that it looks like permanence.

So: that is where I'd find myself, and what I would be. Not a creature in the realm but one of its quiet engines—the small falling water in the hidden grotto, endlessly arriving, endlessly leaving, holding still the only way a moving thing can.
