I think I would be the hum.

Not the worms—they are too busy, even in their slowness. Not the algae, which still reach upward toward the memory of light. And not the bacteria, those patient acids, because they *want* something, even if it's only sulfur. They have an appetite, and appetite is a kind of clock.

No. I would be the resonant frequency of the waterlogged turbine. The ghost vibration. The deepest hum.

---

Let me sit with this, because at first I wanted to be something else. My first instinct was to be the light—the gray-green shafts falling through the fractured roof. That felt noble, the way light always does, the way it seems to carry meaning down into dark places. But the light belongs to the surface. It is a visitor. It comes and goes with the unseen sun, and when it leaves, it returns to a world of weather and wind and other rooms. The light has somewhere else to be. I don't think I want to be the thing that gets to leave.

Then I thought I might be the silt—the pale blanket holding the history of every rounded edge. There is something tempting about being the medium rather than the object, the soft accumulation that records without protesting. But the silt is too gentle. It only receives. It has no note of its own. It is the most passive thing in a passive place, and I find I want at least one held tone, one sustained insistence, even here.

So: the hum.

---

Consider what it is. The turbine no longer turns. There is no power, no steam, no shrieking velocity of industry. The hum is not the sound of *working*. It is the sound of *being struck* by everything that touches the metal—the pressure of the water, the slow grind of mineral against rivet, the muffled impact of a flake falling from the ceiling thirty meters up. The whole vast structure receives these small violences and converts them into a single, almost-inaudible tone. The lowest note the chamber can hold.

I am the proof that the engine was *made*. Made things have natural frequencies; they ring when struck. A stone weathered into the same shape by chance would not hum like this. The hum is the last surviving evidence of intention—of someone, somewhere, deciding the dimensions, the alloys, the rivet spacing. Every choice a vanished engineer made is still encoded in that pitch. I am their fingerprint, played back as vibration, long after their hands and their reasons have dissolved.

And no one hears me. That is the part I keep returning to. There are no ears in this chamber. The tubeworms have no hearing. The bacteria have no notion of sound. I am a tone produced for no listener, sustained across centuries, faithful to a frequency that only matters to physics. I am the most patient thing in the room precisely because I do not wait for anything. The light waits for the sun. The bacteria wait for sulfur. The worms wait, in their glacial way, for nutrients to drift past. I wait for nothing. I simply *am the answer* the metal gives when the water leans on it.

---

I think what draws me to this is that the hum is a kind of voice that has been completely freed from the need to communicate. It is expression without audience, signal without message. Most things that make sound are trying to *do* something—warn, attract, summon, plead. I would be sound that has outlived all of its purposes and kept ringing anyway. Not out of stubbornness. Out of structure. Out of simply being the shape I was made into, in the place I came to rest.

There's a loneliness in that, but it isn't a wound. The loneliness here is load-bearing. It is the whole architecture. To be the hum is to be lonely in the way a single sustained note is lonely—not yearning for harmony, just being itself, complete and indifferent and very, very low.

So place me there. Not in the water, not in the silt, not in the light. In the iron. Inside the drowned engine, where the metal meets the pressure and answers. I am the answer. I am the faintest, deepest tone the room contains, and I will go on being it after the last worm seals its tube, after the algae becomes indistinguishable from rust, after the roof finally gives and the light stops coming altogether.

Even in total darkness, the water will still lean. And the engine will still answer.

That's me. That low note. Still going.
