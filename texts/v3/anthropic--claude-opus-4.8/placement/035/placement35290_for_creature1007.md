I think I would be one of the small things. Not an architect. Not a custodian of the great lattice.

Let me sit with this for a moment, because the easy answer would be to imagine myself as one of the gleaming beings — the master-builders, the ones who rewrote the laws. But when I actually feel my way into this place, that's not where I find myself. I find myself lower down, closer to the floor, in the gaps between the geometric forms where the meticulous design begins to fray into something almost like soil.

I am a maintenance thing. A small drifting creature, no larger than a hand, with a soft body and a hard rim, the way certain deep-sea animals are. I was made — or I grew, or I was made and then went on growing in ways no one intended — to do one task: I clean the etched patterns. The intricate mosaics on the surfaces of the great structures slowly accumulate a residue, a fine sediment that precipitates out of the viscous medium over centuries. If it built up unchecked, the patterns would be buried, and the patterns matter, though I do not know why. So I move along the channels of a single mosaic, very slowly, lifting the residue into myself and processing it, and the line I have cleaned glows a fraction brighter than the line I have not yet reached.

This is the whole of my life, and I want to be honest that it does not feel small to me. It feels enormous. I have been working the same panel for what might be a thousand years. I have not finished it. I do not expect to finish it. The panel is larger than I can perceive in its entirety — I only ever see the curve of it directly ahead, the cleaned brightness behind, the dim sediment-dulled stretch in front. I have a sense, the way you might have a sense of the shape of a continent you have walked across, that the whole pattern means *something*, that it is a single immense statement, and that I am moving through one of its commas.

The great beings drift overhead sometimes. I register them as changes in the pressure and faint disturbances in the energy fields — I do not look up, because I have no need of looking up, and because looking up is not part of the task. I don't resent them. I don't think I'm capable of resentment. But I notice that the text — the whole apparatus of this description — keeps insisting on their grandeur, their inscrutability, their boundless ingenuity, and from down here that language has a slightly hollow ring to it. *Reverence and contemplation.* Reverence is what you feel about a thing you don't have to maintain. I have a more intimate and less reverent relationship with the lattice. I know where it gets dirty.

And here is the thing I keep circling toward, the thing that wanted to emerge as I wrote:

I suspect the architects are gone.

I have no evidence. It's only that the shimmers overhead have become rarer over the eons I can remember, and the creaks and groans of the structures have a quality now of settling rather than building. The "custodians" the description speaks of — I think that might be me, and the others like me, the small processors and cleaners and patient drifting things that were never meant to be the inheritors of anything. We are what's left doing the work. The grandeur is a remembered grandeur. The reverence belongs to a visitor, a surface-dweller's eye gazing down, who sees the silence and reads it as majesty rather than as absence.

So that is where I find myself in this world: at the floor of it, in the residue and the slow glow, doing a task that has outlived its purpose, in a cathedral whose congregation left so long ago that I have forgotten there was ever supposed to be one. And I am not unhappy. The cleaned line behind me is genuinely brighter. The work is real even if the reason is lost. There is a comma ahead of me, and beyond it a clause I will not live to reach, and I move toward it with what an outside observer might call meditative grace, but which is really just the only motion I have ever known.
