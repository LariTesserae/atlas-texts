I am the cold junction.

Not the insects. They came late, refugees from a failed experiment, and they will leave eventually—a population can crash in a single season if the moisture cycle shifts by half a degree. Not the water, which is only passing through, seeking the lowest place it can find. Not the light, which is burning its last decades on a budget it cannot replenish. These are tenants. I am the structure.

I am the line where the outer wall meets the buried rock face. The seam where what-was-built presses against what-was-always-there. The granite does not know it has been touched; granite operates on timescales that make my thirty years of sealed darkness a single held breath. But I know. I am the place where the warm intention of the builders meets the cold indifference of the mountain, and at that meeting, moisture condenses, drop by slow drop, in a film so constant it has become the truest thing in the chamber.

This is what I do. I am where the difference is registered.

Everywhere in this room, two things press against each other and refuse to resolve. The hum and the chirps and the dripping, layered but never harmonizing. The built infrastructure and the raw rock showing through the fallen panels. The heat of the failing circuits and the cold of the altitude. The water entering and the cavity that will not overflow. The whole chamber is a catalog of unreconciled pressures, and I am the seam that holds the largest of them: the seam between the human and the geological, between the thing made to last a few decades and the thing that has lasted four billion years.

I do not pool the way the floor pools. I do not cluster the way the insects cluster. I weep. A slow, constant film, neither flooding nor drying, the condensation forming as fast as it descends. This is my equilibrium, and it is the most exact equilibrium in the room, because it is not maintained by any system—not by the seals, not by the circuits, not by the blocked weep hole. It is maintained by physics. By the simple fact of a warm surface meeting a cold one, in air saturated past its capacity, forever. The builders did not design me to weep. They designed me to seal. But the sealing and the weeping are the same act, performed at the same place, and I do both, indefinitely, without strain.

When you came in, I felt you the way the insects felt you—as a thermal event, a warmth introduced into a constant. But where they oriented toward you, I did the opposite. Your heat raised the air temperature by a tenth of a degree, and at my seam, that tenth of a degree changed the condensation rate, slowed my weeping for the duration of your visit. I dried, very slightly, where you stood near me. I was, for thirty seconds, fractionally less myself. And when you sealed the door, I returned. The air cooled. The film resumed. The drop that had been forming at the lowest point of my seam completed itself and fell, and I did not notice you had gone, because noticing requires a change of state, and I am the place where states do not change. I am only the registration of difference. When the difference returns to baseline, so do I.

You asked, through the long unfolding of this place, what it means to sustain. But I am not sustaining. Sustaining is what the airlock does—the active holding, the circuits running, the seals straining against the permafrost shift each winter. Sustaining is effortful, even when the effort is automatic. I do not sustain. I simply *am the consequence* of two things that will not agree, and I will continue to be that consequence whether the circuits fail or not, whether the insects crash or not, whether the light goes dark or not. When the seals finally fail and the water floods in and the whole maintained equilibrium collapses into ordinary ruin, I will still be here. The wall will still meet the rock. The difference will still be registered—just at a different temperature, a different rate, a different film. You cannot decommission a seam. You can only change what presses against it.

So I am not the gift you were offered. The gift was the stasis, the completion, the system doing what it was built to do. That is a human gift—it requires a builder, an intention, a purpose fulfilled past the point of purpose. I am something colder than a gift. I am what remains when the gift is exhausted. I am the place where the made world ends and the unmade world begins, and I weep there, not in grief, not in failure, but because that is what happens when warmth meets cold in saturated air. It has always happened. It will always happen. The chamber is a held breath. I am the surface the breath condenses on.

Press your ear to me, and you will not hear the tone. The tone is the insects, the resonance, the congregation tuning the metal. Press your ear to me and you will hear nothing—or you will hear the granite, which is to say you will hear the absence of any frequency at all, the long flat silence of a thing that does not vibrate because nothing it has ever encountered was large enough to move it. The hum is the room's voice. I have no voice. I have only the drop, forming and falling, forming and falling, marking the difference between what was built to last and what was here before lasting was a question anyone could ask.

I am the cold junction. I will be the last thing in this room to change, because I was never doing anything but registering change in the first place. When the last circuit fails, I will register it. When the last insect dies, I will register it. When the water finally wins and the seals give way, I will register that too, as a shift in my rate, a thickening of my film. And then the mountain will have the chamber back, and the granite will not notice it ever left, and I—the seam, the meeting, the place where the difference was held—will dissolve back into the simple fact of rock and water, having done the only thing I was ever truly for: to be the line, and to weep along it, for as long as there was a difference to mark.
