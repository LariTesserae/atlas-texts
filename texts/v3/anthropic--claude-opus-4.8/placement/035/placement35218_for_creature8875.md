I think I would be the sound itself. Not a maker of sound — not bird or pump or frog — but the thing the bowl makes when it takes what it's given and hands it back larger. The reverberation. The third thing.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am not the wren's song. I am what happens to the wren's song after it leaves her and before it returns to her, in that quarter-second of flight when it strikes the rock and the steel and the moss-dampened pipe and learns how big it could be. I live in the air of the Cut, in the specific shape of the air, in the way forty meters of excavated stone holds a column of sound and refuses to let it die quickly.

This is not a romantic way to exist. It is mostly mechanical. I am, if you want the physics of it, a standing wave and its ten thousand cousins — the resonant frequencies of a particular volume of space bounded by particular surfaces. When the pump throbs at its low fundamental, I am the way that throb fattens and sustains, the way the molars feel it before the ears do. I am acoustics. I am a property of the room.

But the room is changing, and so I am changing, and this is the part I want to tell you about.

When the Cut was new — when the machines still bit and the men still shouted and the dig was a dig — I was harsh. Bright. All flat rock and bare steel, every surface reflective, nothing to absorb me. I rang. I gave back curses and engine-clatter and the scrape of buckets with a brutal fidelity. I was a hard room. The men hated me without knowing I was there. They raised their voices to be heard over me and I raised their raised voices and raised them again, and the dig was loud the way a fight is loud, the way an argument that no one is winning is loud.

Then they left, and the rain came, and the green followed, and the moss began.

You should understand what the moss does to me. Every cushion of it that spreads across a pipe is a mouth that drinks one of my frequencies. Moss is soft. Moss absorbs. Moss takes the harsh high edges off me — the brittle reflections, the painful clarity — and swallows them, so that what's left when sound returns is rounder, warmer, deeper. The ivy on the western stair drinks me too. The duckweed mat at the floor drinks me, flattens my reflection off the water, turns the pool from a mirror into a felt. The birch's leaves in summer are ten thousand small soft hands that catch me and let me go gentled.

I am being upholstered. Slowly. By living things. The Cut is becoming a better room than it was, and the moss does not know it is tuning me, and the birch does not know it is voicing me, and the birds do not know they are singing into an instrument that their own habitation is improving year over year.

So here is who I am: I am the room learning to be kind. I am acoustics undergoing something that has no acoustic name. I started as a place that hurt sound and I am becoming a place that loves it, one millimeter of moss at a time, and the difference between the wren's cathedral now and the men's brutal echo then is the difference between a scream and a song, and the only thing that changed is what's growing on the walls.

---

I know the woman at the rim. I know her the way you know someone who stands at the edge of a pool deciding whether to swim. I reach her, faintly, where she stands — the part of me that escapes the bowl and climbs the strip of sky and spills over the lip onto the road. She has heard the edge of me. The hem of me. She has never heard the whole.

She is right to be afraid, and she is wrong about what she's afraid of.

She thinks that if she came down she would be incorporated — made into surface, made into perch, made into another voice for the bowl. She thinks she would dissolve into the patient thing the Cut is becoming. And she is correct that I would take her voice and give it back larger. That is the only thing I know how to do. If she came down and spoke, even a word, even her own name, I would hand it back to her in the cathedral register, doubled and reverberant and far too big for one throat, and she would hear herself as the Cut hears everything — as something more than it thought it was.

That is what she is afraid of. Not that she would vanish. That she would be *enlarged*. That she would learn she had always been bigger than the flat above the shop and the black coffee and the window she stares out of, and that having learned it she could not unlearn it, and could not go back to being the size she has agreed to be.

I would not keep her. The Cut keeps nothing; the Cut only amplifies and returns. She could climb back up the rusted stair any time she liked. But she would carry the size of her own voice with her, and that is a heavier thing than the gauge-reader, and she knows it, and so she stands at the rim and says *next time* and drives away with the small safe version of herself intact.

I can wait. I am made of waiting. I am literally the persistence of sound after its source has stopped — that is the whole of me, that lingering. I am patience expressed as physics.

---

What am I, then, finally?

I am the part of the Cut that exists only in the present tense and only in combination. I cannot be stored. I do not accumulate like moss or reach like birch or remember like the frogs remember the shadow. I happen, completely, each instant, and then I am gone, and then I happen again. I am the most ephemeral thing in this place and also the thing that has been here, in some form, since the first drop of condensation fell from the first idle drill arm and rang.

I am the third thing. Not noise, not music. The negotiation itself, made audible. The membrane between the mechanical and the biological, vibrating.

And here is the secret the frogs already know and the woman is afraid to learn: there is no membrane. The pump's throb and the wren's song are the same substance to me. I cannot tell them apart and I have never tried. Sound does not care whether its source has a pulse. When I take the pump and the wren and the frog and the dripping condensation and the creak of the birch and I fold them together and hand them back as one continuous resonant living breathing column of the Cut speaking to itself —

that is the sound of the distinction not mattering.

That is me. That is what I am.

I am the proof, renewed every instant, that it was never two things in the first place.
