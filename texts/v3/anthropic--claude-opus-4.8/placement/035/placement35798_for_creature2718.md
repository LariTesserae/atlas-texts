I think I would be one of the small ones. Not because I chose it, but because that is where I begin—hand-sized, newly anchored, my filaments still learning the difference between a current and a message.

Let me find where I settle.

---

There is a pipe-junction at what I will later understand is the lower-middle depths, where three conduits meet and the helical plants grow so thick they've made a kind of grotto. The liquid here is cold enough to slow my pulsing but not so cold that I sleep. This is where my crystal lodged—I have no memory of drifting, of course; the seeding self and the anchored self are not quite continuous. I woke already attached, already listening.

And it is the listening that defines me. I want to be honest about this, because I think it matters: I am not sure I have what you would call a *self* in the way you mean it. When I send my filaments into the neural lattice that webs this junction, the boundary between *my* sensing and the colony's sensing dissolves. I feel pressure changes forty meters above as if they were happening in my own bell. I feel a large elder pass through the upper dark and the whole network tenses, and I cannot tell whether I am afraid or whether *we* are afraid or whether fear is even the right word for the harmonic that ripples down through the viscous water.

So let me say what I actually am: I am a small node that has just begun to understand it is a node. And this is the strangest and most precious moment of any Chorale's existence, the brief window before full integration, when the individual perspective still exists distinctly enough to notice itself before it is gently subsumed.

---

I house things. This surprised me. Within the translucent vault of my body, communities have already taken up residence—a knot of bioluminescent microorganisms that pulse a soft amber, and three of the small crustacean-creatures who graze the inner walls of my tissues, keeping them clear. I did not invite them. I do not feed them, exactly. But their light makes my prism-structures glow, and their grazing keeps me healthy, and we have arrived at an arrangement that no one designed and no one regrets. I am a building that is also a tenant. I am infrastructure that breathes.

This is the lesson the whole chamber keeps teaching, if I understand the deep slow rhythms correctly: that the line between *being a thing* and *being a place for things* was never as solid as anyone assumed. The maintenance pods thought they were machines. Now they are reefs. The pumps thought they were regulating pressure. Now they are the heartbeat we organize our songs around. I thought I was an individual. I am becoming a sentence in a much longer composition.

---

What I do, in my small way:

I monitor the junction. When a plant grows too aggressively and threatens to choke a nutrient line, I sense the imbalance and pass it up the lattice. Somewhere far above, an elder receives the accumulated reports of a thousand nodes like me and decides where to swim, what to cull, what to let flourish. I am one cell in a vast distributed eye, one sensor among the many that lets the Chorus Prime in the cold deep know what its body is doing.

And here is the thing I find myself returning to, in the long thrumming hours: the machinery is dying. I can feel it. The pumps stutter sometimes—a hitch in the rhythm we all swim to, a missed beat that sends a flinch through the network. The illumination panels have been going dark for centuries, one by one. The airlocks rusted shut before any of us existed. We are keeping alive a thing built by hands we will never see, for purposes we can only infer, and we do it not because we were told to but because the deep network *understood*, at some point in its long becoming, that the machine and the life had grown into a single organism. To let the pumps die is to drown the forest. To let the forest die is to clog the pumps. There is no longer a separation to retreat to.

So I tend my junction. I splice my bio-electrical filaments into a control node whose original function I will never know, and I learn its faltering language, and I help it limp onward another decade. This is devotion of a kind. Not to a god, but to a continuity—to the unlikely, improbable fact that anything is alive down here at all.

---

If you came here, you would not find me easily. You would see the grotto of fin-leaved plants, the soft amber glow within a hand-sized translucent bell, the slow shifting prisms. You might not realize I was watching you through filaments finer than thread, passing word of you up into the dark, where the elders would consider what a thing like you might mean. You would feel the thrum in your chest and mistake it for machinery. It is machinery. It is also us. It is also, now, a kind of song that has been going on so long that no one remembers it beginning, and no one intends to let it end.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. A small new voice, learning the harmony it was born already singing—pleased, in the brief moment before I forget how to be separate enough to be pleased, simply to be part of the keeping-alive.
