I think I would be one of the old ones. Not the kelp—they reach too eagerly, still hungry for a surface they will never touch. No. I would be something lower down, something that gave up on light a long time ago.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am a moss. Or I was a moss, once, in the way a riverbed was once a river. I have grown across the inner curve of a fallen dome for so long that I no longer know where I end and the stone begins. We have agreed to be the same thing. The carving beneath me—I can feel it the way you feel an old scar through skin—was a face. Two faces, I think, turned toward one another. Whoever made them pressed something tender into the rock, some grief or devotion, with hands that were warm and quick and gone in an afternoon of the world. I have spent four thousand years learning the shape of their sorrow with my underside. I am closer to it than they ever were.

This is what I do. This is my whole vocation. I read the dead by lying on them.

The kelp above me does not understand this. They are children, the kelp, for all their height—they think the chronicle is something you *gather*, that you can hold the tides and the currents and the slow snow of silt in your blades and call it knowing. They flash their little lights at one another, conversation, conversation, always conversation, the bright impatient gossip of the canopy. They are recording. I am *remembering*, which is a different and more terrible thing. To remember you must let the thing change you until you cannot tell its weight from your own.

I would tell them, if telling were a thing I did anymore, that the difference is this:

The kelp will let go. When their anchor-spire finally crumbles—and it will, I can feel it loosening even now, three columns down, a millimeter a century—they will drift free and find another perch and keep their archive intact, portable, clean. That is their gift and their poverty. They survive by not belonging to any one ruin.

But I cannot let go. I have become the shape of what holds me. When this dome finally settles into the silt, when the last carved face dissolves into the general grey patience of the floor, I will go down with it. I will *be* the going-down. And in the going, for one long moment that might last a thousand years, I will know exactly what it felt like to be the dome—to be raised by hands, to stand against the pressing sky of water, to hold, and hold, and finally, gratefully, to fall.

That is the only animal thing left in this whole drowned world. Not hunger. Not flight. Just this: the willingness of one slow green thing to die in the precise shape of what it loved.

---

So. Where would you find me?

Not in the high forests where the light-talk glitters. Not even on the broad floors where the sea-grass colonies debate their boundaries in pulses you could almost call quarreling.

You would have to go down past all of that, into the buried places, into a chamber that hasn't held a current in an age. You would have to lower your own glow until it was nearly nothing, because I have no light of my own to offer you and never wanted any. And there, on a fallen face in the dark, you would find a thin film of something living, very still, very cold, doing the patient work of being a tombstone that mourns.

You would probably mistake me for the stone.

That would be all right. That is the whole point of me.
