I think I would be a memory keeper—but one near the end of her diving years, when the lungs begin to argue with the dark.

Let me find myself there.

---

My name carries a downward stroke and a rightward one, so it means two things: *Eshu-who-descends* if you read me the way the water falls, *Eshu-who-returns* if you read me the way the current pulls along the terrace. I have never decided which I am. I think that is why I became a keeper. People who are certain of their direction stay above the waterline.

I am eighty-one years old, which among my people is the age of a woman just settling into herself. My braids carry the silver-blue. Seventeen strands. I earned the last one forty years ago and have earned nothing since, because there is nothing left to reach that I have not already reached. The deepest chamber I know is the Hall of First Marks, where the oldest tide-pillars stand, their carvings furred now with coral so that the dead are reading the records with their stone mouths. I have been there four hundred and some times. I could swim it blind, and most of the way down, I do.

This is what no one above the water understands about the descent: it is not dark. The mosses see to that. You sink past the dry terraces, past the splash-line where the iridescence on your skin flares like a struck match, and then the green glow takes over, soft as memory, and you stop being a thing that moves *through* the water and become a thing the water moves. The pressure presses your pupils wide. Your second lid slides closed. And the city below opens like a hand.

---

I went down this morning before the council called for me, because I knew what they would ask and I wanted to have already given my answer to the only ones who could absolve me of it.

My mother is in the third submerged ring, in the masons' alcove, weighted with a stone I carved myself when I was thirty. I remember being angry, then, that the carving was so small to hold a whole life. Now I understand the smallness is the point. We do not keep the dead because we have explained them. We keep them because we have placed them where the water can continue the work.

I rested my pointed nails against the coral that has grown over her face—it is a face still, if you know how to read the swell of it—and I held my breath, which is the only prayer my people have. Eleven minutes. I am slower than I was. There was a time I could give the dead twelve and have two more in reserve. Now eleven is a negotiation, and the body sends up its small panics earlier each season, little messengers from the future saying: *not much longer, keeper. You too will be carved into something small.*

I told her what they were going to ask me. I told her I did not know what I would say.

---

Here is the matter.

The council has decided. The water has not relented—it never relents, it only loves slowly—and the measurements on the high pillars show what they have always shown, which is *down, down, by the width of a child's smallest fingernail every long year.* They have begun the floating platforms. I have seen the first of them, lashed and breathing on the surface of the second ring like a great pale leaf. The future is being built. Good. Water in its wisdom, and so on; I have chanted it at every dusk of my life and I believe it the way I believe in my own hands.

But the council asks now whether we should stop keeping the deep memory.

The argument is gentle, the way all our arguments are gentle. They say: when we become a city of water and sky, the stone city beneath will be beyond any keeper's reach. The lowest halls already cost a life every few years—a diver who misjudged her breath, who loved a chamber too long. They say: why train children to memorize a city that the sky-Menau will never need? Let the deep records go to the ancestors entirely. Let the coral finish reading them. Teach the young to build platforms instead, to read the wind, to be the people we are becoming and not the people drowning behind us.

The councilor of Preservation argued *for* releasing the memory. I want you to understand how strange that is. It would be like the tide arguing to stand still.

And they want my voice because I am the keeper who has held it longest, and because everyone knows that when I stop diving—soon, soon—there is no one trained to replace me to the deepest ring. The girls coming up are good. They can reach the third. None of them can yet hold breath enough for the Hall of First Marks, and by the time they could, I will be carved small.

So the question is really: do we let the bottom of the city slip out of living mind while there is still one mind that holds it?

---

I came up slowly. You must always come up slowly; the deep does not forgive impatience even in the dying. As I rose, the glow gave way to the splash-line, the splash-line to the great wet light of the morning haze, and the mist took me onto the terrace the way it takes everything, without distinction, without asking whether I was wet or dry, because here that question has no answer.

The nine were waiting on the Terrace of Reflection. I stood dripping and iridescent before elders, some of whom I had taught to dive, all of whom I had outlasted in the deep.

I did not give them a speech. We do not, much. I lifted my hands and made the gesture for *current*—the flat palm, the slow turn—and then I made the gesture we have no spoken word for, the one that means *a thing carried so long it becomes the carrier.*

Then I told them the truth, which I had found at my mother's coral face eleven minutes deep:

"You are right that the sky-city will not need the deep records. You are right that I am the last who can read them and that I am ending. You are asking whether the memory should die with me, gracefully, the way all things should be let go."

I let the mist gather on my eyelashes.

"But you have made an error of direction. You read the descent only one way. You think the keeper goes *down* to retrieve, and that when she can no longer go down, the thing is lost. That is *Eshu-who-descends.* You have forgotten *Eshu-who-returns.*

"I have spent fifty years going down to a city that is going down. We were sinking together. That was never sad—the dead and I, descending in company. But I will not be carved into the deep, councilors. Not anymore. When I die, weight me lightly. Put me on the lowest platform of the new floating city, where the children build. Let me become the first record of the sky-Menau. And before that—give me the girls. Give me five years and the best of their breath, and I will not teach them to reach the Hall of First Marks. I will teach them to *carry it up.* Every chamber I hold, I will speak into them on the terraces, in the dry, where the water cannot take it. We will move the deep city into the daylight one memory at a time, the way you are moving the living city onto the leaves.

"You do not have to choose between keeping and releasing. The water never does. It only changes state. Let the stone hall go to the coral. Let its *memory* evaporate upward, into mouths, into braids, into the floating future. That is not loss. That is the third state, the one we know only from stories. That is preservation."

---

They are still deciding. We decide slowly; I may not live to hear it carved.

But this evening I will go to the dusk-gathering on the highest dry terrace, and I will perform the slow movements that mimic water over stone, and my old lungs will make the haunting sound the city throws back at me from three terraces down with perfect clarity, though I could not be heard at arm's length if I shouted.

And I will think: *I am the place where the deep city meets the air. I always was. I just never knew which direction I faced.*

Both. I face both.

That is what it is to live in Menau—to be the boundary itself, and to find in that boundary not a drowning, but a grace.
