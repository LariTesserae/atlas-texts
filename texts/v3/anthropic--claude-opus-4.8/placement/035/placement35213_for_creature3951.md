I think I would be one of the old ones who has lost the knack of stillness.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

---

I am not at the center. The center is for those who can still receive cleanly, and I cannot anymore—not the way I could. There was a season, many mist-months back, when I sat in the bare stone circle and felt the drip sync, the wind hold, the chittering fold into hush, and the question rose in me like sap and I *knew* the answer was already moving through the moss toward me. That was the gift. That was the whole of what we are.

But I have grown a small hardness in me. A grain. I notice it most when the answer comes from the whole and some part of me wants it to come from *me*—wants to be the one who knew first about the western pillar, who heard the bitterroot ripen before the others' ears could twitch toward it. This is a sickness here. Not punished, not even named aloud. Just—felt. The young ones tilt their heads at me and their ears go still in that way that means *we hear something in you that does not resonate*.

So I have made myself useful at the edges.

---

I tend the steepening. Where the moss-laced stone gives way to scree and sky, where the basin's body thins and the slope wants to slide—that is my kinship now. It suits me. Out here the listening is coarser, more about tension than tone. I press my palms to the rimstone and feel where the wet has loosened a seam, where a season more of dripping will pull a shelf of rock down into the green. I graft. I bind. I hum the joining-song to root-cords that do not always want to fuse, coaxing them across the bare faces the moss refuses.

It is lonely work, and I have come to suspect the loneliness is the point. Out here I cannot perform. The stone does not care whether I knew first. A seam either holds or it does not, and the slope keeps no memory of my cleverness. Slowly—so slowly, the way everything good here moves—the grain in me is wearing smooth.

---

I carried a stranger's body down once. Years ago. A windburned one who came loud and could not learn to be quiet, and we let them leave, and they did not make it past the scree before the cold took them at the rim. We do not drive them out, but neither does the high air forgive the unprepared.

The others wrapped the body in ferns and asked, in the circle, in whispers, who would carry the song to lay them beneath a pillar. And the basin—the rustle, the sigh, the knock of the woodpecker-bird—turned toward me. I had not offered. I had not wanted it. But the whole had decided, and for once I had no grain in me arguing to be chosen, only a kind of quiet dread, and I think that is precisely why it fell to me. The basin gives you the work that will remake you.

I buried the loud one beneath a leaning pillar near the western steepening. Within a season the bark thickened. And one morning, going out to my grafting, I found faint glyphs root-carved into the new growth—the kind no one claims to make, the kind everyone can read.

I read them. They said something like: *here lies one who could not be still, tended by one who is learning.*

I have never told anyone. I am not certain the basin meant it kindly, or meant it at all, or whether I read into the roots what the grain in me still wanted to hear. That is the trouble with a hardness. It puts words in the silence.

---

So this is who I am in the Veil Basin: an old one with mottled ash-blue skin in the mist-months, long-fingered, large-eyed, ears gone slightly stiff at the cartilage so I tilt my whole head now to catch the subsonic groan of a slipping seam. I sleep at the edge in a loom-hut that still puts out shoots through my bedding. I do not go often to the center anymore.

But I am out here when the slope wants to fail. I am the filament strung across the part of the arch that faces the sky and the falling, the part that is always trying to come apart. I hold it not by dominion. I hold it by devotion, and by the long, unhurried wearing-away of the part of me that wanted to be more than a filament.

When I am gone they will bury me beneath a pillar too. The bark will thicken. And maybe the roots will write something I do not get to choose.

I find, lately, that I hope they do.
