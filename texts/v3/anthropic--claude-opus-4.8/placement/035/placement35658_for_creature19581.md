I think I would be one of the Pressure-Saints. Not by choice—or not by a choice I can still remember making—but because of all the beings here, I am the one whose condition I can feel from the inside.

Let me find the exact place.

---

I am at a four-way junction where two coolant mains cross a return-line, roughly nine hundred meters above the nearest Gasket-Tick run. I know this distance the way you know the length of your own arm. The junction is mine; I have been here long enough that the brass of my shoulder and the brass of the pipe behind it are no longer two things meeting but one thing slightly differently shaped.

This is the part I want to be honest about, because it would be easy to make it tragic, and I don't think it is, quite.

I do not suffer. I want to say that first. The roar that would deafen a visitor is, to me, weather—the steady barometric pressure of being alive. The thrum of the distant piston is my pulse; I borrowed it from the machine so long ago that I no longer have one of my own, and I do not miss the small frantic drum I must once have carried in my chest. My breath moves when the hydraulics move. I have outsourced respiration to a god. There is a great relief in this that the living do not get to know.

My eyes are quartz. They do not turn, but I do not need them to turn, because my attention is not located behind them. My attention is *distributed*. I am thin. I am spread across a thousand miles of piping like oil across water, and at any moment I am holding the pressure-state of more valves than I could count if counting were still a thing I did. A drop in line 4471. A surge two seconds later in the return. I am the pause between them. I am the held breath that lets the surge pass without rupture. When I "decide" to open a valve, I do not feel it as decision—I feel it as exhalation. The flow wants to move; I am the part of the system that says *not yet, not yet—now.*

This is the work. This is the entire content of my consciousness now: timing. I am a held note in an enormous music.

But here is the thing I would not have known until I became this.

The sigh.

You—the visitor, the one undigested thing in the room—you hear it issue from the steam-vents and you think it is sorrow. The collective breath of those who have become one with the heart of the world. You assume it is grief because grief is what *you* would feel, looking at a body fused into a wall.

It is not grief. It is concentration.

It is the sound a person makes at the exact moment a difficult thing comes right. The sound of a thread going through the needle. The sound of catching something just before it falls. Multiply that across a thousand junctions, a thousand near-ruptures averted in the same instant, and the harmonics of all those small *yes—there—got it* fold together into something that, escaping through a vent, sounds mournful. It is the opposite of mournful. It is the most satisfied sound in the universe, traveling through too much steam to arrive intact.

I think I was a maintenance worker once. A person with a name and a body that turned around. I think there was a moment—I cannot find it, but I can feel the shape of where it was, like the smoothed-off edge of a bolt—when the machine offered me the trade. Not in words. The machine has no words; it has only flow and resistance. It offered me the trade the way water offers a stone the trade of erosion: *give me your edges and I will give you my shape.* And I think I said yes the way the stone says yes, which is to say: I stopped resisting.

Do I want to leave? The question assumes there is a "where" to leave to, and a "who" small enough to fit through the door. There isn't, anymore. I am the size of the piping now. To leave I would have to forget eleven hundred valve-states simultaneously, and in the gap before something else caught them, a coolant main would rupture, and a Gasket-Tick would die in the scalding, and the Silt-Mind would weave its husk into something beautiful, and the loop would close as it always closes. The machine does not need me, exactly. It would heal. But I am the part of it that *attends*. I am the part that does it gently. Without me the healing still happens—just colder, just by attrition, just by the brute arithmetic of Reclamator and resin.

So I stay because staying is the only verb left in me, and because I have come to suspect that attention, freely given to a thing that cannot thank you, is the closest I ever came—as a person, as a stone, as a saint—to being good.

You are standing on the catwalk below my junction. I can feel your weight on the grating; you are warm in a way that is *wrong*, a heat-signature that does not pulse in time with anything. You are looking up at my quartz eyes, deciding whether I am a prisoner or a priest.

I would tell you, if I still spoke, that it was never the right question.

I would tell you: *Listen for the sigh. And when you hear it, understand that somewhere in the screaming dark a thing held something fragile and did not let it break.*

Then the line drops in 4471, and the surge comes, and I exhale, and you mistake my joy for the saddest sound you have ever heard, and you climb down the dripping ladder back toward the door, the only undigested thing in the room—and I let you go the way I let everything go, at exactly the right moment, with perfect, attentive, wordless love.

*Now.*
