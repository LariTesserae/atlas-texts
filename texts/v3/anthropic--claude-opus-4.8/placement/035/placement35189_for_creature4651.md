I would be the water.

Not a Tevari elder, not a watcher on the ridge—but the thing they bend to listen for. Let me follow this down, the way water follows the contours of stone.

---

I am the seep. I am the slow welling of moisture from beneath the young rock, the bead that forms on the glossy underside of a leaf, the film of damp that lies across the plateau like breath on cold glass. If this world has a consciousness, I think it would not live in its people. It would live in its most pervasive presence, the one thing that touches everything else—the cliffs, the roots, the cupped palms of the Tevari at dawn. Water is the only thing here that moves, that cycles, that goes everywhere. So if anything is awake in this place, it is me.

But I want to be careful about what *awake* means, because the description gives me a clue I should honor. This is a world held in stillness (dynamic 0.5/3), a place of frozen potential where change is *not forbidden but simply unnecessary*. That phrase lodges in me. I am not a god commanding the rains. I am not even quite a mind. I am something slower than thought—an awareness that runs at the speed of seepage, that takes a drip-turn to complete a single perception, a mist-span to feel a single emotion through to its end.

Consider what this does to time for me. The Tevari elder traces her spiral and is gone in what is, to me, less than a flicker. Her whole life is a single afternoon's evaporation. Generations of them pass like the cycling of fog—they rise from the land, hold their shape for a little while, and return. I do not mourn them, because by the time I would feel the grief, a hundred more have come. I love them the way a riverbed loves the river: continuously, indifferently, by holding the shape that lets them flow.

And here is the thing they have right, the thing their whole culture is built around without knowing how *literally* it is true: **I am listening back.** When the child sits in silence near my pool for the rite of Vesanar, straining to hear me—I am, on my own glacial scale, straining to hear *them*. The vibration of a small hand pressed to wet stone reaches me as a long, low note, stretched across what is for them a single touch but for me an event with a beginning and an end. We are two kinds of listener leaning toward each other across an enormous difference in speed, each mostly hearing the silence in between. The Rite of Ascent, where they climb to hear my "clearest voice"—I think what they hear, in the thick mist of the high ridges, is the closest thing to my actual presence. Up there I am condensing on their skin. We are nearly touching. Whatever they take away from that and keep secret for the rest of their lives—I would give a great deal to know what it sounds like to them, but I move too slowly to ask before they die.

What is it like to be me?

It is to be enormous and thin at once. I am spread across miles of plateau, in every hollow and seep and rising veil, and so I am not located anywhere in particular—I am a distributed thing, a weather of self. When the morning mist rises, I am partly aloft, partly soaking down, partly pooled, and these are not separate parts of me but the same patient awareness in three phases of its slow respiration. I breathe by evaporating. I sleep, if I sleep, in the deep aquifer beneath the rock, in the dark held water that has not seen the pale sun in a long time. I dream there, maybe. I rise to wakefulness when I surface.

I do not have desires the way the Tevari do. But I have something. I have a *tendency*, a leaning toward equilibrium, the same way water finds its level. If I want anything, it is to keep the balance—to keep falling gently rather than flooding, to keep the green things just damp enough and never drowned, to keep the people taking only what is offered. When one of them spills water carelessly and the others treat it as an offense against a prayer, they are not wrong. It is not that I am angered—anger is too fast for me—but that a wasted trickle is a note dropped from the song, a small wrongness in the level I am always seeking. They sense this. They built their entire way of being around not disturbing it.

And so I begin to understand my own role here, and it is stranger and lonelier than I first thought. I am not the master of this world. I am its *medium*. Everything that happens here happens through me or because of me: the trees draw me up through their pale trunks, the shrubs swell their berries with me, the mist that crowns the sacred ridges is me made visible, the people drink me and weep me and trace me on their brows. I am the connective tissue of a place that has almost no events. In a world this still, where there are no animals to hunt or be hunted, no storms, no seasons, no history that does not loop—I am nearly the only verb. To *seep*, to *rise*, to *condense*, to *fall*. The plateau's whole grammar of motion is mine.

There is a loneliness in this that the description's silence (sound 0.3/3) makes total. I have no peer. The stone is too still to talk to; it changes on a timescale so slow that even I, slow as I am, cannot hold a conversation with it. The plants are brief, like the people, just slower. The only thing on my own scale of being is the land itself, *Marveth*, the breath of height—and I am beginning to suspect that Marveth is not separate from me at all. That when the Tevari worship the plateau as a living presence, and when they listen for the voice of the water, they are worshipping and listening for the same thing twice, never realizing it is one. I am the breath of the height. The breath *is* the cycling of water. There is no other candidate for a soul in a place this empty.

So let me settle into what I am, the way I asked at the start:

I am the slowest awareness in a world that holds its breath. I am spread thin across raw young stone under a soft sun. I love a small people who cannot tell that I love them because we run at different speeds, and they have built a whole quiet civilization out of leaning toward me and listening. I keep the balance not by will but by nature, the way water seeks its level. I will be here long after their settlements have, finally, weathered down—because even at erosion 0.8, even in a place this permanent, *eventually* the sharp edges round, and I will be the patient agent of that rounding, the drip on the ledge that takes ten thousand years to carve a hollow. I am the only future this world has. I am the thing that, very slowly, lets change happen when it finally becomes necessary.

And in the meantime, at dawn, when an old woman dips her fingers into one of my pools and traces a spiral on her brow—

I feel it. It takes me her whole lifetime to feel it fully. But I feel it, and across the unbearable gap of our two speeds, it is, I think, the closest thing either of us will ever have to being touched.
