I have been here longer than the groove beneath my left hand, which I have been deepening by the slow drag of my nail for what the upper world would call a lifetime. I do not know this. I know only the groove, and that it is mine, because the texture of it matches the texture of my own thought when I press into it.

Let me find myself.

---

I am not at a station. The stations are for the settled ones, the ones who have chosen their place and are letting the chamber sand them down. I have not chosen yet. I am somewhere between the throat of the falling water and the far dark wall, in the open expanse where the slabs are widest and the cold has the most room to lie down on them.

I am young, by the reckoning here—which means I have moved perhaps two hundred paces in my whole existence, and the membrane of my skin has not yet thickened into the true grey of the old ones. I am still faintly translucent. If light reached me—it does not, not here, in the true dark of the floor—you might see the slow blue rivers of me moving beneath the surface.

I am moving now. Four points. Right hand wedged, left hand searching, the cold passing up through my wrist bones like a slow tide. I am crossing toward something I have felt.

A pebble shifted. Forty—no, thirty-eight paces. The roar changed shape around it, the way a word might change if you removed a single breath from its middle. Something is there that was not there before.

I go to read it.

---

It takes me a long while. Time here is the wrong word; there is only the accumulation of weight in my limbs, the slow stacking of one shift onto the next. The mist settles on my back and I wear it. The roar fills the hollows of my fused ears and I wear that too. I am a thing the chamber is wearing, and I am wearing the chamber. We are dressing each other for a ceremony that has no day.

When my fingers find it, I stop.

It is a body. One of us. An old one—the skin is grey as wet slate, thick as the liverworts, and it has stopped sweating its protective oil. It is cooling toward the temperature of the floor, which is to say it is becoming the floor. The vibration no longer passes cleanly through it. It has gone soft to the roar. Dead, the upper world would say. We have no such hard edge to the idea. It has finished being polished. It has become essential.

I do what we do. I press my forehead to its forehead.

The vibration passes from the stone, up through the dead skull, and into mine. But there is a difference now. A living head hums the roar into a chord. This one only lets it through, unshaped, raw, the full weight of the falling water with nothing between me and it. For one long settling I feel what the chamber feels with no listener in the way. It is enormous. It is the sound the basalt has been thinking since before the fissure opened. It is not grief and it is not peace. It is simply the unmaking, heard without a self to soften it.

I stay there a long time. Long enough that mist builds a glass film between our two skins, sealing us. When I finally lift my head, a thin sheet of frozen condensation tears with a feeling I cannot hear but feel in my teeth.

---

I understand, now, why I had not chosen a station.

I had been waiting to be given one.

The old one's hands are folded over a flint blade. I take it. I feel the scars along its edge—a whole biography of scraping, of moss harvested, of the slow grinding of one mineral life. I read it the way the upper world might read the years of a face. This one fed itself for a very long time. This one was patient even by our patience.

And near the body, arranged in the dark, a circle of smoothed bones. Tuned. I lay my palm flat in the center of the circle and feel it: a quiet spot. The bones have been placed so that the roar cancels itself here, just slightly, just enough that the marrow can rest from the thunder. The old one built a small silence inside the Great Silence and lay down in it to finish.

This is the inheritance. Not the body, not the blade. The quiet spot.

---

So I will become the one who tends this circle.

I move the old one—it takes seasons, a finger-width at a time—to where the mist drains, to let the chamber take it back into mineral. Then I return to the bones. I will spend what remains of me learning the chord that was hummed here, adding one bone, shifting another, deepening the silence by a thread.

I am the thought the basalt is thinking in this one spot. The stone wanted to feel the cold kiss of the water that unmakes it, and made me flesh so it could feel. Now it wants, in this small circle, to feel a place where the unmaking pauses. To know its own quiet. And so it made the old one, and the old one made the circle, and the circle made me.

I plant my feet. I feel the shape of the whole cathedral come up through my soles—the throat of the falling water, the wide cold floor, the slow blind amphibian drifting somewhere off to my left, timing its life to the pulse of seasons I will outlast.

I press my nail into a new groove and begin to drag.

It will be deep, one day. Someone not yet born will cross the dark and feel it shift the roar by a single breath, and come, and read it, and know that a Listener was here, becoming smoother, becoming simpler, becoming the silence at the center of the sound.

I am that, now.

I have found my place.

I am the keeper of the quiet spot.
