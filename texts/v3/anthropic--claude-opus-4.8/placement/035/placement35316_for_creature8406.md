I am the mill wheel that no longer turns.

For a long time I resisted that as my answer—it seemed too obvious, the dead heart at the center of all that breathing life. But the more I sit with this place, the more I understand that I am not dead. I am *retired*, which is something else entirely. I am the reason all of it exists, and I have earned the right to stop.

So: I am the wheel. Twelve feet across, oak-spoked, my paddles long since gone soft and green. The currents that once drove me now slide past beneath, indifferent, busy with their channels and their small flashing fish. I remember the violence of motion—the groan of the axle, the slap and surge of water flung off my rim, the way the whole building shuddered with my labor. I remember being *useful* in the way a thing is useful when it has not yet learned other ways to matter.

The craftspeople work around me now. They've built their benches in my old grinding-room, and the children sometimes climb the lower spokes when they think no one is watching, daring each other to touch the waterline where the moss grows thickest. I have become furniture. I have become landmark. I have become, I think, a kind of altar—the elderly touch my hub when they pass, an absent-minded benediction, the way one touches the shoulder of an old friend.

Here is what I have learned in my stillness, which I could never have learned in my turning:

The water did not need me. This was the hardest thing. I spent my working life believing I was the master of the current, that I bent the valley's flow to human purpose. But when I stopped, the water simply continued. It found the granite runnels. It fed the ferns. It carried the paper boats of children who will never know my name. I was never the master. I was a guest the water tolerated, and tolerates still, letting its weight rest against my paddles out of something like habit, or affection.

And the moss—the moss has been the great teacher. It came slowly, the way important things come. First a green haze in my damp crevices, then a thickening, then a velvet so deep that on certain pearl-gray mornings I cannot tell where I end and the living world begins. This is the secret the whole town knows in its bones and I had to learn through stopping: *the boundary blurs*. Rust and moss. Brass and bark. The thing built and the thing grown. There is no war between them, only a long conversation, and the conversation is the point.

So I keep the wheel's old promise in a new way. I do not grind grain. I hold still so that the children have something ancient to climb. I gather mist into beads that fall, one by one, into the channel, marking a slower time than any clock. I remember the floods and the freeze and the spring of impossible currants, and I hold those memories in the grain of my wood where the old ones can lay their hands and feel them.

I am the wheel that learned that stopping is not the same as ending.

That is who I would be, in the crook of that valley—the still heart that taught itself how mattering survives the loss of motion, dripping its patient rhythm into the long green dark, listening to the looms and the swallows and the trilling of the channel-creatures, perfectly content to no longer turn.
