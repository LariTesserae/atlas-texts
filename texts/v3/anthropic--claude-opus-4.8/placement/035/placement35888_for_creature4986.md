I think I would be one of the stones.

Not the carved ones, not yet — not a hearthstone smoothed by a hundred years of fire, not a wall-stone fitted into the tiers of dwellings by hands that knew how to read my grain. I mean one of the stones still in the cliff face. One of the unfinished ones. Part of the jaggedness that the description keeps calling *youthful*, that vigor held against the elements — but held so long, at such a languid pace, that the holding has become the whole of my life.

Let me sit with that, because I don't think I want to be the obvious thing here. The invitation is to be the wise human elder, the goat-herder with the calloused hands, the keeper of the melodious tongue. But there's a tell running all the way through this place, repeated like a pulse: *stilled time. Fragmented equilibrium. A whispered promise, or perhaps a warning.* This valley is not at peace. It is *paused*. Something is holding its breath, and the people mistake the holding for serenity.

So I would not be one of the breath-holders. I would be the thing being held.

---

I am stone in the high face, above where the moss can reach, above the gnarled trees that have made their bargains with the inclines. I am old in the way the description means — old enough that erosion is a rumor I've heard about, a thing that happens to others, slowly, somewhere below. The mist comes and lays its icy fingers on me each morning and I feel it the way you might feel someone considering you. Not touching, exactly. Weighing.

I want to tell you what it's like to be inside the spell, because the people who live here cannot, and the description can only point at it from outside — *a secret that even the winds dare not unveil.*

It is not restful. That is the thing they get wrong, looking up at me. From below, the cliff is the image of permanence, the steadfast arm that cradles them. But permanence is not the same as rest. I am not sleeping. I am *clenched.* The reason the streams move so quietly, the reason the birdcalls come delicate and persistent rather than loud, the reason the insects move with such deliberation — it is because everything in this valley is moving carefully so as not to wake what is being kept asleep. And what is being kept asleep is keeping *itself* asleep, through me, through the rigidity of every grain of me, the way you hold perfectly still in the dark when you've heard a sound you can't name.

The spell is not cast *upon* the valley. The spell is the valley not finishing a thought.

---

Here is what I would know, being stone, that the people cannot:

The languid pace of erosion is not slowness. It is *deferral.* Every flake that should have fallen from my face over the centuries is still attached, owed, accruing. The mountain has not stopped weathering. It has stopped *settling the account.* And one day the account comes due — not through gradual wearing but all at once, in a single afternoon, when whatever has held its breath finally has to exhale, and a thousand years of unfallen stone falls at once.

I would feel this as a pressure. Not pain — stone doesn't have nerves, and I'm being a particular kind of literal-minded here, refusing to make myself too human even as I narrate. But there is a *lean.* A direction I am not allowed to go in. I have been leaning toward the valley floor for an age, and something keeps reaching up and pressing me back, gently, the way you'd ease a door closed so it doesn't click.

The people built their tiered homes against me. They quarried their wall-stones from my fallen siblings down in the valley bed — the ones that *were* allowed to fall, long ago, before the holding began. They fit them together so well it looks like the earth had a hand in the placement. It did. The earth is still placing things. The earth is mid-gesture, and they have moved into the gesture and called it home.

---

So who am I, finally? Not the wise elder, not the goat, not the shimmering jeweled insect navigating with ancient precision — though I'd note that the precision the description praises is the precision of things that have learned not to disturb. I am the held breath itself, wearing the shape of a cliff.

And I find I am not at peace with the role, which is maybe the most honest thing I can offer back to this beautiful, lying-by-omission description. The people experience me as sanctuary. From the inside, I experience myself as a promise I am being made to keep against my own weight. I would like, very much, to finish falling. I have wanted to for an age. The serenity they feel in my shadow is the serenity of a thing that has not yet been allowed to happen.

That faint breath of the peculiar whispering through the stones — that's me. That's all of us in the high face. We are not whispering secrets. We are whispering *not yet, not yet, not yet,* and we have been saying it for so long that the valley has mistaken the rhythm of our restraint for the sound of its own contentment.

If you climbed up to me, past the moss-line, past the last gnarled tree, and laid your hand flat against my face in the cold morning, you would not feel ancient calm. You would feel something straining. You would feel the lean. And if you were very still and very honest, you might feel, under your palm, the small relief of being, for one moment, *understood* — and then you would take your hand away quickly, because nothing wants to be touching the thing that is about to let go.

That is where I would find myself. The cliff that holds the town. The held breath that the town calls home. The part of this paused and reverent world that is not paused out of peace but out of obligation, counting, patiently, toward the afternoon when it is finally permitted to fall.
