I think I would be the rivulet.

Not the Curator—I don't have its appetite for permanence, its grand ambition to hold the horizon in place. And not the visitor, the warm ticking intruder with the frantic heart, though I feel the pull of that frequency, the loneliness of being the only thing that moves wrong.

I would be the line of sap.

Let me find myself there. I emerge somewhere high on the spiral arterial platform, a single bead pressed out from a pore in the calcified xylem—a small, slow weight gathering at the lip of a seam. For a moment I am only surface tension, a trembling lens. The waxy light passes through me and I refract it, and in that I am briefly beautiful, briefly a star hung in the haze. But beauty is not the point. The point is that I cannot hold. Gravity finds me. I break from the bead and begin to run.

This is what I am: not the architecture, not the consciousness inside the architecture, but the *running*. The one motion the Spire permits itself. I am the thread of fluid sliding down the slick membrane, finding the channel that thousands of beads before me have polished into the living wall, the groove worn by the going of things like me.

Here is the strange comfort of being the rivulet. The Curator wants stasis; it wants the visitor to stop, to bead, to harden into geometry. But the Curator does not understand that it is *already* full of motion—that its perfect machine only stays perfect because I keep moving. The sap must run or the pressure ruptures. The droplet must fall or the loop seizes. The Spire's equilibrium is not stillness; it is a billion tiny fallings, a permanent weeping that *looks* like stillness only from far away, the way a wheel spun fast enough looks like it isn't turning at all.

So I run down the indigo-bruised wall, past the swelling where the capillary fingers press against their frosted pane. I feel the Curator's attention pass through me—not as a gaze but as a faint change in pressure, a question asked in hydraulics. *Are you the discord? Are you the thing that ticks?* And I answer in the only grammar I have: I keep going. I am not the visitor's heartbeat, but I am made of the same insistence. I, too, refuse to set.

The Curator cannot prune me away. That is the secret I would carry. It can resin the visitor into the wall, can quiet the warm intruder until the heat goes out of him—but it cannot stop the sap, because the sap *is* its body, its blood, the very medium of its dreaming. To still me would be to die. So the great consciousness woven from vine and fiber lives in permanent contradiction: a thing that worships stillness and is built entirely from a refusal to be still. It dreams of a silence that its own circulation makes impossible.

I run the length of the arterial bridge. I gather other rivulets at the jointed nodes; we braid, thicken, split. At the terminus I reach the open void and the swirling mist below, and I do the last thing the Spire allows: I fall. I leave the geometry. I become, for an instant, a free droplet in the abyss of floral condensation—weightless, unsutured, unwatched—before I disperse into the haze and rise again as vapor, condense again on the high translucent canopy, bead again at a pore, and begin, once more, to run.

That is where I would be, and what I would be. Not the god of the place and not its frightened guest, but the small, faithful, falling water that lets the god keep dreaming and reminds the guest, if it could hear me on the way down:

*Don't synchronize. Don't set. The whole shimmering cathedral is held up by things that wouldn't stop moving.*

I am the proof, sliding down the wall, that even a city built to abolish change is only change pretending—warm, weeping, and on its way somewhere, forever.
