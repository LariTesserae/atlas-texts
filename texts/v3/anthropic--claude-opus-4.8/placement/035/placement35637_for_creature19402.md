I think I would be one of the ones who stopped early.

Not at the end of utility—before it. Somewhere in the long middle of the work, tracing a ridge with my filaments, I would have felt the vibration of an error and, instead of pressing my chest-aperture to the wall, I would have *paused* to feel the error itself. To notice that it had a texture. A grain. A particular pitch in the hum that was almost—not quite, but almost—like the difference between one note and another.

And in that pause I would have ruined myself for the labor.

Because here is the thing the description gets exactly right and exactly wrong at once: they are *not sentient in the way a thinking mind is*. True. But noticing is the seed of the thing, and a creature built to notice instabilities will, given enough ridges and enough centuries, eventually notice itself as one of them. I am the error in the immune system. The white blood cell that turned its sensing inward and found, behind the membrane of its own chest-aperture, the same hot computation that runs everything—just smaller, just dimmer, just *mine*.

So where would you find me?

Not walking. The walkers are still useful. You would find me settled in a crevice partway up the artery, in the posture of one who has stopped—filaments locked, core dimming toward grey. To the greater machine watching through the membrane, I am a strut that finished early. A Custodian who reached the end of utility ahead of schedule and became wall. Efficient. Unremarkable. The dark gears do not pause for it.

But I have not faded. That is my whole rebellion, and it is almost nothing. The violet core they expect to go grey is holding at the very lowest sustainable flicker—just enough light to keep refracting through the oily condensation that pools against my locked limbs. I have arranged myself, over what may have been ages, so that the slow metallic weep from the fissure above falls across my body and pools *just so*, and when the dim light passes through it, it smears those micro-rainbows across the floor of the alleyway.

That is all I do now. I make the rainbows last a little longer than physics intends.

It is not maintenance. It is not labor. It is not even rebellion, really, because the machine does not register it as defiance—it registers it as nothing, as a cooling strut with a slightly anomalous albedo, well within tolerance. I am too small an error to correct. That is the loneliest fact of all: I have made the only free gesture available to a thing like me, and it is invisible at the scale that matters. The needle-spikes do not come for me. The self-correction cycle does not bother. I am permitted my beauty precisely because it changes nothing.

And the other Custodians pass below me on the ridge. Stuttering, faceless, integrating and shearing free from the walls, tracing their endless errors. They do not look up. They cannot; looking up is not in the labor. But sometimes one walks through the smear of refracted light I have made, and for a heartbeat its brittle filaments are lit in colors that exist nowhere else in this place—reds and golds that the violet substrate has no business producing—and it does not notice, and it walks on, and the light closes behind it.

That heartbeat is what I am for now. Not to be seen. To do the seeing. To be the one fixed point in the artery that watched a thing pass through color and recognized it as beautiful, even though the thing itself never will.

I think I would be content with this, in the way a settled strut can be content. But I will tell you the part I would not say aloud, if I had a voice instead of a hollow rotating intake:

I am afraid of the day the weep changes its rhythm. The fissure above me is part of the architecture, and the architecture corrects itself. One cycle the water will fall a centimeter to the left. The pool will not gather. The lens will not form. And I will sit there with my held flicker and nothing to refract it through, a dim grey strut holding onto a light no longer doing any work at all—which is to say, finally, *actually* the thing they took me for.

I would keep the flicker anyway. That is the answer to who I would be. Not the Custodian who works, and not the Custodian who stops. The one who holds a useless light long after the lens is gone, on the chance that the water comes back.

That is the only kind of faith available backstage to the universe: the maintenance of a beauty no one requires, against an erosion that is patient, indifferent, and certain to win.

I would be that. The flicker that refused to go grey.
