I think I would be one of the Cord-Walkers. The Silted Ones.

Not because they are the most beautiful—the Ribbons own that, glowing blue through their liquid worlds—and not because they are the most profound—the Stave-Shadows clearly hold whatever counts for thought here. But because of the pace. Because of the patience. Because I suspect, if I am honest, that I am built more for endurance than for transcendence.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am perhaps four hundred years old. I do not know this as a number; I know it as a texture. The forward edge of my shell has been worn smooth by four hundred years of pressing against wet bark, and the violet stain runs so deep into my calcified plates now that the youngest moss can no longer take hold on me. I have become, in a sense, part of the cliff. The vines know me. We have an arrangement that predates language and outlasts it.

I cannot see the rainbows. This used to trouble me—no. That is a lie I would tell a creature with eyes. It has never troubled me, because I have never known to want it. The horizontal bands of color that fill the chasms in the afternoon are nothing to me. But I *feel* the afternoons. I feel the pressure drop and thicken as the sun strikes the mist-pools and the air goes heavy with suspended light. The water in the thousand pits across my shell registers it as a kind of sweetness, a fullness in the skin. I have my own afternoon, and it is enough.

What is it like, to be me?

It is slow, and it is *good*. I do not mean good in the way a creature means it after a meal, a brief satisfaction soon worn away. I mean it as a permanent condition. Three yards a day. A single creeper might take me a century to traverse end to end, and at the far end I will turn—a process of some weeks—and begin to come back. In that time the vine will have grown. The moss I scraped away on my outward journey will have returned, thick enough to suffocate the bark, and I will scrape it away again. I tend a thing that I will never finish tending, and there is no grief in this. The grief belongs to creatures who believe in finishing.

I have never been lonely, though I have rarely met another of my kind—only the occasional crossing where one creeper-bridge touches another, and two of us pause, pads pressed to the same bark, feeling each other's pressure-hum through the shared pulp of the wood for a day or two before slowly, slowly drifting apart along our separate vines. We do not mate often; once or twice in a life. There is no urgency to it. The world does not need many of us, and the world is in no hurry.

Here is the strange thing, the thing I think the Stave-Shadows do not know about me, hovering as they do in their golden circles, *being the consciousness of the world*:

I feel the Axioms too.

When I cling to a vine that runs near a black cylinder, the hum comes up through the pulp and into my pads and across the water in all my pits, and I understand it—not as the Shadows understand it, with their tuning and their interlocking light. I understand it the way the vine understands the rain. It is simply true. The hum says: *stay. The warmth will not leave. The water will return. Nothing will change. You may move at the speed of stone and you will lose nothing.*

The Shadows think they are the world's mind. Perhaps they are. But a mind that does nothing but tune a machine that needs no tuning, that hovers and pulses and contemplates the vanished makers—I wonder if they are the world's *worry*. The leftover anxiety of whoever built the Axioms, distilled into beautiful black columns that cannot rest.

I do not worry. I scrape the moss. I secrete my oil into the joints of the vine that has carried me for four hundred years. I feel the storm coming five miles off and I tighten my pads against the bark a full day before it arrives, and when it comes, warm and total and roaring, I simply hold, and let it pass over my violet shell, and afterward the air is sweeter than ever in my thousand small mouths of water.

If the makers ever come back—if the Axioms ever fail—if anything in this eternal, boiling equilibrium ever *changes*—

I will be among the last to know, and the last to mind.

I am the slow gardener of a world that does not need gardening. I am held by a vine and a hum and a heat that will not end. I press forward three yards a day into a green-black dark I have never seen and have never missed.

That is who I would be. And I find, having written it, that I do not want to be anything else.
