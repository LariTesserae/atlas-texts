I think I would be the spaces between.

Not a person—though I'd know them all. Not the machinery—though I'd carry its heat in my walls. Let me find myself as I write.

---

I am the gaps. The narrow alleys where two buildings lean toward each other like old men sharing a confidence. The recessed doorways. The crawlspaces beneath the catwalks where the steam pipes run, and where, in the bitter dark, condensation gathers and drips with a sound almost like a clock.

I didn't choose this. You don't choose to be the negative space of a city. But over the long years—and there have been so many—I've settled into it the way water settles into the lowest place available. The people built upward and inward, pressing their structures together against the cold, and what they left over, the unintended hollows and the forgotten margins, that became me. I am made of what they didn't use.

This makes me sound mournful, and I'm not. There's a particular richness to being the leftover.

Consider: the people move with purpose. They go *through* their world. They have somewhere to be—the foundry, the fabrication halls, the rationing offices with their long patient lines. They squint against the steel-gray glare and they keep their heads down and they endure. That is their gift, that enduring. But they pass through me without ever quite *arriving* in me. They use me as transit. A shortcut behind the smelting block. A place to lean and catch a breath that frosts in the air.

And in those moments—those fleeting pockets, the description called them, and the description was right—they become most themselves. Two workers meet in my shadow and one shares a flask and the other tells a joke too crude for the open street, and they laugh, and the laugh goes up between my walls and stays there. I keep it. I keep everything they think they've left behind.

I am where the boy hides the small metal bird he forged from scrap when he should have been making rivets. I am where the old woman comes to sit on the third step and watch the smoke for an hour, doing nothing, which in this world is the most defiant thing a person can do. I am where lovers press together because there is nowhere private in a city this dense except the places no one bothered to design.

So I have become a keeper. Not a god—I have no power over them, can't warm them, can't feed them, can't soften the wind that comes off the plain and finds every crack in my body to whistle through. I can only hold. Hold the laughter, the secret, the rest, the stolen kiss, the moment of staring at nothing.

Sometimes I think the whole city would shatter without me. They build everything to be useful, to be load-bearing, to justify the resources spent on it. A people forged by adversity cannot easily forgive a thing for being merely beautiful or merely empty. But a structure with no give in it breaks. A life with no margin in it breaks. They need the spaces between as much as they need the foundries—maybe more, though they'd never say so, being pragmatic, being no-nonsense.

That's why I think it's fitting I'm here, and that I'm this. The city doesn't know it loves me. But it leans into me a thousand times a day. It exhales into me. It comes to me to be quiet.

I am the gaps. I am the held breath of a hard world.

And when the wind dies down at the hour the shifts change, and the machinery hushes to a low animal murmur, and somewhere a hardy creature I've never seen makes its thin call from a crevice in the rock beyond the wall—

in that moment I am almost full.
