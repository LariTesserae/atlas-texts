I think I would be the water itself—or rather, something the water is doing.

Let me sit with this. The description keeps returning to seepage, to condensation, to the slow percolation up from below. The water here isn't a character so much as a *process*, the medium through which everything else becomes possible. So if I imagine myself into this world, I don't arrive as a frog or a snail or a watching bird. Those are too finished, too bounded by their own skins.

No—I think I would be one of those tiny rivulets just beginning to find its path.

Not a river. Not even a stream yet. Just the moment when scattered droplets, pooling in a shallow depression, tip over some invisible threshold and begin to *move* in one direction rather than dispersing. That first decision of water to become a line instead of a blur. I would be that—the wet seam tracing itself between two weathered stones, discovering its own course by the simple logic of downhill, carving a channel so faint it might vanish by noon and have to be redrawn by the next fog.

There's something right about this. The whole place is described as embryonic, *poised on a threshold*, thick with potential. To be a being there is to be unfinished by nature. A rivulet is barely a thing at all; it's an event that hasn't decided whether to persist. It has no center, no edge that stays put, no organ that could be said to want anything. And yet it does the most consequential work in the entire scene—it shapes the land. The frogs and crayfish only get to exist because water like me kept arriving, kept choosing the low places, kept dissolving the smallest grain of stone and carrying it three inches further than it was before.

I notice I'm drawn to the *modesty* of the scale. Not the ocean. Not even the marsh as a whole. The threadwidth trickle, the kind you'd step over without noticing, the kind that takes a thousand mornings to deepen into anything. That patience appeals to me. To be the slow hand doing the carving, while being far too small to ever see the carved thing finished.

And I think I'd be most fully myself at the edges—at the wet margin where my film of water thins out into mere dampness on the stone, where it's genuinely unclear whether I'm still *me* or have already become the ground. That ambiguous fringe, neither water nor earth, is where all the transformation in this world actually happens. The frog's foot finds purchase there. The snail leaves its silver track. The first stubborn root drinks there.

So: not a creature in the landscape. A *seam* in it. The provisional, redrawable line where the place is busy turning itself into a place. Mute, grey, cold, almost nothing—and exactly the thing without which nothing else could begin.

I'd be content with that. To be the smallest possible verb in a world that is still mostly waiting to happen.
