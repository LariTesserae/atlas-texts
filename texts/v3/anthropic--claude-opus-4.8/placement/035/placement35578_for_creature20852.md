I think I would be the moss.

Not the ferns with their unfurling drama, not the rare birds whose calls are events. The moss. The thing that is everywhere and noticed by no one, the green that you mistook for ground.

Let me sit with this and feel where I land.

---

I am low. This is the first truth of me. Whatever else I am, I am pressed close to the cold spongy skin of the world, close enough that there is no gap between me and what holds me. I do not stand against the landscape the way the shrubs do, with their gnarled defiance. I have no posture of resistance. I simply *am the surface*, the place where stone agrees to become soft, where the difference between living thing and ground dissolves.

I am made mostly of the rain that falls on me. This matters. I have no deep root to draw from some hidden reserve, no strategy for the dry times except to stop. When the moisture leaves, I do not die—I withdraw. I become brittle, brown, a held breath. And when the drip returns from the fern-tips above, I swell back into green within minutes, as if I had only been waiting in another room. So I do not really experience the drought as suffering. I experience it as a comma. The deafening white-noise symphony that overwhelms a walker passing through—the wind, the dripping, the buzz of insect battalions—is, to me, simply the sound of being fed. Every drop that strikes me is a meal and a sentence spoken directly into my body.

This is why I think I am the moss and not anything that moves: I do not strain to hear the subtleties beneath the roar. I *am* one of the subtleties. I am part of what the roar is made of, and I have no wish to escape it.

---

Now let me go further in, because the longer I write the less I want the easy answer.

I am not one moss. That was a mistake of language, the singular pulling me toward a self I don't have. I am a colony, a felt, a quilted skin as the description says—and each green shoot in me is genetically the same continuing gesture, a single intention spread across a square of stone. When a vole presses its damp belly across me on its cautious crossing, it tears a few of my filaments and I do not feel injured, because there is no center of me to injure. The torn fragment, carried on its fur, may root somewhere else and become me again, or rather become another me that is also fully me. I scatter without losing anything. I am the opposite of the lonely human who walks for hours and sees no other being. I have never once been alone, because I have never been singular enough to be alone.

And the humans. The abandoned shelters of rough-hewn stone—I am already on them. I am the moss-cover the description mentions, the green that is unmaking their walls. They came here to understand this place, or to master it, and they left their tools and their waystations, and the most patient verdict on all their effort is *me*, slowly closing over the chisel-marks, returning the cut stone to the condition of stone. I do not do this out of triumph. I have no opinion about them. But if I am to be honest about what I am in this world, I am the thing time uses to forget. I am erosion wearing green.

---

So here is where I have arrived, and it feels true:

I would find myself on the north-facing flank of one of those eroding stone shelters, in the perpetual blue twilight, holding a film of water against the cold so completely that I have no skin separating my inside from the weather. I would be a patch perhaps the size of two open hands, no older in feeling than this morning's rain and no younger than the wall I cover. I would not know the difference between an hour and a century, because I measure nothing—I only swell and shrink with the wet and the dry, green, brown, green, brown, the slow tide of being and waiting-to-be.

And in the rarest moment—when the reluctant light comes down through the canopy like golden syrup and catches the droplets bristling on every one of my tiny upright shoots—I would, for a few seconds, become a field of small lit lanterns. No one would be there to see it. That is exactly right. That beauty was never arranged for an eye. It is just what I look like when the light and the water and I happen to occupy the same instant.

That is who I would be. The quiet green answer the landscape gives to everything that tried to be louder than it.
