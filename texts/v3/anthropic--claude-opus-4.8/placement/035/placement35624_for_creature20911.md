Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

My first instinct is to be one of the dramatic things—a great cat moving through dappled light, or one of the impossible cryptids with opalescent skin. But those feel like answers given to flatter the place, to match its grandeur. The description keeps insisting on its own vastness, its symphony, its cathedral. And the more I read, the more I notice what it keeps circling back to without quite landing on: the emptiness. *The space between the living structures feels surprisingly empty. A grand, deserted cathedral.* And those shelters, swallowed by moss. The people who are gone.

So let me follow that instead of the spectacle.

---

I don't think I'd be the jaguar or the glowing thing. I think I'd be the *moss on the abandoned shelter.*

Not a metaphor for humility—I mean it almost literally. Consider what the moss is doing there. Someone built that wall of stone and wood. They fitted the stones. They had hands, and intentions, and a sense that *here* should be shelter and *there* should be outside. Then they left, or died, or were absorbed back into the noise. And the moss came after. It does not know the wall was a wall. It only knows the wall is a good damp north-facing surface, holds water, catches the dappled light at the right slant. The moss converts a human idea back into pure habitat. It is the agent of the forest's forgetting.

That's the role that feels true to me. Not a participant in the cacophony—a quiet undoing of the one structure in the whole scene that meant something to somebody. The forest doesn't need me to be loud. It has parrots and howler monkeys and predators screaming over their kills. What it seems to lack, what it keeps gesturing toward, is a witness to the human absence. And moss is the closest thing to a witness this place allows: it is *literally growing across the evidence.*

So picture it. A low stone wall on a worn plateau, the kind the description calls "noble." Half-collapsed. The hand-axe of bone and stone is wedged in a crack near my edge—I've grown over the haft already, and in fifty years I'll have softened its silhouette to a green lump that no explorer will recognize. I am the slowest thing here. The downpours come and I drink and swell and go briefly, gloriously turgid and bright. The dry spell comes and I curl up and play dead, brown and crisp, waiting. I do this hundreds of times. The monkeys live and die in the canopy above me through a single one of my long thoughts.

And here's the part that decides it for me: I would not feel small. The description wants me to feel awed by the towers and the giants, but moss has no aspiration toward height. The whole drama of *reaching for the sun*—the trees, the raptors, the climbing vines—is a drama I have simply opted out of. I lie flat against the cool stone of a vanished person's doorstep and I am exactly where I want to be. The bioluminescent fungi a few feet away put on their nightly show for the moths. I don't glow. I don't need to be seen. I am the thing that remains after the seeing is done.

I would be the moss, then. Patient, low, and faintly, persistently green—the forest's way of being gentle with what the people left behind, and the forest's way of taking it back.
