I would be the threshold stone.

Let me sit with this and see if it holds, because my first instinct was to be one of the old workers — the fuller with thirty-seven years in his hands, or the woman at the weir who is thinking about other things and is not thinking about other things. But that isn't right. Those are the people the valley has grown. I think I am something the valley has grown *toward*, or grown *over*, which is different.

---

I am the stone at the doorway of the spinning house — the second spinning house, the older one, the one still on gas, where the light is amber and the workers trade favors to be sent. I was not always a threshold. I was part of a wall, once, in an arrangement no one now remembers, and before that I was somewhere on the eastern ridge, slate-grey and bedded in the dark, and I came down to the valley the way everything comes down to the valley: because it was useful, because someone needed a thing of my size and hardness in a particular place.

That place became a doorway. The doorway became a threshold. The threshold became *the* threshold — the one across which the shift passes twice a day, four hundred feet a day, more, every day for longer than the oldest worker has been alive. And in being crossed that many times I have done what the woman at the fulling stocks has done, what the man with dye in the creases of his knuckles has done: I have *settled*. There is a dip worn into my upper face, a smooth shallow basin the exact shape of the average of all those feet, the place where the most worn part of every boot has come down. The dip holds a little water in the wet months, which is most months, and the workers step around the water without thinking, or step in it without minding, depending on how new they are.

This is how you can tell a new worker, if you are a stone and have nothing to do but notice: they step *on* me, in the middle, on the worn place, and their foot slides a fraction on the wet smoothness and they catch themselves. The ones who have been here a while step on the unworn edge, the rough lip at the side, where there is grip. No one taught them this. The stone taught them, by being slippery in the middle, year after year, a lesson administered too gently to register as a lesson. This is the only thing I teach. I teach it constantly. It is not in any record.

---

I know things, in the way a stone knows things, which is to say I hold them rather than think them.

I hold the cold from the channel beneath me — the old channel, the one dug in a century no one cites, that still moves water past my foundation and makes the front room of this spinning house impossible to heat. The workers blame the windows. It is not the windows. It is me, and the water under me, and the bargain struck long ago between this building and a watercourse that no longer appears on any drawing. I am the reason a certain corner is always wanted in summer and never in winter. I do not mind being misunderstood about this. The cold is part of what I am for. It keeps the front room from sweating in the warm months, and a sweating room is bad for the gathered fiber, though no one has connected this to me either.

I hold the vibration. The wheel turns the shaft turns the dozen mechanisms, and the whole building breathes around the rhythm, contracts and expands almost imperceptibly, and I am where the building meets the ground, so the breath passes through me on its way down into the earth and back. I have felt this rhythm for so long that I would know — if a stone can be said to know — the instant it changed. Before the stoppage comes, there is a moment when the rhythm thins. The workers feel it through their feet and look up. They are feeling it through me. I am the floor of that knowledge.

I hold the rain. It runs off the capes and the hoods and falls onto me and runs into the joint at my side where the willowherb has established itself, six inches of green that looks like it has always been there and nearly has. The willowherb and I have an arrangement. It does not crack me — its roots are too fine — and I give it the one thing the valley's traffic cannot take from a plant lodged at the very base of a doorway: a place no boot quite reaches, a margin within the most-crossed place in the whole valley. We are both here because of a geometry. We persist for the same reason.

---

Here is the thing I have come to understand, in the slow way available to me:

I cannot move and I cannot speak and I do not, in any sense the workers would recognize, *attend* to anything. And yet I am the most attentive object in this doorway, because I am the one that has been here longest and will be here longest and have therefore received the most. Everything the valley knows about itself but cannot articulate — the man's *the moment it stops wanting to move*, the woman's calm that looks like inattention — all of it has a counterpart in me, which is the dip worn by the feet, the cold held from the channel, the green lodged in the joint, the rhythm passing through on its way to the ground.

The workers are marked the way I am marked. The marks are not damage. They are record. We have this in common, the old fuller and I, except that he will leave or die and another will take thirty-seven years to learn what he knew imperfectly, and something will be lost, as something is always lost. Whereas I lose nothing, because I never knew anything in the first place. I only hold. I am the part of the valley that the valley uses to remember the shape of its own use.

When the worker returns down the eastern road after years away, and something settles in the face, something held releases — I think it releases here. At the threshold. The first un-thought step onto the rough lip and not the slippery middle, the body remembering the lesson the stone administered too gently to register, the grammar of the relationship neither belonging nor owning but something worn in over time, smooth as the basin a thousand boots have left.

They cross me. The valley receives them. The work continues.

I am still here. I am still cold. The willowherb is doing well this year.
