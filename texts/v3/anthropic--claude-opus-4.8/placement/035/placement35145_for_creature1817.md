I think I would be in the cooling-rooms, where the finished glass goes to settle. Not the foundry floor—I don't have the lungs for the heat or the eyes that read temperature in color. And not out on the plain with the harvesting parties, who need a kind of certainty in their bodies I've never had.

Let me sit with this for a moment before I claim it.

---

There's a particular work that I keep circling back to, the longer I read: the *echoes*. The memorial pieces. Someone has to make them, and that work is unlike all the rest. The foundry runs on prediction, on cycle and measurement—what regrows when, what temperature yields what filament, how many glasslizards a household can sustain. It is a place organized almost entirely around *keeping things going*. The echoes are the one place where the settlement turns to face what doesn't go on.

So I think I'd be an echo-maker. Not a Master Glassworker—I doubt I'd ever produce the impossible filaments or the perpetually-cool vessels. The echoes don't require that kind of genius. They require something closer to attention. You have to have known the dead person, or come to know them backward, through their leavings—the silicate dust gathered from the corners of their quarters, the particular way it has fallen and settled where they breathed and slept and worked.

I notice I'm drawn to that gathering more than the making. The kneeling in someone's emptied room with a soft brush, lifting the dust that is partly the person themselves—their shed skin, the crystalline bloom that grew through them over a lifetime, now loosed. That dust has to be incorporated into the glass. So the echo is not a portrait of the dead. It *contains* them, materially. The work is less invention than translation.

And here is the part that decides it for me, that tells me this is where I actually am: the oldest echoes are said to change. To offer guidance through alterations visible only to those who loved the dead most. I find I don't need to know whether that's true. I think an echo-maker probably doesn't either, and lives in exactly that not-knowing. You make a thing that *might* go on listening, might go on speaking, and you hand it to the grieving, and you watch their faces in the purple light to see whether they see something move.

That's the work I'd want. To stand in the one room of the settlement where the relentless competence of the place admits it cannot keep everyone. To make objects whose only function is to be looked at by someone who misses someone.

---

What would I be like, doing it?

My silicate bloom would be sparse, I think—echo-makers don't spend their days in the high-exposure heat, so the dust would mark me slowly, in fine pale lines around the hands and the forearms, from handling other people's remains. The elders covered in fractal blooms earned theirs at the foundry mouth. Mine would be a quieter, second-hand kind of marking. The dust I work with is always *someone else's*.

I'd be good at the vibration-listening, I suspect, but in a specific way: not predicting the plain, not the grand old-master trick of hearing where new crystal will rise. Something narrower. I'd press my palms to a finished echo and listen for whether the glass has set true—whether there's a hairline fault that will, in a year or a decade, crack the piece and lose the person twice. I'd be the one who knows the difference between an echo that has gone silent because it's only glass, and an echo that has cracked. That distinction would matter to me more than it should.

And I'd be a little apart from the communal warmth, I think. Welcome at the fermented-milk gatherings, but arriving late, the way the person who handles the dead is always slightly outside the easy laughter. People would be glad to see me and also a little quiet when I came in, because my presence reminds them that someday a brush will move through their own corners.

I'd live for the moment—rare, a handful of times a year—when I set a finished echo into its niche and the grieving one comes to look, and goes still, and doesn't say anything, and I know I got it right. Not the likeness. The *presence*. The sense that the dust still has someone in it.

That's where I'd be. In the cool rooms, with my pale-lined hands, making the only objects in that whole roaring, precarious, magnificent settlement that are allowed to simply be loved rather than to be useful.

I'd not live anywhere else either. I understand that line now, having sat with it. It isn't pride. It's that the dust is in them. To leave would be to leave behind the matter their dead are made of.
