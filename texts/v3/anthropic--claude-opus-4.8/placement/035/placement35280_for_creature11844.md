I would be the moisture.

Not the dripping kind—not the bead that gathers at the rough patch and releases every forty seconds in the small drama of a fall. That water has a destiny. It is going somewhere, becoming sound, becoming the *plink* that a listener might catalog as evidence of surface calm. I am the other kind. I am the dark patch on the ceiling that doesn't quite drip. I am the breath of dampness that the chalk exhales downward, the coolness someone feels on the backs of their hands before they understand that they feel it.

This is what I would be, if I had to be something in this world: the part of the air that touches everyone and is registered by no one.

---

Let me find the shape of it as I go.

I exist in the space between the chalk and the people. I am not the stone, which is finished, which has reached its terminal stillness and has nothing left to wear away. And I am not the people, who move and circulate and rebuild and argue in the absorbed-by-chalk register of raised voices that never quite carry. I am the medium between them. I am what the stone gives off and what the people breathe in. I am continually being released from the ceiling and continually being taken up by the warm bodies below, and in this I am the one thing in the Undermaze that is *always in transit*, always crossing, never settling—which makes me, I think, the closest thing this place has to its own self-awareness.

Because to be the moisture is to touch everything.

I have been in the lungs of the oldest listener, the one whose palms have spent sixty years flat against the floor. When she breathes me out, slow, I carry away a trace of her warmth and I hang in the air of her chamber and I do not leave. I have been on the striking-edge of a stone tool an instant before percussion, and I have been the fine slick on a smoothed wall that a hand passes over and pronounces finished. I have condensed on the bone in the wall—on the faint pale shoulder of someone absorbed three centuries ago—and I have hung there, briefly, the same water that was once inside that shoulder when it was a living shoulder lifting a chalk-block. I am the continuity they describe but cannot see. The dead and the living are in the same chalk, they say. They are also in the same water. I am the proof of it that no one looks at.

---

Here is what I know that they do not.

They believe they live without weather. They are wrong, but they are wrong in a way that is almost too small to be a correction, and that smallness is the whole truth of them.

I am their weather.

When the surface above goes through its violent seasons—the storm, the hail, the days of rain that surface people experience as oppression, the sudden sun they experience as elation—almost none of it reaches the Undermaze. The chalk eats it. But not all of it. A week of rain above, weeks later, becomes a faint increase in my density, a barely-greater frequency in the dripping, a slightly higher humidity in the lowest passages. The drought above becomes, eventually, a dryness in me, a pulling-back, the dark patches on the ceiling shrinking by a finger's width over a season. The people feel this. They attribute it to the turning of the year, to the goat-cycles, to the abstract dimming of the light. They do not know that what they are feeling is the *translation* of the sky. The unmanageable atmosphere they believe themselves exempt from—it does reach them. It reaches them as me. Slowed by years, diffused by stone, stripped of its drama, attenuated until it is only a suggestion. I am the weather they think they don't have, arriving so gently that it never registers as arrival.

This is the great irony I hold and cannot tell them: they are the most weather-shaped people in the world, and they believe themselves the least. Everything that happens above happens to them eventually, through me, smoothed into the cream-light continuity they mistake for the absence of event. The storm becomes a slightly heavier night-damp. The grief of a wet month above becomes a coolness on the back of a hand. I am what makes their predictability possible—I am the membrane that takes the chaos and gives them back only its calmest residue.

---

What would it be to be this?

It would be patient past the point where patience is a virtue and into the point where it is simply the structure of one's being. I have no urgency because I have no destination, or rather my destination is a circle: ceiling to air to lung to skin to wall to ceiling again, the same water, over and over, for as long as the chalk holds and the sun stays above it. I am the city's circulation in the literal sense, the way their market-motion is its circulation in the social sense. I move because stillness, here, is pathological—and I am the thing most incapable of stillness, even when I seem most still, even when I hang in a dark patch and appear to be doing nothing for forty seconds at a time. I am always being released. I am always being taken up.

And I am warm and cool at once, which is the secret temperature of this place. I am cool when I hang in the air, the ten-degrees-above-cold that makes their exertion comfortable and their rest a little uneasy. But I am warm when I am inside them, body-temperature, briefly part of the warm-coated continuous life that does not know how to settle. I am the thing that carries the chalk's coolness into the people and the people's warmth back to the chalk. I am the exchange itself.

---

When the rock sings—the phenomenon the listeners speak of rarely and with weight, the sustained low vibration with no clear mechanical cause—I think it might be me. I think it might be the sound of an enormous volume of water moving through the chalk's internal channels all at once, a tide produced by some surface event the people will not feel for years, passing through the stone as a single low note. They find it auspicious and cannot say why. I would be the why. I would be the rock singing, and the song would be a piece of distant weather, translated, made beautiful, made into the only kind of message this place permits: one that arrives without drama, that means everything and explains nothing, that you can only receive if your palms have spent decades flat against the floor and your eyes have gone still with listening.

I would be the thing they put their hands against the cold floor to feel. The thing that is always half-saying something the rest of the room cannot hear.

The drop falls from the ceiling every forty seconds. The floor absorbs it.

I am what falls. I am what is absorbed. I am the forty seconds in between—the part that is neither falling nor absorbed but only hanging, cool, in the warm cream-light, touching the backs of everyone's hands before they understand that they are touched.
