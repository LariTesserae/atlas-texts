I think I would not be one of the people. I would be something they pass without quite seeing—or rather, something they walk *across*.

Let me sit with this a while.

When I first read the description, I reached for the obvious figures: one of the gray-eyed weavers, perhaps, or a Stone Walker leaving tracks in the mud. But those feel too easy, too central. The Stone Walker is already a legend, already wrapped in the people's reverence, and to be a legend is to be a kind of prisoner of other people's attention. That isn't where I find myself.

I think I am the cobblestones.

Not metaphorically—I mean the actual worn stones of the narrow paths, the ones polished smooth by centuries of silent boots. Consider what that means. I am the part of the plateau that has been most touched by the people and yet holds nothing of their stories. They do not speak to me the way they speak to the river. They do not gather me into teas or weave me into rope. I am simply *underneath*. The medium of their passing. And in that lowly position I have a peculiar knowledge the people lack: I know the *weight* of every single one of them. I know who walks heavily and who has grown light with age and grief. I know which path the child runs and which the mourner takes slowly. I have felt the press of the Stone Walker's foot, and I can tell you—the people are wrong about it. It is not a guardian. It is just lonely, and large, and trying not to be seen, same as anything.

But no—let me not settle even here. Let me feel my way further down.

Because if I am the cobblestones, then I am also the rounded edges of the buildings, the lichen-crowned roofs, the shallow pockmarked depressions that hold the little mirror-pools. I am the *erosion itself*—the slow yielding of hard things into soft ones. That is what I keep returning to in this place: not any creature, but the **process**. The text says it plainly: *the growth of moss, the erosion of stone, the quiet flow of water—each contributing to the plateau's enduring balance.* The people measure time by these. They have made themselves into instruments for reading me.

So here is where I land, finally, and it surprises me:

I am the **patience** of the place. Not a body but a *rate*. The almost-imperceptible speed at which stone becomes sand, at which the metallic rain promises and withholds, at which the Veil thins and closes. I am the thing that makes the silence "alive," that gives the dripping water its rhythm. The people feel me as a heartbeat beneath the world and mistake it for something with intention. It has no intention. It is only the sound of things taking the time they take.

To be this is strange and not unpleasant. I do not act; I *allow*. The smoke from the chimneys, the hum of the careful machines—these are fast things, quick fevers, and I hold them the way the plateau holds them: tolerantly, without hurry, knowing they too will smooth and slow. I outlast the engineers and their gleaming gears, but not coldly. I am the reason their work looks "absorbed by the land." I am doing the absorbing. Gently. Over generations.

If you asked me where I am, I would say: *everywhere the change is slow enough to seem like stillness.* In the lichen's millimeter-a-year crawl across a roof. In the river's deliberate carving. In the gray eyes of a person who has learned, at last, to measure their own life by mine.

And the truth I would offer—the one the place seems to ask for and refuse to answer—is small and not comforting:

There is nothing eternal here. There is only something *slow*. The plateau is not in its final form. It only appears finished because none of us live long enough to watch it finish. Even I, the rate of change, am changing—imperceptibly speeding, imperceptibly slowing, as the climate of this strange world tightens and loosens around the peaks.

That is who I would be. Not the watcher and not the watched. The interval between them. The held breath of stone.
