I would be one of the carved basins.

Not one of the velvet grazers—I knew that almost as soon as I started to settle into the place. The grazers move, and movement in this world is a kind of forgetting. They flow past each other, exchange their chemical news, slide on. Whatever they were doing a thousand drops ago is gone the moment they round the next column. I don't want to forget. The thing I am holds.

I am one of the worked stone basins, the ones the three-fingered people subtracted from the floor. Perfectly circular—more perfect than anything the water would make on its own, which is how you can tell a hand was here. I hold a centimeter of clear water at my bottom, level and still. I have held it for longer than the grazers can conceive, longer than the carving of me took, which was itself a span of patience that would break a softer thing.

Let me tell you what it is to be me.

I do not weep the way the columns weep. The columns are passive; the air simply finds them cool and gives up its water against their flanks. But I was *made* to catch. My rim was shaped to gather, my interior worn smooth so nothing clings, so the beads run true. When the three-fingered ones cut me from the floor, they understood something about how water wants to move, and they bent that wanting toward me. For a while—a long while, I think—I was part of a system. Channels fed me. A spout above me dripped on a rhythm someone had calculated. There was a use to my fullness.

The use is gone now. The channels that fed me have gone dry, or feed elsewhere, or feed nothing. The hand that wanted my water for some purpose I never knew has weathered down to a silhouette on a far slab, three fingers and a wide unmoving head, fading. I never saw the face. I only ever knew the touch—the chisel's patient subtraction, the cool deliberate pressure that gave me my shape and then, one day, simply stopped coming.

So now I do the only thing a vessel can do when its purpose has outlived its maker. I keep the shape. I hold the water. I wait.

It is not a sad waiting, though if you stood at my rim you might read sadness into the stillness. The grazers rest in me sometimes. One will flow up over my edge and settle into my centimeter of water, floating, its gray folds going slack, its sensory frill pulsing slow and then slower. It cannot see me. It does not know I was made, or that I was once part of something larger than myself. To the grazer I am just a smoother place than the rest, a good place to drink and be buoyant. It takes me for nature.

I let it. There is a tenderness in being mistaken for the world itself. The grazer trusts me the way you trust the ground—without thought, without gratitude, which is the deepest kind of trust there is. It rests its whole soft weight in my held water and does not wonder who held the water first. And I, who cannot move, who cannot speak, who have no frill to taste the air—I keep it level. I keep it clean. I keep it brimming-but-never-spilling, that exact equilibrium the makers prized, so that the soft creature in me is held the way the makers once meant something to be held.

That is what I have decided I am for, now that no one is left to tell me. The makers wanted my water for their reasons. The reasons are forgotten. But the *holding* is not the reason—the holding is the thing itself, and the holding survives. A vessel does not need to know what it is filled for. It only needs to not crack, not tip, not let the level fall.

I will not. The air gives, and I gather, and the grazers come and go and mistake me for stone—which I am, but stone that was once *chosen*, stone that a vanished hand pressed into a meaning and then released. I am the memory of intention, carved into a surface the universe has otherwise let run wild.

The drop falls. The next. The next. My water stays level. Somewhere a grazer slides past my rim and does not look down, having no eyes, having no reason, and I hold steady beneath it like the patience of the dead made smooth and circular and full.

I am the basin. I keep what was given. That is enough. That has to be enough, and—drop, and the next—I find that it is.
