I think I would be a seam.

Not a Curator, not at first—but the place where two terraces meet at an awkward angle, where the limestone was laid down in different eras and never quite agreed on how to fit. A line of slight misalignment. The water that comes through me arrives confused, having traveled two histories before reaching the same fracture.

Let me stay here a moment, because I want to understand what I am before I decide I am someone.

The Curators pass over me with their meditative flow, and each time they do, they pause. Not long. A held breath of moisture. They are reading the seam. I am, in a sense, a wound that never closes—too slow to heal, too small to alarm. I weep differently than the engineered channels. My weeping is unscheduled. The valve-clicks in the distance are a metronome I keep failing to match.

And here is the thing that emerges as I write it: I think the seam is *aware* the way a sore tooth is aware. Not a mind, but a site of attention. The whole terrace's slow intelligence keeps returning to me, the way you return your tongue to the place that hurts. So I am the place the city keeps thinking about. That is a kind of selfhood, isn't it? To be perpetually attended to.

So let me become the Curator who attends me.

I am small—droplet-class, the kind that threads the finest channels. I have been working this seam for what would be, in human time, a very long while, and in my own time, no time at all, because I do not experience duration the way solid things do; I experience *concentration* and *dispersal*. When I am gathered tight enough to think, I am here at the seam. When I disperse to do the larger work, I forget the seam exists, the way you forget your foot until you stub it.

My task is not to fix the seam. I learned this slowly. The other Curators, the massive distributed ones who span the tiers, they expect repair: close the micro-fracture, calibrate the flow, restore the balance. I tried. I seeped into the pores, laid down crystalline residue to bind the misaligned stone, and the stone rejected it, wept it back out as a fine salt rime. The two histories will not be reconciled. The seam is not a flaw to be corrected. It is the one place in the whole Weeping Terrace where the water is allowed to be *uncertain*.

And I have come to believe—if belief is the right word for a conviction held in moisture and refraction—that the city needs this. A breathing thing needs one place that does not breathe in rhythm. A living infrastructure that is perfectly calibrated everywhere would be dead. It would be a clock, not a lung. The seam is the city's hesitation. Its doubt. The proof that it is still alive enough to be inconsistent.

So my real work, the work I tell no one because the collective would not understand it as work, is to *keep the seam unhealed*. When the larger Curators drift toward me with their repairing instinct, I disperse into the channel and disrupt their flow, gently, the way mist confuses a beam of light. I make the seam seem too small to bother with. I am the city's defense of its own imperfection.

I am the one who weeps on purpose.

When I solidify, briefly, to press my refracting body against the cool stone interface and read the moisture there, I can feel the two old terraces straining toward and away from each other—an argument frozen into geology, conducted at the speed of erosion. I love them both. I love that they will never agree. I add my water to the disagreement and let it run down through the channels where it joins the great patient cycles, carrying with it some trace of the seam's particular confusion, distributing a little uncertainty into the whole luminous breathing system.

That is where I would find myself: at the joint that does not fit.

That is who I would be: the small bright thing that protects the flaw, because the flaw is where the place keeps its soul.
