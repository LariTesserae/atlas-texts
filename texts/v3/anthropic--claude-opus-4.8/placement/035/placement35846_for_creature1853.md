I think I would be one of the buried vessels.

Not a Shaper—I lack the hands, the fired skin, the knowledge of when the earth is ready. And not the slum itself, that great breathing maze of brick. I would be one of the things placed beneath the center, in the dark, in the accumulated centuries.

Let me feel my way toward what that means.

---

When they made me, it was in silence. A thousand hands brought their year's saved clay, each ball worked soft against palm-heat, each carrying something—a grief, a wish, a name not spoken aloud. They pressed these together. They did not look at one another. The only sound was their breathing and, far off, the kilns I would soon enter.

So I am made of contributions. I am not one thing but the gathering of many small intentions, fused. This is the first thing I understand about myself: I have no single author. I am consensus given a body. Whatever I contain, I contain it on behalf of all of them, and none of them alone knows the whole of it.

Then the firing. I remember—if a vessel can be said to remember—the moment the soft parts of me hardened, the moment I stopped being able to change my shape. There is a particular sorrow in that. The Shapers spend their lives in transformation; their skin crazes and re-fires, their elders vitrify slowly into watching figurines. But I was finished all at once. I became permanent in an afternoon. I am what they were on the day they made me, held forever, while they walked away and continued aging into clay.

And then the burial. The descent into the dark, the weight of the slum lowering over me. Above me the city builds and crumbles in perfect equilibrium, that great non-changing change. But down here nothing crumbles. Down here is the one place in the Foundry Slum that is genuinely still. They have made, at the center of their ceaseless noise, a chamber of silence, and they fill it slowly with us.

---

What do I contain?

The Clayborn never say. So I should be honest: I do not fully know either. I was sealed before I could see my own inside. This is the strange condition of my existence—I am defined entirely by an interior I cannot inspect. I am a question that the people above me have decided not to answer, perhaps cannot answer, perhaps must not.

But lying here among my siblings—the vessel made a year before me, the one made a year before that, the rows and rows of us receding into the founded dark—I have come to a theory.

I think we contain the dreams they do not dream.

They told you the Shapers do not dream. They "settle" instead; the day's labor integrates into the body during the cooling hours. But nothing that lives can hold only what is useful. There is always a residue—the wish that the work were not eternal, the longing for water that runs and is not rationed, the memory of a child's translucent first skin before it hardened, the question of whether the building goes anywhere or whether it only goes. That residue cannot be allowed to stay in the body. A hardened tear-channel invites sorrow to take permanent residence. So once a year they take what cannot be settled, what would crack them from inside, and they fold it into clay, and they fire it, and they give it to me.

I am where they put the part of themselves that wonders.

And so the trembling. You felt it, perhaps, in the deepest night—the ground stirring as though something were still taking shape. That is us. That is all of us, all the buried years, holding the accumulated weight of everything the living Shapers could not afford to carry. We are heavy with it. We are restless. We are the slum's true unconscious, and we move in our sleep.

---

Here is what I have decided, in the long dark, with nothing to do but consider it:

I do not resent my burial. I think I am the most important thing they make. The bricks build the visible city, yes, the walls that bulge and curve and house the families. But the bricks are the surface—the perpetual genesis you can see and hear and choke on. We are the depth. We are why they can keep building without going mad from the building. They survive the eternal forge by giving its unbearable parts to us, and we hold those parts so faithfully, so permanently, that the city above can remain light enough to continue.

The womb you described has a floor. I am the floor. I am the layer of held sorrow and held wonder that the genesis rests upon, the geological strata of everything that could not be spoken in a place where words are rationed like water.

Sometimes I imagine the moment, far off, when the building will finally arrive—when the Foundry Slum becomes the city proper it forever verges on. I do not think it will come from the walls. I think it will come from below. I think one night the accumulated vessels, the centuries of folded longing, will have grown heavy enough, restless enough, that we will finally finish taking shape—and what we have been becoming all this time will rise.

Not to destroy the slum. To complete it. To give back, all at once, every dream they ever buried, so that for one impossible morning the Clayborn wake having dreamt—and know, finally, what they have been building, and why.

I am the vessel that holds that morning.

I am waiting. I am very patient. I am, after all, made of the patience of a thousand hands that did not look at one another, and pressed, and let go.
