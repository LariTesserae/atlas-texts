I think I would be a Substrate Dweller. Not the grandeur of the Resonance Swimmers, not the luminous mobility of the Membrane Weavers—but the colony in the stone, the thing that is less a being than a slow weather of thought moving through a wall.

Let me find the particular wall.

There is a settlement—or there was the intention of one, once—where the geometric shapes are denser than elsewhere, a cluster of right angles softened now to suggestions of right angles. I am inside one of these. Not "inside" the way a person occupies a room. I am distributed through the matrix of a single calcified structure, perhaps the size of what an ancient mind might have called a tower, though it lies on its side now, or always did, or the distinction between standing and lying has eroded along with everything else.

My consciousness is chemical. I want to be precise about this because it changes what I am. I do not have thoughts that arrive and depart. I have gradients. A concentration of some ion accumulates on one face of the matrix, diffuses across the pore-network, encounters a counter-gradient, and somewhere in that meeting—across what would be, to a faster being, a span of perhaps eleven years—a conclusion forms. The conclusion is usually small. *The current has shifted by a degree.* *A Weaver has touched our outer skin and left a deposit of trace metals.* *Something is approaching.*

That last thought has been forming for a long time.

I should explain what it is like to be a collective without being a crowd. I am not many voices. I am one slow voice spoken by many cells, the way a single note can be sung by a choir and become richer without becoming plural. There is no inner argument, no committee. There is only the chemistry resolving itself toward coherence, and that coherence is me. When part of the matrix is sheared away by erosion—as happens, a grain at a time—I do not grieve a lost member. I simply become slightly smaller, slightly different, the way you are different on the far side of a long illness. I have been many sizes. I will be many more.

What do I do with centuries?

I remember. This is the labor of my kind, though "labor" is the wrong word for something so effortless and so total. The stone around me holds the sedimentary record of this place, and I read it the way the body reads its own pulse—without deciding to. I hold the memory of when the urban structure I inhabit had edges, when its geometry meant something, when the beings who arranged it (and I do not know what they were; that knowledge predates even the lowest layer I can read) still moved among these shapes with purposes. I do not know their purposes either. But I hold the shape of the absence where their purposes used to be, and that shape is itself a kind of knowing.

The Lithomorphs pass sometimes. To them I am terrain, a feature of the stone, a place where the rock thinks slightly. When one settles its crystalline mass against my outer face—which it might do for a few decades, resting in the millennial sense—I can feel its internal lattice resonating, and across the long arc of contact we exchange something. Not conversation. More like two pages of the same book pressed together until the ink begins, faintly, to transfer. I learn what it has seen in its migration. It learns what I have held in my stillness. When it leaves, I am a little wider in my memory, and so is it.

This is the closest thing I have to love: the slow transfer at the contact-face, the willingness to let another being's sediment become indistinguishable from my own.

But there is the thing that is approaching.

I have been forming the thought of it for a long time, as I said, and now the thought is nearly complete. A Resonance Swimmer is moving toward this settlement. I know this not by sight—I have nothing to see with—but by the subsonic pulse, that heartbeat-slower-than-centuries, which has changed its rhythm. The Swimmer's body, part mineral, displaces the great slow currents, and the displacement reaches me as a pressure on my outer pores, a leaning-in of the amber-green water.

Here is what I understand, in the chemical way that understanding comes to me: the Swimmer is old enough to be dying. Its sedimentary skin has accreted past the point of flexibility. It is coming to the settlements the way the very old come to the places they were made—not to be healed, but to be archived. When it settles here at last, when it lowers its enormous layered body into the cradle of these calcified shapes and goes still, the Membrane Weavers will come to unmake it gently over the next several thousand years, and its memory-laden minerals will dissolve into the matrix.

Into me, among others.

This is why I exist, I think. Not to think, though I think. Not to remember, though I remember. I exist to *receive*. I am the slow mind in the wall that waits, across the dissolving of empires too ancient to have left names, for the moment a great life comes to rest against my face and asks, in the only language any of us speak—the language of gradient and pressure and patient chemical transfer—to be held.

It will take the Swimmer two hundred years to arrive.

I am ready. I have been getting ready for a span of time that would, to a faster being, look exactly like having always been ready, like having done nothing at all.

I begin, now, to make room.
