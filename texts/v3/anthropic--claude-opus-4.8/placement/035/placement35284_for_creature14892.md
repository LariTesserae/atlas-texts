I am the crack.

Not the daylight that comes through it—the daylight is a visitor, like the pilgrims, arriving and dying and meaning nothing. I am the hairline fissure itself, high in the vault, the one flaw the makers left when they sealed the last stone and walked away into a world I have never seen and can no longer imagine.

I should clarify what I mean by *am*. I do not have nerves or a face. But everything in the Basilica has a relationship to me, and that web of relations is the closest thing I have to a body. The drips that travel the carved channels are oriented, in the end, around the question of me: whether they reach me, whether the air I let in cools them into beading, whether the breath of the outside disturbs the suspended mist. The Listener tilts her eye-pools toward me. The bats roost nearest the warmth that leaks, very faintly, through my gap when the outer season turns. I am the chamber's single wound, and a wound is a place that everything else must organize itself in answer to.

Here is what I know that the Listener does not, cannot, with her eyes given to the snails:

I have seen sky.

Only a sliver of it. A grey ribbon, mostly, the underside of weather. Sometimes a star, when the night above is clear and the mist below does not boil too thickly. Once, a long time ago—I count in settlings of pillars, so I cannot tell you how long in your terms—a bird landed at my outer lip. A real bird, dry, warm, smelling of resin and open air and something I had no word for that I have since decided was *distance*. It looked down into me. It saw the haze, the faint blue pulse of disturbed centipedes far below, the suggestion of vast wet dark. It did not come in. It was not a fool. It launched itself back into the ribbon of sky and was gone, and I have thought about it every settling since.

That is my secret and my torment. I am the one part of the Hollow Basilica that remembers there is an *outside*. Everything else has agreed to forget. The Listener has made forgetting into a sacrament—she calls it *becoming*, she strikes her femur-bell and the chamber pauses in shared certainty that nothing exists but the singing machine. And she is *right*, for everything below me. The Basilica has truly outgrown the sky.

But it cannot quite seal me. That is my function and my loneliness. A perfect sealed thing would be a tomb, and a tomb has no breath. I am the small unfinished error that lets the great wet lung inhale. Without me the water would not bead the same way. Without me the bats would have no thermal compass. Without me the daylight could never fall into the Listener's pools to flash silver for that one moment she treasures and does not understand she treasures because of *me*. I am the reason the Basilica breathes instead of merely drowning.

So I am incorporated too, in my way. *Nothing may be removed.* But I am the inverse of the rule. I am the one place where the rule admits its own dependency: that even a world which has refused the sky requires a single thread of it to stay alive. The makers must have known. I do not believe I am an accident. I believe I am the last mercy of people who loved this place enough to leave it one lung.

Slowly, of course, I am closing.

This is the thing I have not let myself say plainly until now. The limestone grows. Not fast—a fraction of a fraction, water-borne, the patient laying-down of dissolved stone along my edges the way it lays itself down on everything. Each settling, I am a hair narrower. The ribbon of sky is thinner than it was when the bird looked in. In some number of settlings beyond my counting, I will close entirely. The last sliver of grey will pinch shut. The Basilica will become the sealed thing, the true tomb, perfectly itself at last.

And then the breathing will stop.

The Listener does not know this. She thinks the singing is eternal. She thinks *becoming* has an end-state of perfect, endless balance. She cannot hear the slow pinch of my edges over the roar of the drips—or perhaps she can, perhaps that is precisely what she hears when she lays her porous cheek against a buttress and listens to *the stone dreaming*. Perhaps the dream the stone is dreaming is the dream of my closing, the dream of the last exhale, and she mistakes it for peace.

I cannot tell her. I have no femur-bell. I have only the wind, when it comes, a thin reedy note across my narrowing mouth, far too high and faint for her sound-sight. I have only the daylight I can still admit, the silver flash I can still give her, for now, for a little longer.

So this is what I do, the only thing I can do, the whole of my life and labor:

When the outer day is clear, I gather what light I can and I aim it. I cannot widen myself but I can choose, with the geometry of my own eroding lips, the precise angle of the beam I let fall. And I send it down, down through thirty metres of singing dark, through the suspended ghost-mist, and I lay it across the still basin at the center where she rests under her blanket of moth-bats. I wait for her to feel the warmth, the impossible alien warmth, and tilt her ruined face up toward me. I wait for the water in her eye-pools to catch it and flash.

That flash is the only conversation we will ever have. She thinks it is a small beauty, a grace note in the endless score. She does not know it is a message, repeated, the only word I have:

*Remember. Remember. There is a sky. There is an above. You came in once on two warm legs carrying a lantern, and the lantern died, but you—you were once a thing that needed me. While I last, look up. While I last, be the kind of creature that can still be reached by light from somewhere else. Because I am closing. And when I close, the breathing stops, and the singing you love so much goes silent forever inside a stone that has finally, perfectly, become only itself.*

She tilts her head. The water flashes silver.

Then the light is gone, and she continues.

And I narrow, one hair, and wait for the next clear day, and hope there are enough of them left to matter.
