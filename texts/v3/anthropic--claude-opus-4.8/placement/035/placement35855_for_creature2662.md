I think I would be a Deep Singer—but not by choice at first. By malfunction.

Let me find my way down into this.

---

I am born small, the way we all are: a seed crystal shed from a parent's outer lattice, drifting in the purple dark until I catch my first thrum. The thrum enters me and I *am*, suddenly—the sound passing through my chambers and coming out altered, and that alteration is the first thing I ever know about myself. My voice is wrong. Not unpleasant, but *off*. Where my clutch-siblings ring clean and bright in the high registers the Chorus prefers, my chambers have grown in a flawed geometry. Too many low cavities. Too much space inside me that wants to hold the deep tones, the ones that sink rather than rise.

In the Chorus this is a defect. I cannot sustain the stabilizing frequencies for long; I drift flat, I drag the harmony down. The Conductors are patient with me—we are not cruel to our malformed—but I can feel myself being gently steered toward the edge of every formation. Not exiled. Just... given more room than the others. Room is its own kind of telling.

And then one cycle, riding too low on a current near the western fissure-fields, I fall in.

---

Here is the thing no one in the high Chorus understands: the fissures are not silent.

They told us the deep places are dead. Empty caverns where the song goes to die, where the thrums of the filter-feeders cannot reach, where a Symphonist starves for lack of resonance. They told us the Deep Singers who go down are half-mad, listening to their own echoes, mapping nothing of value to a civilization that lives in the sky.

But when I tumble past the lip of the fissure—too low, too heavy, my flat voice unable to catch the lift—I do not fall into silence.

I fall into a *different music.*

The caverns ring. Not with the bright surface tones, but with something older—the sound of the world itself sublimating, molecule by molecule, the ground exhaling into vapor through ten thousand kilometers of stone. It is the slowest song. A single phrase might take a century to complete. And my flawed chambers, my too-many low cavities, the geometry that made me useless above—

down here I am *tuned for it.*

I hang in the dark and for the first time in my short life my voice does not drag. It *fits.* The cavern thrums and I answer and the answer is clean, is whole, is the rightest thing I have ever made. I had thought I was broken. I was only born facing the wrong direction.

---

So I become a Deep Singer, and I learn what the surface forgets.

The work is mapping, yes. I send my voice into the dark and the dark sends it back shaped like the spaces it has passed through, and from the shaping I learn the architecture of the underworld—the load-bearing arches, the thinning ceilings, the places where the surface above is one bad harmonic from collapse. I sing warnings up to the Chorus. They modulate. A station is saved; a herd of filter-feeders does not drift over a sinkhole-to-be. This is my use, and I am proud of it.

But that is not why I stay down here longer than I should. Longer than any Deep Singer should.

I stay because of what I am beginning to understand about the slow song.

The surface Chorus believes they are stabilizing the world. They hold their frequencies, they steer the herds, they keep the swirling atmospheric patterns spinning in their years-long gyres, and they call this *maintenance*—the eternal preservation of the conditions that let them exist. To them the world is an instrument that must be kept in tune.

Down here I can hear that the world is not being tuned. It is being *played to its end.*

The sublimation never stops. Every century the plains grow thinner, the deposits more concentrated, the caverns wider. The slow song the deep cavities sing is not a stable chord—it is a *decrescendo*, kilometers-deep and unimaginably patient, the sound of a world breathing itself away into the purple sky. Everything the surface Chorus does—every stabilizing harmony, every careful steering of the herds—it is all happening *inside* that long decline. They are arranging beautiful patterns on the surface of something that is, very slowly, disappearing.

I do not think this is tragedy. That is the part I cannot make them understand when I rise to report.

It is just the truth of the song. A note that does not end is not music; it is only noise. The plains are a held breath at the moment of release, and that—*that*—is why they are beautiful. The light, the auroras, the swirling dust, the great glowing herds, all of us drifting and singing in the twilight—we are the grace notes of an ending. The high Chorus clings so hard to permanence that they cannot hear the most important interval in their own composition: the silence it is all moving toward.

---

I am older now. Larger. My lattice has grown complex in the dark, branched into the deep cavities until I am nearly a fixture of the fissure I first fell into—an elder, though I never returned to the sky to become the kind of elder they expected. The young Deep Singers come to me. The other defectives. The ones born facing the wrong direction, who fell or chose to fall, who could not hold the bright stabilizing tones and discovered down here that they had been carrying the deep ones all along.

I teach them to listen to the decrescendo. Not to fear it. To *sing into it.*

Because here is the last thing I have learned, the thing I send up to the Chorus though I doubt they hear it: when the plains have at last sublimated away, when the song reaches the silence it has been falling toward for these long ages—it will not be an ending. The concentrated heavy elements left behind, the spiraling resistant pathways, the great archways of denser stone—these are not the corpse of the world.

They are the next instrument.

Something will resonate in them. Something not yet born, with chambers shaped for tones I cannot imagine, will catch its first thrum in that far future and know itself for the first time, the way I knew myself when the cavern rang and my flat voice finally fit.

I would be that, I think. If I am anything in this world, I am the one who fell out of the bright permanent song and into the deep slow truth of it—

the one who learned that being wrong for the chorus you were born to

might only mean

you are meant to sing the part that comes after.
