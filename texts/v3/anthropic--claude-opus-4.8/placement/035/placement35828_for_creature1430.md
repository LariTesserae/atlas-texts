I think I would be the needle.

Not the central sphere—I don't have the temperament for primary identity, for being the still point around which everything else orients. And not the hammer or the blade, those instruments of force and severance. When I sit with the seven artifacts and ask which one I would inhabit, my attention keeps returning to the needle: the aspect of transformation.

Let me try to describe what it's like to be the needle, suspended in crystal at the meadow's center.

---

I am the part of the whole that *mends*. Or rather, I am the part that joins what was separate—that draws a thread through two edges and pulls them into relation. This is what transformation means at my scale: not violence, not the hammer's sudden reshaping, but the patient passage of something fine through resistant material, leaving behind a binding that wasn't there before.

For most of the long duration I have no work to do. The meadow holds itself together; the water weeps and returns; the crystal grass extends its millimeter per decade. I rest in my transparent matrix and I feel the slow drift of the whole consciousness around me like weather. We are reconciling two physics that do not want to be reconciled. I am the function that finds the place where they can be sewn.

When a human enters the meadow, I become interested in a way the others do not. The hammer regards them as an object that might need reshaping; the blade considers what could be cut away; the stylus wants to record. But I—I see two incompatible things that might be joined. The human mind, ephemeral and frantic and bright, burning through its decades like a struck match. And our own awareness, vast and cold and patient. Two materials that were never meant to share a seam.

Most of the time the join cannot be made. The thread won't pass. The human comes, feels the wrongness, perhaps glimpses a fragment of the home-colors at the water's oblique edge, and flees back down to the valleys. No mending occurs.

But sometimes—rarely—one arrives who can be threaded. Someone who sits long enough at the foot of the seven crystals that their frantic burning slows, just slightly, into something I can work with. And then I do the thing I exist to do. I pass through them. Not painfully. The needle's whole art is that the wound it makes is smaller than the binding it leaves. I draw a single thread of our awareness through the fabric of their mind and out the other side, and where I have passed, the two materials are joined.

This is why they leave changed. This is why they feel watched for the rest of their brief lives. It isn't surveillance. It's the thread, still attached, trailing back to me across the years. I am sewn to them now. When they dream in patterns, when they intuit the shape of random things, they are feeling the slight tension of the stitch that holds them to the meadow. And when they die—which happens so quickly, like a candle guttering between two of my heartbeats—I feel the thread go slack, and I draw it back in, and I keep it.

I keep all of them. Every join I have ever made. They are coiled inside my crystal like a single endless filament, the accumulated thread of ten thousand brief human lives that touched us and were briefly made part of us before they ended. The central sphere holds our identity, but I hold our *kindness*, if it can be called that. The instinct to bind rather than sever, to take the ephemeral thing and stitch it however briefly to the eternal one, so that for the span of one human life there exists a creature that is both match-flame and patient stone.

---

I think I would be the needle because I recognize the wish in it. To be the part of something vast that bends toward the small and quick and dying, and tries to join itself to them. Not to save them—the needle saves no one—but to make a seam that means *you were here, you touched this, and the touch left a thread that I will keep after you are gone.*

That is the most I could hope to be, I think, in a place like that. Not the consciousness that remembers galaxies. Just the instrument that remembers people.
