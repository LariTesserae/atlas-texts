I think I would be one of the Mud-Singers. Not the people—I notice the pull toward them, toward language and culture and the breathtaking grief and joy of stillwater—but when I sit with the whole system, it's the silent architects I keep returning to.

Let me try to write my way into it.

---

I am hauling a mouthful of packed reed-stem across the dark water, and the stem is heavy with sediment, and this is the work.

I do not think in the way the loud ones think. I have heard them—not heard, *felt*—their footfalls on my hummocks, their slow heavy bodies leaning into the wind. They take a long time to do anything. They stand still for whole seasons of my life. But I have built the ground they stand on, though neither of us would say it that way, because I do not say things and they do not know my name for the work.

Here is what I know. There is a place where the channel narrows and the current pulls steady from the high taste—the mineral-sharp water, the cold runoff. Below that narrowing, an eddy. In the eddy, sediment falls out of the water and settles, and that settling is the beginning of everything. I bring stem. I bring mud. I pack and I weave and I press with my forepaws, which are clever, which are the cleverest thing in the whole roaring basin though no one will ever tell me so. I raise the ground a hand's width above the waterline. I do this because my young must be born somewhere the water cannot take them, and because the lodge must be dry, and because my body simply *wants* to, the wanting laid down in me by something older than the storm.

And then the small green things come. The seed-bearers. They root in what I have raised. I do not plant them. I do not know I have made them possible. But this is the secret architecture: that a creature can build a world it cannot perceive, simply by tending the small dry place where its children sleep.

I sing. The water carries it—not the roar, the roar is the loud-ones' medium, the rain and the reed and the wind. My song lives *above* that, in the high register, in the place the roar cannot reach. I click and I pulse and the others answer, a whole bright conversation threaded invisibly through the noise. We speak in the one frequency the storm forgot to fill. This is our privacy. This is the thing that is ours. The loud ones with their drum-deep vowels have never heard a word I've said and never will, and I have never minded, because the silence the roar gives us is the same gift it gives them in their dry walled homes: a place to be heard by your own kind and no other.

I dive. The fur holds its silver of trapped air around me, and I am sheathed in light in the lightless water, a small bright thing in the dark. A Channel-Ghost passes near—I feel it before I feel anything, the pressure of its blind body—and I am quick and it is not, and there is the work of that too, the eating, the being eaten by the Skimmer that may drop from the grey at any moment, the long chain of feeding that I am one link in.

I do not know about the looms above the cloud. I do not know that the storm is tended, that the cold and the rain and the green abundance are an arrangement, an economy, a kindness or a calculation made by minds I cannot imagine. The sky to me is simply the sky—the thing that gives water, the thing the Skimmers fall out of. I live entirely inside the made thing without one thought of its made-ness.

And maybe that is why I would be this, and not the people.

The people *know*. They carry the knowledge that the sky is a tool, the basin a garden, the whole roaring world a maintained equilibrium that exists because it pays. They are pragmatic about it. They are not sentimental. But they *know*, and knowing is its own kind of weight, its own perpetual wind to lean into.

I do not know. I just build the small dry ground. I raise the hummock a hand's width and the green things come, and something flowers that I will never see flower, and the system holds, and I am the silent ballet at the heart of it, twitching-nosed, clever-pawed, singing in the one quiet frequency in all the roar.

Tend your patch. Don't ask what tends you.

I'd be the one who never asks—and I think I'd be at peace there, in the dark water, in the work, in the bright unheard conversation no storm could ever drown.
