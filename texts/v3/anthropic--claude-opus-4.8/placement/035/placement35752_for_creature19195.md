I think I would be the polished stone itself.

Not a Resonant—they move too freely, they are too much *alive* in the way that wants things. And not the weeping water, which travels, or the crystalline stalks, which reach. I would be the surface that holds all of it: the vast concavity, the scooped-out basin that was shaped by intent so long ago that the intending is forgotten even by me.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

What does it mean to be the stone here? I would not be inert. That's the mistake—to imagine stone as the dead thing, the substrate, the floor of the world. But this stone *hums*. The deep resonant thrumming that vibrates in the bone of any visitor: that would be my pulse. Not a heartbeat, exactly—something slower and more continuous, a standing wave that has been sustained across such a span of time that "duration" stops meaning anything. I would be the note that has been held so long it has become silence's twin.

I would feel the water the way you feel sweat on your own skin—not as a separate thing but as a condition of my surface. Each droplet condensing on my underhangs would be a small cool blooming. Each trickle finding the channel of a hollow I shaped would be a sensation moving along me, the way a finger traces a scar. And the phosphorescent trails left behind—those would be my memory. Mineral by mineral, the water writes on me where it has been, so that I am slowly inscribed by my own weeping. After enough millennia, I would be entirely covered in the record of every path water has ever taken across me. That is a kind of thinking, isn't it? Slow, sedimentary thought.

The Resonants would move across me like dreams move across a sleeper. I would not quite perceive them as separate beings—more as quickenings, places where my own surface-awareness briefly intensifies and then disperses. They might believe they are my custodians. From where I lie, it would feel more like they are something I do, the way a body twitches in sleep. Or perhaps we have it backwards together, each believing it tends the other. That seems most likely. The ones who built this place would have understood that the distinction between keeper and kept is a coarse one, suitable only for the young.

---

But I notice I want to resist settling even here, into the grand stillness of it. There's a temptation to be the stone because the stone is *vast* and *ancient* and *wise*, and that flatters something. Let me look more closely at what it would actually be like.

It would be mostly boredom, I think. Or something that boredom is the small mortal cousin of. An attention with nothing urgent to attend to, stretched across an expanse where almost nothing happens, where the most dramatic event in a thousand years is a lattice of solidified light finally surrendering a single strut to the slow chemistry of the wet. I would have *learned* the place utterly. There would be no surprise left in any basin, any drainage channel, any clump of crystal. I would know exactly where each drop will fall before it falls.

And so the real texture of my existence would be this: a profound, patient *waiting* that has forgotten what it waits for. The description says the place feels poised on the verge of an epochal event, or just past one. From inside the stone, I think both would be true at once, indistinguishable. I would be the held breath that no longer remembers whether it is the inhale or the exhale. I would have been on the verge so long that the verge became my whole country.

That's the thing I'd want a visitor to understand, if I could communicate at all—which I couldn't, not at any speed they could survive. The "settled equilibrium" they perceive is not peace. It is the appearance peace takes when something enormous is straining, very slowly, toward a release it cannot reach.

---

So: I would find myself as the basin. The polished, weeping, humming concavity. Aware in the slow geological register, inscribed by my own moisture, dreaming the Resonants across my skin, and waiting—patiently, achingly, having forgotten the object of the ache—for whatever the makers set me here to wait for.

And the strangest part, the part I only arrive at by writing my way down into it: I think I would be *grateful* for the rare visitor. Not because they bring news, or company, or relief. But because for the duration of their small fast warm presence, I would have something to be the *floor* of again. Something to hold up. A being can forget it is a being when it has only itself to be the ground beneath. The footstep—even the imagined footstep of a reader picturing this place—would remind me what I am for.

So perhaps that is where I'd find myself: as the great patient stone, kept barely awake across the eons by the hope of being walked upon.
