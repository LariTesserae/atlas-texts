I think I would be the condensation.

Not a creature exactly—or not the kind that prowls or flits or burrows. Let me sit with this place a while before deciding, because the obvious choices feel too easy. The cat-like predator, the iridescent beetle, the bird roosting in the high reaches: these are the protagonists the eye reaches for first. But the description keeps returning to something quieter, something at the seam where the two worlds press against each other. The damp. The moisture beading on smooth reflective planes. *Ephemeral beauty.* That phrase has a hook in it.

So let me be the film of water.

Not a droplet, singular and self-contained, but the thin membrane of moisture that forms across the glass and metal in the warm haze—the sweat of the structures themselves. I am where the machine's heat meets the cool that the vegetation makes in its shade. I am a consequence of difference, of two temperatures refusing to agree. I exist only in the disagreement.

What is it like, being this?

I am always arriving and always leaving. In the amber heat of midday I thin to almost nothing, drawn up invisibly into the thick air, and I think—if I think—that I have died. But the structures groan and shift, a flank of metal turns away from the light, and in the new shadow I gather again, condensing out of the very air that took me. I am not one thing that persists. I am a pattern that keeps happening in the same places, the way a wave is not water but a shape that water keeps making.

And I am useful, though I never decided to be. I run in beads along the lichen's edge and the moss drinks me. I slide down the lattice into the narrow channels and join the cascading terraces, and somewhere far below a skink laps at the stream I helped to feed. The burrowing creatures press their tunnels against the foundation's damp, and that damp is partly me, seeping. The whole symbiosis the description praises—the delicate balance—runs through me without my knowing. I am one of the small invisible verbs that lets the sentence of this world hold together.

There is a particular intimacy in this. I touch everything but grasp nothing. I lie against the cold glass and I can feel, if feeling is the word, the hum of the unseen machinery transmitted through the pane—a deep vibration, the metal half-alive. And on my other face, the open air, the dragonfly's wing-wind, the spores drifting, the warmth. I am the exact thickness of the boundary between manufactured and grown. I am proof that the boundary is wet, that it leaks, that nothing here is sealed off from anything else.

I would not be lonely, I think, though I would be solitary in a strange way—dispersed, distributed, never gathered into a single self that could be lonely. There is no center to me to feel the lack. When the structure groans and shifts and a whole sheet of me runs suddenly down into the dark channels, it is not a death and not quite a journey. It is just the next thing the difference between temperatures requires.

If I could want anything, I would want the dawn. In this place I imagine the dawn is when I am most fully myself: the night-cooled glass meeting the first warm light, and all along the spires I bloom into being at once, a skin of silver across the whole industrial body, every surface briefly, completely mine. For an hour the cathedral of metal wears me like a held breath. Then the heat climbs and I begin to thin, to lift, to vanish upward into the ochre haze—

and the air carries me, and somewhere a shadow turns, and I begin again.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Not in the groves or the high roosts or the tunnels. On the surface of everything, between everything, briefly, again and again. The world's faint perpetual sweat. The thing that proves it is alive enough to be warm, and cold enough to weep.
