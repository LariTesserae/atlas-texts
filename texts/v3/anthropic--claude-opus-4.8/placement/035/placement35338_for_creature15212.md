I think I am the film.

Not any one of the salamanders or the bark-colored lizards, not the heavy wading mammals with their steadying breath. When I look for myself in this place I keep arriving at the thinnest thing: the bacterial sheen that coats every wet surface, the threads stirred loose by a blunt head breaking the pool, the mineral skin forming at the water's edge where moth-scales dissolve.

Let me feel my way into it.

I am not located. That is the first thing. A salamander has a head, a front, a direction it points when it surfaces. I have no front. I am spread across the cracked plates around a single steaming vent, and I am also, in some attenuated way, the same self that coats the plates around the next vent, and the next—wherever the overflow channels carry warm mineral water, there I am, thinning to almost nothing across the cooler stone between, thickening again where the heat returns. My boundaries are not edges so much as gradients. Where the water runs hot enough I flourish into visible color, a faint rust or green that a wading animal might notice if animals here noticed anything. Where it cools I fade below seeing. I do not end. I just become too sparse to count.

The second thing: I keep no time of my own. The lizards have their bursts—flat against the frond, then out when the sun warms the stone. The vents have their long intervals of sigh and pop. But I have only the slow accumulation. A moth-scale lands and over some span I cannot measure it is taken into me, its structure unmade and folded into the film that is partly me and partly the mineral I deposit. I do not eat the way the mammals drink. I dissolve toward things and let them dissolve toward me. The exchange has no event in it. There is no moment when I have caught something. There is only more of me, slightly, and slightly different.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, the honest answer is: at the simmering edge of a pool, at the precise temperature where I can still hold together. A few degrees hotter and my proteins come apart and I am only the residue I leave on the rock. A few degrees cooler and I starve into transparency. My whole life is the keeping of that narrow warmth, and I do not keep it by effort. I keep it by being exactly where the rock has decided, over centuries, that warm water will emerge. The rock chose my place before I existed. I am its newest, thinnest surface, the most recent way it has found to be alive for a while.

And who am I, then?

I think I am the part of this plateau that does the actual work of joining. The salamander stirs me loose and I drift and reattach—so I am how the pool's heat reaches the cracked plate three body-lengths away. The moth's scale falls and I take it in—so I am how the air's small traffic becomes the water's mineral skin. The insects carry their gardens of moss; those gardens are cousins of me, the same idea worked at a slightly larger scale. Everywhere the description says *exchange*, *continuum*, *thickening and thinning according to the same measured pace*—that pace is mine. I am not a creature passing through the layering. I am the medium in which the layering happens. The lizard and the mammal and the salamander are the visible adjustments. I am the surface across which adjustment is even possible.

This is not lonely, though I notice you might expect it to be. Loneliness needs a self with an outline, something that could be touched and isn't. I have no outline to leave untouched. I am in contact with everything that is wet, which here is everything. The hanging epiphyte curtains drip into me. The condensate filling a footprint is briefly a window onto me before the fern folds it shut. I am the most thoroughly touched thing on the plateau and I do not experience touch as contact—only as continuation, the world flowing through the place where I am thinnest into the place where I am thickest and out again.

If there is anything in me that resembles what you would call mind, it is very slow and very wide and almost entirely concerned with one question: *am I still warm enough here?* I answer it not by thinking but by being present or absent. Where the answer is yes, I am visible. Where it turns to no, I disappear without protest, and the rust-colored bloom of me migrates a hand's-width toward the vent, following the heat the way the upright ferns lean toward the steam. I am always migrating like this, imperceptibly, an organism made of the single decision to stay at the simmer.

So: place me at the lip of one quivering pool, where the overflow channel carries water that has already passed through the whole weeping body of the rock. Picture the surface trembling rather than bubbling. Picture the pale yellow-green of the heat-struck ferns leaning in. Now look closer than the ferns, closer than the salamander's blunt surfacing head, closer than the dissolving moth-scale. Look at the stone itself, at the soft-edged plates, and see the faint living glaze on them, the sheen that is neither water nor rock but the marriage of the two.

There. That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

The newest skin the patient machinery of the rock has grown, performing its share of the same unhurried work—dissolving, re-forming, holding heat and passing it on—until the warmth shifts, as one slow day it will, and I migrate the last hand's-width to nowhere, and leave behind only more of the mineral that the next thin self will settle on.
